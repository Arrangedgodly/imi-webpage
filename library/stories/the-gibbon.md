# The Gibbon

*A parody of "The Giver"*

Jonas was careful with language, because in the Canopy Community, language was everything. You used the right word or you were corrected. You didn't say you were "starving"; you said you were hungry. You didn't say you were "bored"; you said you were ready for a task.

Every evening, the family shared their feelings around the supper branch, and every feeling was received politely. "I felt impatient today when my troopmate was late to the banana delivery," his mother would say, and everyone would nod, and the impatience would be filed away like a folded leaf.

In the Community, everything was the Same, and everything was calm. All the trees were kept level with one another, so no monkey ever climbed too high or felt too small. Every morning the distribution crew delivered bananas to each family treehouse — and every banana was exactly the same dull brown, because Sameness kept everyone safe.

Jonas never questioned any of this. Why would he? Everyone was the same as everyone else, more or less, and December was coming.

At the Ceremony of Twelve, all the new-twelves received their Assignments. Asher, who forever mixed up his words — once asking for a "snack" when he meant a "smash" and being gently, endlessly corrected — got Assistant Director of Recreation. Gentle Fiona got Caretaker of the Old Growth. One by one they were called up, praised, and sent back down the branch, and each of them said, "Thank you for your childhood."

Jonas was not called.

He sat in agony while the Chief Elder skipped his number entirely, and then, at the very end, she summoned him alone.

"Jonas," she said, "you have been selected to be our next Receiver of Memory."

The current Receiver sat apart from everyone, at the canopy's edge. He was the oldest monkey anyone had ever seen, silver from crown to rump, and when Jonas scrambled up beside him, the old one chuckled.

"You may call me the Gibbon," he said. "Everyone does."

The rules of training were strange. Jonas could ask anyone any question, and they had to answer. He could not tell his family anything about his training, not even his dreams. And he was permitted — this one made his fur stand up — to lie.

"Have you ever lied to me?" Jonas asked his father that night, because now he was allowed to.

His father laughed. "Of course not," he said, and for the first time in his life, Jonas heard the sentence and did not believe it.

The Receiver's rooms were at the canopy's edge behind a door with a lock. Jonas had never seen a lock before. Nobody locked anything in the Community; there was nothing to steal and nobody who wanted to. And the walls — the walls were BOOKS, hundreds of them, when everyone else Jonas knew owned exactly three: the dictionary, the rulebook, and the canopy manual.

The training itself was simple and impossible. Jonas laid his hands on the Gibbon's back, as if to groom him, and the memories came.

Sunshine — real sunshine, blazing warmth flooding his fur until he yelped. A sled ride down a hill of cold white powder, whooping, in a banana-leaf sled. And then, one afternoon, the Gibbon gave him color.

"Look at the fig tree," the old one said.

Jonas looked up and gasped. One fig, hanging amid all that brown sameness, blazed scarlet. Red. The Community had fruit, but it had no red, and Jonas suddenly could not bear to give the color back.

Not all the memories were gifts. Some days the Gibbon pressed Jonas's hands flat against his shoulders and gave him an afternoon in a troop war: mud and thrown stones and a young soldier monkey begging for water while his friend held him and could do nothing at all. Jonas came out of that memory weeping.

"Why do I have to hold that one?" he begged.

"Because someone must," said the Gibbon. "The Community chose safety long ago. But wisdom is just memory with scars. You are the place we keep our scars."

"Was there ever another?" Jonas asked. "Another Receiver?"

The Gibbon was quiet a long moment. "Her name was Rosemary," he said at last. "Ten years ago. The memories grew heavy, and she asked to be released. They came for her the same evening." He looked out at the level canopy. "She was my daughter. You may tell no one. I never have. But you — you are permitted to ask, and I am permitted to lie, and tonight I won't."

At home, the questions piled up. Jonas had begun taking a little pill each morning, after he woke from a dream about grooming Fiona's tail and told the dream at breakfast and his mother had smiled tightly and said, "The Stirrings have come." But memory made the pill feel like a fog, and one morning he dropped it into the river, and the world came back brighter, and he let it.

"Do you love me?" he asked his parents that night, feeling brave.

"Jonas," said his mother, "precision of language, please. 'Love' is a very unspecific word."

Why no color? Why no hills? What was the Wild, the Elsewhere beyond the canopy? And what did "released" mean — the word they used when the Old were sent away, or a newling too small, like little Gabriel, the fretful infant his father had brought home, who could not sleep through the night?

Gabriel, it turned out, could sleep — if Jonas gave him memories. A sail on a lake, a hammock, sunlight. As Jonas rocked him, he whispered things no one else in the Community knew, and the baby sank into his fur and snored like a small engine.

Jonas's father, tender and smiling, worked at the nurturing center. One day Jonas was allowed to watch a release on the wall-screen. A newborn twin, the smaller of two, was weighed, wrapped, placed in a crate marked Z-O-O, and carried across the river, out of the Community, forever. His father waved goodbye and chuckled kindly at the little sleeping face.

And Jonas understood, finally, what released meant. Gone. Never coming back. Not dead — in a way worse than dead: alive, and lost, in a crate, behind glass, with strangers tapping on the window.

That night, Jonas and the Gibbon made a plan. Jonas would take Gabriel and go — out, into the Wild, where the planes could not easily follow. The memories Jonas carried would spill back to the people the moment he was far enough away, and then the Community would have to feel everything.

"Someone must stay and help them bear it," said the Gibbon. "They will need me. And when they no longer do —" he smiled, old and silver and tired — "perhaps I will ask to be released. Rosemary waited long enough."

When the moment came, Jonas took Gabriel, whose odd silver eyes were wide and wakeful in the dark, tucked him against his chest, and swung out along the river branches toward the Wild. Search planes droned overhead, and when their heat-eyes swept close, Jonas sent the memory of ice into his own skin, so the cameras found nothing but cold.

The Wild was hard. Jonas found fruit and shared it, though he was starving — starving, the wrong word and the true word. He climbed until his arms shook, and Gabriel grew heavy, and it seemed they could go no farther. The planes gave up, but so, almost, did he. His legs shook; the cold crept in; Gabriel cried with a thin little hungry sound, and Jonas told him about sails on lakes, and sunshine, and sleds, and ran out of warm memories to give.

And then Jonas felt a memory rise up to meet him — the sled — and there, impossibly, was a hill, and a banana-leaf sled waiting at the top of it, and lights below that he was certain were lanterns.

He wrapped Gabriel in his arms and went down, down, down, whooping into the cold, toward a sound he was certain was singing — a chorus of howlers, wild and warm, welcoming them in.

Behind him, across the vast distances of canopy and time, he could feel the memories flying home to the Community, giving back color and cold and courage to everyone he had left. Somewhere in a treehouse, a mother blinked at a scarlet fig. Somewhere a father put down a clipboard and, unaccountably, wept. And an old silver Gibbon, sitting alone at the canopy's edge, felt the flood coming like weather and opened his arms to it, because someone had taught him long ago what it was for.

He thought, from that far-away place, that he heard them singing too.

But perhaps it was only an echo.
