# The Capuchin at the End of the World

*A parody of "The Cabin at the End of the World"*

Wen was catching grasshoppers in the front yard of the treehouse when the stranger arrived.

She named every grasshopper she caught, then let it go; it made the enterprise fairer. She had just caught a big one and named him Emperor Fig when she looked up and saw an enormous gorilla at the edge of the clearing, holding wilted daisies.

He was huge. His knuckles were polite. He looked sadder than a zoo in the rain.

"Hello, Wen," he said. "My name is Lenny. We've never met. We're not here to hurt you, or your daddies. We need your help to save the world."

"Okay," said Wen. "Come in for tea, then."

Inside, Wen's daddies — Eric and Andrew, who had adopted her when she was a bundle of ears and opinions — were making breakfast smoothies. Lenny removed his hat. Three more strangers came up the path behind him: Sabrina, an orangutan schoolteacher with kind eyes and a bandage on her forehead; Adriane, a gangly gibbon who looked like he hadn't slept since the Dry Season; and Redmond, a mandrill in a stolen tracksuit who took one look at the door and said, "This is the one. Told you. Treehouse. Aesthetic."

Each of them carried something odd. Sabrina held pruning shears. Adriane carried a ladle. Lenny held the daisies and, in his other hand, a large banana hook of the kind used in fruit warehouses.

"Are you robbers?" asked Andrew pleasantly, still blending.

"No," said Lenny. "We've never been wrong."

He explained, with the apologetic tone of a monkey reading bad news off a cue card: the world was ending. Today. Unless Wen's family made a choice. Unless one of the three of them came with them, freely — a sacrifice. The implements were not weapons. The daisies were for kindness.

"How did you even find us?" asked Andrew. "Nobody knows this treehouse exists."

"We saw it," said Sabrina, touching the bandage on her forehead. "All four of us, the same morning. This kitchen. That window. The little one's jar."

"I don't have a jar," said Wen, holding her jar.

Eric laughed — then stopped, because Redmond had already let himself inside.

Nobody agrees on exactly how the door happened. There was boiling smoothie involved. There was a chair. When the dust settled, Redmond was down, the strangers had the family in the main room, and Wen had her back to the wall, furious, holding Emperor Fig in her fists like a grenade.

The strangers were very sorry about the jump ropes. They tied Eric and Andrew to the kitchen chairs with the jump ropes from the garage, apologizing the whole time, and Wen noticed that Sabrina retied Andrew's knot twice so it wouldn't bite his wrists. That was the worst part, somehow. They were kind. They had come to end the world, and they were kind.

"You're not who you think you are," Sabrina said to Eric, gently. "And the planes are falling."

Outside, over the hills, came a distant boom, a silver streak that could have been a plane, and the teacups rattled twice and went still.

Andrew turned on the little radio, hands shaking. Static — then a voice reading, in a monotone, what sounded like a shopping list. The television showed only a tide going out, and out, and out.

"I won't choose," said Eric. "You want me to believe the world ends unless I hand you my family. That's not faith. That's a knife with extra steps."

Lenny's eyes filled. "I wish you were right," he said. "We've never been wrong."

While the daddies argued with the strangers, Wen sat cross-legged by the window with the jar, catching her grasshoppers one at a time. Lenny watched her for a long time.

"You name them all?" he asked.

"You have to," said Wen. "Otherwise it's just taking."

Lenny nodded slowly, as if she had said something he'd been trying to remember for a thousand years. "We four made a list of everyone we could possibly tell," he said. "You were at the top. Because of the naming."

Then Redmond, moaning on the floor, stopped. Just like that, mid-breath. Sabrina closed his eyes and said something in a language Wen didn't know, and the sky went the color of an old bruise.

"You see?" whispered Adriane, rocking. "You see? It's accelerating."

The day went by like that. The strangers did not threaten. They tidied. They sang, once, an old camp song, badly, and Wen hated that it was her favorite song now. The rain began at four o'clock, stopped at half past — mid-drop — and did not resume, the half-finished drops hanging like beads. Every bird in the county rose at once, all facing the same direction, and would not come down. At some point Eric, concussed from the door, slept, and woke with a start and said, "I dreamed the door opened, and it was full of stars," and Andrew told him to hush, and the strangers all looked at each other.

In the kitchen drawer was the dart gun from Eric's zookeeping days. Andrew had been furious about it for six years — "this is exactly why I said no dart guns" — and it was the only argument they ever had twice. Eric got one hand free. Nobody but Wen saw him do it.

One by one, the strangers began to die. No blood, no drama. They simply finished, like sentences. Sabrina went mid-morning, apologizing, of all things, for the short notice. Adriane broke then — grief, all at once, the ladle shaking — and came at the chairs with no plan and no kindness left, and Eric shot him once in the haunch with a tranquilizer dart. Adriane slept in four steps. And then he finished too, asleep, mid-snore, and there was no way on this green earth to know, then or ever, which one had finished him: the dart, or the world.

At last it was Lenny alone with the family, huge and gray and sad, holding his wilted daisies.

"Please," he said. "I don't want to be right again."

Eric and Andrew looked at each other for a long time. Wen looked at both her daddies, and she did not cry, and Emperor Fig kept perfectly still in her hands.

"We're a family," said Andrew finally. "All three of us, or nothing."

"No," said Lenny softly. "I understand."

He sat down heavily on the floor, and he too finished — mid-breath, mid-sorrow, the daisies falling from his enormous gentle hand.

The radio came back on with perfect clarity: *—and in national news, nothing whatsoever has happened, officials confirm.*

Wen went to the window. The tide had gone out past all reason, and the sea stood far off, glimmering, like something holding its breath.

"Maybe it's just low tide," said Wen.

Her daddies took her hands, one on each side. On the way to the window, Wen stopped at the door and opened the jar, and Emperor Fig went out over the gravel, toward the sea, in enormous unhurried hops. She had named him, so it wasn't just taking.

The three of them stood at the window and watched the end of the world not happen yet.
