# The Wise Ape's Fur

*A parody of "The Wise Man's Fear"*

There are three things all wise apes fear: the sea in storm, a night with no moon, and the anger of a gentle monkey.

The Wayvine Inn stood quiet in the treetops of Newairre, and a silence of three parts lay over it. The first hung in the empty common room, where the fig-ale barrels sat unbroached. The second pooled in the rafters, where a silver-bright gibbon — Bast, the innkeeper's student — crouched listening for a sound that was not a sound. The third, thickest silence belonged to the innkeeper himself: Kote, red-furred, green-eyed, far too still for a chimp keeping a backwater grog-shop.

A grizzled spider-monkey stumbled in out of the dark with ink-stained fingers and a story he thought was his own.

"Chronicler," said Kote, reading him at a glance. "Waylaid on the road by screech — the singing jungle-spiders. Lucky it was me that found you, and not something worse. You want my story. Everyone does. Nobody believes it when they get it."

"Kvothe the Arcanut," Chronicler breathed. "Kvothe the Furless. Kvothe, who spoke to the Cthaeh and lived."

"Careful," said Kote, with a small, terrible smile. "That last one isn't bragging, it's a diagnosis. I'll tell it true. We start at the University, where I was eleven, brilliant, and the cheapest kind of arrogant."

He told it in pieces, while Bast hovered and the wind moved the vines.

The tuition interviews, where the masters bid his fees up and down like auctioneers and Master Fleamme smiled each term as he wrote a number to beggar a troupe of players. ("They will tell you it was spite. It was spite.") The medicine trials that earned him the name *Furless* — a whole winter bald as a peeled plantain. Denana, always arriving like weather, always leaving like weather, always wearing some other monkey's ring. ("Names choose you. I would have chosen better.")

Then the Maer of Severine, who hired him to court a lady, hunt the bandit troop bleeding his tax-vines dry, and — not incidentally — keep the Maer himself alive. His personal arcanist, a silk-furred fraud named Caudicuss, was mixing the Maer's daily tonic with a devotion that should have been the first clue. Kvothe tasted it, ran the numbers twice, and found a slow poison dressed up as a cordial.

"You are being murdered," he told the Maer, "one hospitable sip at a time, by the monkey who compliments your fur."

Caudicuss departed that night in a hurry and a small fire. And it was in the Maer's court that Kvothe met old Bredon, a courtier who taught him two things worth keeping: the game of tak, and the sentence at the top of this account.

"The wise ape fears three things," Bredon said, setting the board. "The sea in storm. A night with no moon. And a gentle monkey's anger. Not a soldier's, not a king's. A *gentle* monkey's." He turned a piece over in his paw. "The first two kill you quickly, boy. The third has been thinking about you for years."

Then the bandit hunt, and the Old Growth, and the scout named Tempi — a langur of the Odem, the mercenary troop who speak rarely, because in their culture words are like bananas: precious, rationed, never wasted on strangers.

"Their hands talk constantly, though," Kote said, and his hands moved briefly in the lamplight, quick and precise. "Two fingers flicked — *this one grieves*. A brush along the forearm — *we are safe to sit*. They taught me the Lethani, the right way of doing a thing, which is not a path and cannot be spoken, only walked."

The raid on the bandit camp went wrong the way his plans did: brilliantly, and then sideways. A storm, a burning camp, an arrow he never heard, and Kvothe woke not in the Old Growth but in the Fae — the twilight jungle where the fruit is always ripe and never falls — face to face with Fururian, whose fur is the reason for songs, from whose grove no monkey returns because none of them *want* to.

"I wove a cloak of shadow there," Kote said. "And a song. 'The Ringing of the Vines.' You'll never hear it sung properly, and you are welcome."

"You could have stayed," Bast said softly, from the rafters, in the voice of someone who had heard this part before and would happily hear it again.

"Everyone could have stayed. That is exactly her power." Kote looked into the fire. "I asked her one question, and that was my mistake. I asked about the Cthaeh."

"The oracle in the banyan," Chronicler said, hushed. "They say it sees all futures, and everyone who speaks with it is a plague ship."

"The Cthaeh," said Kote, "is a butterfly the size of your fist, sitting in a tree at the middle of everything, and it *will not stop talking*, and every word is chosen with perfect knowledge to cause the maximum possible harm. I asked it nothing. It told me everything. I will not repeat what it said about Denana, or the Maer, or you, Chronicler — yes, you. Even Fururian went still when I said I'd spoken to it. She carried me out of that grove the way you carry a kit away from a snake: fast, both hands, no eye contact."

He went on, because the night was long. The Odem had recalled Tempi for the crime of showing his hands to barbarians, and Kvothe — because he was Kvothe — had followed him to Haert, the hidden city of the Odem, to testify.

"Vashet met me at the gate. My teacher. She could kill me with her thumb, told me so on the first day, and by the tenth I believed it. They gave me three tests. The Lethani spoken in hands, so your own body cannot lie for you. A bout in the stone circle against a fighter with a face like a shut door. And the question: *why do you want this?* — to which there is no right answer, only the wrong ones you rehearse in advance." He moved his fingers again, briefly. "They gave me a name. Maedre. It means the ripe fruit, the falling branch, and the monkey who owns neither, all at once. Then they gave me a sword, which was redundant, because they had already given me the Lethani, and one of those you carry forever. The sword is called Peelcere. Its first owner called it Saicere. Both names are correct, and someday I'll explain that when I want to see you cry."

"And then home," Chronicler guessed.

"And then banished with a purse," said Kote. "The Maer married his lady, learned his savior was a rootless Edema-Ruh player with a reputation, and discovered simultaneously that he owed me his life and could not stand the smell of me. It is the natural arithmetic of courts: the debt and the distaste are added together, and the sum is a very polite letter and a very long distance. Even the Cthaeh couldn't put it funnier."

Chronicler laughed — and stopped, because the door had opened without the wind's help.

The stranger who walked in wore an oiled traveling coat and somebody else's smile. His eyes took in the room one monkey at a time, in the wrong order. When he moved, his joints bent with the loose, wrong-way ease of a rope with no bone in it.

"Rain's bad," the stranger said pleasantly, from several mouths away from where his voice had started. "Mind if I warm—"

He did not finish, because Bast hit him with a lamp, the table, and the doorway, in that order, and the fight that followed taught Chronicler more about fear in forty seconds than the road had taught him in forty years. Kote did not move at all. Kote watched, the way a fisher watches a line. When it was over — when the thing inside the stranger finally let the body drop and lay twitching its long fingers in the spill of the lantern — Kote said, quietly:

"Skin dancer. They ride a body the way a tick rides a dog. Rare. We were lucky."

"Lucky," said Chronicler, when he could speak.

"Finish the night's work," said Kote, standing — and for one moment Chronicler saw him move, perfectly, the sword there as if it had always been there, three shadows dropping off the porch rail into the dark. Then Kote caught himself being Kvothe, put it away, and was only an innkeeper again, holding a lamp against the night.

"That's not the end of the story," Chronicler protested.

"No," agreed Kvothe the Arcanut, Kvothe the Furless. "It's the end of *tonight's*. Three days, as agreed. Then you'll know what the wise apes fear — and why I am what's left."

He turned the lamp down. The Wayvine Inn held its three silences, and waited with him.
