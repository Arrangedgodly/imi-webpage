# The Sea of Monkeys

*A parody of "The Sea of Monsters"*

Percy Gibbon's sixth-grade year ended the way his school years always did: with an explosion. In his case, a dodgeball game against a team of giant cannibal baboons, who cheated by using actual cannonballs and eating the referee.

The only good news was Tyson — a big, gentle little gorilla who followed Percy like a shadow and talked to power tools. The two of them fled to Camp Half-Banana, the last safe place in the jungle for young monkey heroes — and found it falling apart.

Thalia's Great Banana Tree, planted at the border where a hero once fell, had kept the camp safe for years. Now its leaves were yellowing and its golden bunches dropping one by one — and with the tree dying, the magical borders were coming down. Worse, monsters had gotten in; Percy found himself fencing a pair of bull-headed colobuses on the climbing wall, which the lava made memorable.

Worse still: Chiron the activities director had been fired, replaced by Tantalus, a monkey cursed by the gods so that food forever snatches itself away from him at the last second. To a monkey, the gods could devise no crueler punishment. Tantalus took it out on everyone, and greeted every campfire with the toast, "Remember: it could always be worse. It could be you."

"The tree was poisoned," Percy said. "Somebody poisoned it."

"Correct," said Annabeth, a wise grey monkey with storm-grey eyes. "And the only cure for a poisoned tree is the Golden Peel."

Every monkey at camp knew the legend: the Golden Peel, torn from a dragon-guarded banana tree at the dawn of the jungle, could heal anything it touched. It lay at the far edge of the Sea of Monkeys — waters inside the Triangle of Tangles, where compasses spin and ships vanish between one wave and the next — in the cave of Polyphemus, a colossal one-eyed orangutan with a sheep farm and a temper.

Percy already knew Polyphemus was real, in the way you know things at three in the morning. He was dreaming, every night, through his empathy link with Grover — Percy's best friend, a young horned howler, lost somewhere out there. In the dreams Grover was in a bridal shop, hiding, whispering three words with enormous dignity: "HE. LIKES. ME." Then a wedding veil floated into view, and Percy woke up sweating.

Clarisse, a warlike young mandrill and daughter of Ape-res, god of war, won the official quest, and Tantalus handed her the blessing with open glee ("Finally, a quest I can sabotage with my eyes closed"). Percy, Annabeth, and Tyson — who, it turned out, was also a son of Bananadon, the sea god, and therefore Percy's little brother — snuck out after dark to run the quest their own way.

They rode a rickety vine-taxi driven by the Gray Sisters, three withered old baboons who shared a single eye between them and argued about the fare the entire trip. ("EYE ON THE ROAD!" "I gave you the eye last mile!") The Sisters dumped them at a roadside mart whose clerk had winged sandals on the wall and a name tag reading HERMES.

"Prophecy says you need help," said Hermes, ringing up nothing for a price of everything. "So: one thermos, containing the four winds. Shake before opening. Point the spout away from your face. And take care of my son Luke for me, if it comes to that." His smile went sideways. "It'll come to that."

The Sea of Monkeys earned its name quickly. Their raft — the survivors' wreckage of Clarisse's iron war-raft, the CSS Birmingham, which had already exploded once that week — passed a hulk crewed by zombie monkeys ("Don't tip them," said Annabeth. "Zombies can't swim, but they hold grudges"). Then came the island spa of the sorceress C.C., who greeted Percy with cucumber water and flattery, gave him a "healthy smoothie," and turned him into a pygmy marmoset — the smallest monkey in the world, small enough to fit in a teacup, which is precisely where they kept him.

"Your boy has become very compact," Annabeth observed, fishing Percy out of his cage. She bought him back with Hermes' vitamins.

Then came the Sirens, and the worst hour of Annabeth's life. She had told Percy, quietly, on a calm stretch of water, that she wanted to hear them — that no monkey who ever heard the Sirens regretted it, only survived it — so they lashed her to the mast with vine-cord and rowed past the rocks. The singing reached out across the water, and Annabeth, storm-grey and fierce, began to cry. She struggled, gently at first, then not gently; she worked one paw free reaching for something none of them could see. What she heard, she said afterward, was her family whole, and the sister she'd lost, and her mother Athena saying *well done* — everything the Sirens could find in her that wanted repairing. They got her clear of the rocks with three bruises and a broken thumbnail, and she sat wrapped in a blanket eating cold rations and saying "I'm fine" until it was almost true.

"I'm sorry," she said at last. "That's the thing about the Sirens. The song isn't a lie. It's just a promise nobody can keep."

Scylla and Charybdis closed the route behind them: a six-headed horror in the cliffs on one side, a whirlpool with a digestive opinion on the other. The raft ran the gap. It nearly made it. In the smoke and spray of the near miss, with the Birmingham's ammunition cooking off, Tyson stayed at the tow-line a second too long — and the blast took him over the side, and the sea closed over the little gorilla who talked to engines, and Bananadon's son went down in his father's own water.

Percy did not speak for a day and a half. Annabeth did not try to make him.

At last: Polyphemus's island. There, in a cave of mutton bones, they found Grover — in a full wedding veil, stalling for his life. The cyclops had poor eyesight, worse breath, and a proposal pending.

"I am NOT marrying that," Grover hissed through the veil. "The veil was a STRATEGY."

When Polyphemus discovered the groom was actually a rescue party, the island shook. Percy played the oldest trick in the scroll: when the cyclops demanded his name, he answered, "Nobanana." A sharpened fig-branch stake, a stampede, and one heroic cliff dive later, they were scrambling up the Golden Tree itself, where the Peel hung glowing like a small sun — while Polyphemus howled from the cave mouth, "NOBANANA! NOBANANA HAS BLINDED AND HUMBILIATED ME!"

Clarisse arrived — furious, alive, having survived her own sinking — since the prophecy said the quest must be hers. After a battle involving a giant orangutan, a flock of demon sheep, and a warlike mandrill with a very good arm, she carried the Peel home under her hat. On the way back, Luke Chimpellan, the traitor who had poisoned Thalia's tree in the first place, fought Percy aboard his luxury cruise liner, the Princess Banana-ndromeda, complete with traitor buffet. Luke confessed everything; the duel went badly; and Percy was rescued — barely, hilariously, in the nick — by a stampede of wild centaurs with paintball guns: the Party Ponies, Chiron's rowdiest cousins, who carried Percy off with one hoof over his head like a trophy melon.

Back at camp, the Golden Peel was pinned to Thalia's tree with a bronze tack. The tree flared back to full gold health — and then did something no one expected: it gave the Peel back its own payment, and the spirit within the tree woke up. Thalia — daughter of Zooze, lord of the skies — climbed down out of the branches, alive, twelve years old again, and immediately in competition with Percy for every prophecy in camp.

Chiron was reinstated the same afternoon, the camp council having concluded that an activities director you can't feed is worse than one you can't fire. Tantalus was recalled to the Underworld still reaching for a departing donut, shouting that he'd be back, that they couldn't keep him out forever. The donut did not agree.

"The Peel heals everything," Annabeth said, watching Thalia and Percy size each other up. "Including things we didn't know were wounded."

The borders of Camp Half-Banana held. The Sea of Monsters kept its monsters — it just got a little better at sharing them. Tyson, alive, rinsed ashore the next week on the back of a rainbow hippocampus named Rainbow, with a wrench and a grudge against a whirlpool. And somewhere offshore, a colossal one-eyed orangutan sat alone in his cave, nursing his bruises, his sheep, and a lifelong loathing of one particular name.

It was, by his own account, the second-worst day of his life. The worst had been the day he first heard it.
