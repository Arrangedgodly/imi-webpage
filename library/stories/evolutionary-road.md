# Evolutionary Road

*A parody of "Revolutionary Road"*

The final curtain of the Laurel Players' production of *The Petrified Banyan* fell to a silence so complete that April Squirrel could hear the moths in the floodlights. She stood center stage with her leading monkey, both of them frozen in a smile of stone, and waited through five, six, seven ticking seconds for the troop of the Orchard Hills Auditorium to remember itself and clap.

They clapped, eventually, the way you pat a child who has dropped its banana.

It had been April's idea, the little theater group of the Revolutionary — of the *Evolutionary* Road set. She had been its star, its founder, its hope; and tonight she had been terrible, and everyone in the dark had known it, and she had known they knew.

The drive home through the orchard suburb was where the first and greatest battle of their marriage was fought. Frank drove with his jaw working. He had drunk two brandies at the party afterward, and both of them could feel the joke building in him like sap in a boil, and April, staring straight ahead through the windshield at the identical gray trunks, said: "Don't, Frank. Whatever it is, don't."

"I'm sorry, did the lady say something? I was just thinking what a *triumph* —"

"Frank."

"No, but really. TheReviews! 'Miss Squirrel brought to the role of the paralyzed matriarch a stillness not attempted since the actual petrification of the actual banyan' —"

"Stop it."

"— and her supporting cast of suburban woodlice —"

"Stop it, stop it, *stop it!*" And then she was screaming at him, and he was screaming back, laughing and screaming in the way of monkeys who have been happy together and are finding out, in a moving car on a smooth new road, that they may never be happy again, and the fight rolled them out of the car, up the lawn, and into the pretty gray house, and did not spend itself until both of them stood in the kitchen, shaking, ashamed, hearts going like drums.

"You want to know the real difference between us, April?" Frank said, low. "You're always finding new ways to be tragic about nothing. I mean, my God, it's a little theater group. It isn't your *life*."

The terrible thing — the thing neither would say for weeks — was that it *was*.

Because understand what the Evolutionary Road colony was: the great experiment. Twenty years ago the boldest young monkeys of the city — the pamphlet-readers, the destiny-talkers — had come down out of the trees. Deliberately. Walked down the trunks, planted orchards in grids, built split-level burrows with picture windows and two-car grottos, and called it the next step. *We are not swinging anymore,* the sales brochures said. *We have evolved.*

Frank and April Squirrel were the crown of the experiment, and they knew it. Young, handsome, witty; he with his talkative charm and his interesting job at the Nutrix Company (where his father had worked thirty joyless years), she with her dark eyes and her theater; the couple who had come down from the trees *last*, with the most to prove. The neighbors — mild Shep and dear, doomed Milly Campbell — spoke of them with awe. *They're different. They're going to do things.*

It was April who found the way out. She came to Frank one night in her old brave voice, the theatrical voice, and laid it before him like a scene she'd rehearsed: they would sell the burrow. They would go — *up*. Back to the Old Canopy, the wild high forests of the far interior, where monkeys still swung. She had a job lined up: canopy-work, gathering, good money. Frank wouldn't work at all. Frank would *find himself* — read, think, figure out what he actually was, instead of playing Nutrix-ape in a gray office for a gray promotion.

"Me, carried," Frank said. "Carried by my wife. Up a tree."

"By your wife, Frank, to the only place that's ever been *real*. The first monkey in history to be ashamed of going back up." Her eyes were wet and shining. "I want to feel the wind through my fur again, Frank. Real wind. I don't want to look back some day from a picture window and realize I spent my whole life on the ground, being sensible."

And Frank — who deep down feared nothing so much as the test of his own promise — heard himself say yes. May! They would go in May. The colony buzzed. Milly Campbell cried a little at the cookout. It was the subject of the Evolutionary Road for a month; most monkeys talked, but the Squirrels — the Squirrels *swung*.

Then the machinery of the ground began, quietly, to reclaim them. The boss at Nutrix called Frank in — smartest young monkey we've got — and dangled the promotion, the gray office with the gray window, the salary that made the Canopy plan look like a kit's fantasy. And Maureen Grub, a small plump secretary chimp with admiring eyes, asked Frank to lunch, and Frank, terrified of May, went, and the afternoon that followed was the easiest, saddest thing he ever did — being admired for nothing was all he had ever wanted, and it had never once satisfied him.

"Frank," April said, reading it in him across the kitchen (she always read it; that was her gift and her doom), "don't you dare pretend you still want to go."

"Of course I want to go! What about the plan?"

"Tell me the truth. Look at me. Has it ever once occurred to you that I might be serious about anything?"

What undid them finally was a visit from the Givingses' son, John — the truth-teller of the book, the one certified monkey on the block. John Givings had been in the sanctuary for a nervous collapse, and his mother, Mrs. Givings (the realtor who had settled the whole Evolutionary Road and boasted of her discernment), brought him around to meet the interesting couple. John, all electric eyes and needle wit, saw them instantly: the showcase primates, the zoo's prize exhibit, the two monkeys performing *evolution* for the neighbors.

"Oh, this is rich," John said, delighted. "A whole colony of down-trodden monkeys congratulating themselves on walking. You're going *back* up? You? Then you're the only two here with a working tail nerve. But you won't go. You'll have the baby instead, or the promotion, or the whole bright boring bag of nuts, and you'll rot here on this road like the rest, going quietly bananas in your lovely gray cage."

He came again, later, when the Canopy plan had died of both baby and promotion — for April was pregnant again, third kit, and Frank had taken the office with the window — and John, told by April that the plan was dead and she'd never meant it, looked from her face to Frank's and said the thing April would carry to the last branch of her life: "I get it. You're the sick one now. You've found the one way to break his spirit without leaving the burrow."

In the last month, April went quiet and distant as a closed theater. She told them all it was a difficult pregnancy. She told them she was fine. And one gray afternoon she walked out of the burrow, down through the orchard, to the old wild fig at the county line, the one the county children called the Strangler, whose crown stood a hundred feet over the grid; and she climbed it, alone, to the highest springing bough, past the branches any sensible monkey would stop at; and she leapt — the Great Solo Glide, the unlearned leap, the one they tell kits about — and for one bright instant, witnesses swore, she flew.

She did not land. The hospital kept her alive one night. The kit was lost before the fall. Frank sat in the corridor with his fur full of burrs and his whole loud future canceled, and John Givings, in the sanctuary again, asked his mother how the Squirrels were, and laughed like a hyena at whatever she answered.

They stayed on the Evolutionary Road, after, the two children orbiting gray Frank, who grew quieter every year and told visitors, when they asked about April, that it had been an accident, a fall, these things happen — and who never once, after, climbed anything higher than the gutters.

And Mrs. Givings, walking her rounds, still told the story of the Squirrel burrow to new buyers, though it had become a hard story to end. She told it that evening to her husband Howard, on their porch — the one upstanding member of the road, Howard, who was deaf, and old, and content; deaf especially to the Evolutionary Road, to Nutrix, to the whole gray grid of it.

"...and of course, poor things, but you never really *knew* them, Howard; that was always the trouble — they never seemed to fit in somehow, and of course after the tragedy one was almost *glad* —"

Howard, who had heard his wife's voice for forty years and required none of it, reached up with a mild paw, switched off his hearing trumpet, and sat back in the dusk with a smile of perfect monkey peace while, above the orchard grid, the first bats went swinging by.
