# Anne of Green Gibbons

*A parody of "Anne of Green Gables"*

Matthew Cutcub, the shyest old spider monkey in Avon-tree, trotted to the station expecting to fetch a boy from the missionary society — a sturdy youngster to help with the fig harvest at Green Gibbons, the grove he shared with his sister Marilla.

Instead, under the great ficus, sat a small gibbon with bright reddish fur, enormous gray eyes, and a carpetbag woven of leaves.

"Isn't it all perfectly splendid?" she burst out, before Matthew could open his mouth. "I've been imagining the whole way. That bend in the path is the White Blossom Way, and that pond is the Lake of Shimmering Water, and it would hurt their feelings terribly to be called anything plainer. Oh, I'm so glad there's such a thing as a scope of imagination. If you have that, you're never lonely — you just look about and imagine things better than they are."

Matthew wished heartily that he was back at Green Gibbons. The gibbon talked the entire ride, pausing only to gasp.

"We sent for a boy," said Marilla grimly, when they arrived.

"I know, and I'm sorry I'm only me," said the gibbon. "Nobody has ever wanted me before — not properly. But if you kept me, I'd love this grove so hard it would ache. I'd call it home. I've never had a home. Just borrowed branches."

Marilla opened her mouth to say no. Then she caught the look on Matthew's face, and the look on the gibbon's, and said instead, "You may stay. On trial."

The trial lasted roughly a week before it was no longer a trial at all.

There was the matter of Diana Berry, the girl next tree, who became on the spot what Anne called a "kindred spirit" — bosom friends, sworn among the bromeliads. There was the matter of Gilbert Blythe, who on the first day of school called out "Rusty! Rusty! Rusty!" and got Anne's slate brought down on his head with a crack that split it clean in two.

"Anne," said Marilla, "you have a detestable temper."

"It's not temper," said Anne with dignity, holding the two halves of her slate. "It's a sense of honor. And it will never, never be mended — not with Gilbert — not until he apologizes in Latin."

There was the matter of the hair dye, purchased from a peddler, guaranteed to turn her unfortunate red fur a fashionable raven black. It turned her a brilliant, unmistakable green.

"Green!" wailed Anne, staring into the polished water basin. "Not even an interesting green. I look like an unripe banana. Mrs. Rachel Lynde says I look like a tree frog, and I had to agree with her — it's no use pretending about frogs."

"Your fur is green, Anne," said Marilla, "and it will simply have to grow out. It will teach you not to meddle."

"Everything in life is teaching me something," sighed Anne. "There are so many things to learn and only so many fur-lengths in which to learn them."

There was the matter of the raspberry cordial, which Anne served to Diana at her first afternoon tea and which turned out, upon investigation of everyone's manners, to be currant wine — three tumblerfuls of it. Mrs. Barry declared Diana should associate with no monkey who made her child stagger home singing, and the bosom friendship was sundered, and Anne grieved as only a gibbon with a freshly broken scope of imagination can grieve.

And then, one midnight, there was the matter of Minnie May.

Diana came hammering at the gable window, out of breath, her fur stiff with frost. Minnie May had the croup — badly — and the parents were away at the far orchard, and the young ones had sent for weren't back.

Marilla had raised a troop of young ones and knew croup from snuffles. She poured ipecac down the small throat — the dose measured by Anne, who had learned it caring for three sets of imaginary twins and one very real government of young cousins in her before-life. By three in the morning the crisis was over, and the young vet arrived to find nothing left to do.

"You saved that baby's life, Anne," he said. "You knew more than I did at the crisis. What a good little monkey you are."

Mrs. Berry came to the gable window that very week, personally, to say that Diana might have her bosom friend back. Anne afterward ranked it as the most romantic moment of her life, narrowly ahead of puffed sleeves.

But the puffed sleeves — the puffed sleeves must be told. The week of the Sunday-school concert, Matthew, shy as ever, sidled up with a parcel and shoved it at her.

"Puffed sleeves," he mumbled, going red around the ears. "The shop-monkey said they was fashionable. I — I didn't think they'd mind."

Anne tore off the wrappings and burst into tears. "Puffed sleeves! Ooh! This is the happiest moment of my life!" She clutched the parcel to her chest. "Nobody has ever done anything so perfectly kind for me in my whole life. I shall wear them until they are rags and then frame the rags."

The years went by the way good years do, too quickly to file. Anne studied for Queen's Academy with Matthew driving her to exams in the old cart, quiet with pride; she won the Avery scholarship, the biggest thing that had ever happened to a Cutcub, with Gilbert Blythe — inevitable as sunburn — coming second. She was going to Redmond College in the fall. She stood in the orchard the evening the news came and thought the whole sky was her diploma.

It was that same spring that the bank failed. And Matthew, who had heard the news read aloud, put down his paper at the table with a small sound, and stood, and was gone before he reached the door — the heart of the gentlest old spider monkey in Avon-tree stopping like a clock that had done its work perfectly and needed no more winding.

Anne grieved the way she did everything: entirely. "I shall never love any place the way I loved him," she whispered at the window. "It seems impossible that the grove goes on."

But the grove had needs of its own. Marilla's eyes were failing; the specialist in town said glasses could not save what was left. And the bank failure meant Green Gibbons would have to be sold to cover the debts.

"Marilla," said Anne, the night the decision hung, "I'm not going to Redmond. I'm staying to teach. There's a school at Carmody, and I can walk it, and we will keep Green Gibbons."

"Anne, you can't give up your scholarship —"

"I can give up anything except you and this grove," said Anne. "Everything I ever imagined turned out to be a person or a place I already had. I used to think the way ahead was the important path. But there's a bend in the road, Marilla — and I've a strong suspicion that what lies around the bend is just more of the road, but seen from somewhere kinder."

The school at Carmody did not, in the end, need a gibbon. The school at Avon-tree did — because Gilbert Blythe, who had been offered it, went to the trustees and declined it in Anne's favor, saying only that he had made other arrangements, and never told her, and she found out anyway, the way things are found out in villages.

"Gilbert Blythe," she said, cornering him at the brook, "you gave up the Avonlea school so I could stay with Marilla. Why would you do that for a monkey who broke a slate over your head?"

"Because you've been the most interesting monkey in Avon-tree since the day you got off the train," said Gilbert, "and because you once told me my apology had to be in Latin." He cleared his throat. "*Ignosce mihi.* I'm sorry, Anne — for Rusty, for everything, for five years of being too proud to say so."

"Latin," said Anne, with her chin up and her eyes wet, "is the most beautiful language in the world when you use it properly." And she put out her paw, and Gilbert took it, and the feud of the slate ended on the bank of the brook — which is the proper place for feuds to end.

That evening Anne stood at the window of the little room in the gables of the old fig tree, and looked out over the grove Matthew had planted and loved and left her.

"Marilla," she said, "do you know what I think? Tomorrow is always fresh, with no mistakes in it yet."

"No mistakes yet," agreed Marilla, smoothing her apron. "Which is more than can be said for today."

"Then I shall begin again tomorrow," said Anne. "Green Gibbons is home now. I've looked for home my whole life — imagining it, naming it, hunting for it — and it was here all along, halfway up a fig tree, all along."

And Marilla, who did not hold with sentiment, went quietly to the pantry and got out the good bananas. The bend in the road lay just ahead, and there was no mistake in it at all.
