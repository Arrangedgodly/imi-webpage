# The Bananation Game

*A parody of "The Damnation Game"*

Marty Strauss was released four years early, into the rain, with a cardboard suitcase and a job offer he didn't understand.

"Personal assistant," said the driver, a sleek macaque in a raincoat, opening the car door like a waiter. "To Mr. Whitehead. You were recommended."

"I was recommended by *nobody*," said Marty. "I'm a banana-runner. Twelve years, three convictions, no friends."

"Exactly," said the driver, and drove him out of the city and up the long drive of the Sanctuary: a walled estate of lawns and glasshouses, at whose heart, behind a wall of its own, stood a white house like a temple to somebody's luck.

Joseph Whitehead was waiting on the porch: a silverback gorilla of great age and greater presence, fur gone snow at the shoulders, eyes quick as a dealer's.

"Babyface Marty," he said. "The chimp who never lost his temper in twelve years inside. A monkey who can wait — do you know how rare that is?" He peeled a banana with tremendous ceremony, took one bite, and handed Marty the rest. "That is the entire job, Mr. Strauss. You stay beside me, and you wait, whatever happens. Can you do that?"

"For how long?"

Whitehead looked past him at the winter sky, and something old crossed his face like cloud shadow.

"He comes in the spring," he said.

Marty should have asked who. But the Sanctuary was warm, the pay was absurd, and there was a girl.

The girl was Whitehead's daughter, Carys — a lean, sharp-eyed bonobo with a bob of black fur and a habit she didn't hide: little silver packets of fermented pellets she chewed at night to be somewhere else for a while. "Don't," she said, the first time they talked, on the greenhouse roof. "Don't be kind about it, everyone's kind about it. Ask me something real instead."

"All right," said Marty. "Who's He?"

And Carys, who mocked everything, didn't mock that. She looked down at the garden, where the peacocks were shrieking like rusted hinges, and said: "There's a room in this house with no door from the outside. Dad calls it the chapel. There's a card table in it, one chair, a revolver — unloaded, I checked at twelve — and a pack of cards so old they're soft. Every year on the same night Dad goes in alone and deals two hands and touches neither. I looked through the vent once." Her knuckles whitened on the gutter. "He was crying, Marty. My father, who outbid governments. Crying like a baby chimp."

"Whose chair is the second hand for?"

"That's the thing," said Carys. "It's *occupied.*"

Spring came early that year.

The first sign was the dogs. The Sanctuary kept a pack of four, woolly pale beasts the size of small bears, and in the first warm week of March they began to sit at moonrise in a row facing the front gate, silent, ears up, like an honor guard — and nothing Marty or the handlers did would move them until dawn.

The second sign was Toye.

Toye had been Marty's cellmate for six years: a big loyal baboon with a talent for locked doors. He turned up at the wall one wild night, wanting to rob the place — "one job, Marty, they got a fortune in there" — and Marty talked him over the wall and into a rhododendron, and it was almost funny until the dogs found Toye and did not touch him. They sat around him in a ring all night, patient as chauffeurs, and at dawn Toye walked out the front gate, gray-faced, and gave himself up at the nearest police station just to be somewhere with electric light. He told anyone who'd listen that a monkey had been standing *inside* the wall — an old narrow monkey in a long European coat, leaning there like fog, saying, "Not this one, children. He isn't on the list."

The gate bell rang on the first true day of spring.

Marty answered it because everyone else was suddenly, terribly busy being deaf. On the step stood a langur: old, elegant, narrow as a folded ruler, coat out of another century, face like a closed ledger, cologne of sweet tropical rot — overripe fruit on the turn.

"Mr. Mamoulian," said the langur, and bowed, precisely. "For Mr. Whitehead. Tell him: the game resumes. And give him this."

The card he passed Marty was a joker, soft with age, printed in a style dead eighty years, smelling of the inside of a suitcase left too long in the sun.

Marty delivered it. Whitehead's paw shook so hard the tea rang against the saucer, and behind him, at the window, Carys had gone the color of the milk in it.

"Dad. Who is he?"

"He's what I owe," said Whitehead. "Come to the chapel, both of you. If it's going to happen, it happens in front of witnesses. That was always the rule."

The chapel was white and bare, and the story came out of the old gorilla in pieces, over the card table, while somewhere in the house a door closed for every sentence.

"February, 1944. I was a private, in a camp, dying of a fever they had no name for. And a prisoner walked in who was never processed — no record, no country; the guards swore he'd always been there. He found me one night with the last thing I owned: this pack of cards. He offered me a game. He called it Bananation." Whitehead turned the joker over and over. "Five hands. The winner's prize: survival, luck past all decency, and forty years of being the richest monkey in England. The loser pays the debt. He never said in what. I was dying, children; I thought *what debt beats this?*"

"You played," said Carys flatly.

"I played. And in the last hand I felt a card slide to me from my own sleeve — and I have never knowingly cheated before or since, but the ace came *hot*, like it wanted to be mine, and I let it come. And the langur looked at his hand, and looked at me — and folded. He *let* me win. 'You cheated, little ape,' he said, 'and now the win is mine, and so, when I collect, are you.' That was the clause. He has let me ripen for eighty years. He told me once there used to be others — a whole silent troop of them, dealers in debt. He calls himself the Last Langur-o-pean. And he collects in spring."

"Collects *what?*" said Marty.

"That," said Whitehead, "is the game."

They played by lamplight, the old gorilla and the old langur, Carys and Marty witnessing at the wall, the dogs sitting outside in a silent ring, the house ticking like a clock.

It was Bananation — five hands, dealer's choice, the pack soft as moths. Whitehead played like a monkey inspired; twice Marty saw him pull cards from nowhere, aces arriving hot and eager, and he understood: the *luck* was real, forty years of it piled in Whitehead's sleeves like savings, and he was spending it back across the table, one miracle at a time.

And Mamoulian folded. Every hand. For an hour, for two: patient, courteous, folding, folding, while Whitehead won and won and grew old before their eyes — until the gorilla's luck ran to its last ace, and the langur finally, gently, turned over his own cards and showed the table what he had held all night, every night, for eighty years.

Nothing. He had never once been dealt a winning hand. He had never needed to be.

"The debt, Mr. Whitehead," said Mamoulian, "was never the money. A monkey who would cheat Death himself at cards — and *rejoice* — signs a different sort of paper. You have had your forty years of winning. The stake is that you now understand precisely what it was all *for.* That is my collection. Regret, complete. The house takes it. The house always takes it."

Carys was already moving — the silver packet, the pellets, everything she'd swallowed for years to be somewhere else — flung into the white fireplace, where they caught with a smell like a greenhouse on fire, like a decade of hiding going up. In the smoke and the shouting, Whitehead did the one clean thing left in his ledger: he stood, gathered eighty years of aces to his chest like a monkey gathering his own harvest, and walked into the smoke.

By morning the chapel wall was scorched, the estate's east wing stood black and honest against the sky, and on the table they found all that was left of Joseph Whitehead: one card, the ace, laid face up — a debt receipted.

Of Mamoulian there was nothing at all, except — Marty found it later in his own coat pocket, and never could say how it got there — one soft old joker and a calling card in a beautiful dead hand:

*Until the next hand. There is always a next hand. — M.*

Marty and Carys were married in the spring, quietly. Years later, at a fairground, a gypsy chimp offered Marty a wager on a game of chance, and Marty — babyface Marty, who had watched a whole fortune folded away like a trick — put his paws flat on the table and said the only thing the Sanctuary ever taught him:

"Deal me out."

The only winning move in Bananation — and the only move the house can't take.
