# Nit Watch

*A parody of "Night Watch"*

The night was storming, the city was in flames of rumor, and Commander Sir Sam Vines of the Ankh-Morepork City Watch was hunting a baboon.

Carcer Dun had killed two coppers with a knife and a grin, and now he was cornered on the roof of the Unseen University Library, in the middle of a magical thunderstorm — on the worst possible night, because across the city, in the Rimside Hospital, Lady Sybil was nine months and several strong words into labor.

Lightning hit the Library. The world went white.

Vines opened his eyes on wet cobbles, a darker city, and the smell of a revolution two days from boiling over. The Library's accident had thrown him and Carcer thirty years into the past.

Vines knew the history. In two days the Glorious Revolution of Treacle Vine Road would happen — the barricades, the speeches, the cavalry charge that killed thirty people, among them one honest copper named John Keel, who had taught a young hoodlum named Sam Vines what a watchman could be.

So Vines climbed the steps of the old Watch House.

"Name?"

"Keel. John Keel. Sergeant-at-arms, transferred from Pseudopolis."

Somewhere in a pauper's field the real Keel was already dead — Carcer had seen to that within an hour of arriving — and history had a hole in it shaped exactly like one dead sergeant. Vines stepped into the hole.

The recruits were babies. Young Fred Colon, pink and earnest. Young Nobby Nobbs, small and alarming, an odour in a tunic. A howler named Reg Shoe, justice in his knapsack, who would one day be so devoted to the cause that not even dying would interrupt his attendance record. And in the second row, a lean young monkey with a snarl he'd borrowed from somewhere and was still too young to pay for: Sam Vines, seventeen, whom nobody had yet taught anything worth knowing.

"Right, you 'orrible shower," said Sergeant Keel. "Lesson one. You're coppers. Not soldiers, not politicians. People out there will wave flags and call you heroes of the revolution, and they'll be lying. You do the job in front of you. The job in front of you is *people*. Anyone who throws eggs at you, arrest. Anyone who throws eggs at the crowd from behind you, arrest harder."

"What if the law says one thing and the sergeant says another?" asked young Sam Vines.

"Then you remember the law's a mirror, lad. It only shows you what's put in front of it. The sergeant's the one who has to look at himself tomorrow."

Young Sam wrote that down. He wrote down everything. Vines tried very hard not to look at him, because when he did he could see the whole street ahead of the boy, every dark alley of it, and his own name written through the middle.

The Unmentionables were the other problem: Captain Swing's secret police, taking citizens away in the night for remarking that things were a bit unfair. Swing himself was a thin, reasonable monkey with a cabinet of calipers, who measured skulls.

"Crime is in the shape, Sergeant Keel," Swing explained, over tea Vines did not touch. "Give me an ear measurement and I will give you a thief. You have the look, incidentally, of a monkey who will one day cause trouble. I measure that, too."

"Measure this," said Vines, and arrested him. One by one, wearing borrowed shadows, Vines took the Unmentionables' safehouses apart and freed the prisoners — among them a wide-eyed youngster who needed only a square meal and someone to say he was worth something. Vines fed him and set him on the path to becoming a proper copper.

Then, on the twenty-fifth of May, the barricades went up on Treacle Vine Road. Furniture, floorboards, cartwheels, whole orchard-loads of branches, and every spare tea-urn in the city. The watchmen wore sprigs of lilac in their collars, so they could tell each other apart in the smoke.

"They're singing," said Reg Shoe, from the barricade top.

*All the little howlers howl up, howl up, howl up —
All the little howlers, howling in the dark —
There's a better canopy coming, boys,
Somewhere up above —*

"Don't sing it," said Vines quietly. "It's a nice song. It gets people killed. You want to be proud of something? Be proud of the ones who didn't fire, not the ones who did."

He did what Sergeant Keel had never had time to do: he made the barricades a Watch matter, every post manned by a copper, every gap left open at the back, and when the singing started he sent the children home to bed through the alleys, growling, on the grounds that a revolution that needed children was a revolution that had already lost.

The night before the end, an old sweeper langur named Lu-Tze, who had been sweeping the same corner with terrible significance all week, materialized beside him. "You could stop it now, commander. Keep the barricades standing. History is mostly the things people decided not to accept that morning."

"And break everything downstream? There's a boy in this house whose whole life is being Sam Vines. If history bends, he might never get to be. And there's a boy across the river, thirty years from here, waiting to be born tonight."

"Then you must do the hardest thing," said the sweeper, "which is let it hurt."

The charge came at dawn. Sergeant Keel did what history required — he held the line so the crowds could get out the back ways — and by the time it was over the barricades were down, Reg Shoe was dead (Reg Shoe did not let this stop him; he reported for his next shift as usual, grey, damp, and complaining that the paperwork had gone down with him), and the city held its breath.

Carcer had been wearing the revolution's sash by night and the Unmentionables' badge by day, killing cheerfully for both sides. Vines took him on the rooftops, above the smoke, while below them the last of the lilac moved through the crowds.

"You're a killer, Carcer."

"You're a killer too, commander. We're not so different, you and me."

"No," said Vines, cuffing him. "We're both alive, and only one of us is going to stay that way *free*."

The last thing Vines saw of the past was young Sam Vines at the door of the old Watch House, hand raised, holding it like a promise.

"Goodbye, lad," said Vines. "See you in thirty years."

"Yes, sir!" said young Sam, and meant it for both of them.

The lightning took them home. In the present day nothing had changed, and everything had: lilac bloomed in the University gardens on the morning of the twenty-fifth, and Vines wore a sprig in his collar, as did old Fred Colon, old Nobby Nobbs, and even Reg Shoe, who was dead and without circulation but wore his anyway. Vines went, before anything else, to a small grave in a pauper's field, and left flowers on it: JOHN KEEL, it said. *He did the job in front of him.*

Then he went to the hospital, where the midwife handed him a small, furious, red-faced monkey with a grip like the long arm of the law.

"Sam," said Lady Sybil, tired and radiant. "Like his father."

"Which one?" said Vines, and his voice did something uncommanded.

He held his son at the window that night, looking out over the city.

"How are you feeling, Sam?"

"Like I did the job in front of me," said Vines.

That was all the epilogue he needed.
