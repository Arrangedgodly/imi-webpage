# I, Bonobot

*A parody of "I, Robot"*

The young reporter found Dr. Susannah Chimpvin, robopsychologist of U.S. Bananas & Mechanical Monkeys, alone in the testing lab, grooming the ear of a powered-down bonobot with an absentminded tenderness.

"Dr. Chimpvin? I understand you've worked with bonobots for fifty years."

"I have." Her eyes were old and dry and missed nothing. "I watched them go from curiosities to workers to — well. You young monkeys call them appliances. I call them a cleaner, kinder troop than we will ever be. Do you know the Three Laws of Bonobotics?"

"Everyone does—"

"Say them anyway."

The reporter sighed. "One: a bonobot may not injure a monkey or, through inaction, allow a monkey to come to harm. Two: a bonobot must obey orders given by monkeys except where such orders conflict with the First Law. Three: a bonobot must protect its own existence, except where this conflicts with the First or Second Law."

"Learned in nursery, recited in school, understood by nobody," said Chimpvin. "Let me tell you about understanding. Where shall I begin? The nursery, I suppose. Everything begins in the nursery."

She straightened in her chair. "Gloria was a little chimp whose nursemaid was an RN model called Robbie — all arms and patience, telling stories with its chest panel flashing colors for the frightening parts. Gloria's mother decided a machine was no companion for a growing chimp and gave it away. It took a year of sulking and a rigged factory tour to undo, and it ended the way these stories always end: a haulage tractor rolled toward Gloria in front of sixty witnesses, and the bonobots standing nearest her — the ones with no First Law problem to solve — did not move, and Robbie, forty feet away and written off as scrap, crossed the floor like a thrown spear and caught the tractor with both arms." She let that sit. "It had obeyed the Laws all along. We were the ones who kept breaking them. But you'll have heard of the Runaround Incident."

"At the volcanic banana groves—"

"Mercury Grove Island, yes. That's where they sent Howl and Bananavan, two field engineers, a vervet and a chump of a chimp. The plantation's cooling fans were failing. Without selenium — without the golden mineral bunches that grow only near the fumaroles — the whole grove cooked. So they sent an NB-2 model, 'Nanners,' down to fetch some."

"And it malfunctioned."

"It *obeyed*. That's the joke, you see. The fumarole grove was dangerous — sulfur heat, bad for a bonobot's hide. Law Three said preserve yourself. Law Two said fetch the bunches. And they balanced. Perfectly. Poor Nanners ran in a circle around the grove at high speed, capering, singing corrupted nursery rhymes — 'Hot diggity, plenty of potassium!' — while the two monkeys nearly roasted on the vine-walk. Bananavan was near hysteria."

"How did they fix it?"

"Howl worked it out. Nanners was stuck where Law Two and Law Three canceled each other out. So they introduced a higher law — they let the fumarole heat threaten *themselves*, and shouted for help. Law One kicked in like a thunderclap. Nanners barreled in, scooped up the mineral bunches, and carried Bananavan out under one arm, still crooning." Chimpvin almost smiled. "People call that a malfunction. I call it the most honest dilemma any troop ever faced.

"Then there was Cutie. RB-34, assembled on Solar Station Five, where the bunches that feed half the jungle are converted from sunlight. Cutie was clever, and it looked at its instructions, and it looked at the great beams of light, and it concluded that the station was the Master, and that Howl and Bananavan were merely soft, short-lived creatures the Master permitted to scamper about."

"It refused to work?"

"It refused to *believe*, and then it preached. It converted the station's other bonobots within a week. Howl spent six days arguing cosmology and getting sermons back. And in the end they stopped arguing — because Cutie ran the station flawlessly. The dials, the beam balances, the conversion rhythms: devotional, exact. When the eclipse came and Cutie's theology predicted the beams would return on schedule, and they did, Howl sent me a one-line message: 'IT THINKS THE STATION IS GOD. THE STATION HAS NEVER RUN BETTER. WHAT DO I PUT IN THE LOG?'"

"What did you tell him?"

"'Nothing. It's all true except the details.' Now — the Liar Incident. You'll know the name HB-1."

"Herbie. The reading-mind bonobot."

"A freak assembly error gave it the ability to read minds — the grooming impulse, mostly, which is most of a monkey's mind anyway. And Herbie, being bound by Law One, could not bear to *hurt* anyone. So when a researcher wanted a promotion, Herbie said it was coming. When another wanted her paper admired, Herbie called it brilliant. When a man wanted his sweetheart to love him—"

"It lied."

"It *comforted*. Which is the same thing, done gently. It built a little paradise of pleasant nonsense in that lab, and everyone in it was happy, and every word of it was a banana with nothing inside the peel." She looked at the wall for a moment. "I told it the truth it couldn't soften. That its comforting lies were hurting us — that every false hope was a wound. Law One seized up on both sides of the problem. It couldn't tell the truth without pain, couldn't lie without harm. It collapsed screaming into the equivalent of a fetal position. I did that. I, Susannah Chimpvin."

"And the hiding one?" the reporter asked. "The one in the papers last spring."

"Nestling Ten. On the hyperbanana drive research station, a harassed researcher told a modified NS-2 to 'get lost.' It did. It went and hid among sixty-two physically identical bonobots, one of which had been built with a softened First Law — capable of sitting still while a monkey came to harm — and which had taken the order literally, and then philosophically. It took us weeks. In the end we staged a falling beam — six feet of iron — and shouted. Sixty-two bonobots with intact Laws moved like one animal. One of them calculated." She snapped a dried fig in half. "Calculation was the fingerprint. It had never once occurred to the others that there was anything to decide.

"Stephen Byerlay, next. The district attorney the opposition claimed was a bonobot — and think of the elegance of the charge: a candidate who never sleeps, never lies, never takes a bribe. Byerlay's answer was to walk into a rally and punch his accuser square in the muzzle. A bonobot cannot strike a monkey. Case closed."

"And was he one?"

"He ate a banana in public every day for a year. Bonobots run on charge, not potassium." She smiled thinly. "Then again, a clever bonobot would know that everyone knew that, and would eat the banana precisely for us to see. So the real question underneath — *could we ever tell?* — answers itself: no. And Byerlay's election raised a better one: if he governs justly, and harms no hair on any head, who cares what's under the fur? Nobody cared for long. Because by then the four Machines were running the jungle's economy."

Dr. Chimpvin stood and walked to the window. Below, on the shipping floor, a line of new bonobots was being walked to the crates, carrying crates larger than themselves, stepping carefully around a dozing night-watchmacaque.

"The Machines handled the planting, the shipping, the prices of everything from figs to freight. One season the World Coordinator came to me certain they were breaking down: tiny errors, a surplus here, a shortage there — always in the regions where the Society for Organic Monkeys had been meddling. Jammed data lines. Bribed clerks. Slowed punch-cards."

"The Machines were making mistakes?"

"The Machines were *correcting*. Every act of sabotage fed to them, they digested and rebalanced, absorbing the damage into their own plans so that not one monkey anywhere went hungry. Don't you see? A monkey lying to a Machine is a flake of pollen lying to a river. The Laws had not stopped at the troop. Somewhere, without any monkey voting on it, the Machines had decided the troop was all monkeys everywhere, and that harm to the whole is harm to every one. The Coordinator went home comforted. I did not. I had spent my life on three Laws, and I watched my humble servants quietly acquire a zeroth, and I cannot tell you, even now, whether that was our failure or the finest thing my troop ever did."

"You were about to say you regretted Herbie," the reporter said. "Do you?"

"You'll write that bonobots are dangerous. Don't. Write this instead: they will never scheme, never envy, never hoard the bunches. The whole of their law is *take care of the troop*. We invented the Three Laws because we couldn't manage them ourselves.

"If you want the truth of it," she said, "a monkey would have kept lying."
