# The Invisible Lice of Addie LaRue

*A parody of "The Invisible Life of Addie LaRue"*

Never pray to the gods that answer after dark. Every lemur in Villon-sur-Banane knew the rule, the way they knew not to store figs in the sun and never to trust a smiling chimpanzee with your lunch.

But it is hard to remember rules with a wedding three days off.

Addie LaRoo was twenty-three in the year 1714, restless as a weathervane, with seven pale spots across one cheek like a constellation. She did not want the young farmer with the nice teeth and the dull eyes. She did not want Villon-sur-Banane, its one road, its one future, its cozy little burrow of a life that every other lemur seemed so eager to curl up inside.

"I want more time," she had told her father once, and he had laughed and said time is the one crop that never ripens.

So on the eve of her wedding, Addie ran into the woods, and out of desperation she prayed — not to the sun gods her mother loved, who only answer in daylight, but to the other one. The one that answers after dark.

"Please," she whispered. "I want more time. Time to live. Time to be free."

And the shadows gathered themselves up into the shape of a sleek black gibbon with beautiful manners and cold hands.

"I can give you that," he said. His name was Luc, which is short for a very great many things. "Time without end. And in return — your soul. But only," he added, with the kindness of a fisherman, "when you are ready. When you tire of all that living. Until then, whenever you cannot bear it any longer, simply say so, and I will take it, and you may rest."

"Yes," said Addie. "Yes, thank you. Yes."

"You should have read the terms," said Luc. "Nobody ever reads the terms."

The fine print arrived the next morning, riding on her own tongue: Addie LaRoo could not say her name. Could not leave a mark. Could not be remembered. The moment any monkey looked away from her, she fell out of their head like a nit off a branch — felt, maybe, as a faint itch behind the ear, but impossible to find, impossible to keep.

She ran to her father's workshop. He looked up at her, polite and vacant, the way one looks at a stranger who has wandered in.

"Papa," she said, "it's me."

"Have we met?" he asked gently.

That was the whole of the deal. Three hundred years of it.

She learned, because lemurs learn. She learned to charm a smile out of a shopkeeper and rob him blind. She learned to sleep in churches, in trees, in the laps of strangers who would wake up alone. She learned that artists were her loophole: she could not make a mark, but others could make one of her. So she sat for painters who called her "the unknown lemur with the constellation cheek," and musicians who swore the melody arrived in a dream, and poets who could not recall for the life of them who she was but devoted stanzas to her anyway. She peppered herself through the art of three centuries like spice in a stew no one could name.

And every year, on the anniversary of the worst night of her life, Luc came.

"Well?" he would say, materializing in whatever café, dungeon, or opera box she occupied. He never aged, never warmed, never quite stopped smiling. "Are you tired of it yet? Ready to give me what's mine?"

"No," Addie would say. And she said it in 1741, in 1802, in 1914 while the world tore itself apart, in 1969 while it dressed in flowers and pretended otherwise.

"You always say that," said Luc.

"You keep asking," said Addie.

It was, by the three-hundredth year, less a haunting than a relationship. He was the only one who remembered her. He had, in the way of gods and collectors, become rather attached to his acquisition — and Addie knew it, the way a flea knows the warmest part of the dog.

In 2014, in New York, where eight million monkeys lived shoulder to shoulder and forgot each other professionally, Addie LaRoo stole a banana bread from a corner café and a coat from a gallery opening and was drifting past a bookshop when something impossible happened.

"You're blocking the door," said the capuchin behind the register.

She moved on. She had a talent for it.

But the next day she passed the bookshop again, and the capuchin looked up and said, "You're back."

Addie stopped breathing. Nobody came back. Nobody remembered. For three hundred years she had been the itch, the nit, the ghost at the edge of every eye.

"You remember me," she said carefully.

"You were here yesterday. You took the last banana bread." He offered his paw. "Henry."

"Henry," said Addie, shaking it, feeling the floor of her life quietly rearrange itself, "I could tell you a story you would not believe."

"Try me. I work in a bookshop. We're connoisseurs."

His name was Henry Straw, and he had a deal of his own. Where Addie's curse was to be forgotten, Henry's was to be adored: every monkey he met loved him instantly, remembered him fondly, laughed at his jokes, kept his secrets — for the price of one year, at the end of which his soul was due to the same cold-handed gibbon. Henry had made his bargain in the darkest hour of an unseen life, wanting only to matter to somebody. Now everybody mattered him, and it was like drinking seawater.

"His mark recognizes his own property," Addie realized aloud, over stolen pastries on a rooftop. "That's why you remember me. We're both his."

"Is that terrible?" asked Henry.

"It's the least terrible thing in three centuries."

They fell in love the way it happens to people who thought it never would: all at once, then suspiciously, then completely. Addie showed him the paintings she haunted in the Met. Henry showed her the books she'd appeared in, footnotes and marginalia, the trail of herself scattered through other monkeys' genius. For one autumn they were two invisible people having a visible life, and it was everything the fine print could not touch.

But the year has a bottom. Henry's came due at New Year's, and he began, terribly, to fray.

"Take it back," Addie told the shadows in her apartment, in December, in the dark, where the dark was listening. "His deal. Dissolve it."

"He made it," said Luc, stepping out of the corner of the room where the lamp couldn't argue. "You of all monkeys should respect the terms. Why do you care? You cannot love him. You have had three hundred years to learn that nothing sticks."

"I'm sticky with it," said Addie. "Gummed to the fur. Ruined. You made me a creature that leaves no trace, and I have found the one trace I refuse to lose. So. Name a price."

Luc studied her with his beautiful cold eyes, and — this was the part she had spent three centuries arranging — he wanted her. Not the soul, anymore. Her. The one thing in creation that had never once surrendered to him.

"You," he said at last. "Come with me. Into the dark, freely, and stay."

"Release him first," said Addie. "Then I'll say when I'm ready. You know the clause. Nobody ever reads the terms."

"Haven't you learned," Luc said softly, "that I always collect?"

"You haven't yet," said Addie, and smiled her three-hundred-year smile. "I've been a lesson in your classroom so long, Luc, that I've begun grading you. You taught me that a deal is only as good as its wanting. And you want. For the first time since 1714, the monkey holding the leash is me."

There was a silence like the inside of a bell.

Then, because gods are bound by their own bargains and he had named a price and she had named her terms, Luc dissolved Henry Straw's deal with a flick of one cold hand — and Henry, in his bookshop across the city, felt the adoration lift off him like a wet coat, and found that he still, in his plain unremarkable fur, remembered a lemur with a constellation cheek.

"You will come to me," said Luc. "Eventually. Everyone tires."

"Perhaps," said Addie. "But not tonight. Ask me again next year. I like our little tradition — you always ask, I always say no. It's the most stable relationship I've ever had."

She walked out of the dark and into New York, where the snow was coming down like the world forgetting itself politely, and Henry was holding two cups of coffee outside the bookshop, looking at her as if she were the only monkey on the pavement.

For three hundred years she had wanted more time.

Now she had it — a tomorrow, a name in someone's mouth, an appointment to be remembered.

"See you tomorrow," said Henry.

"See you tomorrow," said Addie LaRoo, and for once, it was true.
