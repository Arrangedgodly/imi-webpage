# The Invisible Mandrill

*A parody of "The Invisible Man"*

The stranger arrived at the Coach and Horses inn in Aping on a snow-swept February afternoon, wrapped muzzle to tail in bandages, wearing blue goggles, a slouch hat, and gloves with the fingertips cut off. He was, Mrs. Hall the innkeeper thought, either a burn victim, a tropical explorer, or the ugliest macaque in three counties.

He was, in fact, a mandrill. And he was invisible.

His name was Gribbons: Doctor Gribbons, formerly of University College Jungle, a chemist of savage brilliance and worse temper. Six years ago he had cracked the problem of pigmentation. A mandrill's face is a flag — crimson and cobalt, the loudest colors in the canopy — and Gribbons reasoned that a monkey bleached to transparency would be the freest creature alive. So he'd done it. To himself. First. His father had lent him the money without asking questions, and when the money ran out, Gribbons had robbed him, and the old mandrill had died of it; and Gribbons had felt, he would say later, "nothing yet," and meant the *yet* as a promise.

He took a room in Aping, filled it with books — *Jungle Metaphysics*, *Comparative Anatomy of the Great Apes* — and did experiments at all hours. He paid in advance. He had no visible face. He was the worst-kept secret in town, and the town gossiped about it anyway.

The Vicarage Job confirmed things: the vicar and his wife, called away from their kitchen by a voice that knew precisely when their bananas were out of the oven, came back to find the fruit gone, a chair moved, and a sneeze — a distinct, damp, *empty-air* sneeze — in the passage.

It came apart at the inn. The villagers arrived with warrants; Gribbons lost his temper, stripped off his bandages, goggles and all, and stood before them as a set of empty clothes — then not even that. Empty sleeves flailed and struck. Mrs. Hall fainted. The constable went down swinging at nothing. Furniture flew about the tap-room in a way furniture should not. Gribbons tore down Aping's single high street like a riot in a laundry basket, hooting with laughter, and vanished over the hedge into the snowy lanes.

That was when he made the great mistake of his reign: he needed a face, so he recruited one.

"Monster," said the tramp, kneeling in a ditch with an invisible grip on his collar. "I'm not a-spying on you, sir, I never saw you, I swear I never saw nothing!"

"Your name," said the Voice, "is Marvel."

"It's Thomas Marvel, sir, and I'd be obliged if—"

"You are now my hands, Marvel. My legs. My visible monkey. You will carry my books, order my meals, and hold my money, and in the fullness of time, one hundredth part of my kingdom. Refuse, and I will follow you into your dreams."

So the reign of the Invisible Mandrill acquired its prime minister: a shambling, muttering, thieving old tramp who was marched at invisible paw-point into Aping's shops, into the General Emporium, into every bank and baker between Aping and Burrock, while a Voice behind his ear cursed him fluently for dropping things. Marvel hated every minute of it, and stole every minute of it. By Port Burrock, with three diaries of Gribbons's research, a fat purse of the Invisible Mandrill's money, and the whole county in pursuit of *both* of them, Marvel stopped pretending to be loyal, shouted "All right! I give you up!" to the empty air, and ran for the hills with his pockets full of the future.

Gribbons, robbed of his hands, went to ground — and came, at last and at night, to the rooms of his old colleague Doctor Chimp, in Burrock, and stood, unseen, dripping, at the foot of the bed.

"Gribbons!" cried Chimp, jolted awake. "I'd thought you dead — or worse, in industry."

"I'm invisible," said Gribbons, plainly. "It's me. Look, I need a bed, a meal, and every bandage in Burrock."

"Your *face* — Gribbons, your colors—"

"Are INVISIBLE," Gribbons thundered, and the water jug trembled. "Do you understand what I have done? I am the freest mandrill in the world. No net can hold me. No troop can exile me. The reign of the Invisible Mandrill begins today. Epoch One! A Reign of Terror, frankly, is the working title. I'll have this town under my thumb by Friday. Food, shelter, warm fur to sleep against, on demand, forever. I've already begun: I smashed every window on the high street last night, as a courtesy announcement. And you, Chimp, will be my accomplice. You will be my chronicler. They will write of us."

"Or?"

"Or I'll be your nightmare. You've never been alone in a room with something you can't see. Try eating breakfast while I watch."

But Chimp had already seen the flaw — the same one Gribbons had been outrunning for weeks, and which the whole county was about to learn. Invisibility isn't power. It's *hunger*. You cannot carry food. You cannot carry money. You must eat quickly, or your stolen bananas float visibly in your belly for an hour, a bobbing yellow lantern announcing you to every monkey in the market. Rain outlines you. Snow outlines you. Ungroomed, you trail dust like a comet. Sleeping dogs still hear you. Unshod, you leave prints. And every soul you terrify tells everyone else exactly what to look for: nothing.

Chimp wrote to the constables. Gribbons caught him at it, and there was blood on the letter, and the war was on. They sealed the food-stores of the whole county. They locked every kitchen, troop-store and granary. They laid flour and ash at doorways and strung bell-wires across the lanes. Gribbons was reduced to throwing bricks through windows from a rooftop and issuing proclamations nobody could find him to obey. He went from menace to myth to joke in nine days flat. And when the police came at last for him in Chimp's house, Gribbons smashed his way out through the siege — shot the chief constable, old Adye, at the garden gate, wrecked the rooms, and promised Chimp he would be saved for a slower hour — then fled, starving, into the open street, where every monkey in Burrock had been issued a sack of flour.

He should have kept to the ditches. He came instead, mad and magnificent, through the market square at Juggling, and the air went white with flour, and a netting fell, and one well-thrown overripe banana split across his chest and painted him, in a single yellow stroke, into existence.

A young laborer hit him with a spade.

They crowded around as the invisibility died. And here is the thing they saw, the thing the papers never got right: as Gribbons faded out of nothingness, his colors came back. The crimson muzzle. The cobalt cheeks. The great gaudy flag of the loneliest mandrill in England blooming across his face like a sunrise, at the very end of the story, when it could no longer do him any good at all.

"Chimp," he whispered, with the last breath in his invisible chest, "the colors. They've come back."

"Great heavens," said Chimp, kneeling. "He's beautiful."

He was buried in Aping, under a stone no one visits, in a plot famously, deliberately, kept bare of flowers — because for one week every spring, nobody in Aping can quite bring themselves to mow it.

As for Thomas Marvel — fat, solvent, and unindicted — he took the Invisible Mandrill's money, and opened a pub with the name, and does steady trade there still. He keeps the three diaries locked in a drawer; and late at night, when the bar is empty, the locals say, he takes them out and reads a page or two, and smiles at something only he can see. He has never explained what. He is, in his way, the only monkey who ever made invisibility pay.
