# Because of Swing-Dixie

*A parody of "Because of Winn-Dixie"*

That summer, the Preacher — who was my papa, a quiet orange orangutan who moved slow, like a turtle wearing a fur coat — moved us to a new troop in a grove so far south the moss grew sideways. I did not know a single monkey my age. I did not know anybody except the Preacher, and he was too sad about Mama to talk much. Mama had gone off to live with a far troop two summers before, and she wasn't coming back, and that was that.

The first thing that happened was the banana market.

I was sent to the Swing-Dixie Banana Market for a box of 'nanas, and I walked in on total chaos. A scruffy red-haired monkey was loose in the produce section. He had knocked over a display of plantains, sat himself in the middle of the mangos, and was grinning up at the manager like the whole thing had been his idea.

"Call the zoo-catcher!" the manager hollered. "This monkey is going, going, GONE!"

And I don't know what got into me, but I hollered back, "Wait! That's my monkey! He got away from me this morning!"

The scruffy monkey looked at me. And then he smiled — pulled back his lips and showed every single one of his teeth, a great big wide 'nana-split of a smile. The manager laughed so hard he forgot to be mad.

So that is how I got Swing-Dixie, named after the market, because it was his fault, all of it, everything that happened that summer.

The Preacher preached at the Open Arms, which was a troop church made of a borrowed tent and four hopeful poles, and the very first Sunday I brought Swing-Dixie, a field mouse ran across the altar, and Swing-Dixie caught it — caught it! — and then let it go out the flap, and smiled at the congregation like he'd done them a favor. "That monkey," said old Sister Frangipani, fanning herself, "has a calling." The Preacher said the sermon was about welcoming the stranger, and everybody agreed they had never felt the point of it so strongly.

That night I asked the Preacher to tell me ten things about Mama, one for every year since she was born when I was little — no, I never get that right. Ten things, just ten, so I could know her like she was still around. He sat down slow, like he did everything, and held up his fingers, and counted.

"One: she could peel a banana with her feet. Two: she was a fast climber, fast as fear. Three: she loved to plant things and every one of them died. Four: she could not cook a fig to save her life. Five: she knew all the old songs and got the words wrong with total confidence. Six: she laughed before the punchline, every time, which ruined jokes and improved stories. Seven: she never met a stray she wouldn't feed. Eight: she was sad in the rainy season and never once pretended she wasn't. Nine: she could whistle through her teeth, and she did, everywhere, so you always knew where she was. Ten—" and here the Preacher stopped for a while. "Ten: she loved you worse than anything, if worse is the word I mean, and I believe it is not."

I wrote all ten on a leaf and kept it folded in my fur all summer, and when I missed her so much my chest creaked, I read it over, and it helped some, the way shade helps.

Swing-Dixie made friends the way rain makes rivers. He dragged me to the grove library, where a tiny old langur in spectacles named Miss Franny Sock sat at the desk, and when Swing-Dixie smiled at her she gave us peanut-butter sandwiches and told us about the time she stared down a leopard right there in the reading room.

"He came in through the window," she said, "and settled his haunches like he had a library card. And I stood up — I was younger, but not much — and I looked him in the eye and I said, 'Sir, this is a QUIET room.' And when he did not move, I picked up the biggest book we had, which was War and Peas, both volumes, and I threw it at him. Missed, of course. But he respected the attempt, and left, and to this day no leopard has a card at this library, and I intend to keep it that way."

"A librarian," said Miss Franny Sock, "is never afraid of a cat."

He dragged me to Gertrude's Perches, where a long-armed gibbon called Otis played guitar to the parrots and the ferrets and the pythons, and they all came out of their cages to listen, swaying. There was a red parrot there named Gertrude who knew exactly one word — "MONKEY!" — and she screamed it every single time Swing-Dixie walked in, and then laughed her metallic parrot laugh, and Swing-Dixie smiled back at her like they had an arrangement. Otis had done time in a cage himself once, and didn't like to talk about it. "You can't judge a monkey by the things they done," said Gloria Stump, when I told her. "You got to judge them by what they are doing right now."

Gloria Stump was a great old half-blind gorilla who lived at the edge of the grove with a jungle full of hanging gourd-jars on strings. "My mistake tree," she said. "Every mistake I ever made, I hang a gourd, so the wind rattles them and I remember. But the thing about mistakes, child, is the jungle keeps growing anyway."

The Dunberry boys — Dunlap and Stevie, two pinch-faced macaqs — teased me every time they saw me. "You got a PET monkey?" said Stevie. "That's like a monkey getting a pet monkey!" "That doesn't even mean anything," I said. "It means you're weird," said Dunlap. But when Swing-Dixie smiled at them, they smiled back before they could stop themselves, and after that they teased me about other things, which I was grateful for.

We decided to have a party at Gloria's — me, Miss Franny, Otis, Gertrude the parrot in a traveling cage, the Dunberry boys, a grumpy she-chimp named Amanda whose nose was always red, and one little marmoset called Sweetie Pie who mainly wanted to pet Swing-Dixie. We strung up gourd-lanterns right next to Gloria's mistake-jars so the whole tree clinked and glowed. There was fig jam and pickled eggs and Dump Punch — which is fruit punch with a whole extra fruit dumped in — and Otis played "Gloria" on the guitar, and Gertrude screamed "MONKEY!" in time to it, and Swing-Dixie smiled so wide he fell off his branch, which set off the mistake-jars, which made Gloria laugh until she had to sit on the ground.

Miss Franny brought a box of candies: little gray banana drops called Litmus 'Nanas, made from her great-granddaddy's secret recipe, "candy that tastes like sorrow." Everybody sucked one and went quiet. Otis tasted the monkeys he'd played for in the pet shop. Gloria tasted the mistakes on her tree. The Dunberry boys tasted a fight they'd started years ago and never apologized for, and looked sideways at each other and didn't say anything, but sat closer. The Preacher — who came, which surprised everybody — tasted Mama. And me, I tasted the empty place where she used to be.

Then the sky turned the color of a bruise, and the thunder came.

Swing-Dixie is not afraid of anything except thunder. He went banshee. He bolted off into the rain and the dark, and the whole party turned into a search party, hollering him through the wet trees while lightning cracked the sky. I cried and hunted. I was sure I had lost him, like I lost Mama, because it seemed like everything I loved, I lost. Even Gertrude was no help, screaming "MONKEY!" into the storm at nothing.

We came back soaked and heartbroken — and there was Swing-Dixie, under the Preacher's cot, where he'd been the whole time, hiding from the thunder and howling along with it, smiling his 'nana-split smile in the dark.

The Preacher pulled him out and hugged him, and then he hugged me, first time in a long while, and I understood what Gloria meant: you can't hold on to anything by keeping it. You hold on by letting it rattle in the wind.

That night I read my leaf of ten things over one more time, and then I didn't have to anymore, because by then Mama wasn't only on the leaf.

Just about everything good that happened that summer — the friends, the sandwiches, the sorrow-candy, the party, the hug — happened because of Swing-Dixie.
