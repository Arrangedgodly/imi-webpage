# Hairy Potter and the Deathly Howlers

*A parody of "Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows"*

Hairy Potter fell out of the sky seven times the night the war began. He was the most famous monkey alive — the Chimp Who Lived — and Voldechimp's Death Chimps knew it, attacking the escort flight in the dark above the jungle canopy until the night was full of falling fur and jetpacks.

Dumbledoo was dead and buried. The Ministry of Monkey Magic fell within a month. The last happy afternoon of the old world was a wedding — Bill Weasel and Furie Delacourcour, a marquee full of monkeys, a cake the size of a cauldron — and it ended mid-toast, when the silver patronus of the Minister himself burst through the canopy: "The Ministry has fallen. The Minister of Monkey Magic is dead. They are coming." The sky filled with black hoods over the cake. And so, on what should have been Hairy's seventh year at Hogwarts, three friends — Hairy, Rondald, and Hermonkee — went not to school but to war, carrying a stolen locket and a mission: find Voldechimp's remaining soul-fruit, the hidden pieces of his banana-split soul, and destroy them one by one.

The locket was one of them. Wearing it was like carrying a rotten banana next to your heart: it whispered. It made Rondald leave in a storm of jealousy one night, tail lashing, gone between the trees.

That was the year Hermonkee told the Tale of the Three Brothers, from her copy of The Tales of Beedle the Bard (illustrated, she noted, by a very early engraver of primates). Three brother monkeys met Death on a riverbank at night, and Death, cheated and annoyed, granted each a prize. The Elder Vine, a wand of infinite power. The Resurrection Stone, to summon the dead. And the Invisibility Fleece, to hide from Death himself — and it worked, for the third brother simply groomed, and lived, and greeted Death as an old friend.

"Together," Hermonkee whispered, "they're the Deathly Howlers. Master all three, and you master death. But nobody believes—"

"I believe," said Hairy, who had a piece of the tale already around his shoulders.

The coldest stop was Godric's Hollow — the village where it all began, dressed in Christmas, unbearable. They laid a wreath on his parents' grave, and a very old lady monkey named Bathilda Squeakshot beckoned them inside — and turned in the hallway into something with too many vertebrae: Voldechimp's snake, wearing the dead historian's shape like a coat. The house shredded itself; Hairy's wand snapped in the escape; and they fled with nothing but each other and the cold.

They wandered, hunted, and nearly starved, and the war found them anyway, in the woods, with a snatch of the forbidden word — and suddenly they were prisoners in Malfoy Manor, in a cellar full of their friends and a chandelier quivering overhead, while Bellabananatrix Lestrange interrogated everyone about a sword. It was the cellar's smallest prisoner who saved them: Dobby, a house-marmoset with tennis-ball eyes, who could apparate through wards the way gossip goes through fur, and who dropped the chandelier on the Death Chimps and got the prisoners out.

All but one. Bellabananatrix's dart found Dobby on the lawn, and Dobby died free in Hairy's arms — "Dobby has no master. Dobby has a friend" — and Hairy buried him by hand, without magic, which was its own kind of spell.

Rondald came back. That mattered. He returned in a hail of branches to save Hairy from drowning in a frozen pool — following a silver doe that had led him there, a Patronus Hairy had watched walk across the ice like moonlight given an errand — and drew from the pool's depths the Sword of Gruffindor, silver, goblin-forged, and utterly unimpressed by dark magic. He destroyed the locket with one stroke, and apologized without once using the word "sorry," which for Rondald was an epic.

They chased the last soul-fruit across the jungle — a vault in Grabbings Bank where a dragibbon guarded the hoard and Hermonkee had to ride out on its neck screaming *Banacarys!* while the trio clung to its shoulders through a burning collapse; a cup; and then home: Hogwarts, where the last piece was hidden, and where Voldechimp marched that very night with his army.

The Battle of Hogwarts came at last — the castle's statues leaping to its walls, Professor Sprouttree hurling armed greenery, the Weasel twins' fireworks going off in the corridors like courage. The battle took as much as it gave: Fred Weasel fell laughing, mid-prank, in a corridor he had defended with jokes, and the castle's grief had no counter-curse at all. Hairy destroyed the diadem in the Room of Requirement, where Dracco's cabinet had spent a year smuggling in the war, and the room, asked to hide fire, obliged so thoroughly it nearly took the askers with it.

And then, in Snipe's dying memory — given up at last in the Shrieking Treehouse — Hairy learned everything: that Snipe had loved his mother, always, and spied for Dumbledoo, always, and that the silver doe had been Snipe's. *After all this time?* Dumbledoo had asked him, once.

"Always," said Snipe — in love, all his life, with the one monkey who ever saw past his sour fur. And that his own death had been planned, asked for, promised, the last terrible chore of the war. And worst of all: that when Voldechimp had tried to kill baby Hairy and killed Lily instead, a piece of the Dark Lord's soul had lodged in the survivor. The final soul-fruit was living, parasitic, in the last place anyone would hide it: inside Hairy himself.

He walked out to die. And on the way, in the dark of the forest, he turned a small cold stone in his paw, and the dead walked beside him — his mother, his father, Serious, Lupine — not as ghosts but as company.

"Why are you here?" Hairy asked.

"Because you are," said his mother.

Into the moonlit clearing where Voldechimp waited.

"Avocado Kedavra."

And Hairy woke in a bright white King's-Cross of a station, a station hung with banana bunches like chandeliers, where Dumbledoo sat on a bench waiting for him, twinkling and half-transparent.

"Professor," Hairy said, "am I dead?"

"That," said Dumbledoo, "is up to you. Tell me, what would you say if I told you to go back?"

"Don't be ridiculous."

Under a nearby bench something whimpered: a small, raw, frightened thing, past helping. "You cannot help it," said Dumbledoo gently. "Don't be sorry for it. Just go."

He went back.

The battle broke like a storm. Mrs. Weasel herself dueled Voldechimp's best Death Chimp into next week — "NOT MY DAUGHTER, YOU BANANA" — and Neville Longtail, of all monkeys, pulled the Sword of Gruffindor out of a sorting hat and ended the last snake. The Elder Vine, it turned out, had never belonged to Voldechimp at all. When the final curse left his wand, it rebounded, and the last of the split souls came apart like an overripe banana hitting the ground, and the jungle was free.

Nineteen years later, Hairy stood on Platform Nine-and-Three-Quarters with his mate, little monkeys of their own tugging at his tail.

"All was well," he told them. "And if the trolley lady asks — yes, everything on the cart. We'll take the lot."

Which, as every monkey knows, is how you finish a story: not with a howl, but with a feast.
