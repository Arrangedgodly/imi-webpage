# Nit

*A parody of "It"*

The rain came down in ropes on the troop-tree at Derry, Maine, and Georgie Denbrough's boat was faster than Georgie.

It was a fig leaf folded in half, a proper sailing boat, and it shot down the gutter-stream while Georgie galloped after it, whooping, until it slid into the dark of the storm drain and spun, just out of reach.

"Hiya, Georgie," said the drain.

A face floated in the dark down there. A clown, silver-furred, with a ruff of orange pom-poms and a smile with a little too many teeth, like a banana that had opened on its own.

"Wanna get your boat back, Georgie?"

"Is it stuck?" asked Georgie, who was six and had been told not to talk to strangers, but not about drains.

"It floats," said the clown. "Everything floats down here. Bananas float. Berries float. And down here, Georgie, nobody ever itches."

The clown's name, it said, was Penny-Nit, and it reached up out of the dark with arms like smooth gray branches. This story is friendly, so let us say only this: the troop-tree never saw Georgie again, and Derry learned to forget, the way Derry always did. Derry forgot six missing juveniles that year, and a whole summer's worth of posters, and had been forgetting like this — every twenty-seven years or so — since before anyone kept track.

Georgie's older brother Bill was the one who could not forget. Bill — *Ch-Ch-Chimp*, the other juveniles called him, though his stutter was made of grief — gathered a troop of his own the way lost things gather: Ben "Haystack," the new monkey who smelled of library dust; Beverly, the only girl with a slingshot and a father-shaped storm cloud; Richie "Trashmouth," who could not stop talking the way a river could not stop; Eddie, with his eucalyptus inhaler; Stan, who needed the world to be orderly; and Mike, who kept the troop's history and therefore knew too much.

Ben, it should be said, had been recruited by the Nit before Bill ever met him: he had arrived in Derry fat, friendless, and fond of the library, and had passed his first week doing homework in the history section, where Mike later found what the town had let rot — troop records going back three hundred years, and the same pattern every twenty-seven years: the mill fire, the lodge fire, the Black Banana Grove fire of 1930, each one with a clown's grin sketched in the margin of some witness statement. Derry's rate of missing juveniles was six times the jungle average, and Derry's rate of caring was not measurable. "This town," Ben wrote in his diary, "doesn't have a pest problem. It has a landlord."

They earned each other in the weed-lot, the day Henry Bonkers and his gang of knuckle-draggers came to drown them in the mud. What saved them was the quietest thing Ben ever did: he read Henry's aim, threw a handful of gravel sideways, and yelled "SCATTER!" — and seven juveniles fought back with rocks and river-clay until Henry Bonkers fled, gibbering, trailing both dignity and fur. "That," said Richie, "is the last time anyone tries to eat us without a reservation."

The Nit came for each of them after that, one by one, wearing whatever frightened them most. It shambled out of the derelict Frozen Banana Factory at Ben as a mummy in rotting bandages. It met Beverly in the school grooming room, where the drains erupted — a geyser of gray banana pulp, flooding the sinks, splattering the mirrors — and a voice inside it said, warm and horrible: "This is berry serious, Bevvie." It found Stan under the synagogue fig, wearing his own bar-mitzvah suit with the zipper over the face. It blew Ben's library valentine — *your fur is a haiku* — back at him down the stacks in a dead monkey's handwriting.

Eddie met it under the porch. It said, "Hiya, Eds," and Eddie blasted it point-blank with the inhaler and screamed "Battery acid, you fuzzy freak!" — and here is the whole trick of monsters and bananas: you get what you believe. Eddie believed, and the mist burned. Which is why the belief of seven juveniles, pooled, is a weapon of mass grooming.

In the weed-lot below the troop-tree they dug a clubhouse, and in the clubhouse they told each other everything, and the telling was the beginning of the end of It. They saw it whole, once, in Ben's library book — *PRIMATE PARASITES OF THE MAINE WOODS* — when the photo album bled and the pages turned themselves to a picture of a clown with its arm around a boy who was later listed as *missing, 1957*, and another, *missing, 1930*. All of them missing. None of them forgotten by the album, which kept better records than the town.

On a gray July they followed the storm-drains down to the hollow beneath Derry — a nest of eggs like black pearls — and above it, brooding, the Nit dropped its clown suit and was what it was: a louse the size of a rhino, glossy, ancient, its little claws combing the air.

The Ritual of Chew'd, Bill had read, is a contest of tongues: you hold your tongue out to the monster and make it laugh before it makes you cry. They tried it. Richie told jokes. Mike recited troop history at it, deadpan, for six straight minutes, which nearly worked. Bill swore at it in his stutter, which somehow, syllable by broken syllable, hurt it worse than anything.

"Ki-Ki-Kill it," roared Bill. "Them! Whatever!"

Then they went in swinging, all seven, and they stomped the mother-nit until it cracked like a stale biscuit, and Beverly's slingshot burst the eggs, and when the thing tried to speak Richie shouted "Beep beep!" right in its mandibles.

Afterward they split a banana seven ways and swore the oath: if It ever came back, they would come back too. Then Derry's forgetting settled over them like fog, and one by one they moved away and forgot one another — everything except the oath, which sat in them like a seed.

Twenty-seven years later, the phone calls came.

"Hi, it's Mike. It's back. You swore."

They came back: a lawyer, a librarian, a limousine driver, a statistician, a writer — gray-furred, grown, ordinary, none of them complete. (Stanley could not be reached, and would not speak of Derry, and we will be kind and leave him in the sun.) At the Jade Fig restaurant they gathered for the reunion dinner, and the forgetting peeled off them course by course, until by dessert they were shrieking old jokes with their arms around each other, and the waiter had to ask them twice, twice, to leave. On the sidewalk outside, they saw the missing posters going up fresh on the lampposts, and stopped shrieking.

The Nit was weaker now, and it tried the old promises in the old drain-voice — *down here nobody ever itches* — and Eddie met it once more under the streets, and this time, believing, believed in all seven of them at once. And the truth, laughed at last:

"We all itch down here," said Richie, fifty and balding and still unable to stop, "and you know what? We learned to comb."

That was the thing the monster never understood about a troop. Laughter ends monsters; grooming ends nits; and Derry slept deep that night for the first time in three hundred years.

Beep beep, Nit.

And well done, all of you.
