# The Jungle Ook

*A parody of "The Jungle Book"*

The otters found the singing egg — which is to say, the cradle of leaves with a baby gibbon in it, washed down the flooded river and caught in the reeds. The otters gave it to the macaques, the macaques gave it to the Council at the Banyan Rock, and the Council hooted the question of the age:

"Ook?"

"Ook," agreed Raksha the mother macaque firmly, which is monkey for "he stays."

The Council was not so sure. The Law of the Jungle said one thing clearly, and it said it in capital letters: THE TROOP IS THE TROOP. A gibbon child in a macaque troop was a riddle with teeth. Old Greyback, the eldest male, observed that the cub had no tail, and that a monkey without a tail was either a mistake or an omen, and that the Council had bad luck with both.

"He is neither," said Raksha. "He is *mine*, and he is *hungry*, and those are his two qualities at present."

Baloo the bonobo — teacher of the Law, speaker for the troop, proprietor of the school branch — snuffled the foundling and shrugged his vast shrug. "The Law says the troop is the troop," he said, "but the Law also says the troop is whoever the troop says it is. That's the clever bit. Everybody forgets the clever bit."

"I'll pay for him," said Bagheera the black panther, sleek and sardonic, stepping out of the shadows as though she had been shopping there. "The price of an outsider's footing in a troop is a feast. I'll give the Council my winter store of honey-figs. All thirty. I was saving them for a bad month."

"You'll regret it," said Old Greyback, already calculating what thirty honey-figs weighed.

"I'll regret it more if I don't," said Bagheera, who had looked at the baby gibbon for exactly one second too long.

So the Council ate the figs, and the cub stayed, and was named Mookli, and grew up wild in the Seeonee treetops: macaque by law, gibbon by birth, and hopeless at everything involving a tail he didn't have.

"The strength of the troop is the monkey," Baloo would boom at the school branch, "and the strength of the monkey is the troop. Say it back, man-cub."

"Gibbon-cub," Mookli would correct, and Baloo would bonobo-shrug. Either way.

Baloo taught the Law of the Jungle — the grooming code, the fruit-rights, the Peace Rock truce where thirsty enemies drink side by side and nobody starts anything till everyone's finished. And he taught the Master Words, the oldest words there are: *We be of one troop, thou and I.* "Say them to anybody who's hunting you," said Baloo, "from a macaque to a mango-forest python, and watch them go quiet. There's no argument against one troop. There's never been one."

Bagheera taught the shortcuts, the exits, and the exact location of every escape route in Seeonee, three times over.

"Now," said Baloo one golden afternoon, scratching his vast belly, "forget about your worries and your strife. Look for the banana necessities — the simple banana necessities. Ripe ones drop when they're ready. Yellow ones swing for free. I mean the old banana necessities — mother nature's recipes — they bring the banana necessities to me."

"Could you say that once without bananas?" asked Mookli.

"I could," said Baloo, "but why would I?"

They were mid-lesson when the Bandar-log came — the monkey-people, the chatterers, the lawless ones, a screeching brown river of fur. They wanted Mookli, because the Bandar-log have no law, no memory, and excellent taste in novelties, and a tailless monkey who had been bought with thirty honey-figs was the best novelty in three watersheds.

"We are taking the gibbon-macaque!" they shrieked, pouring over the canopy. "He will be our leader! Or our mascot! We haven't decided! We never decide! We are the greatest! We said so! We think we said so!"

"HELP!" yowled Mookli, snatched away. "BALOO! BAGHEERA! MONKEY BUSINESS!"

Bagheera ran beneath them, low and terrible; Baloo thundered behind, roaring the Law in its angriest grammar; but the Bandar-log travel by the high roads where a panther can't follow and a bonobo shouldn't. They poured away toward the Cold Ruins — the ruined temple where no troop lives, because troops need law and ruins have none — and Mookli, bounced along in a hundred careless paws, did the only clever thing available: he said the Master Words, out loud, to anybody.

*We be of one troop, thou and I.*

The Bandar-log dropped him. Not out of obedience — out of confusion. A troop? Them? They had no troop. They stopped to debate whether they had a troop, which is the one debate they cannot win, and while they debated, the shadows of the Cold Ruins filled with something long and golden-eyed.

Kaa the rock python poured silently along a forgotten branch, singing low and golden:

"Trussst in me... jussst in me... ssso, monkey-people, look at me and sssleep..."

The great abduction ended abruptly in a heap of snoring monkeys, and Mookli slid down Kaa's coils like a fireman's pole into Baloo's arms.

That was the night they went into the Cold Ruins anyway — for the Bandar-log had not been acting alone. In the ruined temple sat King Lulu, an orangutan of enormous ambition, who had heard about the gibbon who sang, and who had sent his lawless subjects shopping.

"You," rumbled King Lulu, leaning down out of the dark, eyes gleaming. "The singing folk. The voice that calls the sun up. I have apes and I have ruins and I have a crown I made myself, and the Bandar-log laugh at me, because a crown with no song is just a hat. Teach me the song, little cousin, and I'll make you a prince of the Bandar-log. I wanna sing like you-hoo-hoo."

"You can't *take* a song," said Mookli. "You sing it, or you don't. It isn't fire you can steal."

"A song is exactly like fire," said King Lulu, "and I want it. Give it to me. I'll trade you the Bandar-log — all of them. You can't beat that. They're priceless." He paused. "Please take them."

But a gibbon's song, Baloo had taught him, is not for kings. It is for troop-mates, for mornings, for anyone who sings *back*. So Mookli climbed to the highest broken tower, and took the deepest breath of his life, and sang the true dawn song of the gibbons — the one that carries across watersheds, that says *I am here, where are you?* — and he put every lonely year of a gibbon raised by macaques into it.

The whole watershed answered. Macaques hooted, the otters whistled, Baloo's laugh boomed up from the valley, Bagheera yowled her sleek yowl, Kaa hummed his one golden note, and from every far tree came gibbon voices Mookli had never met, calling back in harmony — because that is what gibbon song is *for*.

The Bandar-log scattered like confetti. And King Lulu, a king nobody would sing with, sat alone on his self-made crown in a temple full of sleeping subjects, and understood for the first time exactly how alone, and slunk into the dark of the ruins and was never loud again.

"Now," said a new voice from the temple door, "that's the racket I've been hunting." Share Khan the tiger, who never shared anything, stepped into the moonlight, Tabaqui the giggling jackal at his heel. "I hate singers. Singing gave away my last ambush. At the Peace Rock I vowed I'd have the tailless one, and I keep my vows — a tiger's the only thing in this jungle that does."

But Mookli had stopped being afraid somewhere during his song.

"That was your mistake," he said. "Vows and ambushes. You do everything *alone*, Khan — you hunt alone, you sulk alone, even your jackal only giggles." He pointed up, where Baloo and Bagheera had arrived, and Kaa behind them, and beyond them the awakened treetops, full of eyes, every eye a monkey who had sung back. "*We be of one troop, thou and I.* Say it, Khan. I'll wait."

Share Khan looked at the eyes. He counted them, cruelly but accurately, the way he counted everything, and the arithmetic said *leave*. He left hungry, with Tabaqui giggling — and of all the night's defeats, the Bandar-log agreed, that was the funniest one.

The rains came, and the river fell, and one bright morning a family of gibbons appeared at the edge of Seeonee, singing a song with Mookli's note in it — a song they had followed down the watershed, note by note, through three seasons, because that is what you do when you hear your own calling back. There were tears, there were figs, and there was a compromise, brokered by Baloo over a ceremonial banana: mornings with the singing folk in the high canopy, evenings with the troop at the Banyan Rock, honey-figs shared both directions forever.

Mookli kept both laws, all his life — Baloo's and the song's.

**The strength of the troop is the monkey, and the strength of the monkey is the troop. But the joy of the jungle? That's the singing. Ook.**
