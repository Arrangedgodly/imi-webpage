# The Midwich Ook-Ooks

*A parody of "The Midwich Cuckoos" by John Wyndham*

The troop of Midwich macaques fell asleep at ten past ten on a Tuesday, and the whole jungle agreed never to speak of it again — after, of course, spending a fortune speaking of nothing else for a year.

Richard and Janet Howler had been away on their holidays, up the coast branch, which is why they were alive to tell it. Coming home at dusk, Janet put a hand on Richard's shoulder.

"The sentries aren't challenging us," she said. "Midwich never misses a stranger."

The rangers at the troop boundary were asleep on their feet. A moth flew in over the invisible line and dropped like a stone. A vulture flew over and dropped like a stone. Richard lobbed a mango through, and the mango was fine, but a ranger who stumbled into the circle folded up mid-step, snoring.

The army arrived within a day, in the person of Colonel Westhowl, who cordoned off the troop with trip-lines and mango-cannons and then devoted a fortnight to dropping things into the circle to see what would fall down. A flutter of birds: down. A rolled fig-leaf: down. A photograph of the Queen: nothing, which the Colonel logged as "inconclusive." Soundings found a metal something under the banana grove, curved and vast and humming like a hive in winter, and while the experts argued about how to dig it up, it went away overnight — no hole, no mound, no goodbye — leaving nothing behind but sleep, and, as it turned out, luggage.

For a day and a night — the Dayout, they called it ever after — Midwich slept inside its circle. Then the troop woke with headaches and a vague collective feeling of having been inventoried.

Six months later, to the week, the infants arrived.

Every mother who had been in Midwich during the Dayout bore her baby that same week, willing or baffled — the willing being, Janet noted, the ones who had stopped asking questions earliest — and the babies did not look like the troop. They were pale silver from brow to tail-tip, compact and calm, and their eyes — this was the thing the Midwich mothers could not stop whispering about — their eyes were gold, and they caught the lantern light like a pair of coins at the bottom of a well.

Old Zellaby, the silverback — silver by age, silver by grandchildren, and by far the cleverest ape in three troops — sat watching them at their weaning ceremony, all fourteen in a knot at the edge of the clearing. Not playing. Not squabbling. Sitting. Watching back.

"You know the howler cuckoo," he said to Richard, in the mild voice he used for the worst news. "She lays her egg in another's nest, and the hatchling shoves out the true chicks, and the smaller birds work their hearts out feeding the thing that will eat the larder and then eat the larder's heirs."

"We're not birds, Zellaby."

"We're somebody's nest, my boy. That is what we have been."

He was right, and it got worse in the way true things do, politely and on schedule. The Ook-Ooks — someone named them for the only sound they made among themselves, and the name stuck like a burr — learned as one. Teach one to crack a fig with a stone, and all fourteen knew by evening, though only one had been in the room. They were not fourteen minds, Zellaby observed, but two: a he-mind and a she-mind, each spread across seven small silver bodies, so that a scolding administered in the north clearing was received, coldly, in the south. When the old disciplinarian, Cousin Hubert, cuffed one for thieving, all fourteen turned their golden eyes on him, and Hubert went over the cliff at noon, in full view of the troop, walking like a monkey being steered.

The troop tried. It should be recorded that the troop tried. The government paid a pension into Midwich — conscience money, Zellaby called it — and proposed moving the fourteen to a reserve far down the river; the barge crew arrived, laid eyes on fourteen pairs of gold coins, and pushed off again with the barge still full. Old Grice the gamekeeper went out one night with a mango-spear and a plan; he came back at dawn, sat down in the middle of the clearing, and stayed sat, with the spear going rusty across his knees, until his daughters led him home. Nobody in Midwich ever asked what it felt like to be held like that. Nobody in Midwich ever asked the Ook-Ooks anything at all.

"They are not our infants," Zellaby said. "They are our successors. And they are waiting for us to finish peeling the jungle for them."

The years went by like that, politely. At six, the Ook-Ooks asked for the Old Granary to live in by themselves, fed through a window by a retired sergeant who was the only volunteer and who took to sleeping in his boots. Ferelly, whose son — she insisted on *son* — was one of the fourteen, went weekly with figs, and stood at the granary door for an hour, and came home having been looked at. Richard asked Zellaby once why the fourteen wanted a building and not their mothers. "Because a building," said Zellaby, "cannot be shamed. They are not building a childhood, my boy. They are building a barracks with a cradle in it."

The rangers wanted to study them. The government wanted to move them. The troop, to its eternal credit, mostly wanted them to say one kind thing, and mostly went to bed disappointed.

"Can we blame them?" Richard asked, on the last evening, walking the boundary with the old silverback. "A cuckoo doesn't know it's a cuckoo."

"No more than we knew, when we pushed the sloths off the good branches," Zellaby agreed. "Blame is a featherweight question, my boy. The heavy one is whether a troop has to let itself be succeeded, politely, on schedule — or whether it is allowed one impoliteness of its own." He sighed, and it sounded like leaves. "I find I am about to be an accomplice to a rudeness."

That evening, Zellaby invited the Ook-Ooks to a lantern-slide show, an old-fashioned pleasure he had been promising the youngsters for months. He set the lantern box on the flat rock, with the woven chest of his winter fig stores right beneath it — unusually close, unusually ripe with fermentation. He showed them the Gorillápagos. He showed them the migration of the rufous peelican. He showed them, slide by golden-lit slide, a world worth inheriting kindly.

*Click*, went the lantern. *Click.*

The chest held no figs that night. It held blasting figs, thirty pounds, with the fuse braided into the lantern cord where no golden eye had thought to look — because fourteen minds that think as one, you see, have only one blind spot between them.

Zellaby stayed to the end, because he was old, and because he had hauled the chest himself, and because he felt — as he told Richard that afternoon — that a troop should not ask of strangers what it would not ask of its own silverback.

They found the clearing lit by a second dawn.

In Midwich, that night, the hens sat their empty nests, and every mother in the troop dreamed of eggs, and nobody, then or ever, wanted to talk about it.
