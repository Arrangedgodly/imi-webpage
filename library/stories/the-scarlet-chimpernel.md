# The Scarlet Chimpernel

*A parody of "The Scarlet Pimpernel"*

The lowland jungles were in the grip of the Revolution, and the Revolution, everybody agreed, had gone from bad to gorilla. Citizen baboons by the thousand thronged the boughs of Paris-Grove, chanting for figs and fraternity, while the Guerrilla Tribunals — gorillas to a male, guerrillas in method — hauled every well-furred aristocrat of the old canopy before the Stump of Liberty.

On this particular morning, the tumbril-cart bore the Comte de St. Ffleur and his three daughters, gentle gibbons of the oldest blood. But the guards had drunk deeply of the drugged fig-wine a fruit-seller had left beside the road, and when the drum-major came for the prisoners, the cages held only banana leaves. On each leaf, stamped small and scarlet, was a little flower shaped — if you squinted — like a monkey's face.

The square, naturally, went ape. By nightfall the whole jungle was singing it:

*They seek him here, they seek him there,*
*those guerrillas seek him everywhere!*
*Is he swinging high, or lurking in the dell?*
*That dratted, elusive Chimpernel!*

Two thousand miles of canopy away, in the London Grove, Lady Marguerite Banananey held court at the Zoological Gala — the most beautiful and cleverest she-monkey in Europe, and the most unhappy. Her husband, Sir Percy Banananey, was the richest chimp in England and the champion chimp of foppery.

"La!" said Sir Percy, examining his cravat of pressed banana leaves through a jeweled glass. "The Chimpernel? Sink me, Tony, what is the fellow — some new kind of banana?"

"The savior of the old canopy!" cried young Lord Tony hotly. "A hero!"

"Hero? Odd fish," yawned Sir Percy. "Never cared for fish. Give me a ripe 'nan and a soft bough."

Marguerite turned away, heartsick. She alone knew why her marriage had frozen: she had once, to save her brother Armand, signed a denunciation that sent the St. Ffleurs to the Stump — and Percy had learned of it on their honeymoon, and had never fully come down from the cold high boughs since.

What nobody in England knew — what Percy would have laughed at, elaborately, in three languages — was that Lord Tony and Sir Andrew Fig-Ffoulkes and a dozen more of the richest, dimmest-jawed young lords in the Grove were the Chimpernel's league. By day they posed in the clubs as scenery; by night they crossed the Channel-vine as fruit-sellers, rat-catchers, and old women of the market, each carrying the little scarlet flower sewn under an armpit, returning with exiled gibbons drooping politely in their carts. Membership requirements were a cool head, a warm heart, and the acting ability of an entire theatre troupe that had lost its theatre.

At the edge of the gala lanterns, Citizen Chauvelin was waiting — a scarred gelada with a smile like a peeled vine. He had been attached to the Republic's embassy for one purpose only, and the purpose was a flower.

"Lady Marguerite," he murmured, "how charming. Your brother's letters have fallen into the Republic's paws. Armand has been aiding the Chimpernel's escapes. Help me trap the Chimpernel tomorrow night at the Grey Gibbon Inn in Calais-hollow — or your brother swings from the Stump by the weekend."

"He will never come while I —"

"He will come for his league. All I ask is that you point him out." He pressed a note into her fur: *A banana-skin is slippery underfoot.*

She went. What else could a sister do?

The next evening, at the Fruit-Opera, a letter dropped from Sir Andrew's cuff into the aisle. Marguerite's paw closed on it before it reached the floor. It was signed with nothing but a small scarlet flower, and it said: *The grey gibbon sings in Calais-hollow on Thursday.*

Chauvelin was beside her before the aria ended. "Give it to me, citoyenne, or give me your brother."

She gave him the letter. It was the coldest thing her paw had ever done; her paw has never entirely forgiven her.

Chauvelin left for France that night, purring. And Marguerite went home to find her husband's study lit — her husband supposedly asleep — and on his desk, tide-charts of the Calais mudflats, a plan of the Grey Gibbon Inn, and a heavy signet ring whose face was a little five-petalled flower, shaped — if you squinted — like a monkey's face.

She heard again a hundred drawing-room yawns — *La, sink me; odd fish* — and understood, all at once, that every yawn had been a key turned in a lock, and that she had spent two years laughing at the bravest chimp in England for loving her.

Percy stood in the doorway, dressed for the Channel-vine, with his lazy drawl buckled on like a sword.

"You knew me last, of all the hearts in England," he said quietly. "The pride of every heart but mine."

"Percy — Chauvelin has your letter — the Grey Gibbon —"

"I know, m'dear." He smiled the smile she had married and never once seen. "Where do you think he learned it from? Sit tight, tie up the curtains, and think of England. I shan't be long."

She sat tight for one entire hour. Then she took the night-vine to the coast like a banshee with a wardrobe account.

At the Grey Gibbon Inn the next night, Marguerite sat rigid in the loft while Chauvelin's soldiers crouched among the fig-barrels. A hideous old fruit-hag cackled by the fire, toothless, bent, and scandalously ugly even for a hag. Chauvelin sprang; the bonnet came away; and the "hag" was Sir Andrew Fig-Ffoulkes of the Chimpernel's league, grinning.

"Sorry, citizen. You've caught the wrong monkey."

"Where is he?" hissed Chauvelin.

"Gone to fetch your prisoners, citizen — the ones you stashed in the dune-hut for the morning Stump. He does so hate an empty cart."

Chauvelin raced for the dunes, and the joke of the century was waiting for him there. In the hut, an old, old fruit-wife — more warts than wife — lay groaning on the straw, watched over by Marguerite and a deaf farm-chimp. Chauvelin's soldiers beat the hag soundly about the shoulders with their staves, and the hag howled, and the hag was stuffed in rags a foot thick, and the hag — being beaten on Chauvelin's own furious orders — was removed as useless to the Republic.

What Chauvelin never knew was that he had personally supervised the beating and the release of the Scarlet Chimpernel, and that when his own cart stuck in the mud outside, it was the hag's bony paw, and the farm-chimp's, and even Marguerite's, that helped him push it free.

"Good hunting, citizen," croaked the hag, as the cart rolled away toward the tide-causeway, loaded with snoring "fruit-sellers" and the Comte de St. Ffleurs.

Outside, a farm-cart of rotting figs rumbled toward the causeway, driven by a loutish chimp chewing a stem. Marguerite knew that lazy drawl from a thousand dull dinner-branch conversations.

"La! Sink me, the wind's dashed chilly tonight—"

"Percy!" she cried, leaping onto the cart. "You are going?"

"No, m'dear," said the Scarlet Chimpernel, turning his foppish mask of a face toward her at last — and it was a face she had never seen. "I am not going. I am *returning*. Coming?"

Behind them, Chauvelin's soldiers discovered that the log-causeway had been quietly unmoored, and stood in the black mud going slowly bananas. Somewhere across the water, in Paris-Grove, the Republic's cleverest gelada sat down at his desk and found waiting for him, wax-sealed and cheerful, one pressed banana leaf stamped with a small scarlet flower, and beneath it one line: *Sink me, citizen — the fish you never cared for got away.*

Aboard the ship, with the rescued St. Ffleurs safely in the hold and Armand at the tiller, Percy let the drawl drop.

"I never guessed," whispered Marguerite. "Not once. I lived with you and laughed at you and never once saw you."

"That," said her husband, "was the point of the whole dashed disguise. You were the only soul in England, m'dear, whose opinion I cared a fig for — so yours was the only eye I built the blindfold for. Demmed clever of me, if I say so myself, which I do, constantly, in here, where it does no harm."

"Your letters, Percy — Armand told them things —"

"Armand is at the tiller, alive and insufferable, and the St. Ffleur girls are below comparing the rescues of history and agreeing this one was adequate. All's well." He took her paw, which still remembered being cold. "It has been a dashed long two years, m'dear. I don't recommend pride as a diet."

"Nor I," said Marguerite, "as a perch."

And in London Grove, next morning, the little scarlet flower bloomed in the grey drizzle of the Zoo — and somewhere overhead, a chimp went laughing on his way, foppish, rich, unhurried, and the most wanted monkey in Europe, worth ten thousand bananas to any guerrilla who could catch him, which, the Guerrilla Tribunals admitted in their annual report, was becoming a monkey-shaped hole in the budget.
