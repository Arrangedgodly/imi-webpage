# Fur Past Midnight

*A parody of "Four Past Midnight"*

There is an hour the old keepers know, between the last feeding and the first birds, when the enclosure lamps hum and every monkey's fur stands up on end for no reason at all. They call it fur past midnight. Four of us kept that hour once, and each told a story. Here they are.

**The Langurliers**

Banana Flight 29 ran the red-eye from Leaf Angeles to Banston: twelve monkeys, twenty thousand bunches of plantains, and — somewhere over the mountains — a rip in the coat of time that nobody saw.

Ten of us slept through it, the wrong kind of deep, and woke to an empty airplane. That was the rule, we worked out later, and the rule was the worst part: the ones who were awake when we slipped the seam of the night were simply gone. Gone like checks cashed by somebody else. No fuss, no seat-belt signs, just eleven headrests with dents and no heads. Toomy the purser should have been asleep. He wasn't, and he wasn't gone either — I never said the rule was tidy. "THE SPOILS!" he kept screaming. "THE SPOILS ARE COMING!" We should have listened sooner. And Dinah — a little blind colobus with her head always tipped like a question mark — heard them before any of us: a far-off crunching, she said, like somebody eating the world's largest salad very politely.

The world below was there and not there. The plantains tasted like wet paper. Our voices wouldn't echo off the fuselage. Even smells wouldn't stick to anything. Bob Jangkins, the mystery writer, figured it out first, of course: "We've slipped through a tear in the night, friends. We're parked in a used-up yesterday. And I would very much like to be gone before the maintenance crew arrives."

The maintenance crew. They came over the ridge like a tide: langurs, thousands of them, and every mouth a row of guillotine blades. They didn't chase us. They ate. The empty terminal. The runway. The used-up past itself, in enormous greedy bites, with a sound like a million-strong termite colony with better posture. Dinah heard them the whole time, poor kid, narrating under her breath: "runway... rotunda... baggage carousel..." Toomy ran to meet them, screaming about the spoils, and what happened next was quick and, the kindest thing I can say, earned.

Engle the pilot hand-pumped the fuel — "I've flown banana hoppers through three monsoons" — while the Langurliers ate the taxiway. We flew into the rip with the horizon coming apart behind us, and came out over an airport so new the dew hadn't been drunk yet.

The sun was coming up. It was absolutely morning. There were eleven of us, one empty seat, and none of us has ever slept on a red-eye since.

**Secret Window, Secret Banyan**

Mort Rainsy was a sleepy siamang writer with a banyan, a typewriter, and a marriage that had died of neglect — Amy had taken the house and left him the porch. The knock came at six. On the porch stood the biggest howler he had ever seen, in a round black hat.

"You stoled my story," the howler said. "'Growing Season.' You stoled it."

Mort knew he hadn't — not this time — and could prove it; he'd published first, and showed the magazine. The howler read the date twice, slowly, like a monkey testing a vine, and said: "You got an extra copy of that magazine? Then we got a problem." And he kept coming back, patient as rot. He burned Mort's house to the waterline, which the fire brigade called a lightning strike, though there had been no lightning, only a smell of kerosene and old bananas. He left the round black hat on Mort's porch rail one morning, and here is the thing the police did not find funny and Mort could not explain: the hat fit Mort perfectly. It fit like it had been measured on him. His estranged wife Amy drove up from the city to see the ashes, held the hat in her hands, and looked at her ex-husband the way you look at a banana with something moving under the peel.

Along the way Mort learned a thing about himself: there had been a story once, years ago, in a dead market — half-remembered, and stolen whole. The howler's story. Better than the howler could write it, which was the worst part.

I'd tell you how it ended. But Mort grows tiny green bananas along his porch rail now, and smiles at visitors with his teeth together, and says only one thing on the subject:

"I know I can. I seen it done."

**The Librarian**

Sam Peebles was a prim little macaque afraid of exactly one thing more than he feared spiders: the Librarian. "Lose a book," his mother used to say, "and the Librarian comes for you. He combs the jungle at midnight with a card-file for a heart." Sam had assumed, for forty years, that this was a metaphor. Then, sorting his dead father's things, he found a notice, brittle as an autumn leaf: THE BARREL OF MONKEYS PARTY BOOK — THIRTY YEARS OVERDUE.

The Librarian came at fur past midnight, exactly as advertised: tall, cold, smelling of paste and patience, with a pocket stamp that clicked. And behind him, worse — the thing that had worn him for thirty years, a memory-tick, grey and fat with everyone's stolen childhoods. Sam understood, all at once, that the story his mother told had been a true one, and that somewhere in his own childhood there had been a tall cold monkey in the rain who said "books come back, little one," and that he had buried the memory so deep it had grown a whole personality of its own, and that it wanted Sam's childhood now, interest and all.

So Sam and the young librarian Angie did the only thing you can do to a story-tick:

They read aloud. Everything. Cliffhangers, sob endings, and the encyclopedia's boring parts — the entry for "vine" alone ran forty minutes. It swelled with plot, and comprehension, and finally burst — all over the returns cart, and nobody's problem after that.

Return your books.

**The Sun Howler**

Kev got the camera at Pop Merrill's Emporium, marked down: a Sun-Snapper 660. "Just a novelty, kid," Pop said, taking the money twice, which was Pop's way.

Every picture it took was the same. A field. A white fence. And something black on the far side of the fence — a hound, or something hound-shaped — a little closer each time.

By the ninth photo you could count its teeth. By the fifteenth, Kev swore the teeth were counting him. Pop, who had inherited the camera with the rest of a bad estate, saw a business instead: SPOOKY SNAPS, $5 A LOOK, and for a week the Emporium did steady trade with monkey kids who screamed on schedule, until the queue noticed the hound in each new customer's photo was closer to Pop's counter than it had been to Kev's, and the queue dissolved.

They tried photographing empty air, the void, nothing at all. The camera developed the hound anyway. The last photo anyone took — Kev, at night, hands shaking — showed the field, the fence, and the gate standing open. Nobody has photographed so much as a sunset since. The camera is in the junk drawer now, under a chipped teacup for ballast. Pop's advice stands in the family forever: there are things you point at, and things you don't.

And sometimes, at that hour, the camera clicks by itself. Nobody develops those.

We told our four stories; dawn came up gray and then gold; and not one of us laughed until the birds did.
