# I Want My Macaque Back

*A parody of "I Want My Hat Back"*

Once there was a big gorilla.

The gorilla was a gentle, patient, unhurried gorilla, the color of storm clouds, and he went everywhere with a little macaque who sat on top of his head. The macaque's name was Pip. The macaque chattered all day long — about figs, about parrots, about clouds that looked like other, better clouds — and groomed the gorilla's eyebrows into two perfect arcs, and stole small bites of whatever the gorilla was eating, and the gorilla had long ago decided that this was, on the whole, an excellent arrangement.

One morning the gorilla woke up, and his head was quiet.

"Pip?" said the gorilla.

Nothing chattered.

"Pip?" said the gorilla again, patting the top of his own head with a hand the size of a dinner plate.

Nothing.

The gorilla looked under the leaves. He looked behind the branch. He looked in the fig basket, where Pip liked to hide even though Pip did not fit in the fig basket and never had.

The macaque was gone.

Something must be done. The gorilla squared his big shoulders, and went out to look for his macaque.

The first animal he met was a red uakari, sitting on a stump with a bright red face and a bright red scowl, like a very angry sunset.

"Have you seen my macaque?" asked the gorilla. "He is small. He is brown. He has a little white ruff. He sits on my head."

"Macaque?" said the uakari. "I haven't seen anybody's macaque. I don't even know what a macaque is. Why does everybody always ask me things? I'm red because of genetics, not because I took something."

"Thank you anyway," said the gorilla.

The next animal he met was a great old tortoise, munching a fern with the unhurried dignity of somebody who had been munching ferns since before the gorilla's grandmother was born.

"Have you seen my macaque?" asked the gorilla. "He is small. He chatters. He sits on my head."

"No," said the tortoise, after a long, long pause. "But I have seen a lot of things. I once saw rain fall up. Ask the river monkeys."

"Thank you," said the gorilla.

The next animal he met was an anaconda, draped over six branches at once, which is rude in any company.

"Have you ssseen my macaque?" asked the gorilla. "He is small. He has a little white ruff."

"I have not sssseen your macaque," said the anaconda. "But if I do, I will be sssure to — introduce myself."

"That will not be necessary," said the gorilla, and moved on rather faster than he had arrived.

He asked a tapir, snuffling by the riverbank. The tapir said no, but that he had heard the frogs were saying the mango tree by the swimming hole was very gossipy these days, if that helped.

It did not help.

He asked a kinkajou, who was up a tree and mostly asleep. The kinkajou said, "Mmmno. Come back at midnight," which the gorilla politely did not do.

He asked a peccary, who said no and then asked three other peccaries, who also said no, and then all the peccaries had a long discussion about whether macaques were a kind of fig, which the gorilla did not stay for.

The sun slid across the sky. The gorilla had walked the river path twice and the fig grove three times, and his head was as quiet as an empty nest, and quietness, on a head that had not been quiet in eleven years, is a very heavy thing.

The gorilla lay down on his stomach in the moss, with his chin on the ground, the way gorillas do when they have run out of anywhere to look.

"My macaque is gone," said the gorilla, to nobody. "Nobody has seen my macaque. My head is too quiet. I never told him he chattered too much. He chattered exactly the right amount."

That was when a marsh deer came picking her way along the trail, knees high, nose down, and stopped beside the enormous gloomy mountain of the gorilla.

"What is the matter?" asked the deer.

"Nobody has seen my macaque," said the gorilla. "He sits on my head. I want my macaque back."

"What does your macaque look like?" asked the deer.

The gorilla sighed the sigh of a heart six sizes tired. "He is small," said the gorilla. "He is brown. He has a little white ruff. He sits on my head and grooms my eyebrows and chatters about clouds, and he has a chatter like a nutmeg grater, and he likes green figs even though they are green, and — "

The gorilla stopped.

"He has a little white ruff," said the gorilla again, slowly, in a different voice entirely.

He sat up. The moss fell off him like rain.

"Wait just one minute," said the gorilla.

He remembered the uakari, who had not been asked one single thing and had answered three.

He remembered where the uakari's stump was: directly below the strangler fig, where the kinkajou naps at midnight and nobody looks twice.

And he remembered that when he had asked the uakari, the strangler fig behind the stump had been swinging, very slightly, though there had been no wind.

"I HAVE SEEN MY MACAQUE," said the gorilla, standing up to his full height, which was a lot of height, and some more besides.

The gorilla went back.

He found the uakari on the stump, and behind the stump the strangler fig, and up the strangler fig, in a hole in the trunk, he found a small brown macaque with a little white ruff, who was chattering — at last, unmistakably, chattering — about how he had only climbed in for a moment and how the hole was very interesting and how he had been about to leave anyway.

The gorilla held out one enormous, gentle hand.

"Pip," said the gorilla, "I want my macaque back."

And the little macaque leapt from the hole, and up the gorilla's arm, and onto the great gray head, and gripped the fur there like a sailor gripping a deck, and chattered, at length, about the hole, and the view from the hole, and how the uakari had told him the hole was famous.

"Uh-huh," said the gorilla, contentedly, walking home.

"Uh-huh," said the gorilla's eyebrows, being groomed back into two perfect arcs.

They shared a fig that evening, one green, on principle — the gorilla holding it steady while the macaque nibbled, chattering all the while about clouds, and figs, and the interesting hole, and how the gorilla's eyebrows had gone all bushy with worry and needed a full hour of grooming to recover.

And if the gorilla's shoulders were a little less stooped, and his step a little less quiet, and his head a very great deal noisier, that is what is called a happy ending.

Some days later, a capybara came by the gorilla's tree.

"Did you ever find out what happened to that red-faced uakari?" asked the capybara. "He has moved to the far side of the river. Very suddenly. Nobody has seen him since."

The gorilla did not look up from his fig.

"I would not sit a uakari on the highest branch of the tallest tree and leave him there to think about things," said the gorilla.

"There is no use asking me any more questions," said the gorilla.

And on top of his head, very quietly, for once, a little macaque smiled.
