# Interview with the Vamprimate

*A parody of "Interview with the Vampire"*

The vamprimate looked twenty-five and talked like a tree that had read everything.

"I assume you'd like my story," he said, arranging the last of the daylight behind him. "Very well. But no screaming, no clinging, and change nothing I say. Tape is forever."

"You're really — a vamprimate," said the boy, adjusting his recorder with shaking hands.

"A dead ape who drinks the living nectar of bananas," said Louis, "and leaves them gray. Yes. I died in 1791. I have been swinging ever since."

He told it plainly, without hurry and without mercy.

"I had a banana plantation south of New Orleans, a brother I had failed, and a fever in me that wanted everything to end. My brother died. I prayed for a plague. The plague came for me — we should all be careful what we ask the jungle. — He found me on the plantation road at dusk. Lestat de Vine: golden-furred, dressed like an opera, with the manners of a knife. 'Do you want to die passively?' he asked me. 'Or do you want to swing?' I said something pious. He laughed, and bit my ankle — the classic hold, on a primate — and the whole jungle arrived in one flash: every banana that ever ripened, every ape that ever wronged me, unbearably bright. Then he opened his own wrist over my mouth. 'Banana daiquiri of the gods,' he said. 'Drink, Louis. One sip and you will never again groom a mortal.'"

Understand what we were, the boy. Lestat drank from the plantation's bananas — the big golden bunches — and left them gray on the stem, and the mortals whispered about the blight, and Lestat found that hilarious. It was his genius to make a joke of appetite. He installed his blind old father in our parlour, a snoring antique who thought I was an angel of the lord, and the only soul on that estate I could talk to. He made me hunt with him in the streets at night and play backgammon with him until dawn, and when I told him I could not bear it, he made the piano louder.

I tried to be the conscience of the firm. There was a neighbor, Babette, a young widow running a failing grove alone, and I used to sit in the dark outside her window advising her like a small gray god of crop rotation — the only good I ever did, and I did it by eavesdropping. When the slaves of the estate began whispering that the masters were demons — a fair reading, the evidence being gray bananas everywhere — Lestat burned Pointe du Lac out of spite, and we stood on the levee, my past and his father, all of it in one orange line of fire. The old ape died in the smoke asking for his son. Lestat watched with interest. I have never stopped being angry about that, and I have had two centuries of practice.

I would not drink the big bananas. I drank rats' bananas — little stolen sour bunches — and stayed in moral crisis for a decade, which Lestat found even funnier than the fire.

"You are the most tedious immortail in Louisiana," he told me, and turned up the harpsichord.

"Then the plague orphaned a baby spider monkey, and I could not let her die, and Lestat could not let me have a reason to leave. He made her. Claudia. — Picture a vamprimate with ringlets and a bonnet and the eyes of a jury. Sixty years old in her mind, forever the size of a coconut. She was the best of both of us, which was the problem. She learned to hunt like Lestat and to grieve like me, and then one year her mind grew up and her teeth stayed small, and there is no tragedy in the jungle like a lady forever winning the children's menu.

"'I'm thirty,' she said to me once, evenly, 'and I look like something you win at a fair.' — 'You're lovely,' I said. — 'I'm a doll, Louis. I'm a banana doll. I want a mother. I want a body that has *happened* to it everything mine did, so somebody in this house understands me at eye level.'

"She poisoned him, in the end. Dead bananas — gray ones, cut from the stems of two drowned plague twins. (Yes. Twins. It is always twins.) He fell, he burned, we buried the jewelry and ran for Paris by riverboat, and one does not surprise a vamprimate twice — he came back, of course. They always come back.

"In Paris we found the Théâtre des Vamprimates, an unholy little stage troupe under the Cemetery of the Innocents — mortals paying to be frightened by real monsters pretending to be actors pretending to be monsters, which even I found structurally ambitious. And Armand, four hundred years old, with the eyes of a lemur who has watched empires fall and taken notes. Armand saw in me a pupil. Claudia saw in Armand her own sentence, and in a little dollmaker called Madeline the mother she had ordered. We made Madeline that spring, the three of us, one small strange family of the made and the making.

"The troupe held a trial for our crimes against Lestat. They condemned the two of them to the aviary — locked her in a cage with the dawn coming — and Claudia, my Claudia, who feared nothing except smallness, was made small one final time, and the sun did what the sun does to a ripe thing. Madeline held her hand through it. I do not speak of it further, except to say that I opened the theater's roof at noon while the troupe slept, and the whole of Paris heard about it."

The boy sat very still. "And after?"

"After? After is the part nobody wants. I burned the theater and walked out of Paris in the smoke. I have crossed the same river four hundred times and mourned on every bridge. I saw the electric lamp invented and hated it at once; I have seen every dead ape I love outlived by their furniture. Immortality, boy, is not more life. It is the same life, with the fruit removed, forever."

"I walked. I swung. I kept fig trees in five cities and left every bunch untouched. I went back once, to New Orleans, and found Lestat hiding in a ruined town house feeding on chicken bananas, terrified of everything, and I gave him the one thing I had left over: I told him Claudia was dead, and watched it matter. Then I came here. I am telling you the truth, which vamprimates almost never do, so attend."

"But you could make another," the boy said, in a rush. "If I asked. If I begged. You'd never have to be alone."

"And the point of that would be — what, exactly?" said Louis.

"I'd live forever."

"You'd be *forever*," said Louis, "a monkey in the shadow of the banana tree, pretending to be a bat." He smiled then — the first real one of the night — and stood at the window with his back to the first gray of dawn. "Go home, boy. Grow old. Groom someone who grooms you back. That is the whole circus."

And the sun came up like a dropped egg, and the interview, after two hundred years, was over.
