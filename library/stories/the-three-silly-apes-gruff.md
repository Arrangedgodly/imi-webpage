# The Three Silly Apes Gruff

*A parody of "The Three Billy Goats Gruff"*

Far up a hillside jungle, where the mist hung about the trees like laundry, there lived three gorilla brothers called the Three Silly Apes Gruff. They were called Silly not because they were foolish — well, not only — but because they giggled at everything: at rain, at beetles, at each other, and most of all at their own names.

They were called Gruff because when they laughed, which was always, they sounded like three landslides arguing over a joke.

The trouble with the hillside was that it was finished. The three brothers had eaten it that way. There wasn't a leaf left at eye level for a mile around, and the hillside now looked like a mango stone after a troop of capuchins — which, the capuchins pointed out, was a professional compliment.

Down in the valley below, the grass grew sweet and green, and the banana trees bent double with fruit. But between the hillside and the valley ran a wide brown river, rushing and gurgling, and across it there was only one crossing: an old log bridge. And under that bridge there lived a grumpy old crocodile named Snappy, who was three-quarters temper and one-quarter teeth, and who regarded the bridge as his personal front door. Nobody crossed without paying, and the toll was: you.

One morning the three brothers stood looking down at the valley, drooling in three-part harmony, and made up their minds: down to the valley, to eat bananas until their bellies dragged.

"Who goes first?" asked Middle Silly Ape Gruff.

"You," said Big Silly Ape Gruff.

"Not me — I'm far too little to go first," said Little Silly Ape Gruff.

"Then you're exactly the right size," said his brothers, and giggled, and nudged him toward the bridge.

"Wait," said Little Silly Ape Gruff, whose knees were shaking but whose wits were not. "If he eats the first one, the first one is me. And if he is full of me, there are no bananas for anybody. This plan is silly."

"We ARE silly," said his brothers, reasonably.

"That's not a reason," said Little Silly Ape Gruff. But he went anyway, because the bananas were bending double down there and a gorilla can only be sensible for so long.

His knuckles went slap-slap-slap on the old logs.

From under the bridge came a voice like a mudslide. "WHO'S THAT SLAPPING OVER MY BRIDGE?"

Little Silly Ape Gruff's fur stood straight up. But he remembered his manners (and his brothers, who were bigger, and his plan, which was tiny but load-bearing). "It is only the Little Silly Ape Gruff. I'm going over to the valley to eat bananas and make myself fat."

"FAT?" roared Snappy. "Then I'm coming up to gobble you up!"

"Gobble ME?" said Little Silly Ape Gruff. "I'm all ears and elbows. I'd rattle. I'm mostly giggle — you'd be burping punchlines for a week. Wait for my brother, Middle Silly Ape Gruff — he's much, much plumper, and far more filling."

"Plumper, is he? Off you go, then." And Little Silly Ape Gruff went slap-slapping over the bridge, and up the valley side, where he hid behind a banana tree and giggled into his fists until the tree shook.

Along came Middle Silly Ape Gruff, twice the size, his knuckles going slap-slappity-SLAP.

"WHO'S THAT SLAPPING OVER MY BRIDGE?"

"It is only the Middle Silly Ape Gruff. I'm going over to the valley to eat bananas and make myself really fat."

"REALLY FAT? Then I'm coming up to gobble you up!"

"Oh ho," said Middle Silly Ape Gruff, who had rehearsed this on the way down and was rather pleased with it. "You and what army of teeth? Besides — you should see my brother, Big Silly Ape Gruff. He's plump as two of me and twice as tasty. He's practically a meal with a chest-beat. Wait for him."

"Twice as tasty! Be off with you." And Middle Silly Ape Gruff crossed that bridge rather faster than he had meant to, and hid behind the same banana tree, and the two of them giggled together in the shade.

"You said I was plump," Little Silly Ape Gruff reminded him.

"Emergency measures," said Middle Silly Ape Gruff. "Shhh."

Then the whole hillside began to shudder. The mist itself got out of the way. Down came Big Silly Ape Gruff, knuckles going SLAP. SLAP. SLAP. — and the old logs groaned like a ship in a storm.

"WHO'S THAT SLAPPING OVER MY BRIDGE?" bellowed Snappy, who by now was terribly hungry and not at all patient, having passed up two perfectly good apes on the promise of a better one.

"ME," said Big Silly Ape Gruff, in a voice like a rockslide learning to talk. "IT IS THE BIG SILLY APE GRUFF, AND I AM GOING OVER TO THE VALLEY TO EAT BANANAS AND GET ENORMOUS."

"ENORMOUS? Then I'm coming up to gobble you up!"

"GOBBLE ME UP?" And Big Silly Ape Gruff beat his chest — boom, boom, BOOM — and hooted the loudest hoot ever hooted on that hillside, so loud the toucans are still talking about it. "COME UP THEN, AND I SHALL HEAD-BUTT YOU INTO THE MIDDLE OF NEXT WET SEASON!"

Snappy lunged up over the rail, jaws wide — and met the biggest, hardest, squarest gorilla forehead in six jungles. BONK. Over the side of the bridge he went, splash, into the very middle of the river.

But crocodiles are stubborn, and Snappy had skipped breakfast. Half-dazed, he lunged a second time and got one set of teeth neatly into Big Silly Ape Gruff's heel callus — which is the toughest part of the toughest ape in the valley, and has the texture of an old canoe. Big Silly Ape Gruff simply kicked, the way you flick a coconut off a branch, and Snappy sailed backwards through the mist, did one complete somersault, and re-entered the river with a splash that soaked a heron on the far bank.

"HE ASKED," boomed Big Silly Ape Gruff, to nobody in particular, and crossed the bridge with enormous dignity, pausing in the middle to look down at the bubbles. "THANK YOU FOR THE WARM WELCOME," he added. "LOVELY BRIDGE. TERRIBLE NEIGHBOR."

The three Silly Apes Gruff met in the middle of the valley and laughed until they cried — and then laughed until Little Silly Ape Gruff fell over into the sweet green grass, and then laughed at that. Then they ate bananas until their bellies dragged, and lay in the shade comparing silhouettes, and agreed that of all the silly things they had ever done, sending the littlest brother first had somehow been the wisest.

"That's the silliest part," said Little Silly Ape Gruff, thumping his full belly. "It worked."

At sunset they slapped happily home over the bridge, singing in three keys, none of which had ever met before. From under the bridge: silence. Perfect, professional silence.

And if the crocodile has not moved away, he lives under that bridge still — but they say that nowadays, whenever anybody crosses, Snappy stays very, very quiet at the bottom of the river and pretends to be a log.

Which, if you ask the Silly Apes Gruff, is the silliest thing of all.
