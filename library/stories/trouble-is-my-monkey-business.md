# Trouble Is My Monkey Business

*A parody of "Trouble Is My Business"*

The gorilla who walked into my office that morning weighed three hundred pounds of solid detective agency, and she was crying.

"Anna Halsee," I said. "What banana got peeled?"

"Phil." Anna Halsee had the office across the hall for years; her trunk was bigger than mine, and her heart wasn't much smaller. She sat down on my client chair and nearly became its permanent resident. "Lindsay Marriott. Rich heir, silly as a peeled grape. He's fallen in with a gold-digger. I was hired to keep her off him — by his mother, and she pays in solid gold bananas — and I failed, and I got fired. Do it for me, Phil. Keep trouble away from that poor sap."

Trouble is my monkey business. I took the case for expenses and a warm feeling, which in Landon buys you half a fig.

"You're a soft touch, Anna."

"I'm an expensive touch gone soft," she said, and blew her nose on the case file. It was going to be that kind of case.

Lindsay Marriott turned out to be a slim capuchin with a monocle and no detectable spine. He received me on a chaise furnished entirely in his own good opinion of himself, and giggled when I warned him about the gold-digger — a torch singer called Frances who worked the Purple Fig Tree Club and could peel a rich heir with her eyes shut. He giggled longer, but less comfortably, when he told me the rest.

"Fact is, old scout, I need a rough chap like you tonight. A lady friend of mine had her jade necklace stolen at the Coconut Club dinner — a string of green-jade banana beads, worth a plantation. I'm taking eight thousand in gold bananas to buy it back. The thieves insist I bring a witness, presumably to confirm how silly I look. Rendezvous is the ravine under the Great Fig. Signal: one blink of a firefly lantern, then two."

He said "a lady friend" the way other monkeys say "a business associate," which told me plenty. I told him the setup smelled like three-day-old durian. He giggled. He'd have giggled at his own funeral, which showed foresight, because that's what it nearly was.

That evening, before the cloak-and-dagger, I did my homework at the Purple Fig Tree. Frances sang torch songs like every lyric owed her money, which they probably did. A string of green-jade banana beads lay at her throat, bright as pond water, and Marriott sat at a front table radiating allowance. An organ-grinder's capuchin tried to sell me a raffle ticket on a used sedan chair. I bought two. Business was good somewhere, and in Landon that is always a warning.

At the ravine the moonlight lay on the dry creek like spilled milk. Marriott blinked the lantern: once, twice. Down the dark came an answering blink, and a voice soft as moss: "Put the sack on the flat rock, little monkey."

Then somebody fed me a knuckle sandwich the size of a plantain. I went down the creek bank and into the black, and my last thought was that trouble had bitten off more than my business.

I woke with a headache that had branches and twigs. Two of the Banyard's finest were standing over me, looking disappointed that I was ambulatory. Lindsay Marriott lay ten feet away with a vine pulled tight around his throat, and the eight thousand gold bananas were gone, and so were the jade beads, which I never saw again except as a green blur on a torch singer's neck in the club that one time.

A detective sergeant named Spanner, built like a tool shed, asked me where I'd been while my client was being vine-garrotted. "Unconscious," I said. "Fed a knuckle sandwich by persons unknown." He wrote down "persons unknown" and underlined it twice, which is how you spell "suspect" at the Banyard. I gave him the lantern signals, the mossy voice, and the sandwich, in that order. He gave me three days to stay findable and a look like a fig a week past its sell-by.

The trail was classic: cigarette butts chewed by a nervous capuchin, tyre — no, tail-drag marks of a big visitor who'd waited in the ferns, and in the ferns a dried fig of the sort Frances tossed from the bandstand. It didn't add up to a plantation, but it added up to a club.

I visited the Purple Fig Tree at midnight. Frances wore a green-jade anklet and a smile like the last banana in a famine. The band was playing something slow, for monkeys who had given up.

"Looking for these, shamus?" She jingled the anklet. "Come and take them."

"I've seen your beads twice now," I said, watching her eyes instead of the jade. "Once on your neck at the club, once on your ankle here, and never on any thief. They're paste, sister. You palmed the real string months ago and have been wearing the receipts. The 'ransom' tonight was theatre — Marriott's own accountant arranged the theft, needed the sap dead before the books were audited, and hired the ravine for the closing night. You were engaged to keep him happy, dizzy, and plausible. That's the second time tonight somebody's used you as scenery. It must grate."

She slapped me. The slap had been bought at a better class of store than her beads.

"Grate," she said, tasting the word like a fig with a wasp in it. "Mister, I have been scenery for eleven years, eight of them in this club. You're the first monkey to notice me all season, and you did it to arrest me. Buy me a drink and I'll draw you a map."

Because torch singers hate being scenery more than they love money, the map was accurate. It led to the accountant who'd be counting the eight thousand — a tight-wound saki called Crimp, who kept Marriott books and kept himself in figs off the skim, and who was at that moment doing sums in a warehouse above the ravine with two gorillas he hadn't paid.

I let the Banyard know, in my own quiet way, and I brought along a little scenery of my own: a string of paste jade I'd had a Coptic jeweller whip up between lunch and dinner. When Crimp bolted down the ravine path with the sack, I stepped into the moonlight, flashed the paste string, and shouted the one thing he couldn't resist: "The real beads, you sap — she kept the real ones!"

He lunged for the string in my paw, dropped the sack to grab with both hands, and found out what the Banyard charges for larceny with a monkey attached. He was so busy grabbing trouble with both hands that the constables did the rest.

The state got its murderer — two gorillas' testimony travels fast once the bananas stop coming — and Frances skipped to the Cara-bean with her paste and her paste, which is the kind of luggage that never gets lost. Mrs. Marriott got her jade banana beads back and asked me, over the agreeable corpse of a bottle of fig-wine, to name my own reward.

She was the lady friend, it turned out. Lindsay had called his own mother "a lady friend," which told me everything I never needed to know about Lindsay Marriott, and most of it was sad.

I named a small one, a very small one. Trouble I take for free.

It's my monkey business.
