# Waiting for Bonobo

*A parody of "Waiting for Godot"*

A jungle path. One tree. More jungle path than tree, really — a scraggly baobab with a single bare stub of branch, no leaves, and certainly no bananas.

Estragoon, an older baboon with tired feet, sat on a stone trying to pull off his climbing gloves. Vervetimir, a vervet with a philosophy degree from the University of Somewhere, stood beside him.

"No bananas to be found," said Estragoon, giving up on the gloves. "Nothing to be done."

"I'm beginning to come round to that opinion," said Vervetimir. "All my life I've tried to put it from me, saying, Vervetimir, be reasonable, you haven't yet tried everything. And I resumed the struggle." He turned one glove hopefully inside out. It remained a glove. "But this morning I looked in the empty fruit-store and thought: no bananas to be found."

The gloves would not come off. He tried the left one, then the right. Neither.

"There's one thing I've learned in all my years of gloves," said Estragoon. "Never climb on a Tuesday."

"Let's go."

"We can't."

"Why not?"

"We're waiting for Bonobo."

"Ah, yes." A pause. "Are you sure it was this tree?"

"A mountain of monkeys couldn't find another tree within a mile. Look at it."

They looked at it. It did not look back.

"Let's hang ourselves from that branch."

"With what?"

"You still have the vine."

"Yes, but it might break."

"And we'd fall."

"We'd hang, then fall, then break. It'd be one thing or the other."

"Or Bonobo might come and be angry that we'd hung ourselves."

"Then we'll hang ourselves tomorrow, unless Bonobo comes."

"Or a banana."

"Or a banana." They waited. Nothing happened. Nothing continued to happen with great skill. "Shall we groom each other while we wait?"

They groomed each other desultorily, picking at nothing, and agreed that the nothing was of excellent quality.

Into the silence came a tremendous crashing, and an enormous orangutan named Pozzo drove a capuchin called Lucky before him on a long vine. Pozzo carried a foldout leaf-chair and a picnic basket. Lucky carried the other baskets, the spare baskets, the stool for the baskets, and a heavy bag of prime-grade fruit, and did not put them down.

"Why doesn't he put them down?" asked Estragoon.

"Because he wants to impress me," said Pozzo, "so that I'll keep him. It is the same in all families." He settled into the leaf-chair and consulted a watch made of a large white flower, which told the time by wilting. "It is afternoon, which will in its turn become evening, which has been known to become night. Behold. Lucky! Dance!"

Lucky put down the baskets, shuffled twice, hopped once on the spot, and stopped.

"He's stopped," observed Estragoon.

"Old-style dancing," said Pozzo. "Now! Think!"

Lucky swayed, dribbled slightly, and produced a torrent: "Given the existence as uttered forth in the banana works of Bananowski of a banana and its peel quaquaquaqua, the peel being white that it ripens, the ripening being yellow that it peels, its personal monkey remaining unfinished quaquaquaqua the trooplevel gods the canopy gods the understudy gods the understudy gods of the canopy quaquaquaqua —"

"WILL YOU STOP HIM!" howled Vervetimir, hands over his ears.

Pozzo snatched the little thinking-cap from Lucky's head, and Lucky fell silent and twitched, like a kettle remembering better days.

"Do you two mind if I sit longer? I am perhaps a little tired." He ate a banana, tossed the skin to Estragoon, who gnawed it gratefully, and wept briefly about the passage of time. "I would not have been seen dangling a friend on a vine when I was young and beautiful. Now I do it professionally. One day he went dumb, one day I went blind, one day we all went deaf. They call that the winds of time. It blows straight through a monkey like a jungle path." He rose, cracked the vine like a whip, and the two lurched off into the trees. "On! On!"

Silence again. Dusk.

A small marmoset crept out and touched Vervetimir's knee. "Sirs, I come with a message from Mr. Bonobo."

"At last! What does he say?"

"Mr. Bonobo says: he will not come this evening, but he will surely come tomorrow."

"You saw him yourself?"

"Nobody sees him, sir. But he will surely come."

The marmoset bowed and scampered away.

"You see?" said Vervetimir. "Tomorrow, everything will be better."

"What do we do if he doesn't come?"

"We'll have waited for nothing, which is at least something to do. We always find something, in the end, to keep us happy." Estragoon slept. Vervetimir watched the evening come on like something owed. "At me too, someone is looking," he said to nobody. "Of me too, someone is saying: he is sleeping, he knows nothing, let him sleep on."

Night. Then, with the arrogance of theatre, day.

The tree had — impossibly — three leaves and one green banana. Vervetimir was pacing, singing:

"A monkey came in the kitchen,
And stole a golden banana —
The cook, in a fright, gave chase through the night,
And beat him with a big wooden spoon — oh!"

"That's got no ending," said Estragoon, waking.

"Everything has an ending. It arrives when you stop singing."

Vervetimir's hat — a broad battered leaf with a chin-vine — sat badly on his head. They swapped hats. Estragoon's brow frowned; Vervetimir's ears wobbled. They swapped back, with the unhurried care of museum curators.

"It's not our hat anyway," said Estragoon.

"No," said Vervetimir, sadly. "We've no hats of our own."

Estragoon looked at his gloves, which had come off in the night and were now, unmistakably, too big. "Everything I take off turns out to be the wrong size."

"The banana," said Vervetimir, changing the subject with both hands. "Shall we eat it?"

"It's green."

"We could pretend."

They didn't. Vervetimir held it to the light and pronounced upon it gravely. "One of the thieves," he said at last, "was saved. It's a reasonable percentage. We are all, in the eyes of whoever is counting, green bananas."

Pozzo and Lucky blundered past again — Pozzo now blind, feeling the air with his knuckles; Lucky now mute, the vine looped between them like bad punctuation. Pozzo did not remember having met them. "I present myself: I am Pozzo."

"We met you yesterday," said Estragoon. "You had all your eyes then. You threw away a banana skin."

"I remember no bananas. I remember no skins. Do not question me! The blind have no notion of time — it comes and takes you and stores you in its cheek-pouch, and nothing you can do will bring you back. They give birth astride of a dung-heap: the light gleams an instant, then it's night once more."

"Let us not then speak ill of our generation," said Vervetimir. "It is not any unhappier than its predecessors. But what was your Lucky, when he could speak?"

"A thinker. It exhausted him. On! On!"

And they were gone, crashing into the trees, the vine humming like a struck string.

The marmoset returned at dusk, wringing his little hands.

"Well? What does Mr. Bonobo say?"

"He says he will not come this evening, but he will surely come tomorrow."

"You saw him yourself?"

"Well — he speaks to my brother, and my brother speaks to me."

"Tell him — tell him we are waiting. Tell him we saw the banana and did not eat it. Tell him we thought of the vine and did not hang ourselves. Tell him there is nothing to be done and we are doing it beautifully."

"I will tell him," said the marmoset, and was gone.

Estragoon looked at the tree. Vervetimir looked at the path.

"What do we do now?"

"Now we wait."

"And if he doesn't come?"

"We'll hang ourselves tomorrow. Unless the banana ripens."

"Or the banana ripens and we don't hang ourselves."

"Yes."

"Yes."

Silence.

"Well? Shall we go?"

"Yes, let's go."

They did not move.
