# Song of Susabanana

*A parody of "Song of Susannah"*

Bananala-come-one, bananala-come-all,
the 'nana is ripe and the ripe one must fall.

Susabanana woke up mid-step, which is a terrible way to wake up, because the step was not hers.

*Who has the wheel?* she asked inside her own head.

*I do,* said Mia. *I have the body, and I am going to have the chap. My chap. My own little banana.*

Mia. The third tenant, grown in the attic of Susabanana's mind like a fig in a mason jar. Susabanana fought with fists and every rude word Detta had ever taught her, but Mia had the legs, and the legs were walking toward the Unfound Tree-Hole, a doorway of bark hanging on nothing at all.

"Mia, you woolly-headed fool," Susabanana snarled. "Where are we going?"

"To Zoo York," Mia said dreamily. "To the Piggy Stand. They promised me a banquet — bananas enough for a hundred troops, all for my chap. Bananala-come-come, chap-a-chum-chum."

"They? Who's they?"

"The Low Monkeys," said Mia, in the voice of a monkey quoting the only sermon she'd ever heard. "They said they'd be family."

Behind them, through the trees, three monkeys were already chasing: Roland, Eddie, and young Jake, following the drag of Mia's longing like a scent.

"The Unfound Door," Roland said grimly. "Whoever's on the other side has been peeling her along for months."

"She's not evil," Jake said. "She's just hungry."

"Hungry plus promises," said Eddie, who had once been a balloon of jungle juice with a chimp attached, "is exactly how they get you."

While the legs walked, the war in the skull went on, quiet as grooming. Mia was stronger at night, and stronger still near fruit, and Susabanana learned to hide her thoughts the way a monkey hides a last banana — in cupboards, behind her back, under the mattress of small ideas. When Mia ransacked the attic looking for the ka-tet's plans, all she found was a memory of Detta cussing a riverboat, and a shopping list.

*You're slowing me down,* Mia snapped.

*Good,* said Susabanana. *You're walking my body down the middle of somebody else's plan. Ask yourself one thing, sugar: when did a Low Monkey ever feed anybody but himself?*

Mia didn't answer. But that night, holding the belly, she hummed a lullaby nobody had ever sung for her, and Susabanana lay still in the dark of their shared head and listened, and began — may the troop forgive her — to feel the first curl of pity.

They stopped once, at the little station by the Tree-Hole, and did two things. First, Roland took from his pack a bad banana, black as a bruise and cold as a stone — Black Thirteen, which whispered, when you held it, that everything would be easier if you just threw it at somebody. They checked it into a locker, wrapped in a sock, with exact change. "Some baggage," Roland said, "you fly without."

Second, Eddie wrote a note and left it with a scruffy storyteller ape who lived under the station ficus with a typewriter and a look of permanent deadline. This was Calvine Tower-of-Ficus, keeper of tales, and the note said: *A fruit cart is going to find you. Do not nap in the road.*

That afternoon, sure enough, a runaway fruit cart came off the hill, wobble, shriek, veer — and two monkeys yanked the storyteller out of its path by his collar.

"You're the one writing us," Eddie said, not quite a question. "Aren't you?"

"I mostly take dictation," the storyteller said, pale. "The story comes through me. I just spell things wrong."

"What happens if the cart had hit you?"

"Then the story keeps going without a teller. And untold stories," he said, "end badly. They end *wrongly*." He looked at Roland a long moment. "You'd know, gunswinger."

"Then keep typing," said Roland. "We'll hold the door."

Through the Tree-Hole was Zoo York, a city of ficus towers where pale monkeys in suits paid nine pebbles for a smoothie. Mia walked the body down Canopy Street to a stand shaped like a grinning pig, painted pink, lit by a hundred little bulbs.

The Piggy Stand.

The Low Monkeys were waiting. They wore tiny fedoras and long coats, and they smelled like a heating pad full of old fruit.

"Welcome, Mia," said the smallest, bowing. "The banquet is nearly ready. Bring the chap."

Inside, the table was set for a hundred, and a maitre d' with a waxed mustache held out a menu with a flourish. The menu had one item, printed in gold. The smallest Low Monkey snapped his fingers, and a busboy wheeled out the high chair with great ceremony, like a crown going to the tower.

"Table for two?" asked the maitre d', and his smile had too many meanings in it.

And inside Susabanana's head, at last, the truth stood up and stretched.

*Banquet,* said Susabanana. *Girl, look at their teeth. Look at their little knives and forks. You're not the guest. The chap is.*

*You lie.*

*Look through my eyes. I've been lied to by professionals.*

Mia looked. Mia saw the high chair at the head of the table. Mia saw the bibs, and they were not for her.

That was when Roland, Eddie, and Jake came through the Tree-Hole into the alley behind the stand — with Father Coconut, a parish monkey of enormous faith and terrible aim, whom they'd recruited at the station chapel for exactly one purpose.

"Mia, daughter of none!" Roland's voice cracked like a whip made of calm. "Come out and talk, or I will come in and screech, and I learned screeching from my mother."

"You can't make me," said Mia. But she was wobbling.

"Consider who promised you," said Roland. "The kind that promise banquets to hungry mothers are the kind that eat the young of their own troop. The Tower is real, Mia, and so is the lie at the bottom of it."

The Low Monkeys surged. Father Coconut stepped forward, held up both paws, and blessed the entire crowd at once — a static crackle of holy zest that made every fedora in the alley stand straight up. "Take the door, children," he said, not unkindly. "I'll hold the porch."

They took the door.

And then Susabanana did the bravest thing a monkey with two tenants can do. She stopped fighting Mia, sat with her in the parlor of their shared skull, and put an arm around her.

"You wanted what every monkey wants," she said gently. "A chap with your eyes and somebody to groom. There's no shame in the want. Only in who you asked."

"They said I'd be family," Mia whispered.

"Family doesn't set the table with you on it."

And Mia, who had never once been offered kindness that didn't come with a fork, let go — the way a leaf lets go in October, all at once. The body went slack. Susabanana caught it.

The chap arrived an hour later, in the alley, in the rain: a small red-faced monkey with a grip like a shop-vise, who seized Eddie's nose, then a banana from Jake's pack, and refused to give either back.

"He can keep it," Jake said, awed. "Prime mate material right there."

The Low Monkeys, banquetless, stood in the rain weeping into their fedoras, and were last seen filing a complaint with the Zoo York Better Business Bureau (three raccoons in a trench coat). Father Coconut was found on the porch, unharmed, blessing the raindrops for their punctuality.

They brought the chap back through the Tree-Hole at dawn. Roland held the door. Susabanana sang to her boy, low and sweet:

Bananala-come-one, bananala-come-all,
you're the ripest little 'nana, and you'll never have to fall.

And somewhere in the branches overhead, a scruffy storyteller ape scribbled it all down on a fig leaf — until Susabanana looked up.

"You," she said. "Out of the tale. This one's ours."

The storyteller went sheepishly, pausing only to leave a banana. It seemed the least a narrator could do.

Behind him, on the last leaf of his manuscript, he wrote the ending twice, to be safe: *And they lived. And they lived.*
