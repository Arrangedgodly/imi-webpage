# The Phantom Howlbooth

*A parody of "The Phantom Tollbooth"*

Milo the young chimp was bored. Not ordinary bored — profoundly, magnificently bored. "There is nothing to swing to," he sighed, hanging by one arm, "and nothing worth swinging for."

So he was extremely annoyed when an enormous package thumped down beside him and began, politely, to howl. It was a booth, purple and gold, with a sign that read: **WELCOME TO THE PHANTOM HOWLBOOTH. ONE RIPE BANANA PER PASSAGE. DRIVE SAFELY.**

Inside: a map of the Jungles Beyond (with the good parts folded in), a rulebook, and one small banana-kart.

"This is ridiculous," said Milo, paid his banana, and drove.

At a place called Expectations, a whiskery monkey in a cloud of dust waved him down. "Welcome, welcome! I'm the Whether Monkey."

"Do you mean the weather?" asked Milo.

"No, no. Whether it rains or whether it shines, whether you will or whether you won't — I decide nothing, I only ask. Expect everything, I always say, and the unexpected never happens!"

It made no sense, so Milo drove on and got lost in the Dolldrums, where lazy lorises lay around doing nothing on purpose. "We're killing time," one explained drowsily.

"KILLING it?" barked a voice, and up strode an enormous howler monkey with a ticking clock set right in his belly. "It's bad enough wasting time without killing it! I'm Tock. HOWL. I howl the hours. Riding with me, if you like. Somebody has to keep you moving."

They rolled into Dictionopolis, the word market, where stalls sold adjectives by the bunch and you could genuinely eat your words. A humbug of a gorilla in a plumed hat was holding forth. "I," the Humbug declared, "once ate a thesaurus. Every word tasted slightly different, but all of them agreed with me."

At the bun stall, a jumpy insect was spelling out S-Y-N-O-N-A-N-A in the air. "The Spelling Flea," said Tock. Milo bit into a synonana bun — it meant the same as a banana bun but tasted more expensive.

They should have gone home then. Instead there was a banquet with King Azaz the Unabridged, a mandrill of enormous vocabulary and enormous nose, and over dessert (nouns, mostly) the whole sorry story came out: how Dictionopolis and Digitopolis, the city of numbers, had gone to war over which was greater, and how the king's two adopted nieces — the twin macaque princesses Rhyme and Rhesus — had ruled that words and numbers were equally precious, and how BOTH kings, furious, had exiled the princesses to the Castle in the Air, in the Mounds of Ignorance, from which no monkey had returned.

"Rescue them, then," sniffed the Humbug, "if you're so clever."

"He shall," boomed Azaz. "But first — you must visit my brother the Mathemagician. Even a rescue needs a plan, and plans, regrettably, involve both of us."

Digitopolis was a city of holes — neat, square, numbered holes — where numbers were dug fresh out of the ground and polished each morning. They were met at the gate by a colobus with twelve faces.

"Welcome!" said all twelve faces at once, one delighted, one stern, two merely thoughtful. "I'm the Dodecacolobus. Which face would you like today? Most visitors prefer the fourth. It agrees with everything."

"How do you carry all those opinions?" asked Tock.

"Evenly," said the Dodecacolobus. "That's rather the point of me."

He led them to the Mathemagician — a vast silverback in a robe of chalk dust, with a pencil behind each ear and a very long one in his fist. "HAR!" he cried, delighted to have anyone to bully. "So you've come to learn about numbers! Excellent! What's the biggest number you know?"

"A million," said Milo.

"Wrong! A million and one! And the biggest number THERE is a million and two! The biggest number, young chimp, is always one bigger than whatever you just said, which means — and this is the beautiful part — it is never actually there! Now! Lunch!"

Lunch was subtraction stew. The bowls were enormous. The stew was delicious. And the more you ate, the hungrier you got.

"I don't understand," said Milo, hollow as a drum.

"Subtraction stew," said the Mathemagician, beaming. "You eat it and get hungrier. If you want to feel full, obviously, you must first eat dinner, and THEN the stew takes it from there. We eat dinner before lunch in Digitopolis. It's very orderly."

And on the way out of town, Milo spotted a sign on a small green island across a pond: CONCLUSIONS. The island looked pleasant, the water looked shallow, and the sign below read simply: ALL ARE WELCOME.

"How do we get there?" asked Milo.

"You jump," said the Dodecacolobus, with every face grinning. "Everybody does."

So Milo jumped — and Tock and the Humbug, grumbling, jumped after him — and it was, in fact, remarkably easy to get to Conclusions. It was getting BACK that was the problem. The water was deep, the pond was cold, and the island was crowded with monkeys who had all leapt at once and now sat around damply agreeing with each other about how they'd gotten there.

"That's the trouble with jumping to Conclusions," said Tock, wringing out his clock. "It's much easier than swimming back."

They swam back. The Humbug took on water in the plume and had to be turned upside down.

So at last — drier, hungrier, and in the Humbug's case very soggy — Milo, Tock, and the Dodecacolobus (who had come along to see the world, one face at a time) set out across the Mounds toward a castle that hung in the sky above the tallest kapok.

The Mounds of Ignorance were infested. A demon called the Everpresent Bananasnatcher intercepted every idea Milo had before he could say it. The Terrible Trivium, a handsome bland-faced baboon, trapped them for an afternoon of moving a termite mound "termite by termite, no hurry," until Tock's clock-howl shocked them loose. The Senses Taker made them fill out forms in triplicate until Milo, reading the fine print, wrote the answers as WORDS and NUMBERS COMBINED, which the form could not contain and exploded.

At last they stood under the castle's invisible stair, which appeared one step at a time only if you climbed without knowing it was impossible.

"We KNOW it's impossible," wailed the Humbug.

"Then UN-know it!" cried Milo, and leapt anyway: "So many things are possible just as long as you don't know they're un-PEEL-ievable!"

They floated up, because the stair believed them.

The princesses Rhyme and Rhesus were gracious, weary, and ready. "It's awfully late," said Rhyme. "You could have come years ago." "But you came NOW," said Rhesus, "and now is when things happen."

They floated down — and the demons of the Mounds came swarming after them like rabid shadows. But behind Milo rolled the armies of Wisdom, and this time they rolled together: the letters of Dictionopolis spelling a wall, and the numbers of Digitopolis counting the demons precisely out of existence, and between the two armies a banner, woven overnight by Azaz's weavers and the Mathemagician's masons, reading in letters AND numerals: EQUALLY PRECIOUS.

There was a great crunching of nothing, and the demons were gone, and there was a feast — dinner first, then subtraction stew for dessert, so that everyone left hungrier and happier — with synonana buns for everyone and a formal reconciliation between two kings in identical bad moods, neither of whom would admit to having been wrong, and both of whom were extremely relieved.

Milo drove home before the dishes were cleared, bursting to tell the troop. He looked back once — and the Howlbooth was gone. Phantom, you know.

"Where did it go?" wailed Milo, who suddenly had SO much to do.

"You don't need it now," said Tock's howl, fading into the evening like a bell. "You know the way. Expect everything."

And Milo found that he did.
