# The Book of Ape-cidents

*A parody of "The Book of Accidents"*

Nate came back to Gully Hollow the way you take a splinter out of your thumb: slowly, and only because leaving it in was worse.

His father was dead. That was the whole headline. Big Nate — Grandfather to Oliver, "Sir" to everyone else, and privately, always, The Old Mandrill — had dropped off his branch one Tuesday, and the county had finally, at long last, run out of Nate.

So Nate, his wife Maddie, and their boy Oliver moved into the old house on the hill above the hollow, because houses are expensive and guilt is free.

Maddie took a job at the county clinic, where she'd grown up and where, she said, the broken arms came in waves, like invoices. "Bad-luck months," the older nurses called them. "Whole valley has a bad-luck month, then a good one, then you could set your calendar by the next." Nate made her stop talking about it at dinner.

Oliver was eleven and drew constantly: birds, branches, gorillas in armor, gorillas in armor holding birds. He drew the house before they arrived. He drew the rooms correctly. He drew the locked study, with the desk in it, and the shapes of the books on it — and when Nate found that drawing, he put it in a drawer very fast, the way you put down something hot.

Nate had grown up in that house. He did not talk about it. He especially did not talk about his brother Chuckie, who went into the woods in 1989 and came out of them in a box. The last afternoon of Chuckie's life, The Old Mandrill had walked both boys up through the fog to the white stones and told them to wait while he "checked the accounts." Nate had climbed a tree to sulk, the way he survived everything — small, quiet, and very good at trees. He remembered his brother's voice going up the hill, and his father's voice coming back down it, and only one shape coming out of the fog. Nate had been a payment that got away. He'd spent thirty years not doing the math on what Chuckie had been.

Deputy Bev Gizzard, a langur with gray eyebrows and a memory like a ledger herself, stopped by the first week with a casserole and a warning.

"Knew you when you were knee-high," she said. "Knew Chuckie too." She looked at the tree line a long time. "Your daddy walked those woods every full moon with a book under his arm, and this county had just enough bad luck to keep it interesting. You want my professional opinion? Keep the boy out of the fog. And if you find any of your daddy's books — those nice handwritten ones — you call me before you read them."

She left. The study lock gave up on the third night.

Inside was Big Nate's life's work: ledgers. Dozens of them, in beautiful, venomous handwriting.

*GULLY HOLLOW BOOK OF APE-CIDENTS, VOL. XII.*

Every accident in the valley, recorded. Dates, injuries, causes. Figs fell. Branches broke. Tails were lost to fans. A generation of bad luck, filed like taxes.

Then Nate turned the pages and found accidents that hadn't happened yet.

The entry for last spring read: *Boy falls from rope bridge at swimming hole. Breaks arm.* Below it, in the same ink, a checkmark.

The entry for Tuesday read: *Mack Brown, tire shop. Ladder. Clavicle.*

Tuesday was tomorrow.

And the entry for the last week of the month read only: *Oliver.*

Just that. A blank space after it, waiting to be filled in.

Nate slammed the ledger shut, and that was the night Oliver said, over dinner, casual as anything: "I met a boy in the woods today. He looked like you, Papa. In your pictures. He says his grandfather is nice to him."

"Oliver," said Maddie, "we've talked about imaginary friends."

"He's not imaginary," said Oliver. "He showed me the stones."

The stones were real. Nate knew they were real, because he had grown up under them: white rings of river stone up in the fog above the hollow, circles his father had walked every full moon with a ledger under his arm, feeding the valley's accidents to something in the circles the way you feed scraps to a crocodile so it doesn't come up onto the bank.

That week Oliver came home from school with a split lip and a three-day suspension. The other boy — a bully with a famous father, the kind of small mandrill who runs a protection racket on a playground — had gone down on the gravel with a black eye nobody had touched. Oliver cried the whole way home, and not because of his lip. "I looked at him and thought how easy it would be," he said, "and then the thought went out and did it by itself."

That night, in the ledger, in handwriting that was not Big Nate's and not anyone's, a new entry appeared: *Rex Mull, playground. Gravel. Eye. Felt good.*

Nate burned that volume alone in the yard and did not sleep.

"Chuckie had this too," Maddie said quietly in the dark. She was an ER nurse; she had known for days. "Didn't he. Your father didn't record accidents, Nate. He recorded appetite."

That weekend, Nate followed his son's footprints up through the fog with a flashlight and no plan, and found himself face to face with himself: another Nate, gray at the muzzle, holding another Oliver's hand — and beside them, in the biggest circle, Big Nate. Their Big Nate. The Old Mandrill, dead three months and standing in the stones like a landlord, ledger under his arm.

"Hello, Son," it said. "There's an outstanding balance."

The other Nate spoke then, urgently, in a voice like his own with the fear aged out of it: "In his world, I died in the box, and Chuckie grew up, and in that world our father was kind. Kind, and it still wasn't enough — he fed it gentle things, little things, one accident a month, till it wanted a grandson. Whatever circle you're in, don't trade with it. Whatever it offers—"

It offered everything. The fog was full of circles, that was the thing Nate saw now, ring inside ring inside ring, and in every one stood a Nate with a book, deciding. It offered Chuckie, alive, thirty-five and laughing. It offered the rope bridge untied. It offered the blank line after OLIVER filled in, in beautiful ink, with the word NEVER.

"See?" said The Old Mandrill, and its voice was patient as gravel. "Everyone signs eventually. I only kept the schedule."

Nate looked at the ledger in the dead grip of his dead father, and he did the only thing you can do with a book of accounts like that.

He laughed at it.

"Denied," he said, and swung the flashlight, and knocked the book into the white stones.

The fire took the ledgers in seconds — every volume, a generation of accidents burning like dry season. The Old Mandrill came apart into flies. The other Nate tipped his head, grateful, and was gone, and Oliver — his Oliver, solid and real — was there, gripping his hand.

Deputy Gizzard came out the next morning, looked at the burned circle in the fog, and wrote something in her notebook that Nate never asked to see. "County's going to have an ordinary century for once," she said. "Somebody'll complain."

The house still creaks at night. The hollow still has bad luck: tires blow, ladders wobble, figs drop.

But now it's just luck. Nate checked.

He keeps a ledger of his own now, open on the kitchen table, where Oliver draws gorillas on the facing pages — and lately, Nate noticed, the gorillas are smiling. It's a short book. Every line says the same thing, in beautiful, unvenomous handwriting:

*HAPPENED TO NOBODY.*
