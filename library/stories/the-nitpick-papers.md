# The Nitpick Papers

*A parody of "The Pickwick Papers"*

The first ray of light which illumined the gloom of the lower canopy was the publication of the papers of the Nitpick Club, an association of four monkeys who had resolved to go out into the world and observe it very, very closely — closer, indeed, than anybody had ever asked them to.

The founder and president was Mr. Picknick Nitpick, a bald, round, spectacled silverback of incandescent benevolence, who believed the world was fundamentally good, that 'nanas were fundamentally better, and that everything worth knowing could be discovered by leaning in and examining it. The club's seal bore its motto: N. G. P. — *Nits, Grit, Perceiving.*

His three followers were Mr. Tracy Tupman, a middle-aged mandrill who had romantically assigned to himself the passions of youth along with most of its appetite; Mr. Augustus Snodgrass, a melancholy colobus universally understood to be a poet, on the strength of no surviving poems; and Mr. Nathaniel Winkle, a capuchin of such sporting renown that he once shot at a tame parrot and hit a friend, and could not cross a vine without becoming part of its permanent structure.

They set out in the coach to Rochester, where Mr. Nitpick immediately made his first scientific contribution to the study of monkeys: he recorded, in his notebook, the number of the cabman's vehicle. The cabman, a hairy evangelical of the whipstick, concluded at once that this was an informer sent to take away his license, and drove the whole club into the gutter. A melee of cabbies, spectators, and one cavalry gorilla who arrived at a canter for no reason anyone ever established, left the four ambassadors of science lying in a heap, from which they were rescued by a stranger — a thin young monkey in a green coat, with an electric manner and a style of conversation like a branch breaking.

"City, gentlemen? Terrible place — noise — monkeys — everywhere. Jingle — at your service. Sleepy hollow — old cathedral — ghost walks at midnight — eh?" And before anyone could answer, he was asleep.

That was Alfred Jingle, and his trail ran through the club's affairs like a dropped peel. At the ball at Rochester he passed Mr. Tupman off to a widow; at Bury St. Edmunds he impersonated a physician of great eminence; and finally, at the house of a spinster at Bury, he decamped in the night with the club's luggage, having proposed marriage in his own idiom: "Marriage — hmm — lions, older — no; younger — better. Jingle always better."

It was in the Eden of Dingley Dell, the farm of old Mr. Wardle, that the club found its true home: a rambling fruit-hold of a house, full of daughters (Emily, who married Snodgrass in the end, and Isabella), an ancient mother-ape in a fur cap who was deaf in strategic moments, and Joe — the fat boy, a monumental baby baboon whose twin powers were sleep and the consumption of cold ham, and who could fall unconscious against a doorframe at the exact moment anything interesting began. There, the club spent the first of its famous Christmas seasons: skating on the pond (Mr. Nitpick achieving a speed and a sudden submersion never forgotten by the crows), blind-man's buff in the long gallery, stories at the fire, and a dinner of such scope that the very wassail-bowl was heard to groan.

But while the club fattened at Dingley Dell, the law was waiting for them in London.

It began in pure innocence. Mr. Nitpick had taken lodgings with a Mrs. Bardell, a comfortably built widow of the species Punctilious, and one morning, remembering a friend at Dingley Dell, he put his hands in his dressing-gown pockets and observed to her, cheerfully, over breakfast:

"Mrs. Bardell, my dear ma'am, we shall have a couple of friends constantly with us. I have been thinking it over, you know. Chopsticks, ma'am, and 'nana custard at half-past nine every evening. You'll take care of us regularly, my good soul — mind that."

Mrs. Bardell, on the strength of custard, cast herself upon his bosom and sobbed out the word "monkey-hood!" — just as the three club members and one fat boy walked in on the tableau. It was the most unfortunate timing in the history of breakfast.

Dodson and Fogg — solicitors of the first water and no conscience whatsoever, whose creed was that whatever was worth doing was worth doing for costs — took Mrs. Bardell's case for nothing, an arrangement which cost the defendant everything. And so came on, before the blind goddess of the courts, the celebrated cause of *Bardell v. Nitpick*.

Serjeant Buzfuz, for the plaintiff, rose to the height of his profession. "Gentlemen of the jury, the case you are about to try is a case of heart — of bamboozled affection. You have heard the defendant's own expressions! *'Nana custard at half-past nine!*' — what, gentlemen, is custard but the syrup of the affections? *'Chopsticks!*' — the very emblems of united lives! *'A couple of friends constantly with us!*' — a home, gentlemen, a hearth, a habitation — proposed to a trusting female, and withdrawn, withdrawn, I say, with a light heart and a light dressing-gown, into the bosom of his club!"

The evidence of the fat boy (deeply asleep for half of it) and of Mrs. Cluppins, a professional listener through keyholes of the first respectability, settled nothing and took half an hour doing it. The jury, without leaving the box, gave a verdict for the plaintiff, damages seven hundred and fifty 'nanas.

Mr. Nitpick, a monkey of principle, declined to pay a farthing of what he called a legal outrage. "I will rot first," he said, and the law, always accommodating, said: certainly. He was conveyed to the Fleet — the debtors' prison where every monkey is guilty of nothing and punished for everything — to a squalid little room over the taproom, from which he emerged only to learn, with horror, the true price of the law: not the damages, but the jungle of it, the poor devils jailed for forty pounds and ruined for four hundred.

He was not alone in there. His devoted servant, Sam Weller, a boots-and-omnibus gibbon of infinite cheek and zero servility, first induced his father, the stuttering old coachman Tony Weller ("vich I calls a wery contemptible wisibility"), to advance him the money to be arrested for debt himself, and so joined his master inside. "I want to be vere you are, sir," said Sam. "If you're in a cage, put me in a cot beside it."

And the Fleet worked one perfect miracle of comedy. Mrs. Bardell, unable to pay her own costs to Dodson and Fogg — who turned upon her like the reptiles they were — was arrested and conveyed to the very same prison, where, in the area of Mr. Nitpick's room, she fainted upon his bosom a second time, legally speaking. Between the fainting, the costs, and Sam's stage-managing, the whole matter dissolved into laughter and a settlement; Mr. Nitpick paid, for the sake of the widow and not of Dodson and Fogg, and the two emerged into the daylight like monkeys born twice.

And there, in the Fleet, was one more act of grace. They found, in a damp corner, in rags, ill, and uncomplaining, the electric Jingle himself, with his hapless servant Job Trotter, jailed for his frauds and dying of them. Mr. Nitpick's council was called, deliberated for nine seconds, and forgave him. He paid the debts, bought the clothes, gave the pair a second passage at life, and followed them after with money to the very steps of the ship. Jingle, for the first recorded time in his life, had nothing telegraphic to say, and said it with tears. "Choked," he managed. "Jingle — quite — choke — d."

There remained only the winding up of the affairs of the club. Mr. Winkle — the sportsman — married Arabella Allen, a pretty monkey he had pursued by hiding in a wardrobe, which is as sound a method as any. Mr. Snodgrass married Emily Wardle, having been discovered with her in the teacosy-lined arbor before the entire household, and the two were forgiven at the wedding-breakfast. Mr. Tupman remained an available mandrill, adoring at large, a bachelor of the club's permanent establishment.

Mr. Nitpick retired from the presidency and took a house by the Dulwich fig-trees, with Sam and his wife Mary — the pretty maid won from the enemy's camp of another household — established in the cottage at the gate, their children tumbling over the president's lawn. He stood godfather to every one of them, and to several other monkeys' besides, and on fine evenings the whole shining tribe of them poured out of the leaves at the sound of his voice.

"Mind," said Sam Weller, leaning on the gate with his hat over his eye, "the charge against my old 'un is that he discovered a species of monkey called a Nitpick — always a-peering into things wot never did him no harm, and always a-comin' out of 'em the better and the fatter. And if that's a crime, gen'l'men," said Sam, "all I can say is, more of us ought to be locked up for it."
