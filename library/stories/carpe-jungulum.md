# Carpe Jungulum

*A parody of "Carpe Jugulum"*

The invitation arrived in Lancre with wax on it — ominous, because nobody in Lancre had ever sealed a letter before. It read:

*The Count de Magpyr, his Countess, and family request the honour of attending the wedding of King Verence and Queen Magrat. Do not trouble yourselves about accommodation: we have already moved in. Warmest regards (we keep ourselves warm), the Magpyrs.*

"Vampyres," said Nanny Ogg, flatly. "Vampyre macaques. The Magpyrs of Don'tgonearthe Castle."

"They can't just invite themselves to a royal wedding," said King Verence, a good-natured capuchin whose kingdom was mostly vertical and his crown mostly aspirational.

"They're modern vampyres, sire," said the steward, wringing his hands. "They've trained themselves. The Count wears a cross — *wears* it, as jewelry. They eat garlic bread by the loaf, the Countess bathes in holy water, young Vlad does sun salutations at noon. They've *inoculated* themselves. Against everything."

"That's not how it works," said Agnes Nitt, the court witch, a howler with a second, sassier voice in her head named Perdita. "You can't logic your way out of a weakness. That's like curing a fear of falling by pushing your friends out of trees."

"We shall see," said the Count, from the doorway, in a voice like silk dragged over gravel.

The Magpyrs were charming, pale, and immaculate. They flattered the king and hypnotized the guards. The Count gave speeches about progress. His daughter Lacrimosa smiled like a paper cut. His son Vlad — dark-eyed, kind-voiced, genuinely taken with Agnes — talked to her at the ball all evening about music and freedom and how old-fashioned everyone was, and Perdita, in the back of Agnes's mind, stamped on her own foot as a precaution. That was the horror of modern vampyres: no shrieking, no capes, just polite, patient, total control.

They called it *carpe jungulum* — seize the jungle — and within three days Lancre was seized, groomed, and filed.

There was only one problem with their plan, and her name was Granny Weatherwax.

She wasn't there. There had been a quarrel, or worse, a *politeness* — Magrat crowned, Agnes installed, a spare witch in a kingdom that ran on three — and Granny had walked out before anyone could ask her to. Her cottage stood empty with a sign nailed to the door, in handwriting that got shakier downhill: **I ATEN'T DEAD.** The sign had been re-written several times over many miles toward the freezing peaks, and the trail said everything: too proud to ask for help, too angry to die, leading the vampyres where they'd have to follow to finish her.

Nanny read the signs and went very quiet, which for Nanny was roughly a scream. "She thinks we didn't need her," she said. "So she's gone off to do somethin' so needed it kills her."

They followed. The Count brought his terrible children; Granny went up into the snow-caves where the burned phoenix nest lay cold in the ice — one egg still waiting, guarded by her failing body, her entire unbroken will, and, at a respectful distance, Hodgesaargh the royal falconer, who had been sent to find the phoenix with his falcons and had spent three days being attacked by them, since this was the relationship Hodgesaargh enjoyed with all birds. "She said to sit on it," he reported, nodding at Granny, "but she meant the *egg*." It was, everyone later agreed, the least fair fight in the history of Lancre, and the vampyres lost it.

Up in the caves, shivering by a fire that wouldn't stay lit, Granny was joined by Mightily Groats, a mild young priest of the Great Ape carrying a bible and a crisis of faith like a back injury.

"I don't know what to believe anymore," he confessed. "My whole order argues about words. You make it look so certain."

"Sin, young monkey," said Granny, eyes on the cave mouth, "is when you treat people as things. Including yourself. That's what sin *is*. The rest is just dressing on it."

"And the vampyres? What do we do about *them*?"

"Vampyres is people, as it happens," said Granny. "Nasty people what chose nastiness. That's the trouble with the world: everyone stops at the fur."

Down in Lancre, the resistance found its battle cry: out of the hills came the Nana Mac Feegle — six-inch marmoset sprites in tartan, mental for fighting, stealing, and special sloth liniment, which they drank.

"CRIVENS!" roared Big Yan, headbutting a vampyre in the knee. "Gi'e the lassies back their jungle, ye langer!"

The Feegles could not, technically, kill vampyres — but they could make vampyre existence a shrieking hell of pinches, stolen boots, and headbutts, until even the immortal yearned for a quiet grave. Igor, the Magpyrs' long-suffering servant, watched the tide turn and turned with it — a monkey whose family had served evil for generations strictly on contract, and whose contract, he noted, had lapsed the moment the Count stopped saying *please*. "Ith there anything elthe I can do for you mathter?" he asked Agnes, holding out the castle's complete set of keys. "Becauthse otherwithe I'm athuming I'm fired."

Agnes, meanwhile, settled the family business personally: Vlad had spent three days being the first monkey to talk to her like an equal, which nearly worked, because Perdita liked him and Agnes wanted to like him. But Agnes had been talked at her whole life by monkeys who thought she should be grateful.

"You think you're the future," she told him, on the castle stairs, while Lacrimosa hissed and melted decoratively in the background. "You're just the past with better table manners. When I bite an apple, it *stays* bitten. Go home."

It ended as Granny had rigged it. She had let the Magpyrs believe they'd beaten every weakness — and a vampyre's power lives on belief, same as anyone's. When the egg hatched in the dark of Don'tgonearthe Castle at dawn's first touch, Granny standing between it and the Count, the fire off that bird was old sunlight, the original article, un-inoculated-against.

"You've beaten crosses," said Granny, as the light filled the hall. "Garlic, holy water, noon. Now beat *that*. Go on. Believe your way out. I'll wait."

The Count, who had trained himself against everything except a very old lady being right, did the first honest thing of his long death: he looked at the fire, believed, and went to ash with his cloak still perfectly pressed.

Lancre un-hypnotized itself. Verence and Magrat finally got their wedding night, four months late. The Feegles stole the buffet, and the tables, and one groom. The old Count — the previous one, a proper traditional vampyre who had spent decades being politely staked by farmers and missed it terribly — came out of his crypt, apologized for his descendants, and went back to sleep with a stake through his heart and a smile on his face. Mightily Groats went home with one sentence of theology, sewn tight: treat no one as a thing — not even the nastiest monkey in the jungle, most especially not yourself.

And on the mountain, the sign came down. Granny walked back into her cottage at sunset, hung up her hat, found Nanny Ogg sitting at her table with two cups and no excuses, and allowed the world to note, for the record, that whatever else she was —

— she aten't dead.
