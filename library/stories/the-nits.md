# The Nits

*A parody of "The Twits"*

What a lot of matted-furred monkeys there are around nowadays!

Mr. Nit was one of them. His fur looked as though it had been combed with a root and never washed in a hundred years, which was nearly true. It stuck out in tufts like a dirty doormat, and it was full of crumbs — biscuit crumbs, banana crumbs, and things better not examined. A monkey who never grooms gets nits, and Mr. Nit had nits in his nits.

The truth is this: a monkey who thinks ugly thoughts grows an ugly pelt. You can't comb ugliness out; it comes up through the roots. Whereas a monkey with good thoughts — well, everyone has met one. Their fur shines like sunbeams. You have met one. You remember them.

"Tricks is monkey business," said Mr. Nit, "and I is top banana at monkey business."

Mrs. Nit was no better. She had a glass eye, and she used to point it at her husband when he wasn't looking, so that he never quite knew which eye to meet, and she carried a sunflower stalk for a cane with which to whack him.

The tricks began on Tuesday.

Mr. Nit caught a tree frog and slipped it into Mrs. Nit's bed, so that at midnight it hopped up beside her nose and croaked. "EEEEEEK!" went Mrs. Nit.

"Ha!" said Mr. Nit. "It's only a frog, my dear. Nice and damp."

So on Wednesday, Mrs. Nit made his dinner: vine noodles with a fat live grub folded through the middle, which he chewed and swallowed before noticing the wriggle.

"Ha!" said Mrs. Nit. "More protein that way."

On Thursday, Mr. Nit tried the cleverest trick of his career. While Mrs. Nit slept, he trimmed a hand's-breadth off the bottom of her sunflower cane and glued the piece onto the top. The next morning, when she leaned on it, the floor was exactly that much nearer.

"I'm shrinking!" she gasped, measuring herself against the door branch. "It's the Shrinks! I've got the Shrinks!"

"Nonsense, my dear," said Mr. Nit, and that night he trimmed the cane again, and again, and by Sunday Mrs. Nit was walking with her chin on her knuckles, moaning, "I'll be a mite by Monday! A MITES' WIFE!" — until she happened to catch her reflection in the birdbath, counted herself exactly the same height as ever, and worked out the whole business by teatime.

So on Friday it was her turn. She tied a bundle of parade balloons to the end of Mr. Nit's favorite sitting-vine while he napped, so that when he shifted his weight, the vine — and Mr. Nit with it — floated gently off the floor toward the roof.

"Help!" shrieked Mr. Nit, gripping a passing rafter. "I'm floating! I'm shrinking! Gravity's got a grudge!"

"You're already half a nit," said Mrs. Nit, holding her sides.

Mr. Nit fetched himself such a fright that he burst three balloons on the thorn-hook, and they didn't speak till Saturday.

The Nits' nastiest business was the Great Sticky Tree — a big dead kapok on their land, which every Tuesday they painted from top to bottom with GORILLA-GRIP GOO, the stickiest sap in the jungle. Birds landed on the branches at dusk, stuck fast, and became Sunday bird pie.

"Four of you again this week!" Mr. Nit would cackle, plucking them off like figs. "Feathered and fresh and free-range. Ha!"

But the Roly-Poly Bird — a magnificent bird with a curl of feathers like a handlebar — flew through the jungle warning every flock: *Never land on the Sticky Tree, never land on the Sticky Tree.* And the birds began, instead, to roost on the Nits' own bath branch, which did wonders for nobody's bath, so the Nits had to bathe under drip.

And then there were the sloths.

Muddle-Wum, his wife, and their three children lived in a thorn-branch cage and were made to rehearse every night for the Right-Side-Up Sloth Circus — forced, in front of jeering crowds, to stand on their hind legs on wobbly poles, which for a sloth, who is built to hang like a hammock, is pure agony.

"Stand UP, you lazy layabouts!" bellowed Mr. Nit, poking them with the sunflower cane. "Standing is what circuses is!"

"I is standing," Muddle-Wum would quaver, trembling like a hammock in a gale. "I is standing very slowly."

Now, the Roly-Poly Bird spoke both languages — bird, and slow. One night the bird perched on the cage and translated the plan, syllable by patient syllable, and the sloths, who needed three days to get anywhere, set out at midnight at once, expecting to arrive by Tuesday. Good, said the bird. Tuesday is glue day.

The plan was glue. While the Nits slept, Muddle-Wum and family — upside down, where God and sloths intended them — painted GORILLA-GRIP GOO all over the ceiling branches of the Nits' nest, working from the edges inward so as not to stick themselves, which took most of the night and one very long argument about whose fault the drips were. And they stuck the furniture to it, and the tea things, and Mr. Nit's boots: everything, upside down, overhead, exactly the way the Nits kept the sloths' world.

The Nits woke and looked up, and their chairs hung above them like beetles. The teapot hovered, spout down. The bed stood on the ceiling like a fly.

"WE'RE UPSIDE DOWN!" screamed Mrs. Nit.

"We're NOT," snapped Mr. Nit. "The nest's upside down. WE'RE — hmm."

"Quick!" cried Mr. Nit, arriving at the terrible logic. "If the world's turned topsy-turvy, all we has to do is stand on our heads, and then WE'LL be the right way up!"

Mrs. Nit thought this was the first intelligent thing he had said in years. And because they were the Nits, they did it. Two hands flat on the floor, two ugly bottoms in the air, they stood on their heads — and the goo that the sloths had thoughtfully painted on the floor as well held them fast, and they were stuck, and they could not get down.

They shouted. They hollered. They threatened the ceiling furniture with the cane, which was stuck to the roof. And all the birds of the jungle gathered at the window to watch, and the Roly-Poly Bird narrated.

Now, every monkey knows what happens to a monkey who stands on its head too long.

It gets the Shrinks.

(And this time there was no trimmed cane to blame it on.)

And the Nits shrank, and shrank, and shrank — into their nightshirts, and into their fur, and then into nothing at all — until all that was left in the nest was a whiff of bananas, two empty nightcaps, and one confused flea looking for a new home with better references.

The sloths hung happily upside down in their own branch at last, right where they belonged, and never stood up again except once, at their wedding anniversaries, briefly. The Roly-Poly Bird stayed for mangoes. The birds never worried about bird pie, and the bath branch got its bath back.

And no monkey in the jungle, ever after, called anybody a Nit — though every monkey made sure to think good thoughts, because everybody had seen what happens when you don't. Sunbeams, or matting. It comes up through the roots.
