# The Bonobody

*A parody of "The Body"*

I never had any friends later on like the ones I had when I was twelve. Okay, part of that is because I eventually became a moderately famous writer of banana-crate labels — Brunch Bunch, Daily Fig, the whole Peel Family line. But mostly it's because most grown monkeys are basic.

That Labor Day weekend, Verne Tarsier came puffing up the rope lane with news. He'd got it by lying under his own porch for two hours, eavesdropping on his big brother, which was Verne's whole job in life and he was good at it. His brother had heard it from a ranger: Little Rocco Bonobo, who wandered off after berry season, had been found by the Figworth trestle, two jungles over, near the River Muddy.

"Dead?" asked Chisp Chambers.

"Bonobody," said Verne, solemn as a Sunday fig.

Chisp, Teddy Duchunk, Verne, and me — Gordy Lachance — traded a look, the same look you'd give free samples or an unattended orchard. Not one of us said it out loud. We all wanted to see a bonobody. We wanted to be the first troop to find one. We wanted, I suppose, to look at death and find out it couldn't climb.

I'll tell you the other thing, since we're being honest. My big brother Dennis had gone to the Great Orchard in the spring, and since then my parents looked through me the way you look through a window — like the view was fine, it just wasn't anything. Rocco was somebody's view. I wanted to see what the end of a story looked like, because mine had stopped making sense.

We told our parents we were camping at Verne's. My mother said "that's nice, dear" to a spot slightly left of my head. Verne's mother would have believed us if we'd said we were emigrating.

The old Figworth Branch Line ran through the canopy on rusted rails where the fruit trains used to run, and we walked the ties telling stories the whole way. Chisp wanted to know who'd win a fight, Kong or forty-nine bonobos, and the debate lasted two miles and settled nothing, as great debates do. At the swimming hole we stopped and built the dam, because you cannot pass a swimming hole and not dam it. We got it up to three feet of good brown backed-up water, looked at each other, and then Chisp kicked it apart, and we watched the river take back its own, which was somehow the most satisfying part.

Then we found the ticks. Verne went in first and came out wearing three fat ones like jewelry, and then I reached into my own waistband and found one lodged somewhere I decline to describe in a family publication. "Oh, gross," said Chisp, with real respect.

That night I told them the legend of Lard Trousers Hogan and the Great Fruit Mash — how he entered the harvest-fair pie contest on purpose, how he brought the whole thing back up in a rainbow of banana, and how the crowd, as one, cascaded after him. A complete and total spew-o-rama. Teddy nearly fell out of the tree laughing.

And when the fire was low, Chisp got quiet, which for Chisp was like a bell not ringing. He told us about the milk money. How he'd taken it — two whole figs — felt bad, and given it back to Mrs. Swain, and how she'd kept it anyway and told the principal that Chisp Chambers never paid at all. How nobody, not teachers, not grown monkeys, nobody, had ever believed a Chambers could be good, so why should he wear himself out trying?

"They tell you what you are," said Chisp, "and then they dare you to be it."

"Not me," I said. "I know how your story ends. It ends with them all being wrong about you. Anyone can be wrong, Chisp. Not everyone can be you."

He didn't say anything. But he gave me the kind of look you give a fig somebody handed you for free, like you intend to keep it forever.

The next morning we crossed the trestle over the River Muddy, because Teddy's old man was a genuine hair-of-the-dog baboon, and Teddy liked to prove his courage the way his father liked to prove his thirst. We crossed slow, in single file, above water yawning brown and far below. Halfway across, the 3:10 mail train whistled behind us. We ran. I have never run like that since. Verne went flat between the ties, and Chisp hauled him up by the tail, and we hit the far bank as it roared past and lay in the ferns laughing — four little monkeys who'd outrun something with a schedule.

And then we found him.

Rocco Bonobo lay under the trestle in a nest of ferns, still and quiet, as if he'd decided the ground was a decent enough place to rest. He was our age. Somebody had crossed his arms over his chest, and I stood there a long time thinking about all the trees he'd never climb, and how his troop would wail tonight in Bramblehook without knowing yet to. I started to cry, quietly, and I didn't care about that either.

Chisp took off his cap. Nobody had ever seen Chisp without his cap. It was the bravest thing I ever watched him do — braver than what came after.

Because what came after was Ace Mandrill and his gang of howlers, dropping out of the canopy hollering, "Finders keepers! That's our bonobody! We're going to be famous!"

Famous for finding a dead kid. That was the dream of Ace Mandrill.

Chisp stood up. He had a reputation going back to third grade, and he spent every last fig of it right there.

"Sic boy, Ace. One branch closer and it'll be your bonobody they find."

Ace laughed. Chisp didn't. And Teddy stepped up, and Verne stepped up — dumpy, scared Verne, holding a stick like it was a scepter — and I stood up too, still crying and not caring, and there is no gang on this green earth that can beat four twelve-year-olds who have stopped pretending.

Ace went pale under his muzzle and led his howlers off, muttering.

We called the rangers from Ongo station and didn't give our names. Then, walking back up the line in the first sun, a doe stepped out of the ferns, looked at me with her black glass eyes, and went on grazing, unbothered by anything at all, as if to say the world had plenty of morning left in it. I never told the others about her. She was mine.

I heard later that Rocco went home in a proper procession, the whole troop wailing. Verne's family moved two springs on. Teddy went into mobile grooming and never came out. Chisp got in trouble, and then out of it, and then into worse, and that is another story — but years on, he became the first Chambers anybody in that county ever trusted with the till, and I like to think the milk money died that morning by the tracks.

And me? I write labels. Brunch Bunch, Daily Fig. But every so often I get one with room on the back, and I write a little story there, about a monkey who was brave once, and I like to think somebody, somewhere, reads it off a banana and decides to be better.

I never had any friends later on like the ones I had when I was twelve.

Great figs, does anyone?
