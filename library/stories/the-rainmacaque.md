# The Rainmacaque

*A parody of "The Rainmaker" by John Grisham*

Rudy Macaque, twenty-two, was in his last year at Tennessee Jungle Law when a professor assigned him a nursing home, a client, and a question that ruined him for anything sensible.

The client was Miss Birdie, a wealthy old macaque who adored lawyers and could not be discouraged. She had opinions on everything and legal work for no one, and she paid Rudy in peppermints and gossip, and he came to love her anyway, which is generally how it goes with clients who cannot pay.

The question came later, in the willow swamp across the river, from Dot Black-Hair.

Her son Donny Ray was a young spider monkey with banana-leukemia, and he was dying on a folded blanket in the shade while his mother told it flat out, the way the poor tell things, because grief is the one luxury they cannot afford.

The doctors had wanted a fig-marrow transplant. It was the only shot. Great Banana-fit Mutual — an insurance troop with a jacaranda office park and a jingle every monkey in three states could hum — had declined the claim, twice, in little letters.

"We paid our premiums for nine years," said Dot, holding the letters out like a paw full of wasps. "He is dying because they wanted to save sixteen thousand bananas."

"I'll help you sue them," said Rudy, before he had the least idea what that would take.

Miss Birdie asked him why.

"Why what?"

"Why the poor boy? You're a student. You'll get nothing."

"Do I need a reason to help somebody?"

"No," said Miss Birdie, looking at him with interest. "But keep the question. It's worth more than the degree."

Then began the education Rudy had actually paid for.

Bruiser Stone, a scarred old baboon with a law office over a juke joint and a client base that filed in through the alley, hired Rudy out of pity and amusement. Bruiser's entire staff was a macaque named Deck, who called himself a paralegal.

"I flunked the branch-Bar six times," said Deck, dealing cards with the calm of a monkey long past embarrassment. "Fell off twice. The trick is landing."

They landed the Black-Hair case, barely, in front of Judge Keeper — an old orangutan with patience like a banyan and a gleam that frightened insurance lawyers from three counties away. Opposing them came the firm of Tinley-Britt: forty gibbons in pinstripe fur and one silver-voiced advocate, Leo Drumming, who could make a denial letter sound like a lullaby.

It did not sound like a lullaby in Judge Keeper's courtroom. Rudy's opening statement very nearly did not happen; his knees knocked so hard the bailiff glanced under the rail to see who was hammering. He stood up anyway, walked to the jury rail with no notes and a dry mouth, and told them about a boy on a folded blanket and two little letters, and the courtroom went quiet the way a swamp goes quiet when something true walks in.

The education had chapters. Drumming deposed Rudy for two days, twelve hours in the chair, gibbons rotating in shifts with questions like small polite chisels, and Rudy answered like a monkey who had nothing left to lose, which by the second afternoon was true, and it read well in the transcript. The Bar results came the same week — he passed, which he celebrated in the alley behind the juke joint with a warm beer and Deck, who toasted him with grape soda and observed that now he could lose cases officially. And at the hospital, on the evenings Donny Ray's chart went steep, there was a kinkajou named Kelly who brought ice chips and had a bruise she explained twice, differently. Rudy made her practice the answer she would give someday if anybody ever asked her what the stairs were like. When the day came that Cliff came down those stairs much faster than he ever went up them, nobody ever asked, and the prosecutor's office, looking at the file, saw only a drunk drill-ape and a fall, which was nearly all of it.

It should also be recorded that Miss Birdie, in the middle of the trial, made a full and frank disclosure of her finances, which were considerable and which she had, in a moment of television, conveyed entirely to the First Church of the Ripe Banana, broadcast nightly. "He's such a good listener," she said. Rudy sued nobody. She had paid him in exactly what he turned out to need.

Rudy read Great Banana-fit's little letters out loud, and then he read the manual. The manual was the thing. Deny, Delay, and Don't Return Calls, the claims boys called it, with a bonus schedule to match, and it came into evidence because a frightened ex-claims adjuster had smuggled it out in a diaper bag — a courier choice Drumming's briefs treated with more outrage than the bonus schedule.

"You've seen this before?" Rudy asked, gently, of the claims vice-president, a sleek capuchin who had preened on his way to the stand.

"Thousands of times."

"Did you pay the young spider monkey's claim?"

"We adjudicated the claim."

"Adjudicated. Is that the one where the patient dies before the appeal?"

Donny Ray Black-Hair gave his deposition from a stretcher, on videotape, too weak to be anything but honest, and he was dead before closing arguments, which the jury knew, because Rudy told them not to forget it, and Dot Black-Hair sat through all of it in her one good dress, and when it was over she said only, "He was a good boy," which was the truest thing said by anybody in the entire trial.

The jury was out four hours. Two hundred thousand bananas, actual damages. Fifty million, punitive. Bruiser Stone wept openly, which frightened his clients more than the verdict frightened Great Banana-fit, which — it should be recorded — did not frighten them at all. The troop folded its tents overnight, went insolvent by dawn, and skipped town owing the world, and the verdict blew away like a peel in the wind, as verdicts do when the defendant is a slogan with no fur.

The aftermath came in the order these things do. The revenue apes came for Bruiser at noon, which surprised nobody, least of all Bruiser, who kept a packed bag under the desk and a fistful of peppermints for the ride, and waved from the car. Drumming went back to Tinley-Britt and the defense of slogans generally. Dot and Buddy Black-Hair were seen through the spring sitting on the porch together, not speaking, which was its own deposition. They gave Dot and Buddy enough for the house and the stone. Not enough. There is never enough; that is the discovery every young lawyer makes exactly once, and then must decide what kind of monkey to be.

Rudy decided fast.

He left the practice of law — left Bruiser's alley and the drum of tin legs on Tinley-Britt's marble — and drove west with Kelly, a full tank, and Miss Birdie's question riding shotgun.

Deck had explained the title once. A rainmacaque is the mythical young lawyer clients flock to like rain to the crops — a monkey so good the work comes to him.

Somewhere out there, to the west, it was raining. Rudy drove on, unhurried, and kept an eye on the clouds.

*Do I need a reason to help somebody?*

Not yet, he decided. Not while the tank was full.
