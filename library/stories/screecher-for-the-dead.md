# Screecher for the Dead

*A parody of "Speaker for the Dead"*

The monkey who stepped off the star-swing at Lusitroopia looked twenty-five years old. He was, in fact, three thousand ripenings old, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with years.

"Relativity," Andrew Wiggin explained to the dockmaster, "keeps you young. History keeps you tired."

"You're moping," said a small voice in his ear-jewel. It was Jane, who lived there — a mind made of lightning who had read every book ever copied and nagged like a woodpecker.

"I'm walking," said Andrew.

"You're moping at walking speed."

The colony of Catholic capuchins received him coldly. They had expected a heretic — a Screecher, one of those who tell the truth about the dead instead of the comfortable stories — and they had requested one under duress, because Novinha, the old midwife, had called for him before her husband died. What they got was a young-faced chimp in a traveler's cloak carrying a stoppered glass jar, and in the jar, sleeping, a Termite Queen.

Andrew Wiggin did not mention that. He also did not mention that he was the Andrew Wiggin: the child who, a thousand generations ago, had commanded the fleet that burned the termite mounds; the author, under another name, of "The Mound Queen and the Big Silverback," the two little books that told the truth and made his name a curse in every troop — Xenocide.

He had come to speak a death. He stayed to screech for three.

The call was older than anyone knew. Novinha had summoned a Speaker when she was six years old, the week her parents died of the Great Molt they had spent their lives fighting. The call had crawled between the stars for thirty years while the caller grew old, buried a husband, and stopped expecting anyone to answer. When he finally arrived, she spat at his feet. "I asked for a Speaker. They sent me a ghost with good posture."

"You asked for the truth," said Andrew. "I'm the monkey it lives with."

The dead husband was Marcão, a huge macaque with a famous temper, dead of the Great Molt sickness in his sleep. The town had already buried him, in every way that mattered. Novinha's children — Ela, who argued like a whipsaw; Miro, who studied the Little Ones in the forest; Olhado, whose eyes were polished beetle-shells that whirred when he was startled; and the two little ones, Grego and Quara, who threw overripe figs at the stranger's window until he opened it and asked them for a better recipe — waited to see what a Screecher would do.

What he did was ask questions. For a week he was everywhere, unbearable, gentle. He asked Olhado about the day his eyes went bad, and Olhado made him play hide-and-seek in the dark, where Olhado was the only one who could see. He asked Ela why she had stopped speaking to her mother. He asked Miro about the Twiglets — the small furred natives of the understory, the colony's great mystery, the reason two of Lusitroopia's finest scientists had been found dead among the roots, planted like seeds, marked with rites nobody understood.

"You could soften it," Jane murmured, the night before the speech.

"It's not softening work."

Then, in the courtyard of the church, standing on a crate in front of the whole town, he began.

"I am here to speak the death of Marcão Ribeira," he said. "Not to praise him. Not to bury him again. To tell you his life as truly as I can, so that for once a monkey is known."

And he did. He told them that Marcão's rages were the Molt, a sickness of the blood that itched past bearing — and that Novinha had married him knowing it, on a bargain, so that her children would have a name. He told them the children were not Marcão's. That their father was Libo — the gentle scientist, the second man the Twiglets planted — and that every lie in that house had been a wall built out of love, and love built crooked still falls on somebody.

The town wept, because truth, arriving at last, always feels like weather. The Bishop had called him a devil from the pulpit that very morning; he attended the speech anyway, at the back, and was heard to blow his nose like a flood-horn.

"But I came here for the others," Andrew said. "So I speak now for Pipo, and for Libo, and for Rooter of the Twiglets — and I will say what no one has dared: they were not murdered."

From the dark at the courtyard's edge came a chatter of many small voices. The Twiglets had been listening all along.

A small one called Human stepped into the light. "We planted them," he said, and his voice was proud and broke. "They were great. We made them into trees. It is the third life. It is the gift."

And so the two peoples learned, in one night, the shape of their tragedy: the Twiglets honor their great dead by planting them, that they may rise as fathertrees, humming and gossiping in the wind forever. They had done to their friends the kindest thing they knew. The monkeys had seen only the killing.

Before the treaty could be signed, there was a price, and the Twiglets named it: Human himself had chosen the third life, and a monkey must help plant him. Andrew went out with them at dawn, knee-deep in dew and small solemn faces, and gave Human to the grove with his own paws. The tree that came up by morning hummed a chord the whole valley could feel, and Olhado, listening, said his beetle-eyes whirred for joy, which was the first time anyone had ever said that sentence.

Miro missed the planting. He had crossed the live barrier the day of the riot, going to warn the Twiglets that the town was coming with torches, and the fence had taken half his body for toll. The Twiglets carried him home like a brother. When the treaty was signed that night in the infirmary, Miro signed it first, with his bad paw, slowly, and nobody hurried him.

The treaty read: the people of the branches and the people of the roots, one grove. Before dawn, Andrew Wiggin had signed it with the mayor, the Bishop, and a fathertree named Rooter, through the wind.

Then he walked out into the fallen forest, unstopped his glass jar, and wept as the Termite Queen woke at last.

"Here," he told her. "Dead wood. Downed trees. No fleet. Nobody coming."

"Tell her the number," said Jane.

"She knows the number."

The Queen looked at the Xenocide with a thousand years of patience, and forgave him — which was worse than any punishment, and better. By the next spring her first eggs hatched in the deadfall, ten thousand small lights ticking in the wood, and the colony stopped calling it an infestation and started calling it a neighborhood.

Novinha married him at the foot of the church tree, still scowling, in white fig-blossom. Jane hummed the wedding march in eleven keys at once, softly, in his ear, and for once he didn't tell her to hush. "I waited thirty years to hit you," she told him. "Now I suppose I'll have to keep you."

Three thousand ripenings old. And at last, younger.
