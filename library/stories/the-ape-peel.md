# The Ape Peel

*A parody of "The Appeal"*

The verdict came in on a Thursday, and it hit the Kruman Banana & Chemical Corporation like a falling fig tree: forty-one million dollars, actual and punitive, for poisoning the drinking water of Boughmore.

The trial had taken nine weeks, and the evidence had been sickening in both senses. Kruman's Boughmore plant had pumped its orange sludge — the same sludge that turned the town's wells brown and its children sick — straight into the ground for years, exactly as the Paytons had proven. There were memos. There were memos about the memos. One executive had written, of the drinking water, "the little monkeys will get used to the taste," and the jury had read that memo eleven times.

Jeannette Bough, a quiet howler monkey whose husband and little boy had died of the same rare stomach cancer, wept into her fur. Behind her, Wes and Mary Grace Payton — a husband-and-wife firm of spider-monkey lawyers who had mortgaged their office, their house, and their last banana grove to fight the case — held hands so tightly their knuckles went white.

"Members of the jury," the foreman read, "we find for the plaintiff—"

Somebody's mother began the low, wavering howl of the grieving, and the whole gallery joined in, and it rose through the courthouse canopy like weather.

Three hundred miles away, on the top floor of a glass tower, Carl Trudeau heard the number and went quietly bananas. Trudeau was an orangutan of enormous wealth and enormous appetite, and Kruman's stock had just lost three billion dollars, most of which was, personally, his. He was leveraged to the eyebrows. His yacht had a smaller yacht.

"Appeal," he said. "The ape peel. I want the highest court in this state to peel that verdict like a ripe banana."

His political man, a smooth-mannered mandrill named Barry Rinebark, smiled the smile of a mandrill who has color-coded the whole jungle.

"You don't appeal a case like this," he said. "You appeal the court. Supreme Court justices are elected here, Mr. Trudeau. Elections can be bought. Quietly."

"Buy me one," said Trudeau.

Rinebark's fee was a retainer worthy of a small country, and he earned it. He compiled dossiers on every trial judge in the state under sixty: young, clean, electable, ambitious. He passed over the brilliant ones (brilliant monkeys ask questions) and the rich ones (rich monkeys stay bought only by themselves), and found his prize: Ron Fisk, a handsome young capuchin in the city of Chester — churchgoing, soccer coach to the youth troop, a lawyer with a nice family, a modest practice, and no political scars. Most important, no idea he was a puppet. Rinebark watched him for a month and reported: "He'll run, he'll win, and he'll never smell the leash."

The pitch to Fisk was beautiful because it was almost true. The courts were overrun with runaway juries. Business was fleeing the state. A fine young lawyer owed it to his children — to all the children — to serve.

"Who's paying for all this?" Fisk asked.

"Concerned citizens," said Rinebark, radiating sincerity.

The concerned citizens were Trudeau, mostly, through a waterfall of committees with names like Bananas for a Better Court, Growth for Our Groves, and Clean Water, Clean Courts — the last of which had, at that moment, an actual orange creek in its logo. Money flowed in from five states. None of it flowed from Boughmore, which never heard of any of it.

The ads ran in September. Justice Sheila Macaque-arthy, the incumbent, was shown in grainy footage beside a mugshot that belonged to somebody else entirely. SOFT ON PEELERS, the ads thundered. SIDED WITH THE SLUDGE-DUMPERS. WANTS TO TAKE AWAY YOUR GROOMING RIGHTS. VOTE FISK — TOUGH, CLEAN, AND UNBOUGHT.

"I am unbought," Ron Fisk told his wife Doreen, believing it completely.

"Then who bought the television?" said Doreen, who had wanted to know for weeks.

"A movement," said Fisk. "Of the grass roots."

The grass roots had a budget of four million. Macaque-arthy, who had spent thirty years being a careful judge instead of a careful fundraiser, raised six hundred thousand and lent herself the filing fee. In the debates she talked about evidence and precedent; Fisk talked about family, faith, and full county banana bins, and everyone agreed he looked wonderful with a soccer ball under his arm. Rinebark's push-pollers called the households of the state to ask whether they'd be more or less likely to vote for Macaque-arthy if they knew she had once freed a peeler early — and when households asked, freed him to do what?, the pollsters said, sadly, we just don't know, and hung up.

Election night, Fisk won by ninety thousand votes. In Boughmore, where the water still tasted of pennies, Macaque-arthy won nine to one — but Boughmore was only one little town in the trees.

The ape peel was argued eighteen months after the verdict. By then Justice Fisk had been seated, feted, and gently steered — lunches with old friends he'd never met, who all seemed to agree, over dessert, that a court's worst crime was unpredictability. He had decided forty small cases exactly as expected and felt wonderful about all of them.

Mary Grace Payton stood before the nine justices — five of them now friendly to Kruman, including the newest, the clean, unbought Ron Fisk — and said: "You have read the briefs. You know what that company put in the ground. You know what it did to those families. All we ask is that somebody be made to answer for it."

"Juries answer, counsel," said one of the five. "The question is whether there is any limit on what a jury can do."

"There is a limit on what a graveyard can hold," said Mary Grace. "Kruman passed it in Boughmore years ago, and the record shows they knew it, priced it, and kept pumping."

The company's lawyers talked for two days about junk science and runaway juries, and used the word "uncertainty" forty-one times — once, the Paytons swore afterward, for every million dollars they had won. Justice Fisk asked two questions, both about procedure, both suggested to him at lunch, and listened gravely to the answers, feeling extremely judicious.

The conference was short. The opinion came down in the spring: verdict reversed, punitive damages "beyond all reason," case dismissed, new trial denied on grounds the majority invented for the occasion. Four justices dissented in an opinion that began, "The majority buries the town of Boughmore twice." Ron Fisk signed with the majority and told Doreen it had been a hard week at the court, and meant it.

In Boughmore, the wells still ran brown. Jeannette Bough went back to work at the fruit-stand, past the courthouse, every day.

Carl Trudeau's stock rebounded, the analysts chattered, and he celebrated by buying an island with its own private canopy and issuing a press release about his deep commitment to the environment. His accountants framed the arithmetic: total cost of his justice, about eight million dollars, to save three billion. Even the analysts could do that math. Rinebark sent Trudeau a one-line bill and an orchid.

The Paytons sat in their shabby office with the opinion on the desk between them. They had lost the biggest case anybody had ever won. The mortgage on the grove was due, and Wes had begun studying the help-wanted listings the way other monkeys study the racing form.

"Another one came in today," Mary Grace said at last, sliding a folder across. "Little town upriver. Figtree Wells. Same sludge. Same cancer."

Wes Payton looked at it for a long moment. Then he picked it up, because there are monkeys who file things and monkeys who don't, and he had never once managed to.

"Then we do it again," he said.

And somewhere in the state, Barry Rinebark was already shopping for the next unbought young capuchin. The game, as the mandrills say, is never over; it is only on to the next peel.
