# Cosmopoloris

*A parody of "Cosmopolis"*

A gorilla billionaire of twenty-eight sat in a white stretch gourd — a custom hollowed calabash forty feet long, cork-lined, sound-proofed, floored in boiled banana-leaf — in the paused traffic on Eleventh Avenue, and said, to no one, to the intercom, to the whole listening city:

"I want a haircut."

"Traffic is stopped, sir," said Torval, the Nordic bodyguard chimp riding shotgun. "The President is in town. There's a funeral proceeding. It is widely regarded, sir, as a serious jam."

"I want a haircut," Eric Packer said again, "across town. At Anthony's. The old shop on the far east side. That is where my father's hair was cut, and my grandfather's, and that is where mine will be cut. Drive."

Eric Packer was the greatest currency speculator of his generation — a gorilla whose personal worth, that morning, was one hundred and four billion bananas, give or take a truck farm — and he wanted a haircut. This is what nobody in the tower at Packer Capital understood, all fifty floors of them watching the white gourd's blip crawl across the city grid: that it wasn't a haircut. It was a target. It was a destination set precisely because the city was jammed, the President was in town, and the whole world was in his way. He wanted to cross it anyway.

The gourd inched east through the canyons. Inside, it was a flying office: cork walls, a media board rippling with numbers, a heart monitor in the armrest, a urology chart hanging like a calendar (the asymmetric prostate — left lobe suspicious — pending its third exam of the day), and a bar with a bottle of forty-year Scotch and a bowl of stem ginger. His chief of theory, Vija Kinsky, sat opposite him in a raincoat, theorizing.

"The yam," Kinsky said, "is watching you."

"I've shorted the yam," Eric said. "The whole position. Everything. The yam is overvalued because it is boring, and boring is a bubble."

"You think the yam is a kind of sleep," said Kinsky, "and that when the market wakes it will roll over and crush the yam. But consider, Eric: cyber-capital moves at the speed of light. We used to measure wealth in bananas, the way my grandmother measured it in yams; now we measure it in the *absence* of bananas — in the interval between one monkey's button and another's. The market used to be a jungle, then a street, then a screen, then a smell, and soon it will be a sense, and then it will be a *temperature*, and then —"

"And then?"

"A mood, Eric. The market will be a mood, and monkeys will die of it the way they die of weather. Time is the only narrative that matters. Money used to make time; you've made it again this morning. Look."

On the board, the yam was rising. It rose all morning. It rose all afternoon. It rose with a smooth, contemptuous, vertical certainty, and every point it rose took one billion bananas out of Eric Packer's gourd, so that by the time the calabash had crossed half of Cosmopoloris he was merely a very rich gorilla, and by the time it crossed two-thirds he was, by his own arithmetic, ruined. He watched the yam the way a monkey watches a snake: without moving his face.

"The data is the new daylight," Kinsky said. "You have more of it than any gorilla alive."

"Then tell me why the yam."

"Because, Eric," his chief of theory said, adjusting her raincoat against nothing, "the market is not a machine for making money. It is a machine for making *futures*, and every now and then it makes one in which you personally are a fact that has been edited out."

At Twelfth Avenue, the funeral procession of Brutha Figs, the rap prophet, rolled past the gourd in a cortege of low-riding hearses with hydraulics, thumping bass, ten thousand mourners in Figs memorabilia walking behind; and at the same moment, out of the side streets, the protest came — thousands of monkeys, the disaffected and the de-yammed, waving the new currency of the dispossessed: dead rats, spun by their tails, some of them painted gold, some of them blue. *A specter is haunting Cosmopoloris*, said the placards, *the specter of rats.*

"They're doing something to the rats," said Torval, from the front.

"I own the patent on the rats," Eric said. "Metaphorically. Past performance is no guarantee."

And it was here, at the edge of the demonstration, that a thin monkey in a beret fought his way through the crowd to the gourd's sunroof, carrying a pastry box, crying, "Packer! Tycoon! Tyrant! I will give you what you give the world!" — and opened the box, and took out, and threw, a perfect banana-cream pie, with tremendous English spin, into Eric Packer's face.

The pastry assassin was dragged off, howling his credo — "This is the message! The meringue is the message!" — and Eric sat in the cream, being photographed by his own media board, feeling the froth slide into his collar, thinking: this is the first honest transaction of the day.

His wife found him like that. Elise Shifrin-Satin, the poet-heiress, twenty-two, remote, flawless, kept meeting him by accident all afternoon across the ruined city — in a bookshop, in a diner of white tile, on a stalled tram — three meetings, three conversations in which the two of them, married six weeks and strangers, circled each other's souls like mirror-gazers.

"We never have sex," she said. "We have data about sex."

"We could have sex."

"You want a haircut," she said. "I can tell. Your hair is a poem that's trying to end itself. Don't let it, Eric. A gorilla who cuts his hair in this city is a gorilla who believes in endings."

"I'm ruined, Elise. The yam."

"There's always the yam," she said, and was gone, and her perfume stayed in the gourd for six blocks, which was five blocks longer than most of his positions.

The day darkened. The prostate exam (fourth) went badly; the riot got its legs and burned a district; the President's motorcade forced the gourd into the old abandoned streets on the far east side; and Eric Packer, the symmetry of his face finally disturbed by cream, by rain, by a small he'd taken at the poker game in the gourd (a loss of nine hundred million to a monkey named Kinski playing blindfolded), arrived at last at Anthony's shop — where Anthony, the old baboon barber, took one look at him and said:

"I know you. You're your father's son. Sit. You have blood in your hair."

"I haven't been cut."

"Not that kind of blood."

And Anthony cut what there was to cut, and gave him a fresh shirt, and Eric stood in the doorway of the ruined shop — a billionaire of zero, give or take — and did not go home. He went to find the man who had been waiting for him all day, the way the yam had been waiting, the way the cream pie had known his address in advance.

Benno Levin's room was two flights up, in a building of SRO sorrows on the far east side: one cot, one hotplate, one laptop, one banana. Benno was an ex-employee of Packer Capital, a failed analyst, a monkey Eric had never once noticed, and he had a confession, which he had been typing all day, all year — "I am having my confession here" — and a gun.

"You don't remember me," Benno said.

"I bought your fund once," Eric said. "I closed it."

"You *ended* it. My job, my wife, my yam plan. I sat across the trading floor from you for three years and you never looked at me, and I decided, that morning, when you said on the board, *I want a haircut*, that you had said the only true sentence of your generation, and that I would be at the end of it."

"You're going to shoot me."

"I shot you already," Benno said. "The hand."

Eric looked down. His left hand had a hole in it, neat as a fiscal period, and had had it, it seemed, for some minutes. The pain was arriving slowly, like a wire transfer from another century.

"Here's what I've learned," Benno said, working himself up to it, the gun wavering between them, the confession open on the laptop for any monkey to read. "You're not the market. You're not even rich anymore. You're a gorilla in a shirt with a hole in his hand, and you came all the way across the city for it, and that means — that means it's interactive. That means we're *in* something together, you and me, the two of us, right now, and the only question left is the interval."

"The interval," Eric said.

"Between this moment," Benno said, "and the next one."

And Eric Packer — who had shorted the yam, the day, his fortune, his marriage, his prostate, and, finally, himself; who had found, at the end of the jam, the one position he could not hedge — looked into the muzzle, which was dark as a board before the numbers came, and felt, for the first time all day, fully invested.

"Do it," said the gorilla who wanted a haircut. "I want to see the close."
