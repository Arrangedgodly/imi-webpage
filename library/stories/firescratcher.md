# Firescratcher

*A parody of "Firestarter"*

The rain hammered the taxi, and Andy Macaque, in the back with his daughter, kept turning his head like a bird watching for hawks. When you have run from the Shop for two years, you learn to check the sky.

The Shop — officially the Banological Applications Shop — was a government menagerie with a souvenir budget and no conscience. Andy knew exactly how it had started, because it had started with him.

"Plot Six," he told the driver, who had asked why his fur was wet though the top was up. "My last year at Mango U. They paid us in banana credits to drink fermented banana-nectar and describe our visions. I saw doors. Another monkey saw her whole family as fruit flies. And a very few of us came out changed."

Andy's change was the Push. He could push thoughts into other monkeys' heads, gently, like sliding a note under a door. Once, to save a friend, he had pushed a mugger into apologizing to every monkey in the market square, one at a time, with a fruit basket. The Shop wanted to weigh him, scan him, and keep him in a very clean cage with a clipboard.

Then there was Charlie. Charlie Macaque, four years old, born with a furnace behind her sternum.

"When Charlie gets scared, or cross, or itchy," Andy said, "her fur prickles. Then she scratches behind her ear — and then things burn. Bananas brown from across the room. Glass goes soft. The air turns tiger-orange."

"Neat trick," said the driver, and dropped them at Irv Mandrill's farm, the last kind place on the map.

Charlie had been doing it since the crib. At one, she'd charred a bowl of banana mush that came in too hot. At two, she'd vaporized her xylophone over a disputed nap. And at three, when the gray hats came up the walk of their little rented tree and her mother went out to talk to them and never came back in, Charlie browned every rail on that porch in one long breath, and the Shop has issued gloves ever since.

The hats found them anyway, at Irv's. Irv met them at the gate with the old shotgun he called Second Opinion. "You can come over my land," he said, "when you monkeys learn some manners." Cap Hollow-branchster stepped out of the biggest van with a clipboard and a smile with a wire in it, and behind him came Rainbird — a scarlet macaw with one milky eye and a patient, thoughtful way of tilting his head.

"Miss Charlie," Rainbird said softly. "I hear you do a trick."

What happened at the gate went into the Shop's files as an "weather event." Andy, shot through the shoulder and grinning about it, pushed calm on eight hats at once — pushed until his nose bled — and eight grown monkeys sat down in the mud and had a nice long think about their careers. Charlie aimed, honestly, at nothing but tires. She hit one van, both fenders, and the county's reputation. Irv got them out the back way and drove all night, saying only, "My land. My rules. My passengers."

They woke up inside the Long Bough compound: white floors, one-way glass, bananas at controlled intervals. The Shop kept them apart — that was policy, "independent baseline data" — and let them meet twice a week in a room with a table between them and a mirror that was nobody's friend.

"How's my kind monkey?" Andy asked, every time, through the glass.

"Saving up," Charlie said, every time. Being four, she said it like a plan and not a comfort, which it somehow managed to be both.

Andy healed and was interrogated gently, twice a week, by Dr. Pynchot, a plump orangutan psychologist who wanted to chat about Andy's "feelings about authority." Andy Pushed back, a thread at a time, patient as rot. By August, Dr. Pynchot had developed a sudden interest in washing dishes. He washed them at home; he washed them at his mother's; by September he was found at 3 a.m. in the compound kitchen, washing dishes that were already clean, weeping quietly, saying only "spotless" in a satisfied voice. The Shop's staff psychologist was given a leave of absence and a very dry towel. Cap Hollow-branchster developed a drink problem, got quietly Pushed into a program, and signed things for weeks afterward with a happy faraway look, including, eventually, one gate.

Charlie, meanwhile, refused her tests. They showed her a candle at forty branches: she wouldn't. They showed her a block of pine: she wouldn't. They brought in a troop of white-coats with clipboards, and she scratched behind her ear — both hands, the way you do when you mean it — and lit the candle, the table, the pine block, the clippings, and Dr. Pynchot's career, all in one tiger-orange breath, and then went back to her breakfast as if nothing had happened, because she was her father's daughter and aimed at equipment.

Only Rainbird could touch her. He groomed her shoulders with one careful claw, offered her sunflower seeds, and told her he was the only friend she had in the world. He watched her through the one-way glass at night, an eye at the mirror, patient as a statue of a bird; she thought it was a friend standing guard. It was a bird with a schedule. He told the hats that before the tests ended, he meant to see her do it all the way. He had, he said, a tick of curiosity.

Andy had one Push left in him — he had been saving it the way you save the last of anything. He spent it in the stable where Rainbird stood over Charlie on the high catwalk. Andy, hurt and grinning, reached up with his mind and pushed: he filled the macaw's skull with sunflower seeds, one at a time, each seed a word. COUNT. THEM. FOREVER. Rainbird shrieked that he could see them, that there were so many seeds, and leapt for the skylight, wings full of mathematics, and did not clear it.

Andy slid down against the hay with his daughter holding his face. "Do it bright, baby," he whispered. "Burn it so bright they can't pretend they never saw. Buildings, Charlie. Only buildings. Be my kind monkey one more time."

Charlie's fur prickled. She scratched behind her ear, both hands, the way you do when you mean it.

The Long Bough burned orange as a sunset, and the wind for miles smelled of caramel. Every monkey got out; she aimed at concrete and filing cabinets, because she was her father's daughter. They walked out through the smoke side by side, past the gates, past the vans with their tires already sagging, and nobody in a gray hat so much as unfolded a clipboard.

The Mandrills took her in. The Senate asked questions in small voices, and the Shop's budget line developed a fever and quietly died. And when she was ready — months later, steady-eyed — she walked into the offices of *Rolling Banana* and told a reporter the whole thing.

The story ran in the next issue, under a photograph of a little macaque with soot still in her fur.

The headline read: LITTLE MONKEY, BIG LIGHT.

And for once, the Shop couldn't push it away.
