# The Ookyssey

*A parody of "The Odyssey"*

Sing to me, O Muse, of the monkey of swings and turns, who was driven far off course after the fall of Fig-Troy — the clever Ookysseus, king of Itchaca, ripest trickster of his age.

Ten years the war had lasted; ten more he wandered. On Itchaca his son Telemachus grew from squeaker to sulker while a hundred and eight suitors — the boldest young monkeys of the isles — camped in his father's fig-hall, eating the royal banana-hoard and wrecking the branches. Penelope, the queen, unpicked her weaving every night and told them, "When I finish this rope-net, I will choose a husband — I promise." For three years the net never got finished. The suitors never got the joke.

Athena, the grey-eyed owl-goddess of wisdom, went to Telemachus in the shape of a family friend and told him to go asking after his father. He visited old Nestor of the fig-plains (who answered three questions with four digressions) and bronze-armed Menelaus, who said wisely, "No monkey alive is as clever as Ookysseus. Also, he once got us out of a war with a wooden animal. Ask him about it. No — don't."

Meanwhile Ookysseus sat captive — a pampered prisoner — on Calypso's island of endless mangoes, being offered immortality to stay and be her prime mate.

"Immortality, yes, lovely," said Ookysseus. "But home is home. My Penelope grooms my memory. I would rather scratch a bough on Itchaca than be a god in anyone's mango-grove."

Athena took his case to the Olympian fig-council, and Zeus sent word: let him sail. At last the wanderer was launched — though the sea, alas, had not finished with him.

Relive his logbook briefly. The Lotus-Eaters, whose honeyed lotus-figs made his crew forget home entirely — he dragged them weeping to the ship. Then the Cyclops: Polyphemus, a giant one-eyed gorilla-shepherd who sealed them in his cave with a boulder and made meals of the crew two at a time.

Nobody had a plan but Ookysseus. He offered the giant a bowl of fig-wine: "My name is No-monkey. No-monkey is my name."

"No-monkey! Your gift I'll give last!" — and then the sharpened, fire-hardened fig-wood stake went into the single eye. Polyphemus howled, and his fellow giants came running: "Who hurts you?"

"No-monkey!" bellowed Polyphemus. "No-monkey is hurting me!"

"If nobody's hurting you, be quiet," they said, and stumped off.

"I'm nobody!" shouted Ookysseus, escaping under the ram-bellies. "I'm Ookysseus, sacker of fig-cities!" — an egotistical mistake that cost him a decade of curses — a lesson to monkeys everywhere.

Next, Aeolus's bag of winds, tied shut — opened by the crew within sight of Itchaca. Then Circe, the sorceress-lemur of the tangled isle, who with a wand and a fig-potion turned his crew into sloths. "Mmm," they said. "Naps." Ookysseus, forewarned, had eaten the protective molly-leaf and refused to slow down; by firm negotiation he made her turn them back into monkeys — and, because she was charming and the roast was excellent, stayed a full year, until the crew pointed out, gently, that Itchaca was the other way.

It was Circe who told him the price of the road home: "You must sail first to the House of the Dead and ask the blind seer Teiresias the way. There is no other route. Also, mind the cattle of the Sun."

So down he sailed, to the land of perpetual dimness, where the dead monkeys drift like tired fruit-bats, and dug a pit and filled it with fig-blood. The shades came crowding. First Teiresias: "Bear the cattle of the Sun, and you may reach home. Touch them, and your ship and crew are lost — and you alone return, late, on another monkey's raft, to find your hall full of suitors eating your bananas." The news was neither good nor bad, which in Ookysseus's logbook counted as wonderful.

Then his mother Anticlea drifted up. "Mother, why are you here?" — "Because I missed you into the grave, my son. Itchaca is still standing. So is she — Penelope. She still weeps for you at night." The dead, he discovered, know exactly how to hurt you and exactly how to fix it.

Then came Agamemnon, fresh-dead, gossiping: "Never trust a homecoming, friend. I got a fig-spike in the bath from my own wife's tribe. Tell Penelope to meet you in the orchard with the baby, first day, with witnesses." And then Ape-chilles himself, first of all the dead, came swift across the asphodel.

"Ookysseus? How did YOU get here? They said you could not even die properly."

"Great Ape-chilles, be comforted — you rule all the dead. A king of shades is still a king."

"Do not speak soothingly to me of death, glorious Ookysseus," said Ape-chilles. "I would rather be the lowest-paid fruit-picker on a poor ape's farm, working the thorn-trees for a hairy landlord, than king over all the shade-monkeys of the pit. But tell me — is my boy famous? Did he grow into his feet?"

"Your boy took the fig-city's tallest tower," said Ookysseus, editing slightly, as old soldiers do. And Ape-chilles went away striding through the asphodel, for the first time in forever, whistling.

Up in the world again: the Sirens — three gibbons whose harmony could make a strong sailor forget his own tail. Ookysseus had himself lashed to the mast with his ears open: "I alone must hear the song." It took four extra vines. His crew's ears were plugged with beeswax, and they rowed on, sullen and unmusical.

Then the strait-horrors: Scylla, the six-necked leopard-lizard above, and Charybdis, the whirlpool below. He chose the lizard — lose six monkeys to Scylla, or lose the whole ship to the swirl. And then, exhausted, beached on the Sun-god's island, where his crew — hungry, weeping, swearing he'd take the blame — slaughtered the sacred cattle while Ookysseus slept. The hides crawled; the meat lowed on the spits. The Sun went straight to Zeus.

One ship left the island. Zeus's thunderbolt split it like a fig. Every last monkey of the crew went down — and Ookysseus alone, as prophesied, clawed onto the wreckage, rode past Charybdis a second time, and washed up at last on Ogygia: Calypso, the mangoes, and seven more years. Which is where this story found him.

The Phaeacians — who prized a good story as much as a good monkey — gave him a ship home. Their princess Nausicaa found him first, washed ashore and modestly holding a fig-leaf, and said to her maids, "Finally, a shipwreck with manners." At their feast the court bard sang of the Wooden Monkey of Fig-Troy, and the scarred stranger wept into his cloak until he finally said: "I am Ookysseus," and told them the whole logbook, which took nine nights and was considered the best entertainment in Phaeacian history.

He landed on Itchaca disguised as an old beggar, grey fur glued on, back bent. The old dog Argus, lying on the midden twenty years and waiting, thumped his tail once, recognized him, and died content. The swineherd Eumaeus, loyal to the last boar, fed the beggar without knowing him; Telemachus, slipped ashore from his own fact-finding mission, got let in on the secret and hugged the grimy beggar so hard the grey fur slid off.

The suitors threw fig-skins at the beggar. Penelope said mildly, "Tomorrow I will hold a contest."

She brought out Ookysseus' great bow of fig-wood, so stiff no suitor could bend it. "Whoever strings it and shoots a fig-seed through the holes of twelve hanging gourds — I marry."

Antinous strained until his face went purple. The others oiled their arms. Nothing budged.

"May I try?" asked the beggar.

"Ha!" they laughed — but Telemachus said, "Let him," and the old nurse Eurycleia, washing the beggar's feet, saw the boar-tusk scar and nearly fainted on the spot. (He caught her: "Mother — hush.")

The beggar strung the bow in one motion, plucked it like a lyre-string, and let fly. The fig-seed sang through all twelve gourds. Then the second arrow took Antinous through the goblet-lifting throat.

"Telemachus," said Ookysseus, flinging off his rags, "the stranger's dinner is done."

The hall erupted. The suitors ran for the armor-room — Telemachus, having planned ahead, had removed the armor. Athena, in the shape of a swallow, watched from the rafters, amused. It was over quickly, messily, and to the lasting satisfaction of every mother on Itchaca whose daughter the suitors had annoyed.

Then Penelope — his match in every scheme — sat calm as morning. "Move the bed for our guest."

"Impossible," said Ookysseus. "Our bed is carved from the living fig-stump; I built the whole bough around it. No monkey could move it."

She smiled. "Only Ookysseus knows that."

And they were matched. Athena declared peace across the isles — and Itchaca was quiet, at least until the next banana tax. But that, O Muse, is another epic.
