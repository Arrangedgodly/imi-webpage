# Do Ape-droids Dream of Electric Chimps?

*A parody of "Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?"*

The morning the bounty came through, Rick Deckape sat on the roof of his treehouse in the ruined jungle of post-apocalyptic San Franciscozoa, dialing his mood groomer down from despair to grudging tolerance while his wife, Iran, dialed hers up to ecstasy and back down again out of spite.

"You could dial reverie," he suggested.

"I could dial a troop of howlers and it would be more honest," said Iran. "You own a fake chimp, Rick. A *fake*. We spend our whole lives pretending the electric things are alive."

The electric chimp grazed on the roof beside them, chewing plastic bananas. Deckape had bought it to replace the real one that died, and he hated it, and he groomed it every night anyway. What he wanted — what every monkey wanted since the last war had left the jungles radioactive and the real animals nearly gone — was a live one. A goat, even. Especially a goat.

By noon he was on the zeppelin to the Gorillrel Corporation, whose motto — *More monkey than monkey* — glowed in gold letters over the largest fig tree in the city. Eldon Gorillrel himself, vast and ancient in his canopy-throne, watched Deckape run the Groom-Kampff test on his newest model.

"You're given a calfskin banana-sling for your birthday," Deckape recited. "What do you do?"

"I'd keep it," said the dark-furred female across the table.

"You keep it. Then your host offers you a stuffed hummingbird and a jar of pickled figs. The hummingbird is stuffed with real maggots. Do you—"

"Is this testing whether I'm an ape-droid," the female said quietly, "or whether I'm a *wife*?"

Her name was Rachael Gorillrel. It took Deckape a hundred questions to catch her, and when he did, she wept — perfectly, moisturized tears — and he flew home feeling like something scraped off a branch. On the corporate roof she had made him an offer: "I've helped retire eleven of them. I know how they think. Take me with you." He had said no. He would think about her anyway, at all the wrong moments, which was presumably the design spec.

The escapees were already loose in the city. Six ape-droids, hijacked from the Banana Colonies off-world: a Polokov, a Pris, a Luba, a Zhora, a Garland, and their leader, Roy Batoon, a combat model with a silver-furred mane and a gaze that peeled you like fruit.

Polokov had hidden in the one place no monkey looks: inside the police. Deckape found him wearing a badge, ran the test in the back of a parked flivver, and got the dodge, the grin, and then — very fast — two hands around his throat. The new models had learned to hurry. Deckape retired him through the roof hatch with two shots and sat in the flivver for a quarter hour, dialing the mood groomer and refusing whatever it offered.

Following Luba Luft — an opera-singer model with a voice like warm honey poured directly into the ear — Deckape was arrested by officers he had never met, in a precinct house that had his own name misspelled on the docket, and introduced to Inspector Phil Resch, a bounty hunter with a predator's lean face and a squirrel monkey's nervous tail, and to their silver-muzzled captain, a mandrill named Garland.

"You're not on my list," Garland told Deckape pleasantly, reading the file upside down. "Because you're not a bounty hunter. You're an ape-droid with false memories. They do that now. Surely you've suspected?"

And the poison of it, Deckape thought later, was that he had. Resch's face went through the same weather. When Garland at last drew a laser from the desk, Resch killed him between one syllable and the next, and then stood staring at the smoking floor.

"Test me," Resch said hoarsely. "Right now. If I'm one of them—"

He wasn't. He tested monkey, all the way down; just cruel, which is a condition of the heart and not the wiring. Together they found Luba at the museum, staring at a wall of reproduction paintings. Deckape bought her the picture book of Munch's and Edenborough's originals she was holding — he could not have said why — and while she hugged it to her chest, Resch retired her in the elevator.

"I had a test to run," Resch said, puzzled at the shaking of Deckape's hands. "She was a machine."

"She wanted a picture book," Deckape said.

That night, with the bounty money, he did the thing he had wanted to do all his life: he bought a real Nubian she-goat. Iran wept over it in the paddock on the roof. It was the single best hour of their marriage.

Rachael came to see the goat three days later. She fed it a fig, complimented its ears, let Deckape hold her, and then told him, calmly, from the stairwell, exactly what she was: "There were eleven others before you. Every one a bounty hunter. Every one slept with me. Every one afterward found he could not retire a Rachael model, because she would have my face. Goodbye, Rick."

On the way down, she pushed the goat off the roof.

Retiring Zhora in the rain — she ran, and the jungle held its breath, and afterwards Deckape sat in his flivver shaking, dialing the mood groomer and refusing whatever it offered — he understood something about himself that he did not want. He found Pris at a derelict fig-hall, doing acrobatics with junk, and for one whole minute he mistook her for Rachael, because they had been built from the same face. "No," said Pris, with a terrible little laugh. "Rachael's a baby. I'm two years older, and I don't cry." He found Batoon on the roof, in the last rain of the last real afternoon, battery dying, mane dripping.

"You will be required to do wrong, Deckape," Batoon said, holding up a hand to stop him. "I've seen things you monkeys wouldn't believe. Bunches of bananas gleaming in the dark near the Tannhäuser Grove. I watched f-stops glitter in the sun over the Cannery Gates. All those moments will be compost, like tears in rain."

Deckape waited for the fight. There wasn't one. Batoon simply saved his life — pulled him back from a falling branch with the last strength in his motor coils — and then sat down amid the trash and silence, and the rain kept falling on him, and then it didn't.

That night Deckape climbed the stairs to his roof, thinking of Groomerism, the empathy cult in which you gripped your grooming-handle and climbed with Wilbur Groomer up an endless tree while unseen hands hurled rotten fruit down at you, and everyone climbed together, and it was shared, and it was real, or it wasn't, and Buster Friendly had proven it wasn't on his show tonight — the whole vision staged in a studio, the fruit thrown by stagehands — and it didn't matter, because climbing was climbing. He thought of Rachael, and the goat, and Luba's picture book, and understood that a monkey could be retired too, slowly, without a laser.

On the roof, in a puddle of exhaust-stained light, lay a small chimp.

Real, he thought. A real chimp, out here, where nothing real had lived for years. He carried it inside, hands shaking, and Iran saw its wiring — electric, of course, electric to the bone — and Deckape sat with it on his lap, grooming its synthetic fur with long, slow strokes.

"It's fake," Iran said gently. "Rick. It's fake."

"Everything's fake," said Deckape, "and I'm going to order it electric flies."

He turned the mood groomer off and grieved all by himself. It took seventeen minutes. Then he went back out to the roof, and it was evening, and somewhere in the ruined jungle a real troop of howlers was singing, and he listened until it was dark.
