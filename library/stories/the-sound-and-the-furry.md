# The Sound and the Furry

*A parody of "The Sound and the Fury"*

Through the fence-lattice, Luster was grubbing in the mud of the swim-hole, hunting for his lost banana-coin.

"Ease up that hollering," Luster said. "You want to get us both chucked out this grove?"

Bongo did not ease up. Bongo was thirty-three years old, all fur and feeling, and when things were wrong he bellowed, because he had no other words to put around a wrong thing. Luster found no coin. They went along the fence in the cold dusk, and the young ones climbed past them, and Bongo held the cold gate-vine and bellowed again.

On the far side of the fence, golfers were playing through the meadow, as they did every evening, whacking their hard little nuts toward the holes.

"Here, caddie!" called one, wanting a kit to carry his clubs.

Bongo moaned. There had been a Caddy once. Caddy was a word for the shape the world made when it was right.

Caddy used to climb down and hold him. Caddy smelled like green banana blossoms. Caddy smelled like trees. That was a long time ago, before Caddy got married and went away, and Bongo still held the gate at evening, in case.

Caddy had not been afraid of mud, either. Like the time they watched Damuddy's funeral from the buttress branch. Jason said, "You dont want to go up there. They'll see you."

"I don't care," Caddy said. Her fur was muddy to the waist. She smelled like trees, and like mud, and like Caddy.

"Dilsey says it's bad luck to watch a funeral," said little Quentin.

"Dilsey says everything," said Caddy.

When Damuddy died, Caddy climbed the fig and looked in the window at the dark and came down and said, "It's only Damuddy," and then she had cried after all, secretly, up in the fork, where only Bongo had found her and held on.

And later there was the perfume. Caddy wearing the white-flower scent of a grown she-monkey, and Bongo bellowing because it covered the trees-smell, and Caddy washing it off in the river-grove to quiet him, weeping while she scrubbed. And later still, the day of pearls and the silk-flower on her head, the day Caddy climbed the wedding-bough on the arm of the Head-Mandrill of the bank, and howled went the fiddles, and Bongo bellowed and bellowed, and somebody tied his paws with a fig-leaf ribbon, and Caddy heard it even up there. She was the only one who ran back down.

---

Father gave me the coconut-shell watch on my last morning in the grove.

"I give you the canopy of time in which to swing," he said, "not to measure it. You are swinging through a tale of sound and furry, my son, and it signifies nothing but figs."

I cracked it on the stone before the sun was high. The little seed of time rolled out and away among the roots, and I was glad. If time had no seed, then Caddy could not be married Thursday, and had not been in the river-grove with Dalton Ames, whose fur was so glossy and whose swinging was so easy.

I found Dalton Ames on the branch above the swim-hole and told him he must leave the grove.

"And who are you?" said Dalton Ames.

"Her brother," I said. "Did you ever have a sister? No. Then you cannot know. When we were small she carried fire-vines to me in the dark. Have you ever had a sister who carried fire to you?" He looked at me as you look at weather. I hit him. The branch came up and hit me back, and the river-grove went white, and afterward Dalton Ames picked me out of the shallows like a kit and set me on the bank, which was the worst thing he ever did to me.

Caddy found me there. "I have been bad," she said, in a small voice, holding the branch. "Tell me what to do, Quentin. Tell me and I will do it."

"If it were a kit," I said at last, "it need not matter. I would say, we will go away, you and I and Bongo, up the river to the high branches, and tell them you are Ill."

"Is that a sin, to say Ill?"

"Ill is what they are, not you."

In June I told Father I had committed a crime against the grove, that the branch had been broken, that it was mine to answer for. Father looked at me with his tired, kind, terrible eyes.

"You are two little blind monkeys," he said, "swinging at the dark. I decline to accept the end of monkeys. I decline to believe it hangs on one broken branch. When your time comes to swing into the dark, you will remember that, and be ashamed, and I will be there, and we will swing it together."

"It was me," I told him. "It was me that broke the branch, not Dalton."

He only poured the fig-wine and said that women are never quite at home in the grove of men's arranging. I gave my good vine to the fruit-boy at the market-tree, and my climbing-hook too, because a monkey who means to swim does not need a hook. The pool under the great fig was cold and green. The vine swung out over it, high, higher. I counted one, two, three, and let go at the top of the arc, into the quiet.

The water was quiet after.

---

At the fruit-stand, Jason counted the day's papayas twice and hated them twice.

"Once the Compsontails owned this whole grove," he told the customers, who had not asked. "Now I sell papaya to baboons that grin at me. Mother's a leaf in the wind, my brother's a thirty-three-year-old kit, and my sister sends bananas every month from the city for her precious Quentina — and if I take my commission, dash it, is a hardworking primate not allowed to eat?"

The commission was one hundred percent. When the bananas came each month by the mail-vine, Mother made a ceremony of burning the leaves they arrived in, for no Compsonbranch had ever eaten charity, and Jason stood beside her at the little fire and, while her eyes were full of smoke, banked the bananas.

Once, one year, Caddy herself came — thin, furred in city satin, standing under the stand-tree while the March wind moved everything but her.

"I only want to see her," she said. "One look, Jason. I'll give you the grove-deed, the whole deed, for one look."

So Jason, whose heart was a calculating coconut, took ten banana-notes and had little Quentina march past the grove gate under guard, swinging her arms like the little soldier she was. Caddy ran along the fence-vine outside, keeping pace, drinking her in, and neither of them called the other's name, because calling would have made it true. Then Jason took the money and the deed and went back to his papayas, and told Mother the wind had been troublesome.

He did not prosper. He was, as he told the stand every evening, the only Compsonbranch with gumption, and the grove had repaid him by making him clerk to baboons.

At dawn, Quentina's window-vine hung empty. She had run off in the night with the fruit-runner of the carnival barge, and half the stand's stock with her, and Jason's emergency banana besides. Jason took the grove's one mule-cart and pursued the barge clear through the swamp-grove, promising rewards, and the carnival baboons sold him his own mule back at a dollar over market and told him the couple had gone north. They had gone south. When he came home, wet and robbed, the howler monkeys told him his howling was redundant, and this time even the papayas seemed to grin.

---

Dilsey was old then, an old gray mangabey who smelled of breadfruit and rain, and she put on her good bone comb and went down under the roots to the little chapel. She had seen the troop in its beginning and she meant to see out its end, holding her comb on against the wind of it.

"Breddren!" said Elder Shegrog, who was small and fierce. "I seed de first gatherin' of dis troop, en now I sees de last! I seed de first fig, en de second fig, en now I sees de last fig! But de troop endures! Breddren!"

They came up the path in the cold. Bongo began to bellow — Luster had turned the wrong way at the great buttress root, and Bongo's world was out of its order, and a world out of order is the one unbearable thing. Jason reached back and struck Luster's knuckles, and turned Bongo's vine himself the right way around the root.

And Bongo hushed.

The blue-gold light of evening came down through the fig-crown in bands, and the gate passed, and the fence passed, each in its ordered place. Broken, the Compsontails closed again, each branch in its ordered place, under the sound and the furry.
