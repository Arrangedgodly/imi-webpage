# This Is Not My Habitat

*A parody of "This Is Not My Hat"*

This is not my habitat.

I took it from a big monkey.

It was too big for him anyway. It fits me just right.

I am talking, of course, about the Great Fork — the finest fork of branches in the whole kapok tree, where the trunk splits into two perfect limbs and makes a cradle of living wood, angled just so, to catch the first warm of the morning sun. Whoever sleeps in the Great Fork wakes up golden. There is a moss cushion on the west limb. There is a fig basket woven into the crotch of the fork. There is a view of the river, and of the flowering vine that the hummingbirds fight over, and, on clear days, of the mountain called the Old Blue Banana by monkeys who have never seen a mountain but have excellent imaginations.

The big monkey had the Great Fork all to himself. Every night. He would sprawl across it, taking up the whole thing, one arm hanging over each limb like a hairy hammock of a monkey, snoring his big snore into the big night.

A habitat like that is wasted on a big monkey.

I know it is wrong to take a habitat from a big monkey. I know that. Nobody has to tell me. But he has the whole rest of the tree. He has the whole rest of the jungle. He is a big monkey, with big monkey arms and big monkey shoulders and a big monkey chest that goes boom-boom-boom when he beats it in the morning. What does he need with a cozy fork?

I only need one. I am a little monkey. I need a cozy place more than he does. Any reasonable monkey would agree with me, and the ones who wouldn't are usually big monkeys, so their opinions hardly count.

So last night, while the big monkey was down at the river gorge stuffing himself with sloth-picked fruit — I watched him go, I made sure — I swung up into the Great Fork, and I took it.

I moved my moss. I moved my three best figs (still green; I like them green; it's a little monkey thing). I moved my lucky seed pod, which rattles. I hung my tail over the west limb exactly the way he does, and you know what? It looked better. It fit me just right.

I am not saying he doesn't care about the Great Fork. I am just saying he won't know it was me.

How would he know? There are over four hundred monkeys in this tree alone. There are little monkeys everywhere. It could have been any little monkey. It could have been that noisy one from the third branch. It could have been the twins. Nobody can tell little monkeys apart, not even little monkeys, and I mean that as a scientific fact.

And even if he does notice — even if he comes lumbering up to the fork tonight, blinking his big slow blink at me — what is he going to do? He can't prove anything. You cannot prove a habitat. There are no marks on it. There is no sign on it. There is just a fork, and moss, and a fig basket, and a little monkey who is sitting in it, resting, minding his own business, as innocent as fruit.

That is why I am leaving this part of the tree anyway. Just to be careful. Not because I'm scared. Because I'm careful. There is a difference, and it is a difference the careful monkey lives to explain.

I am going to the Tangle.

The Tangle is deep in the crown of the tree, where nobody goes, where the creepers grow big and green and close together. They grow so big and so green and so close together that the daylight barely leaks through, just a few green coins of light on the dark, and the branches lie on top of each other like a thousand ropes pulled tight. The vines there are older than the tree. They say a monkey could live in the Tangle for a hundred years and never be found, and then he would be a legend, which would also be nice.

The big monkey will never find me there.

Big monkeys do not like the Tangle. Their shoulders are too wide. Their pride is too wide. It is a little monkey's country, the Tangle, and I will be little in it very comfortably.

This is what I told myself, all the way up through the branches, hopping and swinging with my three green figs and my lucky seed pod, while the sky went pink and then purple and then the color of a bruised plum.

This is what I told myself while I found the perfect spot — a snug green room of woven vine, with one small window of leaves — and arranged my moss, and put my figs in a row, and rattled my seed pod once, for luck.

Nobody followed me. I am almost sure nobody followed me. The big monkey is probably asleep by now. Big monkeys sleep early. They have big easy days, big easy dinners, big easy everything, and then they sleep in somebody's cozy fork — well. Not anymore.

And even if he did follow me — even if, somehow, he found the Tangle, and found the creepers, and pushed through the close green dark where his shoulders barely fit — he would never find this exact spot. There are a thousand spots in the Tangle. There are ten thousand. A big clumsy monkey could blunder around out there all night, huffing and snorting and stepping on his own tail, and he would find nothing but vines.

So.

Here I am.

Cozy as a fig in its skin.

It fits me just—

The vines shivered.

That was nothing. That was the wind. Vines shiver all the time; it is practically their job.

But now the shiver comes again, and it comes closer, and it comes lower, and it is not the wind, because the wind does not breathe, and something out there in the green dark is definitely breathing, slowly, like a big monkey squeezing through a small country.

The coins of light go out one by one.

Nobody will ever find me here.

Nobody will ever—

---

Far below, at the foot of the Tangle, a gecko sat on the trunk beside a crack in the bark. He had seen a big monkey go by earlier that evening, moving fast, huffing, with a look on his face like weather.

He had also, a moment ago, seen a big monkey come back down.

The big monkey was carrying two green figs and a small moss cushion. He paused at the crack in the trunk, caught the gecko's eye, and held up one finger to his lips.

The gecko blinked, once, slowly, which was not an answer at all.

The big monkey swung away through the branches to the Great Fork, where he arranged his moss, and put his figs in a row, and hung one arm over each limb.

It fit him just right.

And up in the deep green dark of the Tangle, very faint and very far away, a small voice said:

"...ow."
