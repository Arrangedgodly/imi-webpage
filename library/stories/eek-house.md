# Eek House

*A parody of "Bleak House"*

London. Michaelmas term. Fog everywhere — a jungle fog of overripe fruit and wet fur, creeping into the grooming salons and the banana exchanges. And at the very center of the fog, like a moldy old fig at the heart of a rotten fruit bowl, sits the Court of Chancery, where the great case of *Jarndyce and Jarndyce* drags on.

*Jarndyce and Jarndyce* has been going for so many generations that no living monkey can remember what it was about. Whole troops of apes have been born into it and died out of it, while the lawyers pick the estate clean, twig by twig, banana by banana. The little apes who play leapfrog under the courtroom barriers grow into the big apes who argue there, then the old apes who doze there, then the ghosts who haunt it — and still the case goes on.

"This is a great system," murmurs young Richard Carstone, a ward of the case along with his cousin Ada Clare. "Surely it must end soon, and I shall be rich."

"Richard," says his cousin Esther Summerson gently, "I would not build upon it, if I were you."

Esther — a plain little chimp with a scarred heart and the sweetest grooming touch in England — has come to Eek House as housekeeper to their guardian, Mr. John Jarndyce, the kindest old gorilla in the world, who keeps a "Growlery" (a room for growling in) and calls every problem "the east wind."

"You will find us poor, Esther," says Mr. Jarndyce, "but you will find us fond of you."

Eek House collects gentle eccentrics the way a fig collects wasps. There is Mrs. Jellyby, the philanthropic chimp who writes letters all day for the banana farmers of Borrioboola, on the far banks of the Nile, while her own cubs tumble into the fire-grate ("I don't mean an unsettled mind," she explains, "only a mind at ease about distant things"). There is Mr. Skimpole, a grown ape who describes himself as "a mere child" and is therefore exempt from paying bills, rents, or ferry tolls — "I never pay, my dear Jarndyce; it is a principle of mine" — and who will one day accept five pounds for a betrayal, protesting mildly that he is constitutionally ga-ga about guineas. And at the rag-and-bottle shop of old Krook — who calls himself the Lord Chancellor, since he hoards papers no one can read and never gives judgment — there lives Miss Flite, a mad little capuchin who attends court daily with her reticule of documents and keeps a flock of caged birds, to be released when "the judgment" gives her her liberty.

"I name them," she tells Esther, dropping a curtsy to each cage. "Hope, Joy, Youth, Peace, Rest, Life, Dust, Ashes, Waste, Want, Ruin, Despair, Madness, Death, Cunning, Folly, Words, Wigs, Rags, Sheepskin, Plunder, Precedent, Jargon, Gammon, and Spinach."

"Gammon and Spinach," said Esther, laughing before she could stop herself.

"The two youngest," said Miss Flite fondly. "I expect great things of Gammon."

At the opposite end of the jungle's moral canopy lives Lady Dedlock — the coldest, most beautiful bonobo in society, bored to death at Chesney Wold. One evening her lawyer, Mr. Tulkinghorn — an ape so old and cold he is practically a fossil in a waistcoat — reads aloud in her presence a new affidavit, written in a certain law-writer's hand; and her ladyship asks, with elaborate carelessness, "And who copied that?" — and Tulkinghorn, who misses nothing, later observes her ladyship secretly seeking out the writer, and her composure shattering.

Something — or rather someape — connects the great Lady Dedlock to a wretched, dying law-writer called Nemo, "no one," who lives above a bricklayer's shop. When Nemo dies alone, only one creature mourns him: a little crossing-sweeper named Jo, a homeless chimp moved along by every constable in London.

"The substance of the law is," says the constable, "to move him on."

Tulkinghorn buys up Nemo's letters from Krook's shop. Young Mr. Guppy, a law clerk with a cowlick and a plan, has already proposed to Esther ("I am devoted to you, Miss Summerson, with my whole heart" — delivered twice, in case she missed it the first time; declined with thanks) and later traces the same letters to a truth he thinks will make his fortune. For Nemo, it will break your heart to learn, was once Captain Hawdon — and the father of Esther Summerson, and the lost love of Lady Dedlock. Esther, the plain little housekeeper, is her ladyship's own daughter, raised in secret by an aunt who told her it would have been far better if she had never been born at all. The scar on Esther's face — left by a fever she caught nursing Jo — is the very thing her mother recognizes her by, secretly, when their paths cross at Chesney Wold, where Lady Dedlock kneels one night at the little governess's door and kisses the sill.

Tulkinghorn pieces it all together, for the pleasure of power over the great. "It was a move in the game," he tells Lady Dedlock, coolly dusting the gun case of his voice. "You are to remember that I am not the guardian of your secrets, my lady — only their custodian. And custodians are owed fees."

Then, one night in his chambers, somebody puts a bullet through the old ape — and the whole jungle assembles its suspects: George, a shooting-gallery gorilla who had quarreled with him; Lady Dedlock, whose glove was found upon the stair; and, somewhere in the fog, the mystery of one very small, very wet footprint. Enter Inspector Bucket of the Detective — a large cool baboon with a fat forefinger and an eye like a fish, who watches everyape and appears to watch nobody.

Meanwhile Richard Carstone is being eaten alive by *Jarndyce and Jarndyce*. He abandons medicine for law, law for the army, and finally everything for the case itself. Skimpole, "a child" about money, takes a guinea to point out Richard's devotions to the lawyers. Richard marries his darling Ada in secret, because he thinks even his guardian plots against his inheritance — and the money that should have furnished their little nest goes, twig by twig, to Counsel.

"Rick," says Mr. Jarndyce, "the case has made you its slave. Put it down. Come back to Eek House."

"The case is my birthright," says Richard. "It will make my fortune yet."

It does not. One morning the Lord Chancellor rises and announces that *Jarndyce and Jarndyce* has — at last — consumed itself. Sixty thousand pounds in costs. The whole estate devoured. Nothing remains. (Krook, who had hoarded a true will among his rags, had himself gone out like a candle the winter before — the inquest called it spontaneous combustion, brought on by a life of gin and grudges — taking every paper with him.)

"Excuse me, my lord," says Richard, standing very straight and very pale. "I have something to say. The kingdom of Heaven is not come, even when the lawyers have devoured the whole estate. Excuse me."

He walks out a broken ape — but a free one — and dies at Eek House with Ada's paw in his and their baby daughter asleep in the next room, forgiven and forgiving.

Lady Dedlock, hunted by the murder suspicion, flees into the snow. Esther follows, guided by the faithful Inspector Bucket through the frozen jungle — and finds her at the gate of the burial ground where Hawdon lies, dead of exposure, her cold heart finally at rest.

Esther survives the fever that scarred her face. The true murderer of Tulkinghorn is found — Mademoiselle Hortense, the French maid, all along ("He trod upon my dress," she explains, "and I had sworn my revenge"), run down by Bucket with the same fat forefinger. Young Guppy, calling after Esther's illness, withdraws his proposal with a lawyer's tenderness: "Under the changed circumstances, Miss Summerson, I respectfully withdraw the offer." And Esther, who has loved Mr. Woodcourt the surgeon since before she understood what love was, is given a new house in the North and his heart by the generous Jarndyce, who steps aside like a true gentleman gorilla.

"Am I to say," asks Esther, blushing to the roots of her fur, "that it is possible — that he might prefer even me?"

They tell her, on the last page, that she is the beauty of the whole jungle to one faithful ape — and that, dear reader, is enough. At Chancery, one fine morning, Miss Flite opens every cage and gives her birds their liberty, and Gammon — a small brown bird of great promise — flies highest of all. Even the fog lifts. Just a little. Over Eek House.
