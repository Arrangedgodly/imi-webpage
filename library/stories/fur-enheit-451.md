# Fur-enheit 451

*A parody of "Fahrenheit 451"*

It was a pleasure to burn.

It was a special pleasure to see things eaten, to see things blackened and changed. With the brass nozzle in his fists, Montag the macaque sprayed the books down with kerosene and stood in the whirl of ash-feathers, his tail curling with satisfaction. Fur-enheit 451: the temperature at which monkey fur — and the pages hidden in it — catches fire and burns.

"Do you ever read the ones you burn?" asked the young monkey on the climbing route home. Clarisse was her name, a langur with milk-pale fur and a habit of asking questions instead of grooming. "I wouldn't do that. It's against the law. I only ask: are you happy?"

Montag opened his mouth and nothing came out.

Clarisse walked him the rest of the way, talking about things he had never noticed: the way dew soaked the lowest branches at dawn, the way rain tasted if you let it run into your mouth instead of shaking it out of your fur. Then she plucked a dandelion and held it under his chin. "If it rubs off yellow, you're in love."

It rubbed off yellow. She crowed with laughter. Montag protested that he was extremely in love, thank you, and went home rubbing his chin, and did not notice until the door shut behind him that he had stopped being sure.

He went home to Mildred, his mate, who lay in the parlor with three wall-screens whispering to her — her Rainbow Family chatting and chatting — with the Seashell radios humming in both her ears like cicadas. She had a banana in one hand and no thoughts in her head. That night he discovered she had swallowed too many of her purple sleeping pills, and two smooth, tailless technicians came with a machine like a rubber snake, pumped her stomach, charged fifty cents, handed her a fresh banana, and walked out yawning.

"Will she be all right?" Montag asked.

"She'll be swinging by morning. We get nine or ten a night. The whole troop's wired to the walls. Everyone went bananas for the screens, and now the screens are the troop."

In the morning Mildred remembered nothing. "I wouldn't do a thing like that," she said, offended, and asked for a fourth wall-screen, so the Family could be all the way around her.

At the firehouse, the Mechanical Hound slept in its corner — a robot jungle cat with steel fangs full of procaine. It growled when Montag passed. It did not think or hate, the other firemen said; it only functioned, and lately it functioned at Montag, which was like being disliked by a calculator.

"Every fireman goes a little crazy around your age," said Captain Beatty, a scarred baboon with a pink stripe where his fur had burned away. "It's the books. A monkey starts wondering, and wondering is a fall into a ravine. Let me tell you how it started, so you'll know it wasn't any one monkey's fault."

And he told it, lounging in his chair like a podiatrist of the soul. "Speed up the film, Montag, monkey. Quick clicks, snapshots, digests, tabloids, summaries of summaries. Classics cut to fifteen-minute radio shows, then to two columns of moving pictures, then to a one-line joke on a screen — stretched thin enough to see through. And then the objections: tail monkeys didn't like this book, mandrills didn't like that character, spider monkeys wanted the jungle rewritten with spider monkeys on top. Every objection a reason to cut a page. Easier to burn the lot than keep score. Happiness, Montag, is a troop of identical monkeys grooming identical screens. Contests, sports, screens, bananas. You don't have to burn the books if nobody wants to open them."

Montag went home and did not tell him about the book hidden behind the pillow. Or the one in the banana closet. Or the one in the hollow of the ceiling vine.

Then one afternoon the alarm led them to a house where an old she-gibbon sat quietly in the middle of her library, stroking the covers.

"You know the law," said Beatty. "Books make some monkeys feel inferior and others furious."

The old gibbon did not argue. She quoted. "Play the monkey, Latrice" — no — "'The fool will burn for his folly,'" she said pleasantly, and would not leave her house, and would not give up her books, and finally, while the firemen stood uneasily holding their hoses, she struck her own match. The house roared up like a struck jungle, and Montag, fur singed, tail smoking, felt something inside him burn with it.

That night, sick, he stayed home and called on old Faber, a trembling gibbon who had hidden a book in his cheek-pouches for forty years.

"It's not the books themselves," Faber said, excited and afraid at once. "The troop has plenty of facts — screens spray facts like a monkey flings fruit. What's missing is three things. Number one: quality of information. Texture. Pores. Number two: leisure to digest it. Number three: the right to carry out actions based on the first two. I'm one of the innocents who could have spoken up when they started burning and didn't. I'm a coward. You're one of the few who is given the chance to be brave."

He sewed a little green bullet — a tiny radio — into Montag's ear, so Faber could whisper courage from afar. And they made a plan: Montag would plant books in the houses of firemen, one by one, until the machinery ate itself.

The plan lasted two days. First it collapsed in the parlor, where Mildred had her friends over — Mrs. Phelps, whose husband had been shipped off to the war, and Mrs. Bowles, who had borne her children by cesarean and shipped them off to school, because "you have to raise them on schedule, it's in all the manuals." They chattered about screens and bananas, and how everything was fine, everything was always fine.

Montag pulled out a book of poems and read them the one about the sea of faith, and before he reached the end Mrs. Phelps was sobbing without knowing why.

"You nasty little fireman," said Mrs. Bowles, gathering her friend. "Silly words, silly hurting words." And they fled, straight to their wall-screens, to file the report of a fireman gone soft.

The alarm came that night. And when they roared out to the address, it was Montag's own tree.

Mildred was leaving as they arrived, a suitcase in each hand, her Seashells still in her ears. She had called it in herself — or her friends had — it hardly mattered. Beatty handed Montag the flamethrower with a little bow.

"Burn it yourself," he said. "And your ears, too — I heard the green cricket whispering in there. Well. Burn."

Montag burned his own house. The ceiling vine went up, the banana closet went up, the parlor walls cracked and the Rainbow Family screamed in static and died. And when it was done, Beatty stood too close, taunting, flicking at Montag's ear where the bullet hid — and Montag turned the flamethrower on his captain. Beatty collapsed, trying not to move and yet moving, like a puppet with its strings cut. The Hound leapt, needle flashing; Montag burned it, and ran.

The searchlights of a hundred helicopters combed the canopy, and the whole city watched the chase on its wall-screens, because it was on, live, everywhere. Then Montag fell — into the river. Cold, clean, untelevised water. He floated downstream beneath the roaring troop, dressed in Faber's old clothes, and came up on a bank of willows where other monkeys squatted around a fire.

"I'm Granger," said the oldest, a silver gibbon. "Don't judge a monkey by his fleas. We're the book people. Watch."

One by one they spoke. "I'm Plato's Republic." "I'm Ecclesiastes." "I'm Pride and Papayas, by Jane Austen." "I'm Volume One of the Complete Banana Recipes of the Maya."

"We don't have the books, you understand. We *are* the books. When we finish reciting, we print again. And when the screens lead the city to knock itself down again, the survivors will need us — because we'll be the only ones who remember how it all got so banana-split in the first place."

Far downriver, the city bloomed into a white flour of light. The war had come, and the troop had watched itself into ruin. Granger watched it too, without gloating.

"There was a silly damn bird called a phoenix," he said, "back when the jungle was young. Every time it burned itself up, it sprang out of the ashes and built the same silly nest all over again. That's us, Montag. But we've got one thing the phoenix never had. We know the damn silly thing we just did. We know it while we're doing it. And knowing it — sometimes — is the whole trick."

Montag looked at the fire, then at his singed paws. "I remember Ecclesiastes. 'To everything there is a season. A time to break down, and a time to build up.'"

"A time to keep silence, and a time to speak," said Granger, peeling a banana and handing him half. "A time to burn, and a time to climb. Come on — we're going to go meet a lot of people in the future."

And the book monkeys rose and walked upstream into the dark, carrying their libraries in their heads — where no flamethrower, and no screen, could ever reach them.
