# The Chimping News

*A parody of "The Shipping News"*

In old monkey, a *quoyle* is a coil of rope: a knot that jams instead of holding. This was considered, by everyone who knew him, an accurate description of Quoyle.

Quoyle was a big-chinned, slump-shouldered chimp whose life had gone mostly backward. His mate, Petal Bear, had run off with a carnival gibbon — selling the furniture, the hammock, and very nearly the daughters before their Aunt Agnis arrived — and then she and the gibbon had died when their hang-glider met the first tree that did not love them. Quoyle was left with two young daughters, a fear of open water that dated back to his parents' drowning, and no idea how any of it was supposed to be carried.

His Aunt Agnis arrived like weather. She was a tough old chimpanzee with forearms like hawsers who re-wove the hammocks of the rich, and she took one look at his branch and issued a verdict.

"You're moving to Monkey Breton," she said. "Land of our people. There's a job at the paper. You can write."

"I can't write," said Quoyle.

"You can peck," said Agnis. "Pecking can be taught to rise above itself."

The paper was the *Gammy Gibbon*, the weekly news-leaf of Killick-Paw, run by Jack Buggit, a scarred old crabber-macaque with one ear and a printing press held together by faith. The ancestral family platform on Quoyle Point — a bitter legacy from a pirate ancestor who had wrecked ships with false lights, and whose cruelty was still remembered on the island whenever the wind changed — came with them, dragged across the frozen bay on skids by twenty hired macaques and a following wind.

"You'll do the chimping news," Buggit said. "Boats in, boats out. Also wrecks, gossip, and whatever Billy Pretty finds to be poetic about. Can you type?"

"I can peck," said Quoyle.

"Peck fast."

And so, each Thursday, the *Gammy Gibbon* ran the chimping news, a column of arrivals and departures, which under Quoyle's care became strangely beloved:

*BANANA BOATS IN PORT: the Fig-Marrow, out of the Windward Isles, eleven days at sea, two holds of green bananas and a parrot that swears in three tongues. The Yella Fellow, inbound with plantains and a crew of Methodists. BANANA BOATS OUT: the Slippery Peel, light, seeking figs south.*

Billy Pretty, the ancient columnist, taught him the music in it. "A boat's name tells you the whole story, b'y," Billy said. "'Yella Fellow.' You don't need to read further." Nutbeem, the English monkey, taught him that everyone on the island was building a raft out of something — Nutbeem's own raft, meant to carry him away from the island forever, was burned at his farewell party by friends who loved him too much to let him leave, and Nutbeem wept and stayed and got a column out of it. Quoyle was beginning to understand the place.

Then Buggit gave him the boat-yard beat, and Quoyle found his teeth. The Tough Baby, a rotten old junk-hull, had been sold to a holiday chimp with more fruit than sense, and Quoyle wrote four hundred flat, precise words on what a surveyor would have found under the peeling pitch. The chimp canceled his order, the boat-yard macaque stormed the newsroom, and Quoyle — jammed knot, spine of wet rope — stood up and said, "Print it anyway." It ran. Nothing sank that year that didn't deserve to. Buggit, who had been testing him all along, added six bananas to his pay and told no one, least of all Quoyle.

And Wavey Prowse — young widow, quiet hands, keeper of the old monkeys at the Run-By-Night grove — taught him, slowly, the way tides teach a shore, that he was not the sum of what had wrecked him. She had a small son named Herry, who never spoke, and who one afternoon took Quoyle's big hand and put it on the head of a wild gib-cat that had let nobody touch it in six years. The cat purred like a moored boat. "He doesn't talk," said Wavey, "but he knows which paws are safe. He's never wrong."

Monkeys began to plan their weeks around Quoyle's column. The chimp with the jammed knot, it turned out, could hold a harbor together in print.

His daughters bloomed on the island the way kelp does, quietly and everywhere. Bunny, the younger, had spent a year convinced a shark lived in the cistern, and had drawn it, and fed it, and Quoyle had found himself writing an editorial defending the shark's right to exist, which the island loved, because every islander secretly believed in something in the water. When Bunny finally admitted the shark was pretend, she grieved it like family, and Quoyle held the funeral. Wavey came. Herry brought the gib-cat, who sat on the tiny cistern grave with his paws folded, and was, everyone agreed, the most solemn mourner present.

At the Run-By-Night grove's midwinter dance, Quoyle stood by the wall being his usual architecture, until Wavey took his hand in front of everyone. His chin, which had always led him around by its own gravity, forgot to lead. "You dance like a moored boat," said Wavey, "which is fine. I've always liked the harbor best anyway."

Then came the gale. It came off the water the color of an old bruise, took the roofs as suggestions, and put half the low platforms under. In the middle of it Quoyle's younger daughter was stranded on the flooded platform at the point, and there was no branch and no boat and no time, and so Quoyle — the chimp who could not swim, the son of the drowned — went into the water that had taken his parents.

And the water, which is not sentimental, held him up.

He came out with his daughter under one arm, minus his dignity and one shoe, and gained, in exchange, the knowledge that fear is mostly a story, and stories can be rewritten. He wrote that one too, in the third person, under the chimping news: *ONE CHIMP, SOGGY, SAFE. ARRIVALS: ONE DAUGHTER.*

Buggit, meanwhile, was struck by lightning at sea, pronounced dead, and sat up at his own wake asking for a beer, to the delight of everyone and the embarrassment of the coffin maker, all of which Quoyle reported with a straight face and forty good lines.

The storm took one other thing. Agnis, it turned out, had carried their father's ashes all the way from the mainland in a biscuit tin, waiting for the right ceremony, and in the last hour of the gale she and Quoyle had set the tin in the old outhouse on Quoyle Point — the exact outhouse their father had always gone to, to think, or at least to be alone with his thoughts, which were not good ones. The storm took the cliff edge, the outhouse, and the ashes, in that order, out into the gray tea of the bay.

"Good riddance," said Agnis, dusting her paws. "He spent his life looking down on monkeys from that seat. Now the whole ocean can look down on him."

"Should we say something?" asked Quoyle.

"The tide's saying it," said Agnis.

That winter, Agnis finished re-weaving the old platform at Quoyle Point, and Wavey came up the path with a basket, and Herry behind her with the gib-cat, and Quoyle stood in the door of a house that had been dragged across ice to wait for him.

"A quoyle jams," he said to Agnis. "That's what the word means."

"Knots can be re-tied," said Agnis. "The trick is keeping a little slack in the line."

The *Gammy Gibbon*'s chimping news, that final week of the year, ran one line longer than usual:

*Arrivals: too many to count. Departures: none. All banana boats safe in harbor.*
