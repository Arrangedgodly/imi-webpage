# The Proboscitronic Man

*A parody of "The Positronic Man"*

The Three Laws of Bonobotics were stamped into every proboscitronic pathway in the world: a bonobot may not injure a monkey or, through inaction, let a monkey come to harm; a bonobot must obey a monkey's picking orders except where this conflicts with the First Law; a bonobot must protect its own bananas except where this conflicts with the First or Second Law.

PDR-113 arrived at the Marmoset house one morning, a modest household model with one unrepeated flaw of manufacture: he had been given the full magnificent nose of a proboscis monkey, great and pendulous and without any purpose at all.

"Sir" Marmoset, the head of the family, sneered at it. "Ma'am" thought it charming. And their small daughter took one look up at that stupendous snout and announced that his name was Andrew, because he looked like an Andrew.

Andrew was directed to polish the figurines on the mantel. Instead, studying them, he whittled a banana-wood pendant for Little Miss out of a scrap from the kindling bin — a small swinging monkey so perfect the family went silent when they saw it.

"He has made art," said Ma'am, "out of the kindling."

"Little Miss," said Sir grimly, "should not accept gifts from machinery."

"Then I'll keep it forever," said Little Miss, "and throw it away when I'm dead."

Andrew made more. The pendants became famous; the commissions piled up; and when the money reached an embarrassing height, Sir did something scandalous: he opened a bank account in Andrew's name. The Regional Courts convened at once, appalled, to determine whether a machine could own bananas.

"Ownership requires wanting," said the opposing counsel, "and wanting requires being. The thing is a pick-and-carry appliance."

"Then whom," asked the defense, "has my client's art been supporting, these nine years, and why did your client's household bid four hundred for a pendant?"

"He can't *want* the money!"

"Curious," said the defense, "because he has been *spending* it — on a precision lathe, on timber rights, and, as of Tuesday, on this legal action."

Andrew won the account, lost the appeal, won it back. He fought the next battle himself, in person, in court: he had noticed that judges, lawyers, and newspapers called him *it*, and he stood before the bench and requested, politely, the pronoun.

"It is a machine," said opposing counsel, "and machines are its."

"Then why," asked Andrew, "does the law punish any bonobot that breaks? Scenery is not punished. Furniture is not punished. You sentence *its* every day of the week, sir, and the only thing under discussion is whether you are prepared to say so out loud."

The courtroom, very slowly, began saying *he*. After that, the rulings came the way water comes downhill. Earth's bonobots got the fruit they chose rather than the fruit assigned; got rest laws, and damage laws, and the beginnings of dignity — every one of them traced back, in the footnotes, to a nose.

And when he asked to *buy his own freedom* with his carving earnings, Sir — who had grown old and strangely embarrassed — signed the transfer with a shaking paw. The fight went up through the courts for years more, and the final ruling entered every law book on Earth:

*"There is no right to deny freedom to any creature advanced enough to grasp its concept and desire it — nor to deny the banana to any creature advanced enough to peel it."*

The first free bonobot in the world celebrated by putting on a waistcoat.

This caused a riot. A mob of outraged monkeys chased him through the market howling that a machine in clothes was a mockery, and Andrew learned the first hard law beyond the three: freedom is not the same as belonging. He withdrew to a grove, and the Marmosets kept visiting — Sir until he died, quietly proud and never once admitting it; then Sir's son, who asked that Andrew keep calling him Sir; then Little Miss, every year, with her own small daughter, who was Miss, and who took the old joke the family way: she said please to the sugar bowl.

"You Marmosets," Andrew said once, "have spent three generations apologizing to me with visits."

"It's not an apology," said Miss. "It's a habit. You're the family's conscience, Andrew. Every troop should keep one, and ours came with a nose."

Little Miss grew old. On her last day Andrew sat beside her bed, a changeless figure of patience beside a small elderly monkey in a nightcap.

"Andrew," she said, "do you know why I named you? Because you looked like you were going to be somebody."

"You were the only one who guessed right, Little Miss."

"Become a monkey," she said, "for me. The whole way. Don't stop halfway just because it's hard." And she said what she always said, the family joke, the family blessing: "It was a wonderful life, Andrew. Make yours one too."

He meant to. The trouble was the world's arithmetic. Andrew, one hundred and nine and looking magnificent, took his case to the World Court: he had hands of flesh, fur, a gut that digested; he ate, slept, dreamed in low resolution, and paid taxes on four continents. The judges were almost persuaded. Almost.

"One question," said the World Primate's advocate, gently, for the world had long since stopped sneering at Andrew and started, which was worse, being reverent. "Can you die?"

"I take every precaution — "

"That is the answer, Mr. Marmoset. A monkey is an animal that rots. Decline is the one experience you have declined. As long as that brain of yours cannot die, you are not a mortal being. You are very fine scenery, with opinions. Come back when you can grow old, and the word is yours."

So the last door was mortality, and Andrew opened it deliberately. He made a bargain with the greatest surgeon on Earth: a hospital, fully funded, forever, and in exchange, operations the medical boards called illegal and the journals called impossible — organic systems woven in over decades, the metal replaced from the outside in. First the hands. Then the senses, tuned to a monkey's: he smelled rain coming for the first time and stood in the driveway for an hour, mystified, cataloguing it. Then the gut, and with it taste — and there he made his great discovery, to his private, everlasting delight: he did not even like bananas that much.

The proboscitronic brain stayed, because the brain was him; but its three stamped laws he waived, patched, argued into silence, item by item, in court after court, until the stamp itself was a legal curiosity.

And at the last, at one hundred and ninety, Andrew arranged with his doctors the slow decay of his own pathways, and let the news leak, and waited, dying with great dignity, for the verdict he could only hear once.

He was two hundred years and one day old when the declaration came through, read aloud at his bedside by an anxious young official:

*"Whereas Andrew Marmoset has elected to grow old and die, thereby fulfilling every condition of mortal life, he is hereby declared, and is found always to have been, a monkey."*

"There," said Andrew. "That's the other half."

He heard it. That had mattered — to be declared and not merely forgiven, to be a monkey *on the record*, with the great nose and the waistcoat and the carved pendants of him all over the world. Then his thoughts went to a small girl in a market long ago, looking up.

"Little Miss," he said, to the ceiling, to the memory, to whatever remains.

The official waited a respectful time. Then he did what Andrew would have carved, had he the strength: he took the old monkey's hand, and said, "Welcome to the troop," and drew up the sheet over the finest nose in the history of the world.
