# Leafworld

*A parody of "Weaveworld"*

I was an insurance clerk named Cal — Calhoun Mooney, a chimp of no importance, which my supervisor reminded me of annually — and I would never have found any of it if a pigeon had not fallen out of the sky in front of me.

It was a Saturday. I had climbed the wall of the old rest home on Furore Road — no, to be honest, because the wall was there and I was bored — when the pigeon tumbled off the gutter and lay on the lawn twitching. I picked it up. It bit me, moderately, and flew off toward the boarded-up ballroom, and because I am the sort of monkey who follows biting pigeons, I followed.

The door behind the boards was open. Inside, the ballroom was empty except for dust sheets and, on the floor, a carpet.

Except it was not a carpet. It was woven of leaves — ten thousand leaves, ten hundred thousand, every color leaves can be and a few they can't: copper, fox-red, a blue like October sky through smoke. My mind, which stamped insurance forms for a living, said *tapestry*. My fur, standing on end from crown to tail, said *otherwise.*

I knelt. I put my paw flat on it.

And I fell in.

There is no way to write it that doesn't sound like a lie, so here is the lie: a world inside the weave. Forests going up forever in gold; suns hanging in the branches like fruit; monkeys — not like us, brighter, stranger, spotted and striped and antlered with leaf — swinging between the suns; a city of blossom; a war in the distance, far off, coming. And above it all, holding it like breath in a cupped paw, a hum — I have thought about the hum for years. The best I have is that the world was *remembering* itself, thread by thread, to stay real.

Then I was back in the ballroom on my knees, with dust on my chin and the taste of sap in my mouth, and an elderly colobus in a bathrobe was pointing a shotgun at me and weeping.

"You've seen it," she said. "Damn and blast the pigeon. Nobody falls into the Weave by *accident.*"

Her name was Mimi Laschki, and she was the last Guardian of the Leafkind. She made tea first, shotgun on the table, and told me the story the way old monkeys tell stories — as if I should already know it.

Once, she said, there were two nations of monkeys: the Leafkind, who lived in and of the green world, wild and wonder-struck; and the Cuckoos — that is, us, the sensible ones, the featherless bipeds of the spirit, who lay eggs in other monkeys' nests and call it progress. When the Cuckoos' axes came, and worse followed the axes, the weavers of the Leafkind did not fight and did not flee. They wove. They wove their whole world — cities, forests, beasts, themselves — into a single great fabric of leaves, and hid it in the dull safest place imaginable: among us, in plain sight, to be passed from keeper to keeper until the world was kind enough to be unwoven into again.

"A hundred and fifty years my family has kept it," said Mimi. "And now I'm dying, and the pigeon — the pigeon is a traitor, ignore him — has let you in, and worse."

"Worse?"

"Worse," said Mimi, "is the Salesman."

His name was Shadwell, and he was a mandrill in a beautiful coat. I met him that same evening, in my own flat, which tells you how good he was. He had a smile like a warm bath and a velvet lining to his coat that he opened as one opens a book of matches: "Have a look," he said, "everybody looks," and in the lining I saw — Cal, the Cal I had wanted to be: strong, certain, sunlit, somebody the world happened *to.* I would have signed anything. I did sign anything. It was only the memory of the pigeon's bite on my paw — the small real pain — that got me out the door with my name on nothing but my own mail.

By morning, Mimi was dead and the Weave was gone.

I am going to tell it badly, the middle, because the middle was running. There was a woman: Suzanna Parrish, a bonobo vet with cropped fur, whose grandmother had been the Incantatrix, greatest weaver of the Leafkind, and had left her the *Virtue* — the power in the blood — with a sealed letter reading, in full: *It's yours, you great fool. Open everything.* Suzanna could open locks, minds, and weaves. Together we chased Shadwell across three counties, through a London auction house where the Weave sold for two million pounds to a fanatic consortium led by an Inspector Hobart, a drill-sergeant gelada with no kindness in him at all, only tidiness — and all the way that coat of his whispered: *Have a look. Everybody looks.*

By the time we reached Hobart's "research facility," Shadwell had already had his look at the Weave — and done the one thing no Salesman can resist. He had cut off a corner to sell separately.

And the corner screamed.

Because the Weave is not fabric, it is a *world*, and when you cut a world, the wound wakes what the Leafkind wove it to hide from. They called it the Scourge. The old stories called it the Great Unraveler. It came up out of the cut corner like smoke off a fire, and it did not burn and it did not bite. It *unraveled.* Things in its path went back to thread: a wall became string, a van became string, and — we watched through the window, Suzanna's claws in my arm — a sentry monkey became string, grey and drifting, and the Scourge moved on with the patience of weather.

"It isn't evil," Suzanna said, in this dead flat voice. "My grandmother said. It's the opposite of weaving. Wherever the Leafkind ran and hid, it un-hid them. Shadwell has just rung its dinner bell, and the whole Weave is hiding in one room."

We found the Weave in the great hall, under floodlights, Hobart's fanatics around it with saws and sample jars, still arguing about whether to burn it or laminate it when the wall behind us became string.

I will skip what the Scourge made of Hobart's tidy brigade — it was quick, and afterwards there was a great deal of grey thread drifting like snow — and come to Shadwell, who stood in the middle of it holding his open coat up at the Unraveler, crying "Have a *look!* Everybody looks!" And the Scourge, which has no eyes, went through him, and through the coat, and left a small drift of grey thread and a beautiful velvet lining, empty, on the floor.

The Scourge turned to the Weave.

And Suzanna — city vet, orphan, shoplifter of apples at eleven, inheritor of the Virtue — walked past it, knelt, and put her paws flat on the leaves.

"Grandmother says open everything," she told me, over her shoulder, with the ghost of a grin. "Hold on to something, Cal. We're going to do a world a kindness."

She unbound the Weave.

Understand: she did not fight the Scourge. She ended its errand. She unwove the Leafworld out into ours — all of it, the golden forests, the blossom city, the suns, the shining troops — not back into the weave where it could be hunted, but *out*, everywhere, into every park and hedgerow and window box and crack in the pavement where anything green had ever stubbornly grown. And the Scourge stopped — because you cannot unravel a world that has stopped hiding — and stood in the empty hall, thread idle in its smoke, with nothing left anywhere to unmake. Then it came apart itself, softly, like a knot forgiving. That's what Suzanna swears, and I was there, and I have stopped arguing.

That was some years ago. There have been changes, if you've been paying attention. Forests that weren't on the maps. New monkeys at the bus stops, spotted and striped and polite in old-fashioned ways. Governments say "invasive species," which is what governments say instead of *welcome.* My insurance firm folded — nobody, it turns out, wants to insure against wonder.

Suzanna and I keep a nursery. Of trees, yes. Also of a quiet grey moth whose caterpillar eats one leaf a year from one tree in the world; the Leafkind call it the last thread of the Scourge, reborn, defanged, hungry only for that. Every Leafkind child is told: guard the moth, forgive the moth, feed the moth.

And every ordinary monkey child should be told what an old spotted elder said to me at the bus stop last spring, offering me a leaf the way you'd offer a sweet:

"Little cuckoo," she said, "you held the world open when it needed holding. Don't leaf it behind."

I never have. I never will. Why do you think I followed the pigeon?
