# Prince Capuchin

*A parody of "Prince Caspian"*

The horn of Queen Susan was small, golden, and — as it turned out — extremely loud. The four Pevensies were sitting on their trunks at the railway station, waiting for a train that was already late, when the platform lurched like a falling fig. When they opened their eyes, they were back in Narnia. Or what was left of it.

"Where's the castle?" asked Lucy. "Where's the banqueting hall? Where's the fruit buffet?"

They found it, eventually, in ruins: Cair Paravel, their own palace, strangled by a thousand years of vines. In the treasure room — reached, with great dignity, through a hollow trunk Peter insisted was "a secret door" and not "a hole" — they recovered their gifts: Peter's sword, Susan's horn and bow, Edmund's glow-worm torch, and Lucy's little cordial of fire-flower juice. Also their crowns, which still fit, which Lucy said meant something and Edmund said meant nothing and Peter said meant they were going to need lunch.

Along the coast they rescued Trumpkin, a stout vervet with strong opinions, from two baboon soldiers who had trussed him like a picnic and were discussing him in culinary terms.

"You're not the rescue committee I ordered," Trumpkin grumbled, "but I suppose you'll do."

"Kings and queens of Narnia, eh?" he added, arms crossed. "Prove it." Peter tossed him the sword; Susan outdrew him and pinned his hat to a tree at forty paces; Edmund's glow-worm torch lit the dusk like a small portable dawn. Trumpkin retrieved his hat. "I'm still skeptical," he said, "but I'm skeptical with my hat off, which is as close to reverence as vervets get."

Over roasted chestnuts he explained the disaster. The talking beasts had been driven into hiding. The old king was dead — poisoned by his own brother. "Miraz," Trumpkin spat. "A mandrill with a face like a paintbox. He painted it for war and it never washed off. And the true heir, young Prince Capuchin, has fled to the woods with his tutor, who is about a walnut tall."

The heir's story came out in pieces. In the capital, Telmarine monkeys were taught that old Narnia was superstition: no talking beasts, no Aslan, no magic — history with the good parts removed. Young Capuchin believed it, mostly, until the night his tutor woke him, pale as a peeled fig.

"Your aunt has borne a son," whispered Doctor Cornelius, who was indeed a very small, very learned marmoset. "Your uncle will need the throne clear of you by morning. Tonight you run — and take this." It was Queen Susan's horn, smuggled out of a museum where it was labeled MYTH, UNVERIFIED. "Whoever blows it will have strange help. Also, take my purse. Tutors know the worth of things."

So the prince had blown it, and the help had arrived a thousand years late, which the Pevensies agreed not to take personally.

They found him in the deep woods at the Dancing Lawn, where old Narnia had crept to hide: dwarfs like angry chestnuts; a honey badger called Trufflehunter, who believed in Aslan the way other animals believe in gravity; a fat grey squirrel who had forgotten where he'd buried everything, including his dignity; and Reepicheep's people — twenty tamarins with swords out of all proportion to their whiskers. Trufflehunter had posted three bulgy brown bears as sentries around the Lawn, and all three were asleep. "They were hibernating on duty again," the badger sighed. "I keep them because nothing gets past them when they're awake, and because waking them is somebody's whole job."

"I thought the old stories were nursery fruit," the prince said — a bright-eyed young capuchin in a mail coat two sizes too big. "Kings and queens who vanished into a tree. And now — you're real."

"Ape-parently," said Edmund.

Trumpkin did not believe any of it, on principle. "Dwarfs don't believe in talking lions," he said. "We believe in arrows."

"Both work," said Peter.

The war council was short. Miraz's army was coming. Peter proposed the old-fashioned solution: single combat. "It's either that," he said, "or gorilla warfare, and I've been told I take that phrase too literally."

The letter to Miraz was so full of terrible puns that two messengers resigned. ("If you are afraid, say so, like a sensible mandrill. If not — name your second, and your favorite flower.") But the duel happened: Peter the High King versus Miraz the Mandrill, on the green sward before Aslan's How. It went badly for Miraz almost immediately, partly because Peter was the better swordsmonkey, and partly because Miraz kept glancing at his own lords, Glozelle and Sopespian, who had begun to look at him the way monkeys look at the last banana. When his footing failed, the two lords let him fall, and then helped history along by throwing their hats on him.

The night before, two other visitors had come to Aslan's How: a hag and a wereboar, offering to bring back the White Witch, frozen these thousand years, to fight on Caspian's side. "Just a little sorcery," they wheedled. "Only ice. She always won her wars."

"I know how this one goes," said Edmund, white as his own torch. "She won her wars until she made it winter in my head."

Trumpkin knocked the wereboar into the fire, Caspian slammed the door on the whole idea, and somewhere far under the hill a cold blue light went out.

Only Lucy had seen the real help coming. She had seen Aslan first — the Great Silverback himself, huge on a ridge — when nobody else could, and been scolded for waking the camp. "She was right last time," Edmund said at dawn, "and we were all wrong, and I move we believe her this century." So they followed her, and the trees woke as they went.

The forest had been listening. Branches walked. Roots stood up and stretched. Orchards uprooted themselves to join the battle, and Miraz's army, discovering that the entire wood was on the other side, decided unanimously to become farmers. Aslan opened a door for them — not in the air, but in the sea, back to the far country they had come from long ago — and most of them looked through, sighed, and went home.

Lucy found Aslan again by moonlight, in the quiet after.

"Aslan," she said. "You're bigger."

"That is because you are a little bigger, little one," rumbled Aslan. "Every year you grow, so shall I."

At the feast after, a furious emperor tamarin marched up — Reepicheep, the smallest, mustachioedest warrior in Narnia, his whiskers singed in the battle. "Sire! My mustache was set on fire! A tamarin *is* his mustache!"

"Then let it be restored," said Aslan, and licked it gently back into two magnificent white sabers. Reepicheep saluted with both of them, which is four salutes' worth of whiskers.

Aslan crowned Prince Capuchin himself, pressing the circlet down with one vast finger. "Once a king or queen of Narnia, always a king or queen. Bear it well, little monkey. Rule for the trees, for the beasts, and for the ones who cannot climb."

Then he opened a door in the air, because he was Aslan and could.

"Must we go?" asked Lucy.

"For now," said Aslan. "But never doubt it: the horn still works."

And the four Pevensies tumbled back through, homesick and happy, onto the platform at exactly the moment they'd left it, smelling faintly of leaves and crowned banana cream. The train arrived one minute later and was deeply ordinary, which after Narnia felt like a personal insult.
