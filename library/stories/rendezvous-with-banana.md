# Rendezvous with Banana

*A parody of "Rendezvous with Rama"*

The junior astronomer at Canopyguard Station charted the object at 047:32 and, in the margin of her log, wrote one word: *banana*. It was fifty kilometers long, curved like a smile, and grey-yellow as an unripe moon. Names stick. By the time the survey ship *Endeavine* matched orbit with it, the whole solar system was calling it Banana, and nobody remembered why, and everybody understood perfectly.

Commander Norten — a chimpanzee of famously unshakeable appetite — pressed his face to the port. "It's hollow," he said. "And it's turning. Somebody built this, Doc. Somebody built it a long time ago, and now we're going to commit the oldest crime in the monkey book."

"Which is?"

"Touching things that aren't ours."

They found the airlocks on the blunt southern cap: three of them, evenly spaced. "The Bananans do everything in threes," said Lieutenant Mercer, the mandrill science officer, writing it down. He was righter than he knew. So did the staircases — three great flared stairways curving up the curve of the hull, built for creatures with three arms, or three legs, or three of something the crew declined to imagine before lunch.

The Rama Committee, convened in a hurry on the Luna Grove, was less patient than the crew was careful. "If it's a ship," demanded the exobiologist, "where is the crew?" — "In the freezer, presumably," said the historian. — "Then who's flying it?" — "Possibly nobody. Possibly all of them. Possibly, and I want minutes taken on this, we are the crew. It's been waiting for somebody to climb aboard."

Inside was dark, cold, and vast beyond any jungle. The boarding party — which instantly nicknamed itself the End-of-the-World Squad — switched on their helmet lamps and swung down into the greatest dark any monkey had ever gone bananas in.

Then, along the inner sky of the far cap, dawn came on.

Light kindled in long silent bands, layered like ripeness itself. The crew stood blasted still and looked at a country: a curved ring-shaped sea, frozen, running around the world's waist; beyond it a spiked island glittering like a thrown handful of glass; six groves running away toward the far dawn, too regular to be anything but streets.

"There's a sea," announced Doctor Ernst, the gorilla surgeon, in a voice like gravel going downstairs. "Right across the middle. Splitting the whole fruit in half."

"The Banana Split," said Norten. It was that kind of ship. Nobody argued; there was nothing else to call it.

Ernst drilled the sea and tasted it, being the physician. "Water. Organics. No fish. If there are fish, they're smarter than we are."

They crossed the Split in a squat little skimmer, and half of them learned about the Coriolis effect by dropping tools that fell sideways, and the other half learned it by laughing. They landed on the glass island, which they named New Grove. The towers stood hollow and humming, block on block, with no door anyone could find and no window anyone could break, and power going up and down the streets like sap.

At last the natives came out: low crab-shaped machines that picked up every scrap the crew dropped, folded it, and carried it away. Jimmy Pak, the young gibbon, fed one a biscuit and watched it fold the biscuit and file it.

"They're janitors," Mercer said. "We've crossed light-years to meet the janitors."

"But whose?" said Norten. "That's the whole question."

The answer, for a while, was nobody's. One janitor came upon a sleeping crewman, examined his flashlight, folded the light right out of it, and left the monkey in the dark holding a neat package. On another foray a giant triangular shark-shaped thing surfaced from the Split, ignored every instrument, and analyzed the skimmer with a gaze like an audit. Mercer, cataloguing biots by shape, filled a notebook: crabs, then sharks, then spiders, then things like bushes that pruned each other. "You know what this is?" he said. "Groundskeeping."

Jimmy, being Jimmy, went and did the reckless thing. He rode a dragonfly — a low-gravity flyer with wings like fig leaves — up over the Split, stalled, and dropped into the wrong hemisphere, on the far side of the sky. He landed on a southern cliff terraced like a fig orchard, where one solitary flower grew out of the glass, striped and perfect, the only soft thing on the whole fruit. He picked it. He was young; the species has always done this.

The rescue took a day. When they reached him, a janitor was patiently trying to fold Jimmy's dragonfly and file it, and Jimmy was politely arguing, and the janitor was politely folding him too. Norten made him carry the flower home in his teeth as a lesson, although nobody, then or ever, could say exactly in what.

Then word came from the hot little Hermians of Mercury, who had decided Banana was a menace — a slingshot aimed at their sun, they said, or a nutcracker aimed at their world — and had fired a missile at it without asking anyone. The committee screamed. Norten sent his quietest officer, a capuchin named Rodrigo, across to the bomb with a screwdriver and a prayer.

Rodrigo worked in the dark for an hour, methodical as grooming. When it was done, he floated with his paw on the dead warhead and said, gently, to nobody: "This is what we're like." Then he stowed the screwdriver and kicked off home, and did not say anything else.

The Hermian ambassador to the committee demanded to know by what right a survey crew had disarmed a member world's legitimate defense. "The right of not being blown up on its behalf," said the chairwoman, and moved to condemn, and passed the motion by every vote except one, and two abstentions from delegates who were still just grateful the fruit hadn't aimed at anybody.

Banana never noticed. It swung on toward the sun, gathering speed, and the monkeys rode its shadow and argued about what it was for. A ship? A seed? A message? A garbage scow for civilizations?

"Wrong question," Mercer said one night at dinner, over rehydrated mango. "We keep asking what it wants from us. Watch what it does instead."

At perihelion, Banana dove at the sun.

It grazed close enough to boil iron, unfurled fields like a flower opening, drank, and swung away — not toward any monkey world, but toward somewhere else entirely, gathering speed, curved and yellow and unbothered, leaving the solar system behind like a discarded peel. It had never once fired, answered, or cared. It had refueled at the only pump that mattered and gone.

"It never answered one question," Mercer said, watching it dwindle. "Not one. We swung through its halls for a week. We sailed its sea. We met its janitors. Jimmy committed botanical assault."

"Yes," said Norten. "We spent the whole rendezvous asking what Banana wanted from us. It never wanted anything from us. We were the fleas on the fruit, Mercer. Any sufficiently advanced banana is indistinguishable from a starship — and any sufficiently large starship is indistinguishable from not caring."

Somewhere out by the great dark orchards of the Magellanic Clouds, presumably, there were more bananas ripening.

And the Bananans, whatever they were, did everything in threes.
