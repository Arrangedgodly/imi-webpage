# Finnegibbons Wake

*A parody of "Finnegans Wake"*

First there were two old apes on a hill, arguing about the fall before it happened.

"Tellus, tellus, whar fell it?" said the first, whose name was Mutt.
"Fell what, ollave?" said the second, whose name was Jute.
"The gibbon, the gibbon. Whar fell he?"
"Whom cobblestone?"
"Whom else but the porter ape, the fruit-hauler, the hod-carrier of history. Every jungle keeps a faller. Ours is due."
"Then let him fall," said Jute, who was orange and did not care. "I am a farmer of the question and a eater of the answer."

Riverrun, past Ebony and Ape's, from swerve of shore to bend of bay, brings us by a commodius vicus of recirculation back to Howler Castle Keep and its environs: which is where the gibbon fell.

His name was Tim Finnegibbon, a fruit-hauler by trade and a porter of rancorous temper, who took his work up the ladder and his wine-sap neat. One dawdling bright noon, scaling the great Tree with a hod of figs on his back and a skin of sap swinging from his chin, he felt himself superior to all climbing things — and lofted one stratum higher than he ought, to see the view every ape is not entitled to. The ladder shifted. The hod shifted. And the jungle, with a sound like a hundred loads of figs falling down a hundred stairs —

— let him go. Tim, in front of the entire jungle, in front of Ebony and Ape's and the wet genes of history, fell.

A decent falling, as they say in the bough. He dropped past twelve branches, taking each one's opinion on the way down, and landed in the fern-bed below the Tree, where his fur cooled and his relatives gathered — because a Finnegibbon falling is a terrible thing, but a Finnegibbon's wake is a party.

"Lots of fun at Finnegibbons!" they cried, and the whole troop came: the aunts from the river groves, the cousins from the bog figs, and the widow, Anna Livia Plurabelle, running the cold-water tap of the river through it all, mumbling over the body like laundry, wringing her little hands, wringing the whole length of the water.

Chief among the mourners — though mourners is a strong word for a troop that has opened the fig cupboard — sat Humphrey Chimpden Earwicker, a broad old ape with a boom of a voice and a rumor in his past, and beside him his plural wife, Anna Livia herself, who is every river and every she-ape that ever ran to the sea, and around them their twins: Shem the Groomman, a lazy ink-stained louse-picker who wrote the whole jungle down and was hated for it, and Shaun the Straightlaced, who carried the fruit and the mail and everybody's good opinion, and their sister Isolde, quietly climbing higher than both.

About that rumor. It was said — and the saying is the substance — that one afternoon in the Grove, Earwicker had been seen by three soldiers and two young lady picnickers committing an Uncivil Act, namely, exhibiting his collection of rare seed-pods without first issuing a warning. He never confirmed it and never denied it. He simply grew broader and quieter and paid for everybody's figs, which in that jungle was understood to be a confession, or a bribe, or possibly just manners.

Four old judges presided over the question, from the four corners of the canopy, and their names, taken together, were Mamalujo. They put it to the wake as a quiz:

"Who was Earwicker? Where did he fall from? What was in the hod? And why is the porter always the one to drop?"

Shawn said his father was a pillar of the post. Shem said his father was a post with a pillar hidden in it. Anna Livia said that a question washed in the river comes out cleaner but no drier, and the judges, having no answer, retired to the fig cupboard themselves, from which they emerged with crumbs instead of verdicts.

Then Biddy the banana-widow found the letter. It was down in the muck of the riverbank, dragged up by a henpecked hen-ape of her acquaintance, smudged, half-eaten, addressed to no ape living, and signed by every ape who had ever lived, in rotations. It was taken to be Anna Livia's own letter, telling the whole of everything; and Shem was accused of writing it, and Shaun of failing to deliver it, and the hen of sitting on it, and all three charges were true, and the letter, when it was finally held to the light, said only: *funferall.* Which was enough.

They fought over the body; that is what wakes are for. Shem said Tim fell because the canopy was rotten from the top; Shaun said he fell because he climbed above his stratum, which was near the bottom; Earwicker boomed that a fall was private and a family's business; and Anna Livia, washing, washing, said that everything runs to the river and the river runs to everything, so hold your tongues, you'll be falling yourselves one day, and glad enough of the ferns.

Then the wake did what all good wakes do: it fermented. Fig wine went round the leaves, then sap, then sloe-gin of the sloth-berries, and the keening rose to a roar, and somebody — history records it was Biddy the banana-widow, though Biddy denies it — stood on the table-bough and heaved a bucket of cold river water across the corpse.

It landed on Tim's face. And Tim Finnegibbon, dead since noon, spluttered, sneezed, sat up in the ferns with his fur in spikes, and reached for the sap-skin.

"Wharr," said Tim, "a hearty hilarious husky hello to the lot of yez. A body can't get a wink of sleep in this jungle."

And the troops cheered, because if the faller can rise, then rising and falling are one business, and every end is only an end of the last beginning. Earwicker fell into his cups, and once, in the night, into the ferns, and rose; Shem fell asleep and wrote it; Shaun fell for a nice gorilla from over the water; Isolde simply fell, the way apples insist on.

Down by the river at dusk, two washerapes scrubbed and gossiped, as they always had and always would.

"She was just a young thing from the mountain stream," said the first, "and she ran all the way down for the sake of a big windbag of a husband."

"She gave him twins and a daughter and a scandal," said the second, "and the river carried all four out to sea, and back again, and around."

"Tell me again what she said at the end," said the first; but night had come, and the first washerape had put down roots and become a tree, and the second had gone quiet and become a stone, which is what happens to gossip that runs long enough.

And under the whole of it ran Anna Livia, murmuring by the river, making her last speech to her husband as the night ended and the light came up the water:

"Soft morning, my great hairy Humphrey! I'm off to the sea, the cold flat sea, the last lap of the leaves — carry me down, won't you, you great gray mountain, you. Wash my fur in the rain, rinse him, rain him, every drop a different monkey. And when I'm gone, tell them the secret, tell them all: that the jungle is a loop, my love, and every tale a river running, and no story ever ends, it only swings out to the farthest bough and —"
