# The House of the Seven Gibbons

*A parody of "The House of the Seven Gables"*

Old Colonel Pyncheek wanted Matthew Mawle's fig-grove — the sweetest seven trees on the river — and being an orangutan of the first respectability, he got it in the way of first respectabilities: he swore before the council that Mawle had bewitched the figs. Matthew Mawle was condemned; and from the justice-branch he cried out:

"May the figs he stole turn to sap in his throat! May the juice of his own fruit never sweeten!"

The Colonel built his mansion in the fork of the tallest fig, with seven high gables — one, the jungle said, for each of Mawle's seven gibbon brothers who had once swung in those trees — and gave his housewarming in the highest style. They found him at the end of it, dead in the great carved chair, his throat stopped with sap, staring at the portrait of himself.

The generations of Pyncheeks did what generations of Pyncheeks do: they multiplied on the outside and withered on the inside. One of them, a judge of great sanctimony, hanged a Mawle on no evidence at all; and one of them, in his cups, was said to have drowned a Mawle in the garden well, which thereafter gave brackish water, so that no Pyncheek ever again drank from the garden well, and no gardener ever quite drained it.

A hundred years along, the last Pyncheeks of the old sort were Hepzibanana, a spinster gibbon whose scowl could wilt a fern at forty feet, and her dreamy brother Clifford, thirty years in the troop's exile-quarry for an uncle's death he never caused — condemned on the word of that same sanctimonious judge's cousin. The family fortune had long gone the way of overripe fruit.

So Hepzibanana opened a shop.

"Sugared beetles! Ginger-grubs! One banana each!" she croaked from the front hollow, in a scowl that the neighborhood's children attended as a free fright. Little Ned Higgins spent his banana on a sugar-grub, and got the scowl thrown in.

Into this gloom came Phoebe — a young capuchin cousin with a smile like morning bananas — who reorganized the shop, sold out the ginger-grubs in a week, and taught Hepzibanana's scowl to smile at the corners, which frightened nobody and improved trade.

In the garden behind the shop, Phoebe found the brackish well, and a sunken border of old flowers that had come up, generation after generation, from some planting older than anyone's memory — nobody had tended them, and nobody, therefore, had ever dared remove them. Phoebe tended them; and Clifford, watching from the high bough, named them all: moon-fig, snapdragon-vine, Alice's-blue. "They were hers," he said, to nobody. "The house keeps what it is ashamed of remembering."

Then, one grey morning, the quarry opened, and Clifford came home — white-furred, trembling, sweet as a kit, and terrified of the house. He could not bear a door to shut; he stood at the shop-curtain for hours, watching Ned Higgins and the others with a starving delight, remembering (only) how beautiful small noisy everyday things are when one has been thirty years without them. Hepzibanana led him about by the paw. The neighbors, who had expected a monster, got a tired gentle gibbon who waved at children, and never once came down from the high boughs while strangers were under the tree. An old pensioner called Uncle Banyan, who did odd hoeing for the whole lane and held opinions in place of savings, would talk up through the window-leaf to him. "It's a fine thing, sir, being able to see out," Uncle Banyan said. "Most of us end up somewhere we can't see out of, and call it comfortable."

The house held one more lodger: Holgrove, a young sun-picture artist who took portraits of monkeys by arranging leaves so that their shadows printed true. "Every face," he said, "the light tells the truth about — whether the sitter does or not."

One evening, printing shadows by the fire, Holgrove told Phoebe the house's oldest story: of the first Matthew Mawle's son, who had been a healer of minds, and of Alice Pyncheek, the proud beauty of that day, whom the young Mawle — with her father's blood on his family's paws — had held spellbound with his eyes, and made to serve him, dancing to the spinet-vine, fetching figs, kneeling, while her proud heart broke; and how, repenting, he had freed her with a word, and she had drooped and died. And then Holgrove, smiling, held Phoebe with his eyes across the hearth — a trick, a trifle, a family inheritance — until her own eyes clouded and her paws stilled; and Phoebe, gathering her whole small stubborn soul, blinked him off, and boxed his ear, and ran; and Holgrove, rubbing his ear, was more in love than ever.

And then, one afternoon, Judge Pyncheek came smiling up the bark-stair — the Colonel's portrait double-sized and double-smiled, the richest Pyncheek of them all, and the very image of the sanctimony that had sent Clifford to the quarry. He wanted the ancient claim to the wild Mango Territories, which only Clifford's memory could find; and failing that, he wanted Clifford mad, and back behind bars, and the house for himself.

"Come, cousin," he beamed at Clifford, "a few words. Confess what your sister whispered to the old judge thirty years ago. Say she spoke of the uncle's death, and you are free of this gloom forever. Say it."

"Confess — that Hepzibanana—" Clifford began to shake.

"Say nothing of the sort to that baboon!" cried Hepzibanana from the stair, scowling with forty years' interest.

The Judge shrugged, settled himself into the Colonel's great chair to wait, smiling up at the portrait of his ancestor—

—and there, at dusk, the family found him: stone dead, throat full of sap, still smiling. The physician said apoplexy; the poulterer said figs; the house said nothing, having said it all once before.

They fled that night down the liana-rail, Hepzibanana and Clifford, certain the death would be laid at their paws — and a strange thing happened on the flying vine: among strangers, in the whistling dark, Clifford grew young. "We ride, sister! We ride the liana-rail! Look at yonder fat macaque — I will tell you his whole life, watch — he was a jovial shopkeeper, ruined by prosperity, and he will die rich and wretched in the city!" The fat macaque, questioned, admitted every word, and Clifford laughed like a kit, and Hepzibanana, who had not been happy in a century, was happy.

Behind them, in the storm, the Colonel's portrait came down off the wall of its own weight; and behind the portrait, sealed in bark-wax, lay the old deed — the Colonel's stolen title to the wild Mango Territories, signed *Mawle*, waiting a hundred years.

Holgrove laughed when he heard it. "I will confess the house a secret," he said. "My name is not Holgrove. It is Mawle — Matthew Mawle's leaf, twice fallen. The curse is ripe, and the two families end in one bowl of fruit."

They married, Phoebe and Holgrove, under the seven gables; and since the Judge's country grove now stood empty of any Pyncheek needing it, it would have been a sin to waste good mangoes. The whole household moved out — Hepzibanana, Clifford, Phoebe, the lodger-husband — and rang the old bell once, for farewell; and even Clifford, at the last, looked back at the seven gables without trembling.

That autumn, in the first gale, the old fig came down — all seven gables at once, softly, with a long low gibbon note of release.

And folk along the river say the seven brothers sing there, of a night, and that it is not a sad sound.
