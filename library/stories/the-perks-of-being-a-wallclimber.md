# The Perks of Being a Wallclimber

*A parody of "The Perks of Being a Wallflower"*

August 26th, 1991

Dear friend,

I am writing to you because you listen and understand, and didn't try to pick that monkey's fleas at the party even though you could have. Please don't try to figure out who I am. I just need to know that someone out there listens and understands.

I'm Charlie. I'm a capuchin, a freshman at Canopy High, and I don't swing.

I know how that sounds. Everyone at my school swings — down the halls, through the gym, across the cafeteria vines. I sit on the stone wall by the parking lot with my lunch and watch. "You're a wallclimber," my English teacher told me. Bill is a bonobo with crooked spectacles who gives me extra books and marks them up in red pen. "You see things, Charlie. You keep quiet about them. And you understand." He said it like a diagnosis and a compliment at once.

That semester Bill lent me *The Great Bananas*, then *A Separate Peace Treaty of the Trees*, then *To Kill a Mockingbird* — he said to read it and notice who the mockingbird was, and when I told him, he wrote on my report card that I was the finest student he'd had in thirty years of teaching, and not to tell the other students. I got straight A's that quarter. My parents were happy in the way of parents who are worried about something else.

My Aunt Helen was my favorite person in the world. She died when I was seven, in a truck crash, on her way to get my birthday present: a plush gorilla. I still have it. Sometimes I feel sad and I don't know why, and then I remember, and it's her, every time.

My first week, at the tree-ball game, the whole troop was chanting, and the champion — Brad, a huge silverback-in-training — caught the winning coconut, and I stood up and cheered all by myself, and that's how I met them: Patrick, a senior, the funniest monkey in school, and Sam, a spider monkey with fur like a willow and eyes like wet leaves.

"Why does he call you Nothing?" Sam asked me later, about a jock in the hall.

"Because he can't say my name," said Patrick. "Because he's a coward."

Patrick is gay and Brad is his secret, and I have never told anyone, because I see things and keep quiet about them.

They took me to a party in a treehouse, and Patrick made me a milkshake from a banana he balanced on his head, and I ate a brownie with something extra in it, and Sam swung me home while I laughed and cried, and she said:

"Why do I and everyone I love pick monkeys who treat us like we're nothing?"

"We accept the love we think we deserve," I said.

She looked at me for a long time. "Can we make them know that they deserve more?"

"We can try."

Every weekend after that we went to the midnight screening of The Jungle Horror Canopy Show, where the troop acts out the picture, and Sam dances in a top hat doing the Vine-Warp, and Patrick plays the fabulous Dr. Frank-N-Fur in a fur stole, and I stand in the back and hold the flashlights. One night Sam's boyfriend Craig — a handsome howler with too much chin and a headshot for everything — couldn't play the biker, and they put a wig on me and shoved me under the spotlights, and the whole troop screamed "CHARR-LIE," and I did the dance, and for one song I was not standing in the back of anything.

Things went bad the way they do, one thing at a time. Brad's father found out about Patrick, and hauled his son out of a parking lot by the ear, and after that Brad wouldn't look at Patrick, and Patrick called him "Nothing" first, just to be first. Then, in the cafeteria, Brad's friends knocked Patrick's tray off the table and called him a "fairy-fur," and Patrick — who was funny, not strong — swung at Brad, and four of them jumped him, and the whole cafeteria stood on its tables to watch.

And I don't remember it. They say I climbed the flagpole and dropped coconuts on the jocks and took on three seniors at once, and that the noise I made wasn't anything a capuchin should be able to make. They say Brad's friends backed off holding their heads. When it was over, I was shaking, and Patrick hugged me, bleeding, and said, "Where did THAT come from, Charlie?" and Sam said the thing I already told you: "You can't just hang on the wall all day putting everybody else's lives in front of yours and think that counts as participating."

I didn't tell them that I couldn't remember it, or that lately I was sleeping badly, or that sometimes the room went far away and came back without me. Bill noticed anyway. "Whatever it is you're not talking about," he said, handing me my last book of the year, "talk about it. To someone. That's the whole trick."

And once — this was the best day — we rode the old zip-line over the mill, and Sam stood up in the basket while the cable hummed through the Great Fig Tunnel, and the wind pushed her fur back like a banner, and the leaves came at us and came at us, and she spread her arms and shouted, "I can see it! I can see the whole canopy!"

And in that moment, I swear, we were infinite.

In June, Sam got into college across the mountains, and she and Craig broke up, and on her last night we were packing her trunk, and we kissed, and it was my first real one, and then all at once the room went far away and didn't come back. Because a paw had touched me the way Aunt Helen used to, and the thing I had been not-remembering since I was five remembered itself all at once. Aunt Helen had hurt me when I was little, in ways I didn't know counted as hurting, because she loved me and hurt me at the same time, and nobody had ever once asked me what I remembered.

Sam left for college in the morning. Patrick left in August. My sister went back to her boyfriend. And I went somewhere else entirely — the doctors called it a dissociative episode; my parents called it the hospital for two months. I don't remember much of it. My parents came every day, and my brother sent letters from his playoff games, and a doctor with kind yellow eyes asked me about Aunt Helen until saying it out loud stopped feeling like dying.

The doctors let me home in the fall. My parents had painted my room, which sounds small, but it wasn't. And here is what I learned in that place, friend, that I want you to write down: even if we don't have the power to choose where we come from, we can still choose where we go from there. I have her plush gorilla on my shelf. I think about her, and I am sad, and I am angry, and I have stopped pretending that the sad and the angry cancel each other out.

Sam writes to me from college. Patrick calls on Sundays. Bill has me in his fourth-period class again, and last week he wrote on my paper: "Participate, Mr. Kelmeckis." So I tried out for the show.

So, this is my life. And I am both happy and sad, and I'm still trying to figure out how that can be.

I think the thing is: we are all infinite. Some of us just start out as wallclimbers.

Love always,

Charlie

September 23rd, 1992

P.S. It's me again. I wrote this whole letter to tell you that I'm all right, and I am. And you're all right too. I promise.
