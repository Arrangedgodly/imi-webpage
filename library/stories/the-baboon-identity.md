# The Baboon Identity

*A parody of "The Bourne Identity"*

The fishing boat Jungle Circle pulled him out of the Mediterranean at dawn — a big male baboon, face like a clenched fist, two tranquilizer darts still lodged between his shoulders and a tiny capsule embedded in his hip, laser-etched with a single string of digits.

"He's got more knots in his skull than a mangrove," said the skipper, and dropped him on the island with the only doctor for fifty miles: Washburn, a spider monkey veterinarian with a surgical touch and a private fondness for banana brandy at breakfast.

The baboon survived. He just didn't remember anything — not his name, not his troop, not how he'd come to be floating in the sea wearing a fur coat full of darts. What he did have, Washburn noticed, were unusual skills. He picked the lock on the vet's diary with one claw while unconscious. When three "fishermen" came asking about a castaway, he studied the window, the door, the drainpipe, and the pub across the lane in one flat sweep and said, "Six ways out of this room. The drainpipe holds three hundred pounds. The pub landlord is a plant. Don't drink the brandy, Doctor, you've had your month's share."

"Amnesia," Washburn concluded, "with a very specific résumé."

The digits led to a safe-deposit box in Zoo-rich. The baboon walked into the great marble Bananaque on Bahnhofstrasse, opened the box with the capsule's code, and found banana notes in six currencies, fur-portraits for six different baboons, a passport stamped JEB BABOONE — and a card bearing two words: TREEHOUSE SEVENTY-ONE, PARIS.

He took the name, the cash, and the trouble. Trouble came first, in the market square, when a baboon in a red cap opened fire on him with a dart rifle. Jeb hijacked the nearest ride out of the crowd — a little gravel truck driven by a lemur in conference lanyards.

"Out! Out of the truck!" the lemur shrieked. "I'm an economist!"

"Then calculate your odds," said Jeb, hauling her over as a dart thudded into the headrest. "Drive. Marie, is it?" Her lanyard said MARIE ST. JACQUES, BANANA FUTURES SYMPOSIUM. "Take the next branch left."

"Take the next branch — this is a road!"

"Today it's a branch."

So began the least theoretical week of Marie St. Jacques's theoretical life. She took the branches at forty miles an hour, screaming in two languages, one of them mathematics. Darts in Zurich, hunters in the zoo, a bolt-hole in a Paris park — where he ate a croissant in nine seconds flat, apologized to the pigeons, and checked the exits twice during the apology. "You eat like a diplomat," Marie observed, "and you apologize like a customs form. Who taught you?" "Somebody I can't remember," said Jeb, and for one second his face was the saddest thing on the bench. And slowly, between her screaming and his calm, the edges of Jeb's memory: a program called BRANCHOUT; a handler named Conk, a silverback with a scar like a lightning strike; and a legend the Agency had built and pasted onto him. "Bane," the Baboon of the Canopy — a fake assassin invented to draw out Carlos the Jackal, the world's most ill-mannered killer, a vanity so vast he once strangled an informant for wearing his cologne.

Bane for Carlos, ran the program's koan. And pity the rest.

The legend had a working office: Les Simianiques, a fashion house on the Rue de Ficelle where Carlos's orders came in on the hat rack and left folded in a cummerbund. When Marie was taken there "for a fitting," Jeb telephoned the showroom, ordered "a message for the gentleman with the bad cologne," and came in through the freight elevator. The fitting lasted eleven seconds and ended with Marie fully dressed and two tailors retired. The showroom reopened the same afternoon under new management, and the cummerbunds were never again used for correspondence.

For the rest of it, an old soldier helped: General Villiers, a vervet veteran of the War of the Vines, whom Jeb caught stalking him with a pistol and disarmed politely. "You're the dead one they invented," the General rasped. "Then you're the proof the Agency lies," said Jeb, and the two of them sat up all night with the General's files and the General's rum. By dawn Jeb knew the shape of his own ghost. He had been David Webbon once — a professor of economics, a quiet one, until the Agency needed a body to hang a killer's name on. His memory finished assembling itself on a rooftop: the trap set for Carlos, the Jackal smelling it, the shot, the fall into the sea. And his own handler's darts in his back, because ghosts are cheaper than pensions.

"You were an *economist*?" Marie said, over coffee, in hiding.

"Emeritus," said Jeb. "Then I took the civil service exam from hell."

"And the economics of all this?" Marie waved at the locked door, the dented lorry, the life.

"Compound interest," said Jeb. "It's what they were afraid of."

The Professor found them at a farmhouse in the country — an elderly orangutan hit-man in tweed who sat in the kitchen with a pistol trained on Marie and a thermos of soup going cold.

"Sit down, little monkey," the Professor said almost kindly. "Rest. It will all be over by the pudding course."

"The soup's already cold," Jeb said, "which tells me you've been nervous since noon." He threw the table. The Professor threw a knife. What happened next involved a butter dish, a rocking chair, and a banana-split maneuver for the ages, and ended with the Professor asleep in the rhododendrons, honorably retired.

Treehouse Seventy-One was a loft in a chestnut, a covert branch of a government that denied having branches. Conk waited there with two riflemen and an apology in his back pocket, which he never got to use, because Jeb took the treehouse apart with a wheelbarrow, the riflemen's ladders, and a pulley.

"You were my best," Conk said from the wreckage, spitting out a leaf. "The chimp-ion of the whole program. You never missed."

"I missed once," said Jeb. "That's why I jumped." He took the apology out of Conk's pocket himself, read it, and pocketed it. Conk looked at the wrecked pulley. "I liked the pulley. That was training." "No," said Jeb. "That was me."

Back on the island, Washburn got a postcard with no signature and a prescription for moderation. He framed both. On the neighboring chestnut, a monkey in a long coat watched the wreckage, tipped his hat, and melted into the leaves. "Another year, Bane," Carlos murmured. The Jackal never did like deadlines, or witnesses.

It ended — as these things must — by the sea, on a little island where a baboon with no past ran a banana stand and a lemur with a very bright future did the books.

"Who are you today?" Marie asked, sorting the ripe from the merely enthusiastic.

Jeb considered the horizon, his stand, and the one louse he'd picked off her shoulder the night before, which on their island was practically a proposal.

"Nobody from Treehouse Seventy-One," he said. "Just a baboon with a banana stand." He handed a customer his change. "It's enough. Branch out — we're closing early."

Marie laughed, and for the first time since the Mediterranean, nobody shot at them at all.
