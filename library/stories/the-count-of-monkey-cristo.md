# The Count of Monkey Cristo

*A parody of "The Count of Monte Cristo"*

On the day of his wedding, young Edmond Dantês could have swung from every branch in Marseilles Jungle and back without touching the ground, so light was his heart. He had just been named captain of the banana freighter *Pharaon*, and in one hour he would marry the lovely Mercédès, whose grooming was the envy of the troop.

"What could possibly go wrong?" Edmond asked his best friend Fernand Mondego, picking a thoughtful nit from his shoulder.

Fernand smiled the smile of a monkey with a banana up his sleeve. "Nothing at all," he said. "Absolutely nothing."

Everything, as it turned out, went wrong.

Fernand wanted Mercédès for himself, and he joined a barrel of jealous conspirators — the envious Danglars, who wanted the captaincy, and the bitter Caderousse, who mostly wanted free bananas. Together they scrawled a note on a banana peel accusing Edmond of treason against the crown. Edmond was dragged before the Deputy Prosecutor, Villefort the Velvet-Furred, who realized the peel implicated his own father.

"I must burn this evidence," murmured Villefort. Then he thought: *but he knows too much about my family tree, and not the good climbing kind.* "Take him away," he said. "The Chimpeau d'If. No trial. No monkey business."

The Chimpeau d'If was a fortress on a bare rock islet — no vines, no branches, not a shrub to scratch on. For six years Edmond despaired, going quietly bananas in cell 34. He swung between hope and a stone wall, and the wall won every round.

Then one day, the floor began to talk.

"I," announced the voice, "am the Abbé Faria, and I have been tunneling toward freedom with a spoon for eight years. Unfortunately, I have tunneled in the wrong direction."

"Wrong direction?" Edmond cried. "You've dug into my cell! That's the first good luck I've had in six years!"

The Abbé was a scholar-ape of tremendous learning. Through a crack in the wall he taught Edmond mathematics, languages, history, swordsmanship, and the fine art of pretending to be richer than everyone in the room — and, most valuable of all, he told him the location of an enormous treasure buried by the Cardinal Spada clan on the isle of Monte Cristo. "Gold, jewels, and a hoard of preserved bananas so vast it could purchase a rainforest."

"Preserved *bananas*?" Edmond breathed. "I was born under a lucky star. A yellow one."

For years they dug together, two monkeys with one spoon. When the Abbé died, Edmond sewed himself into the burial sack, and when the guards tossed the body into the sea, Edmond swam for his life and clambered aboard a passing smuggler's raft. He spent ten years with the smugglers of the Mediterranean canopy, learning every smuggled thing, and one quick stop at the isle of Monte Cristo made him the richest ape alive.

Three months later, a strange, splendid ape arrived in Marseilles Jungle — embroidered waistcoat, sleek fur, pockets full of ripe 'nanas, tipping the fruit-sellers in solid gold.

"I am," he announced, "the Supreme Banana Being. I have crossed the canopy of the world. I have riches enough to buy the entire jungle — twice."

First, intelligence. Disguised as a priest of the order of Saint Banana, the Count called on Caderousse, now a shabby innkeeper, and let slip a diamond "from the executors of Edmond Dantês" — then watched, invisible behind his spectacles, while greed worked Caderousse like a puppet. Caderousse murdered a jewel merchant for the diamond that same night, and was packed off to prison, having confessed the whole conspiracy in between appetites. The Count had his map of the guilty.

The Count went ape on his revenge, and he did it with style. Danglars now ran the Jungle Bank, so the Count opened a limitless line of credit, borrowed magnificently, and then spread a rumor that the banana crop had failed. Danglars panicked, bet everything on rotten futures, and lost the whole treasury in an afternoon. On his knees, he begged for mercy. The Count peeled one slow banana. "You wrote one line on a banana peel," he said. "It cost me fourteen years. I have merely returned the favor — with interest."

Fernand, now a general with a chest of medals and a tail of lies, counted himself safe — until the Count's dinner guests began, politely, to discuss the betrayal of Ali Tebelen, the pasha-ape of Janina, sold to the Turks long ago by a young French officer for gold. And then a young female at the table rose, veiled in silk: Haydée, the pasha's daughter, sold into slavery by that same officer.

"Madame," said Fernand, his medals suddenly weighing a ton, "this has nothing to do with—"

"Say the name of the officer," said Haydée. "Say it, or I shall."

"...Fernand Mondego, Count de Morcerf," said the guest of honor, and the room went as quiet as a peeled banana.

Fernand collapsed when his crimes were read aloud. Mercédès, who had recognized Edmond from the first — "You cannot hide those eyes from me, Edmond, I groomed that face for years" — came to him that night in his study and asked for her son's sake, not her own. "I have no claim on you," she said. "I married a man I did not know, and loved you always, and am punished enough." She took her son and walked out, renouncing every stolen banana. It was the one debt the Count collected with no pleasure at all.

Villefort went stark raving mad in open court. The Count had quietly arranged that the trial of a flashy young criminal — one Benedetto, a charmer raised by smugglers — would end with the prisoner's calm declaration: "You want my name and parentage? Very well. I am the son of the Deputy Prosecutor, Villefort, who buried me alive in the garden the night I was born." A bandit named Bertuccio had dug the box up, raising the cub on spite; the Count had merely arranged the seating. Villefort rushed home to find his young wife — whom the Count's "science" had taught to poison for inheritances — had outdone herself, and that his little son lay dead of it, caught in the machinery of a vengeance aimed elsewhere. The judge sat down among his wigs and wept, mad as a March mangabey, and the Count felt something unexpected: not joy. Horror. He had planned every branch of his revenge, and at the top of the tree the view was colder than he had imagined. A child had paid for a father's crime, and no treasure could buy that back.

So he did what any wise ape does when the harvest is over — he let go of the branch. He saved what could still be saved: Valentine, the sweethearts' daughter, drugged by her stepmother's final treachery, was carried from her own funeral sleep by the Count's last pill and delivered, blinking, to Maximilien Morrel — two sweethearts who had never once lied to each other. He gave them the largest share of his fortune. Then he sailed for the horizon with Haydée, who had long since stopped being a weapon and become a reason, leaving behind only a note:

*"Wait and hope. The wise monkey knows that all wisdom is summed up in these two words — and also in the word 'banana,' which is arguably more useful. May all your climbs be upward ones."*

And somewhere over the sea, a former prisoner with a spoon-forged soul ate the finest banana of his life, at the top of the tallest tree in the world.
