# The Chimp-herd's Crown

*A parody of "The Shepherd's Crown"*

They say the whole jungle held its breath the night Granny Weatherfur died.

The bees came first, humming at the door of her hollow all night, because she had always told them everything. Then the lemurs gathered on the chalk hillside and would not graze.

Tiffape Aching felt it in her teeth. She was the young hag of the downs now, and a hag knows. There was a granny-shaped hole in the world, and the whole jungle whistled through it.

Granny Weatherfur had been the greatest grooming-witch of her age. She could stop a charging buffalo with a look, and her Disapproving Stare had once made a leopard apologize. She had also, being dead now, left everything terribly tidy — which was how Tiffape knew she had seen it coming. On the shelf in the hollow sat the Chimp-herd's Crown: a fossil star-fruit, five perfect ridges, laid down when the downs were a sea. Beside it lay a note in Granny's spiked handwriting:

*About time.*

The witches of the downs came to see her into the ground, and there was tea afterwards, because there always is. It was there that Tiffape learned the first rule of her new life: witches grieve by arguing.

"A witch's power is in ceremony," declared Mrs. Aye-aye, author of *Magick of the Ape*, a thick volume with silvered fur on the cover and no dirt anywhere. "Robes. Circles. Assistants. Witchcraft should be a hierarchy, with — for example — someone at the top."

"A witch's power is in her hands," said Tiffape. "Boots are for walking to folk."

"How charmingly agricultural," said Mrs. Aye-aye, in the tone of one stepping over something.

Poor Lettice Sope, third witch of the downs, agreed with whoever had spoken last, wore a small tiara to feel witchy, and cried into the flan. "I keep expecting her to walk in and tell me to stop slouching," she whispered.

"She is telling you," said Tiffape. "That's the annoying part. She'll never stop."

"The Feral Folk will have felt the gap," said Nanny Fig, arriving with a second banana flan and grief in equal measure. "When the strong one leaves, the nasty ones come back. You can't argue with it, love. It's like weather with teeth."

They came back the very next week. The old stone ring on the hill glowed of nights. Lemur milk curdled in the pail. And the herders began waking tired, their dreams creased and sat-in, as though something had ridden them around a paddock in the dark and put them away damp. One herder's daughter slept three days with her eyes open and her paws folded, neat as a room nobody lived in; when Tiffape sat by the bed, the thing wearing the girl's sleep turned its attention on her, lightly, like a leopard sniffing a lock.

She threw it out with a shout and a kettle of steam, and sat shaking in the dawn.

"They're testing the fence," she said. "They're checking whether the downs still has a bark."

That was when the silverback turned up at the hollow.

He was huge, and gentle as dusk, and he carried a stick he called his Persuader, which he had never once used on anything.

"I'm Geoffrey," he said. "I want to be a witch."

"You're a *male*," said Tiffape.

"Yes," said Geoffrey. "But I'm very good at listening. And I can talk to bees. And I don't want to fight anyone, ever, and my father thinks that's a tragedy, so I left."

Tiffape looked at him for a long time. Granny's note had said *About time*, and Granny's notes never said only one thing.

"Can you make tea?" she said.

"George makes lovely tea," said a small voice from his shoulder. It was a bee. "We've discussed it."

Mrs. Aye-aye said it was unnatural. Nanny Fig said so was she, and thank goodness. Lettice said both, in one breath. And the hill — being the Chalk, and therefore stubborn, and therefore hers — allowed it.

Geoffrey's father came for him a month later, big as weather and twice as loud, with fighters behind him and a lifetime of disappointment in front.

"Come home," he said. "Be a terror. You're mine."

"No," said Geoffrey, and poured him a cup of tea.

Nobody in that yard moved. The fighters watched the Persuader. The Persuader hung loose and idle in Geoffrey's fist, and somehow it was the idle stick that frightened them — the stick that had never been used, and might, and wouldn't.

His father drank the tea, because you cannot punch a host, and even tyrants obey etiquette while it is holding the teapot. "You always were your grandmother's son," he muttered, and stalked off across the downs.

"My mother wanted me kind," Geoffrey explained, to nobody in particular. "So I practiced."

Then the Feral Folk came through on a night with no moon: a troop out of a bad dream, all glamour and teeth, led by their Queen, who wanted the downs the way rot wants a soft fruit.

But Granny Weatherfur had been expecting them for fifty years.

The herders were waiting at the stone ring with their crooks — farmers, fruit-pickers, buffalo-tenders, none of them soldiers, all of them Chalk. The bees were waiting in the thorn bushes. The Nac Mac Fleagle were waiting under the turf, sober, a state they described as "agonal." Nanny Fig was waiting with a flan of terrible purpose. Even Lettice Sope was there, holding a poker like a wand. And Tiffape was waiting with the Crown in her hands.

"We're not fighters," the head herder had said, at the meeting.

"No," Tiffape had said. "You're the downs. That's better. Soldiers go home. The land can't."

The Queen rode out of the ring on a nightmare stitched from other monkeys' bad Mondays, and Geoffrey walked forward to meet her, unarmed, holding his Persuader like a walking stick.

"You want a fight," said Geoffrey, kindly. "But I've brought tea."

The Queen hissed. The night got colder. She looked past him at Tiffape, and her voice went soft as mold.

"Little hag. I could keep you young forever. Unripe. Undying. Never bending, never bruising, never old."

"Green forever," said Tiffape, "is just fruit that never ripened. On the Chalk we call that a waste of a season."

And the hillside began to glow, faint and white, as the Chalk itself warmed under the Queen's winter — for this was herding country, loved for a thousand years, and land like that does not let its monkeys go.

The herders closed in a ring of crooks. The bees made a wall of small thunder. The Fleagles erupted from the turf shouting "Crivens!" and "Nae crown for ye, ye bony fashion disaster!" — for it is well known that glamour cannot survive being laughed at by six inches of red fur.

A stray elf broke for Lettice Sope, and Lettice, too frightened to run, stood her ground and hit it with the tiara. It was so astonished to be attacked by jewelry that it forgot to be beautiful, and she hit it again. Somewhere, Granny Weatherfur's shade said *ha*.

The Queen looked at the ring of torches, the hill, the Crown, and the terrible steadiness of a young silverback offering her tea.

"Sin, ma'am," said Geoffrey, politely, "is when you treat folks as things. You've been doing it for centuries. Also, you're out of cream."

The Queen went out like a frost at sunrise — backward, shrieking, through her own gate, and the stones closed behind her with a thump you could feel in your feet.

Afterward, they put the Crown on Tiffape's head, five small ridges against the dawn, and the herders cheered until the echoes needed a rest.

"It's only a fossil," said Tiffape, embarrassed.

"Aye," said Nanny Fig, re-filling everyone's mugs. "And the downs is only dirt. It's what's been carried on 'em that counts. Now — who's for flan? There's a hole in the world, lovelies. Best we fill it with something sweet."

Mrs. Aye-aye began a speech about how the victory proved the need for hierarchies. They gave her the ladder to hold for the flan, which is a kind of priesthood.

Geoffrey stayed. He kept bees, kept peace, and kept the Persuader unused, and in time even his father came to tea, cautiously, twice, and wiped his feet.

And far off, on a warm night, the bees hummed contentedly, because Granny Weatherfur had told them this was exactly how it would go.
