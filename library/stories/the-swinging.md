# The Swinging

*A parody of "The Shining"*

Jack Torrance got the job because he was the only applicant who didn't drink — on Tuesdays. Mr. Ullman, the manager lemur, tapped his long fingers together. "Winter caretaker of the Overswing Hotel, Mr. Torrance. Three hundred rooms, forty indoor fig trees, and one unbroken record of no incidents, which your famous temper will now threaten."

Jack needed the job: a blocked novel, a bopped pupil on his teaching record, a wife who had stopped asking about the novel. So Wendy packed, and Danny came too.

Danny was five. Sometimes Tony came — Tony was the little ape who lived in Danny's own fur, and when Tony came, Danny's eyes went white and his voice went wise. Tony could swing far out over the dark branches of what-happens-next — the troop called it the swinging. And lately Tony kept swinging back with the same word, which Danny could not read: RED-PLU, said the bathroom mirror. RED-PLUM, said the whole hotel, in a voice like a banana rotting softly somewhere expensive.

Dick Hallorilla, the head chef, walked them through the kitchen — bananas benedict, banana flapjacks, banana flambé — and then stopped, and looked at Danny, and Danny looked back, and the room went as quiet as a fig falling in an empty forest.

"You got the swinging too, huh, doc," said Hallorilla, without moving his lips. "Then hear an old ape. This place shows things; it is old, and warm, and hungry. Stay out of Room 237. And if it turns bad, you swing me a thought, loud as you can, all the way to Florida. I'll come."

Before the staff left, Ullman walked them through the whole house one last time, apologizing as he went. "The Gold Room, our ballroom — closed in winter, of course. The Colorado Fig Lounge. And these are our hedge chimps, sculpted topiary, much photographed." The hedge chimps stood in their plaza like a jury of dark green witnesses. "One thing more, Mr. Torrance. You'll be alone here, and the winters are long, and solitude has turned clever males strange before. A former caretaker, a Mr. Grady, succumbed to — cabin fever. In 1970. I mention it only so you'll take your recreation seriously."

"Rest assured, Mr. Ullman," said Jack, "recreation is the one thing I'm serious about."

Wendy asked about the boiler, and the gardener taught her the pressure valve, and after that she checked it nightly, which was the one chore the hotel never sabotaged, because the hotel had plans for the boiler and none of them were chores.

The passes filled with snow, and the mails stopped, and Jack's novel stayed exactly as finished as it had been in October, which was not at all. He found the scrapbook instead, down in the basement: the Overswing's own album, forty years of galas and gangster apes and one masked ball nobody would explain. He read it all November. He read it all December. He began, in a small firm caretaker's hand, to make notes — as if the hotel were dictating.

By February he typed with the serene industry of a male who has decided to enjoy himself: *All branch and no swing makes Jack a dull ape.* Page after page, and the hotel read over his shoulder, and turned the pages for him when he dozed.

Wendy found the pages. She woke Danny. Jack found them both at the rope-ladder with the look she had learned to dread — the big-toothed grin, the pleasant roar.

"Give me the 'nana, Wendy," he said softly.

She swung a bunch of green plantains at his head. He caught them. That was the trouble with being married to a gorilla.

Danny came back from the second floor with red marks on his neck and a story nobody could bear to hear, so Wendy sent Jack up to Room 237, where a soggy orangutan lady sat in the bathtub, ripening wrong. "You grooming me," she gurgled, following him to the door. "Forever. And ever. And ever." Jack came down the stairs lying through his teeth about the whole thing, and — a detail Wendy caught, and filed, and was right about — smelling faintly of expensive rot.

Downstairs the empty ballroom rang with glasses. "White ladies, sir?" asked the barman, Lloyd, polishing a tumbler. "Whatever's closest," said Jack, "and a little umbrella." And there was Grady, the waiter — Grady, who had always been the waiter, whatever the records said. "You've always been the caretaker, Mr. Torrance," said Grady gently. "I should know. I've always been here."

Around them the masked ball filled up like a tub: ape gangsters in paper hats, a orchestra tuning, forty years of guests all asking Jack how the party was going to come off. "It's coming off," Jack promised. "I have to correct my wife and my son first. They've invited a trespasser."

"A doctor," said Grady sympathetically. "And the boy. Handle it, Mr. Torrance, and the party drinks free forever."

The hedge chimps came alive at dusk. The twin chimps in their little blue dresses held hands and sang to Danny: "Hello, Danny. Come swing with us. Forever. And ever." Danny ran, and on the nursery door he wrote, in plum jelly from the kitchen, the word RED-PLUM, and when Wendy held up her hand-mirror she went cold from tail to crown.

MULP-DER, said the mirror.

"It's plum-derr, Mommy," said Danny. "It means the hotel wants to squash us. Like fruit."

Wendy locked Jack in the pantry with a fig-crate bar and ran for Danny, and the vine-radio Jack had quietly unscrewed at the first snow lay dead in its box — he'd cut them off the world weeks ago, and the sled's runners with it. Through the pantry door, Jack snarled — and then, behind him, a polite waiter's voice cleared its throat, and the latch lifted itself like a fig giving to a thumb.

"You'll want the machete, Mr. Torrance," said Grady. "It's in the kitchen. Guests are waiting."

Jack took the banana machete to the bathroom door where Wendy and Danny hid. It split in slats under his arms, the way a peel gives. A snout poked through, grinning, delighted with itself.

"HERE'S JOHNNY-APE!"

"Here's Jack," said Danny from the hall, very small and very steady, "and here's what he forgot: you can't swing at your own son." Danny held up the hand-mirror, plum-smeared, and Jack saw Jack — gray-faced, overripe, a male going bad from the grin inward — and could not look away, and Danny swung his father one last soft thought, low and slow, like a lullaby on a vine: *sleep.*

In Florida, Dick Hallorilla had felt the boy's fear arrive three days earlier like a bone-white taste in the back of his throat — and then, the night of the machete, a scream that swung him straight up out of a sound sleep with his fur standing like a hedge. He did not pack a bag so much as seize one. The passes were shut, the flights were jokes, so he rented a sled and went overland, talking to Danny across a thousand miles of dark the whole way: *Hold on, doc. I'm swinging as fast as these old arms swing. Do not go outside. Whatever it shows you, do not go outside.*

The hedge chimps tried to hold him at the driveway. They got his coat and a piece of his ear, and he went through them roaring — the oldest and calmest ape in Florida making the single least calm entrance in hotel history — and found the lobby quiet, the pantry broken open, and mother and son wrapped in the banana sled, watching the maze.

Jack sat down in the middle of the topiary maze, and by moonrise he was frozen into a great silverback-shaped bananasicle, grinning forever at nothing.

Hallorilla took them down to Florida on the sled, checked them into a motel with a fig-tree pool, and stood guard on the balcony until the sun came up like a dropped egg. Behind them, in the mountains, the hotel's lobby lay quiet, and on its ballroom wall hung the photograph of the Summer Gala, July 4th, 1921 — apes in paper hats and fig punch — with one figure front and center, unmistakable, grinning like a split banana.

The Overswing would go on swinging.

But it never got what it came for, because it never got Danny — and Danny, swinging high and far over the years and the snow, never once looked back.
