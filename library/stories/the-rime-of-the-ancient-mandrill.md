# The Rime of the Ancient Mandrill

*A parody of "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner"*

It was an ancient Mandrill,
And he stoppeth one of three.
"By thy long grey fur and glittering eye,
Why stand'st thou staring at me?"

The wedding guest tried to point out that he had been one of three monkeys walking to a wedding, and that the other two had sensibly kept going. The bride had paced into the hall; the guests were met; the feast was set; somewhere a cousin was playing the merry bassoon. And now a mandrill older than the oldest fig tree had dropped out of the canopy and taken hold of his arm.

"The feast is ready, old one. Let me by."

"First hear my tale," said the Mandrill. And the guest was held, as if by vines; he listened like a three-years' child: the Mandrill had his will.

"We sailed a raft of woven branches out of the home estuary," the Mandrill began, "seven gibbons and two hundred apes, merry as a barrel of monkeys. We were going to see the sea, because it was there and we were young. The fruit-bats flew over us like living weather, and every ape aboard was somebody's brother, and all of them laughed at my jokes.

"Then the storm came down like an angry troop and chased us south into fog. It stood tall as the canopy and wide as the horizon, and cracked the sky green and white; and through the fog, white as moonlight, came a sifaka — a ghost-lemur, pure as peeled fruit. She perched upon our mast as though she had built it herself, and the moment her paws touched the wood a fair wind rose and filled the sail, and we rounded the Cape of Crocodiles as gently as a mother grooms an infant. Every ape aboard hailed her for luck. She ate nobody's banana. She owed us nothing."

"What happened?" said the wedding guest, and then wished he hadn't.

"With my blowpipe," said the Mandrill, "I shot the sifaka from the mast. She fell like snow.

"The fair wind died that instant, dead as a dropped banana."

"But — why—"

"There was no why. There has never been a why. That is the whole of the tale, young one, and the rest of it is only what a why costs."

The crew, he said, had hung the snow-white pelt about his neck in sign of his crime. And they had opinions, too. "'God save thee, ancient Mandrill, from the fiends that plague thee thus — what makes that pelt so white?' 'It is the sifaka's,' I told them. 'Ah, wretch! they cried, the sifaka to slay, that made the breeze to blow!' — and every ape aboard agreed, with the faces of judges. Then the fog lifted and the moon came out, and they said: ''Twas right, such birds to slay, that bring the fog and mist.' The same mouths. The same certainty. A troop can be right twice a day if it waits for the weather to change.

"Day after day we lay becalmed on the stagnant brown river-sea, and the slime vines crawled with legs upon the slimy water. And oh — the hunger.

"Bananas, bananas, every branch,
And not a one to peel.
Bananas, bananas, every branch,
And every bunch like steel.

"We gnawed green wood. We chewed our own tails. The palms turned away from us; the figs shut their doors; even the lice went on strike, and you have to be a very poor ape indeed to be deserted by your lice.

"Then came a rotten hulk sliding out of the fog, ribs like a picked carcass, and aboard her two specters diced for our souls. One was Scab-Night, and one was Life-in-Itch, and Life-in-Itch whistled three notes as soft as a loris's bite, twice, to show that the game had begun. When it was done she cried, 'The game is done! I've won, I've won!' — and the third note she whistled was my name.

"Four times fifty living apes dropped dead upon the raft. They did not groan or sigh; they dropped their bananas and died — every soul but me. And every dead eye fixed on me, on the pelt about my neck, and would not look away for seven nights and seven days; and I was alone, alone, all, all alone, alone on a wide, wide sea, with two hundred blessings and curses wearing the same faces, and never a monkey left to tell me which was which.

"An ape's heart is only so big, and those eyes wore it thin. Worst of all were the water-monkeys — little shiny swimmers that leapt in the moon's track, flashing their tiny hoots of joy — because they were happy and I was not, and that, I believed, was the whole quarrel between us.

"Then one evening, through a hundred thousand particles of golden light, they leapt and flipped and flashed, beside the raft, beyond the raft, as far as the eye could reach. And something in my heart, dry as a husk, cracked open like a seed, and I blessed them, every one.

"A spring of love gushed from my heart, and I blessed them unaware. The pelt slipped from my neck and sank like a stone. Sleep came down on me like rain on the canopy, and when I woke it *was* rain — real rain, bucketing, wonderful, a hundred ropes of water pouring off the ends of every leaf — and I drank until my belly sloshed, and laughed and drank and cried and drank again. Somewhere in it the dead crew rose, quietly, and took up the poles and the sail. They did not speak. But the wind did — a wind like a great troop singing far off — and it sang the raft north, past the Cape, past the crocodiles, who kept their distance out of respect.

"And in my own quiet estuary, on the shore, stood the Hermit of the Wood, who grooms the orphans of the forest, and with him a little pilot-monkey boy who pointed at my ragged fur and laughed loud and long — the first honest laugh I had heard in a year, and the second kindness.

"'What manner of ape art thou?' said the Hermit. And the moment I began my confession — 'Forgive me, father, for I shot—' — the raft sank beneath me like a diving otter, and I swam ashore, humble as a wet marmoset, and the water-monkeys stood off the shallows and watched me land, and I could not tell if they were jeering or keeping time."

The Mandrill paused, and the glitter of his eye dimmed to something almost gentle. The wedding guest shivered and did not meet it, which was wise of him.

"And ever since, O wedding guest, a strange agony comes over me at odd hours, and I must tell my tale to the next young monkey I meet, or burn inside. I have walked the troop-paths of the world and taught the young to love the little things — the lice, the ticks, the water-monkeys, the small biting glories of the world. Farewell, farewell! But this I tell to thee:

"He prayeth best, who groometh best,
All monkeys great and small;
The sifaka, dear to heaven, that made the wind to blow —

"I shot her for no reason, and the wind forgave me the moment I forgave the world its smallness."

The Mandrill vanished upward into the leaves like smoke.

The guest turned not back to the wedding feast. He went home instead, a sadder and a wiser monkey, and was seen the next morning, brush in hand, grooming the ugliest, grouchiest, baldest old baboon in the entire troop — unasked — and found, to his astonishment, that he was glad.

The bassoon, he said later, was not that good anyway.
