# Mandrill the Warrior

*A parody of "Martin the Warrior"*

The first thing Badrange the Tyrant ever gave the young mandrill was a whip across the shoulders. The second thing was a name: *slave.*

Fort Banank stood on the northern marshes like a rotten tooth, and Badrange — a big, scarred baboon with half an ear and all the ambition — was building it on the backs of captives. The enslaved beasts below the walls called him the Stoat behind his back: the morals of one, considerably worse breath. The young mandrill, whose father Luuuke had forged the finest sword in the northlands before Badrange took it and took him, hauled quarry-stone by day and stared at the walls by night.

"You'll stare a hole through them at this rate," said the mole. "Staring never dug a tunnel yet. Digging digs tunnels."

His name was Grumm, a mild, snuffling creature who had followed his young mistress all the way from the peaceful south. The mistress was Rose — Laterose of Nooncanopy, a gibbon with fur the color of dawn and a singing voice that made even the slaves' buckets seem lighter. She and Grumm had been captured searching for her brother Brome; forced to sing in Badrange's hall, she had used the nights to learn every crack in the walls.

"Escape with us," she told the mandrill, quietly, on the quarry path. "My father will raise Nooncanopy for your war. The south is not like this place."

"My war?" said the mandrill. "I only wanted my father's sword."

"Then you want it back," said Rose. "And Badrange has it. So you do have a war, whether you wanted the trouble or not."

They escaped — the three of them, and Brome, ferried down the coast by an ancient, half-mad tarsier ferrybeast who claimed to have boxed a whale. There were ledges, gulls, and a week marooned waiting for a tide, and by the end of it the mandrill had learned something dangerous: he had friends.

The Rambling Rosehip Players found them half-starved in a fern gully — a traveling troupe of outlaws from Banank's early days, escaped slaves every one: Ballaw the trickster hare, who could juggle anything up to and including sleeping stoats; Felldoh, a lean old colobus with a throwing arm that could put a stone through a sneer at a hundred paces; and Keyla their chief, whom Brome had been seeking all along.

Then came the moment that turned a rescue into a rising. Felldoh, who had suffered Badrange's whip longest, walked out on the marsh before the entire slave-line, called the Tyrant out by name, and dared him to come and collect a stone.

"Badrange!" his voice cracked over the marsh like a whip of its own. "You kept me eight years. Here's my notice!"

He didn't kill him. He did worse: he made the Tyrant ridiculous, stone after stone, in front of his own horde, until Badrange's authority bled out of him through laughter. A tyrant can survive a wound. A tyrant cannot survive a punchline.

The Players did not go home after that. They raised a camp in the dunes beyond the marsh — tents of stolen sailcloth, a flag made from a circus poster — and settled in to besiege Fort Banank with a garrison of jugglers, singers, and one mandrill learning that a war is mostly waiting. Badrange sent his soldiers to scatter them; the camp greeted them with satire, snares, and Felldoh's arm. He sent his champion; the champion defected by lunchtime, having heard the singing. In the evenings Rose taught the camp the round-songs of Nooncanopy, and the mandrill, who could not carry a tune in a bucket, discovered that being laughed at by friends was a different thing entirely from being laughed at by tyrants, and better than either was the way Rose kept saving the harmony's best part for him, though he never once found it.

Then Badrange stopped sending anybody, which was worse. At night, from the dunes, they could hear the drums. And on the night the moon was darkest, Felldoh led a raid to free the last of the quarry-slaves — and Badrange, who had been waiting for exactly that, was waiting.

The old colobus fell at the gate with three spears in him and his arm still cocked, and his last stone, the camp said afterward, found the Tyrant's jaw on the way down. The Players buried their champion in the dune he'd fought from, and the mandrill went to the grave with his father's broken hilt in his fist and no expression at all, which frightened his friends more than weeping would have.

The mandrill himself was taken in the next raid — over the wall, into the pit beneath Banank, in the dark, to be made an example of. From the pit he listened to the fort's timbers creak, and he remembered a mole: *digging digs tunnels.* Grumm, who had followed his mistress a thousand leagues, tunneled for eleven days to reach that pit, and broke through at dawn.

Rose's father would not come — Nooncanopy had gone to peace, he said, though his eyes, looking at Rose, had already gone somewhere further — but the Players' plan went on without him. On the last morning the tide served, and the ferrybeast ran his old hull aground below the wall as a ram, screaming that he'd boxed a whale and this was easier. The slaves inside Banank rose at the signal, up out of Grumm's tunnel like the marsh's own answer; Ballaw led the ram against the gate, juggling torches and screaming jokes that curdled blood; and Brome, gentle Brome, stood in front of the slave-line and refused, singing, to let them break.

In the last assault the mandrill came over the wall with his father's recovered sword singing, cut the Tyrant's horde to rags, and faced Badrange on the rampart as the fort burned.

"You've grown, stripey-face," sneered the baboon.

"You made sure of it," said the mandrill.

It was over in four strokes. What was not over, ever, was Rose.

In the last chaos of the storming — a stray blade, a falling wall-stone, no song ever settled which — Laterose of Nooncanopy fell, and the mandrill came out holding her, and the young warrior who had crossed a marsh to rescue his friends could not cross the little distance between living and not.

She was buried on a green hill above the sea, near her beloved Nooncanopy, and the Players sang, and Grumm dug, and dug, and dug, because digging was all Grumm had instead of tears.

The mandrill did not go south with them. Grumm gave him Rose's little carved bird; Ballaw gave him a joke he couldn't laugh at yet; and Brome, standing where Felldoh had fallen, gave him the whole camp's salute and a promise that Nooncanopy would never again mistake peace for hiding.

He walked north with his father's sword on his back, and the seasons turned under him, and somewhere in a wild country called Mosshowler, a blind silverback matriarch would one day hear of a striped wanderer with a famous blade — and set the whole forest on its course.

"Say one thing for the mandrill," the ferrybeast would tell anyone who'd listen. "Say he was loved. That's rarer than warriors, and costs more."
