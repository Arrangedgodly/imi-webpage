# Unseen Apedemicals

*A parody of "Unseen Academicals"*

The thing about football — the really important thing about football — is that it is not just about football. This is a lesson the wizards of Unseen University took four hundred years and one existential threat to their dinner to learn.

It began, as most disasters at UU do, with paperwork. Ponder Stibbons, a marmoset who had quietly made himself essential to every committee in the University, appeared at breakfast looking grave.

"Archchancellor," he said, "I have found a bequest. The late Doctor Ankhmein-Banananein left the University a substantial income on one condition: that a game of football be played. If we don't play, we lose the bequest."

"Sad for Doctor Ankhmein," rumbled Mustrum Ridcully, Archchancellor, a burly baboon with a crossbow behind the door and a wizard's hat at a sporting angle. "What did the old boy leave, precisely?"

"Eight per cent of the University's income, sir. The eight per cent," said Ponder, "that pays for the kitchens."

There was a silence in the Great Hall so profound that the sound of butter could be heard.

"Brazeneck College," Ponder went on, "has challenged us to a match. Their Archchancellor — the Dean, as was — has been saying things about us in public. The word 'chicken' may have been used."

"Chicken!" roared Ridcully, on his feet. "Nobody calls a graduate of this University chicken! We'll play! How hard can it be? You kick a ball about!"

Therein, as they say, hung a tale, and also a leather shroud.

The football of old Ankh-Morpork bore no resemblance to the gentle sport of today. It had no rules, no teams, and no pitch, merely two goals placed at opposite ends of the city and a morose leather sphere two feet across, fought over by entire districts in an annual rolling riot that rearranged the shopfronts. It had been banned, with enormous relief, sixty years ago — though not before it had killed, among others, the great street player Foul Ronny Likely, who died in glory at the moment of scoring, which his son considered a rotten trade.

Which brings us to the cellars, where the past was about to dribble down the walls.

In the deepest candle-vaults of Unseen University worked two young monkeys. One was Trev Likely, Ronny's son, who had promised his dying mother he would never play football, and who therefore expressed his genius the only way left to him: he would set a battered tin can dancing on his feet for a thousand touches, down the whole length of the cellars, without once letting it touch the ground. It was like watching a monkey negotiate with gravity, and win.

The other was Mr Nutt.

Mr Nutt was small, grey, unfailingly polite, and technically — per the paperwork — a goblin. He was employed to dribble candles, an art requiring strong fingers and no fear of dark staircases. He spoke seven languages, read physics for fun, and said things like "I must endeavor to acquire worth," which he explained meant that worth wasn't given, it was earned, in installments, by a monkey starting from nothing. Nobody asked what a goblin was doing knowing tensor calculus. Nobody ever asks the help anything.

Upstairs in the night kitchen ruled Glenda Sugarbean, a no-nonsense baboon of the old school who could feed three hundred wizards, one orangutan, and the entire night shift on four hours' sleep, and whose pies had been described as religious experiences by men who had technically died and come back for a second helping. Her best friend was Juliet Stollop, a laundry lass so beautiful that visiting academics walked into doorframes, and so dim that she once mailed herself a kiss.

Mr Nutt, it turned out, had read every book ever written on the ancient game, and then spotted the flaws. "The ball should be round," he said thoughtfully, "and light. And perhaps there should be rules against biting the referee." Trev taught the wizards to trap and pass, using bread rolls, boots, and language; the Librarian of Unseen University — an orangutan of few words — turned out to be a natural in goal, on the grounds that nothing gets past an ape who can hang off the crossbar by one foot. "Ook," he explained.

Meanwhile the dwarf fashion house of Screecher & MacApe took one look at Juliet Stollop, gasped "the cheekbones!" and swept her off to model the Diamond Dress of Tsort, a gown so heavy with gems that she had to be winched onto the catwalk, whereupon she forgot how legs worked and stood radiantly still for forty minutes, accidentally inventing an entire style.

Then Brazeneck College played its ace: they hired a ringer, a semiprofessional gorilla built like a wardrobe, and put about the rumor that Unseen University's team would be eleven elderly wizards carried off on stretchers by teatime.

And Mr Nutt, watching the ugly whispering, went quietly to the one monkey who knew his secret — the mysterious Lady who had raised him down in the dark places and taught him everything — and then to Trev, and said: "I think I must tell you what I am. You will not like it."

"Go on, then," said Trev, spinning his tin can.

"I am not a goblin. I am a Kong."

The can dropped. It had never dropped before.

"In the dark not-so-old days, somebody bred my kind to be perfect soldiers. Big. Strong. Obedient. The wars ended, and people said, there are no Kongs anymore, and everyone was very relieved." Mr Nutt looked at his own grey paws. "It is important to understand that this was not a fact of nature. It was a body count. I was left in a cellar under the world with a basket of books, and a Lady who thought that worth could be taught instead of bred in."

"You're a — a giant?" said Glenda later, when the truth came tumbling out across the kitchen table, along with the clotted cream. "They're eight feet tall! You're five foot nothing!"

"That," said Mr Nutt, with dignity, "is what growing up in a cellar with no worth does to a Kong. I am, however, extremely strong, and I dribble a magnificent candle."

It was Trev who settled it, spinning his can in the doorway with his mother's ghost and his father's ghost both sitting on his shoulders like a quarreling pair of parrots. "My dad died playing the old game," he said. "My mum made me promise never to play. But you lot went and made it a new game — a proper one, with rules and everything. That's the difference, right? The old game took. This one gives." He trapped the can on his instep, flicked it up, caught it. "I'm in. And so are you, you daft polite Kong, because if I have to break my mother's heart, you don't get to break yours."

Match day came, and with it the whole of Ankh-Morpork, which had been promised a spectacle and intended to be thoroughly spectacle-d. Brazeneck's gorilla scored twice in ten minutes, using wizards as brushes with which to sweep the pitch. Ridcully was booked for casting a weather spell on the referee's line of sight.

And then, with Unseen U. two down and the crowd ugly, Trev Likely began to juggle.

He took the ball on his instep, his knee, his shoulder, his head — a thousand touches, the crowd counting in one enormous voice — walking through Brazeneck's entire defense like a monkey strolling through a orchard of statues, and chipped it, gently, to the smallest player on the pitch.

Mr Nutt trapped it. Time held still. And into that stillness, from the Brazenneck end, came the cry the world had been waiting to scream: "He's a Kong! Look at his shoulders! HE'S ONE OF THEM KONGS!"

The stadium drew breath to become a mob.

Mr Nutt stood in the center of it, ball under his foot, and said, in a voice pitched to reach the last row: "Yes. I am. They say there are no Kongs left. There is one. I was bred to take. I have chosen to give. I have spent my whole life acquiring worth, one small dribble at a time, and I will tell you the secret of it: worth is not in the fur. It is in the paws. What will you do with yours?"

Nobody moved. Then a very large shape dropped from the crossbar, ambled to the center circle, and put one long orange arm around Mr Nutt's shoulders.

"Ook," said the Librarian. Which, from an orangutan, is a complete philosophy of tolerance.

The crowd cheered itself hoarse, Unseen U. won four–two, Trev scored the winner with his heel, and the tin can was later enshrined in the trophy cabinet, where visiting teams mistake it for the cup.

Afterward came the committees, and Ponder Stibbons chaired them all, which is how a marmoset became the most powerful wizard in Ankh-Morpork while everyone was looking at the football. Glenda got a new kitchen, a raise, and, eventually, a candle-dribbler, and taught Mr Nutt that worth can also be baked into a pie, and shared. Juliet became the most famous face of the age. Trev kept a thousand touches going in every warm-up, for his mum, and one in particular for his dad.

And the thing about football, the really important thing, as any monkey in Ankh-Morpork will now tell you, is this: it is not just about football. It is about what you do when the ball finally comes to you.
