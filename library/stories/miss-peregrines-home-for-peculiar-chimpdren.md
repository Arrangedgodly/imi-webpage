# Miss Peregrine's Home for Peculiar Chimpdren

*A parody of "Miss Peregrine's Home for Peculiar Children"*

Jacob loved his grandfather, but he had stopped believing him years ago. Grandpa Abe's stories were all the same: monsters with no faces, and a troop of chimpdren who could do impossible things — a girl who floated, a boy who was invisible, a little one who could carry a boulder — all watched over by a headmistress who could turn into a peregrine falcon and skip through time like a stone across a pond.

"They'll say I'm crazy," Grandpa always warned, "but you find the bird. In the loop. On the other side of the old gibbon's grave. September third, 1940."

"They say I'm crazy too," Jacob told his therapist, a glossy-coated macaque named Dr. Golan, who nodded and prescribed a smaller imagination.

Then, one terrible night, Grandpa Abe was found in the orchard with his eyes wide open — and Jacob, who had gotten there first, had seen something in the ferns that night: something tall and faceless, all bundled in tendrils, with a mouth where a face should be. It had looked at him. It had wanted to be seen.

"Stress," said Dr. Golan, scribbling.

So it happened that Jacob and his father took a boat to a soggy island called Coconut-holm, where the ruins of an old orphanage stood black and bombed among the palms. Jacob's father, who studied insects, went happily off to hunt beetles. Jacob went to find the truth, carrying an old letter signed "Miss Peregrine, ymbryne."

The ruins were full of dust and tree frogs. But in one bedroom he found a trunk, and sitting on the trunk was a girl — a bonobo, floating three feet off the floor, shining faintly, pointing something at him.

"You're Abe's grandson," she said slowly. "You look just like him. Only younger. And worse groomed."

Her name was Emma, and once Jacob convinced her he wasn't a wight — a hollow thing that hunts peculiars — she led him at a run to the standing stones, through a hollow in the peat that hummed like a struck rope, and out into September third, 1940.

The house, outside of time, was exactly as Grandpa had said. Miss Peregrine — a stern baboon with a pipe, currently a falcon perched on the banister, smoking — presided over it all. There was Bronwyn, a gorilla toddler who lifted Jacob overhead with one hand to say hello. There was Millard, who was invisible; you could watch his dinner chew itself. There was Hugh, whose fur hummed with hornets who adored him; Fiona, with orchids growing out of her pelt; Horace, who dreamed the future and dressed for it the night before; and Enoch, who could shove a mango-heart into a clay monkey and make it dance for a minute, which was illegal in nine forests.

"Every evening," Emma explained, "the bomb falls. And at one second to midnight, Miss Peregrine turns time back to morning, and it never lands. That's the loop. September third, forever."

Jacob watched it happen: the whistle in the sky, everyone calmly trooping to the shelter, the wall of fire — and then birdsong, and morning, and the bomb back up in the sky, waiting.

"Why chimpdren?" Jacob asked. "Why us?"

"Because the world got too ordinary," said Miss Peregrine, smoke curling from her pipe, "and the ordinary world has never forgiven the peculiar one."

It was Miss Peregrine who named Jacob's gift at last. He had seen the faceless thing in the ferns; none of the others could.

"Your grandfather saw them too," she said. "It is a rare and useful peculiarity, seeing the monsters. It makes you a shepherd, Jacob. Peculiars like you guard sheep like us."

Emma walked him back through the stones that evening, quieter than usual. From her satchel she drew a bundle of letters, softened by handling: every one addressed in Grandpa Abe's handwriting.

"He was going to come back for me," she said. "Then the war ended, and he didn't. And after that he stopped writing, and started telling wild stories about us instead, so that somebody, someday, would come and check whether we were real." She tucked the letters away. "You're the check, Jacob."

But shepherds are only needed when wolves are near. And the wolf, it turned out, was already on the island.

Jacob spotted him in the pub: a glossy-coated macaque, nodding, scribbling, prescribing. Dr. Golan. His therapist. Who lived, as Jacob distinctly remembered, an ocean away.

"You're wondering what I'm doing here," said Dr. Golan pleasantly, when Jacob cornered him. "The same as you, my boy. Hunting. Only you hunt for your grandfather's past, and I hunt for dinner." And he smiled, and Jacob saw that behind his eyeteeth the smile went back much too far, and that his eyes were milky at the center, like boiled egg.

He was a wight. They were the hollowgoasts' masters, and they had followed Jacob — the only creature alive who could see their pets — straight through the loop.

That night, the orchard burned. Tendril-faced hollowgoasts came lolloping up the rows, and behind them the wights with torches, to steal the ymbrynes and break the loop for good. The chimpdren fought like the peculiar: Bronwyn with three chimpdren under each arm and one hollowgast over her head; Hugh whistling his hornets down his sleeves and out in a fury; Fiona growing a hedge six feet in a minute; Millard tripping wights who could not see what was kicking them; Enoch clapping mango-hearts into a shelf of clay monkeys, which rose, bowed, and dismantled a pursuer with tremendous politeness for sixty wonderful seconds.

And through it all, in a cage of iron twigs at the wight's shoulder, shrieking — Miss Peregrine, trapped in falcon shape.

Jacob led the seeing, because only he could. "The tall one — left of the well — NOW!" — and Emma, floating high with her lead shoes off, dropped like a cannonball. They chased Dr. Golan to the lighthouse over the sea, where he stood with the cage on the gallery rail, and grinned, and told Jacob the polite truth: that there were more wights than stars, that the peculiar age was over, and that really, boy, he ought to think about his own future.

"The future," said Jacob, and stepped aside at exactly the right moment.

The blind hollowgast, which could only follow Jacob's voice, went over the rail and into the sea with a noise like a wave giving up. Dr. Golan lunged for the cage, missed the cage, and joined his pet. Somewhere below, the sea made room.

They pulled the cage from the rail with a boat hook, but it was too late for the loop. Far off, over the house, the whistle sounded for the last time, and the bomb, for the first time in seventy years, actually fell. Everyone stood on the beach and watched yesterday end.

"Right," said Miss Peregrine from her cage, in a voice made tinny by falconry. "I have one word for you, children, and it is AGH," which was falcon for listen carefully. "An ymbryne resets her shape with her loop. Mine is gone. You must find Miss Avocet's loop, or another, or I am a bird for good, and I do not care for seed."

The chimpdren survived. The house did not. And they stood on the beach at dawn, rigging a boat, while Emma took Jacob's hand.

"You could go back," she said. "To the ordinary world. You could be safe."

Jacob looked at the wreckage, at the peculiar troop arguing over a rigging knot, at Emma floating an inch off the sand because she was excited, and he understood at last what his grandfather had been trying to tell him all along.

"I used to dream about escaping my ordinary life," Jacob said. "But my life was never ordinary. It was extraordinary, and I was too ordinary to see it. I'm coming with you."

And they sailed off to find their headmistress a loop of her own — and to make the monsters very, very sorry.
