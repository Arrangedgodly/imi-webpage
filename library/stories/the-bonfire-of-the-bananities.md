# The Bonfire of the Bananities

*A parody of "The Bonfire of the Vanities"*

Banana of bananities, saith the monkey; all is bananities.

Sherman McCoy believed none of it. Sherman, a mandrill of thirty-eight with a muzzle like an expensive sunset, was a Master of the Universe. Every morning he swung down from his duplex in the great kapok on Park Branch and rode uptown to Ficus & Ficus, the trading floor of the Vine Street Exchange, where he traded banana bonds — yes, actual bonds on future bunches; the whole jungle ran on them — with the serene violence of a silverback at feeding time. He could make nine hundred thousand bunches vanish into a deal before breakfast and feel nothing but appetite. Once, at six, his daughter Campbell had asked him what he did at work. "I trade bananas that don't exist yet," said Sherman. "Why?" asked Campbell, and Sherman had opened his muzzle to answer and found the room, the jungle, and the whole century on the other side of that *why*.

"Cash flow," he told his wife Judy, with the sweat of a monkey one bad season from the edge, "is the problem. I need one more kill."

The universe had scheduled a comedy instead.

It began because Maria Ruskin, a rich young gibbon married to an ancient howler with a vineyard, telephoned after midnight from the airport, stranded, and Sherman, feeling like the last romantic in New York, went to fetch her in the family's big German jeep. On the way back, Maria directing in her silk headscarf, they took a wrong turn off the expressway and slid down a ramp into the wild fig thickets of the South Bough-onx.

The tire went flat with a pop. Two young monkeys stood on the ramp in the dark. Sherman, master of markets, idiot of thickets, got out to negotiate — and Maria, terrified, slid behind the wheel, and the jeep lurched up the ramp with a sound like a mango dropped from a great height, and in the mirror they both saw the small figure crumple and lie still.

They drove. All night, they drove. And Sherman lay awake in the kapok knowing precisely what the young monkey's name would be once the jungle got hold of it: Henry Lemur, of the South Bough-onx, honor student, in a coma — and Master of the Universe McCoy a fugitive.

It was Peter Fallow who did it. Fallow, a dissolute British vervet of the press with a liver like an old fig and a genius for lunch, heard the tale from a drunken vicar monkey and recognized, through the sap-wine haze, the biggest bunch of the season. His story ran under a headline the whole jungle memorized: THE GREAT WHITE BONDO.

Up rose the Reverend Bracken, a magnificent silverback of the Greater Love Temple, who had buried four young monkeys from that ramp in five years. "They were driving," he thundered, "through a jungle they never once looked at, and a child of ours went down like a ripe fruit, and no one — no one! — has answered for it."

District Attorney Ape Weiss, a macaque up for re-election, needed an indictment the way a howler needs a dawn — needed it more each day, as young Kramer, the colobus in his office, made solemn noises to the papers about *the test of the system* while angling to try the case himself. Sherman's perfect canopy began, branch by branch, to come down.

They arrested him at the airport, mid-handoff of Campbell's school bag, and made him do the walk — the perp shuffle past the flashbulbs — and Sherman, who had spent twenty years ranking primates by the shine of their fur, learned how the ranking goes when the fur loses the shine. Ficus & Ficus eased him out with a memo and a cake. The kapok went back to the bank. Friends discovered urgent business in other trees. Judy filed and took Campbell to her mother's. What is the list of a monkey's vanities? He made it out one night in a rented room: money, suit, address, muzzle, troop. Everything, in short, except his daughter's laugh, which cost nothing.

His lawyer was Tommy Kilbaboon, a rumpled old fixer from the courthouse bars with an accent like a tugboat in a monsoon. "The case, me darlin'," said Tommy, patting Sherman's knee as if soothing a nervous gibbon, "is not about what happened on the ramp. Nothing in this jungle is about what happened. It's about who needs what said out loud before November." Tommy secured a trial, a judge, and a mistrial, roughly in that order.

The trial was a carnival. The Reverend Bracken packed the gallery. Kramer ran the witnesses up like dry kindling: first the eyewitness, a bodega macaque who, from three blocks away, in the rain, at night, had seen the driver clear as day, "and what color was the driver?" asked Kramer. "Pale," said the witness. "Big pale monkey." Then Tommy Kilbaboon rose, amiable as Sunday, and asked the eyewitness how far three blocks was in the rain, whether he had been wearing his glasses, and what color the sky had been — and had him down to "a light patch with ears" inside a minute. The gallery laughed. The witness deflated like a festival balloon in November.

Then the tape did the burning. The DA's star wire had caught Sherman's own panic — and Maria at the wheel — and the defense got it in, all of it, and one career after another went up in the flames it was meant to start. Mistrial. Maria, in exchange for immunity, married a count and moved to the Riviera canopy, where she took up yoga with a view of her own deposition. Fallow won the Pulitzer, gave the acceptance speech in a tone of ironic grief, and raised his fermented sap to the cameras: "To the jungle — it gives and gives."

And Sherman? Stripped of the lot — bank account, troop, kapok, vanity, bond desk, headline — he sat one year on in a cell awaiting the lesser federal counts, doing pull-ups on the door frame, leaner and quieter than any monkey who had ever known him would have believed. His cellmate, a small-time fruit hustler from the Bough-onx doing two years for creative bookkeeping with a mango truck, thought him the calmest monkey in the block. "First month I figure him for a psychiatrist," the cellmate told his mates. "Now I think he's the only monkey here who knows something."

Strange to tell, he was. Every banana of it had burned — the whole bonfire, lit under him by one wrong turn — and what Sherman McCoy found underneath, when the flames died down and the smoke cleared, was not ruin at all. On visiting days he sat across the glass from Campbell, who was twelve now and working through the whole thing with a seriousness he recognized, unhappily, as his own. He had begun, in his head, a list of a different kind: what a bond is actually worth, who pays when a bet goes bad, what a monkey owes the jungle he drives through and never once looks at. He had no answer for the *why* yet. But he was a patient trader now.

It was the ground. It was, of all things, peace.

Banana of bananities; all is bananities. Sherman McCoy finally believed it, Master of the Universe, doing pull-ups on the door frame, waiting for the van.
