# Project Hail Hairy

*A parody of "Project Hail Mary"*

Dr. Ryland Gray woke to a chime and a robot voice saying his name was Rocky, or possibly that his mass was 0.5 kilograms, and neither fact felt useful.

"Good morning," said the ceiling. "What is two plus two?"

"Four," croaked Ryland, who was — as far as he could tell — a capuchin in a hospital gown floating in a white room with no doors. "Who are you? Who am I?"

"That is correct," said the ceiling, annoyingly pleased. "Grace period will end soon. Please remain calm."

He remained the opposite of calm. His memory was an emptied banana peel: scooped clean, leaving only the shape of what should have been there. But his hands — clever capuchin hands that could unscrew anything — kept finding tasks, and when the walls unfolded into laboratory equipment, he knew what every gadget did. He was a science monkey.

Slowly, in flashes — usually while eating (and why was the food pureed banana paste? why was *everything* banana?) — it came back.

The sun was dimming. Something was eating it. A tiny particle — a spore, a mote, a golden fleck swarming off the sun's surface — was stealing the light before it could reach Earth. The press called them "the Astro-nanas." They had smeared a visible trail between the sun and Venus — the Petrova Line, a scuff mark the length of a solar system — and in thirty years, Earth freezes, the crops fail, and the great apes go the way of the dinosaurs' less furry cousins.

Unless. A one-celled creature had been found in Venus's clouds that *ate* the Astro-nanas. And a ship — the *Hail Hairy*, staffed by the world's most expendable brilliant scientists — had been flung to Tau Ceti on a one-way trip to breed the counter-organism and send the recipe home.

Ryland, a junior-high science teacher who had once published one (1) paper, had been sent along to do the biology. He remembered being recruited by Dr. Stratt, a mandrill with three stars, absolute authority, and no detectable sense of humor, who had been handed the planet as a budget item. "I can commandeer anything on Earth," she told him, "except optimism. You'll have to bring your own." When he protested that he was a teacher, not a hero, she said: "Exactly. Heroes die usefully. Teachers finish the syllabus."

She wanted him because of the dish. NASA had Astro-nana samples that refused to reproduce anywhere but stars, and Ryland — patient the way only a monkey who has supervised a baking-soda volcano at 2 p.m. on a Friday can be — had coaxed them into dividing in a petri dish in a Palo Alto basement. The first ape to make a star-eater multiply became, by Stratt's arithmetic, the least expendable monkey alive, and she shipped him across the galaxy before he could finish grading his class's quizzes.

He found his crewmates first, slumped at their stations — the launch g's had been too much; only Ryland, small and light and capuchin-shaped, had survived. Commander Yao, gentle as a tree and twice as calm. Dubrowski, who never stopped smiling. Ilyukhina, who had kissed Ryland's cheek at launch and told him he had soft ears. He floated a long while holding Yao's cold paw. Then he got to work, because the data still had to get home.

Then the alarm went off. Something was tapping on the hull.

An airlock opened on an alien ship alongside. And through the tunnel came a small tripod-shaped alien, roughly the size of an oversized grooming target, whose very first word, translated by Ryland's hastily rigged software, was:

"Question. You. Alive. Amaze."

"Rocky," Ryland decided, because the alien looked exactly like a friendly rock with eyes. Rocky was an engineer — a *spectacular* engineer — and his species was dying too: the same Astro-nanas had infested his home star.

Neither could talk. Neither could breathe the other's air, eat the other's food, or survive the other's gravity. So they invented everything else. Rocky's language turned out to be musical — whistles and chords — and Rocky's material of choice was xenonite, a fiber he spun stronger than jealousy, with which he could build anything Ryland could draw. They built a friendship out of metal and mathematics: Rocky built whatever Ryland designed, with impossible precision; Ryland ran the biology Rocky couldn't, because Rocky's world had never invented the microscope. They argued, played Twenty Questions, and traded songs.

"Question. Teacher. What is teacher?"

"Someone who shows small ones how to know things."

"Amaze," said Rocky, who used the word for everything from breakthroughs to burnt toast.

They worked out a life together, one orbit at a time. Rocky slept eleven hours a day, hanging upside down from the ceiling like a wind chime with opinions, so Ryland set alarms to whistles and learned to nap in shifts. Rocky's ship ran on an ecosystem of beetles — "Question. Beetle?" "Amaze," said Rocky, with real feeling — and when Ryland redesigned the beetle farm to double the yield, Rocky built him a woven xenonite hammock as payment, and Ryland cried into it, because it was the first furniture in human history made as a thank-you note from another star.

The science came down to this: the Taumoeba — Rocky's pet microbe, the Astro-nana-eater — could thrive in Tau Ceti's upper clouds, but only if it could tolerate nitrogen, which poisoned it like daylight poisons night monkeys. So Ryland bred them, generation after generation, feeding them poison in tiny increasing doses, running two thousand petri dishes — until one line of Taumoeba learned to eat nitrogen for breakfast and ask for seconds.

"Amaze!" sang Rocky.

"Amaze," Ryland agreed, and had a good cry, and then another banana-paste packet.

It was around then that Ryland realized the translation software had quietly learned a word it had never been formally given. Rocky's chord for Ryland — three rising notes and a clatter — resolved, on the display, as "friend-who-fixes." Ryland spent the rest of the day unable to look at the hull without smiling, which is a hazardous condition in zero gravity.

Then came the bill. The Taumoeba that now laughed at nitrogen had also developed a taste for the ship's fuel, which was, essentially, refined Astro-nanas — and Rocky's return tanks were the dessert bar. Rocky's ship went from fueled to feast to empty. And here the universe played its cruelest joke of generosity: Eridian fuel burned three times hotter than human fuel, so Ryland's remaining tanks could carry Rocky's people home with fuel to spare — but could never carry Ryland. One ship's worth of fuel. Two crews' worth of home.

Rocky did math. Rocky did not like the math.

"No," said Rocky flatly, when Ryland offered him the *Hail Hairy*'s fuel — every gram, his own only ride home. "You come. My ship. My world. You teach."

"Rocky, your gravity would squash me flat."

"Amaze," said Rocky, meaning *I will build you a couch that floats* — and he did.

So Dr. Ryland Gray, junior-high science teacher and sole survivor of the *Hail Hairy*, fell toward an alien world — the first hairy teacher in Eridian history, arriving exactly on time for his first class.

"Question," said Rocky, as the hatch sealed. "You. Happy?"

"Amaze," said Ryland.

The sun, far behind them, shone at full brightness — a little less golden, a little less flea-ridden, and very slightly more banana.
