# The Tale of Benjamin Bonobo

*A parody of "The Tale of Benjamin Bunny"*

One morning a small spider monkey called Peter sat wrapped in a leaf-blanket, feeling sorry for himself. He had lost his little red vest among the banana trees of Farmer Grizzle's grove, and his mother had said, in the mild way that stings worst, that she was surprised at him.

This was a heavy load for a small monkey. Being shouted at is bad, but being Not Shouted At is worse, because there is nothing to shout back at.

His cousin Benjamin Bonobo came ambling along the top of the hedge in his leisurely way, eating a monkey-nut, shell and all, because Benjamin believed life was too short for shelling.

"Hullo, Peter! What's put you in a bunch?"

Peter explained about the vest — his mother had woven it herself from hibiscus bark, with a collar and two buttons of carved tagua, and he had wriggled out of it mid-scramble when the gorilla's shadow fell across the grove, and left it there, and come home vest-less, which in that household was considered worse than coming home limb-less.

"That vest," said Benjamin, sitting down and helping himself to another monkey-nut, "has got to be got back. My father is always saying that a spider monkey without his vest is only half a spider monkey. And nobody wants to be half a spider monkey. It's the swinging that does it — you need somewhere to pin your dignity."

"But Farmer Grizzle—" said Peter.

"Grizzle naps at noon," said Benjamin. "All gorillas nap at noon. It's in the almanac. Besides, my father has told me everything about the grove, mostly twice, which is how you know it's true."

This was not as reassuring as Benjamin seemed to feel it was. But Peter was young, and it was still morning, and the vest was red and waiting.

They swung over the hedge on a long liana — Peter flying, Benjamin traveling in his leisurely way, which meant mostly rolling — and landed in the middle of the banana grove. It was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the fig trees they could hear Farmer Grizzle, the biggest silverback gorilla in the valley, humming to himself and thumping the ground, which is how gorillas hum.

Under the hedge lived a mouse lemur with a permanent frown, who popped out as they passed and said, "Going into the grove? Bad plan. The last family that went in came out as jam." Then she went back in and shut the door, because mouse lemurs consider it their duty to say that sort of thing and then have nothing further to do with the consequences.

They found the vest — and found Peter's little climbing-claws too, the ones he had also lost, stuck on the scarecrow's feet. The scarecrow wore the vest under its floppy hat and the claws upon its toes, and looked, altogether, better dressed than any scarecrow has a right to look.

"I say," said Benjamin. "It suits him."

"It does NOT suit him," said Peter, and got the vest off, which took some doing, as the scarecrow held out a sleeve out of politeness. The claws would not come off at all. Peter pulled until his tail trembled. The claws stayed on. They stayed on that day, and the next, and indeed until autumn, which is the last anyone ever saw of them.

Peter put the vest on and felt whole again, down to the very tip of his tail. He was composing a little speech about going straight home when Benjamin, who had eaten three monkey-nuts and was ambitious, said they ought to take a bunch of bananas back as a present for his aunt, and a papaya, "for the road."

The road being eleven steps long.

They ate some of the bananas under the trees. I am sorry to say they ate rather a lot of them, and they were green ones, and the effect of eating too many green bananas is soporific.

"I'll just close my eyes for a moment," said Peter.

"Certainly," said Benjamin, already asleep.

They woke to the softest, most dreadful sound in the jungle: purring. Before them sat the grove's cat — a sleek jaguarundi with a coat like poured cocoa — regarding them with great interest and slow-blinking yellow eyes.

"Scarecrow!" squeaked Peter, and the two of them dived under the big banana-picking basket just as the cat pounced. The basket rang like a gong. And then the cat did the most cat thing imaginable: it climbed on top, curled up, and went to sleep.

"What now?" whispered Peter.

"Now," said Benjamin, "we practice being patient. My father says patience is the soul of the bonobo, and that panic is for monkeys with somewhere better to be. We have nowhere better to be. That's rather the whole problem."

The cat, hearing whispering, put one paw down through the weave and felt around, the way you would pat a riddle. The paw found the end of Benjamin's tail, played with it, considered it, and withdrew, having decided it was not a mouse and hardly worth the trouble.

They were trapped for five hours.

To pass the time, Benjamin told the story of his uncle, who had once crossed the whole valley seated on a floating fig, "for reasons of state." Peter counted banana-smells — forty-one, all subtly different — and learned that the inside of a basket has very little to recommend it. A snail crossed the ground at a leisurely pace and back again, which is four hours in snail money, and even the snail looked at them as much as to say, well, this is a poor show.

Twice more the cat patted the basket. Once it yawned, which was somehow the most frightening thing of all. The afternoon dragged its knuckles past. And when at last hope had gone thin as broth, they heard a heavy, rolling tread, and a huge old bonobo came through the trees — Benjamin's father, wide as a barrel and twice as calm, with a pipe of dried leafrags going like a small chimney. He took in the scene at a single glance: one basket, two guilty tails poking out from under it, one cat on top.

Mr. Bonobo did not shout. He said, "Hm." He knocked the ash out of his pipe. Then he reached up and snapped a springy switch off a sapling, and the cat, opening one eye and doing the arithmetic — big bonobo, angry switch, no basket worth that — fell off the basket and vanished like spilled milk.

"Out," said Mr. Bonobo.

Out they came, blinking, vest and all, with the papaya still under Benjamin's arm and green-banana sleep still in their eyes.

His father looked at him for a long moment, the way fathers do when they are deciding between speeches.

"You," he said at last, "are switched."

"Sir?" said Benjamin, quivering.

"Switched from after-moonrise privileges to before-supper bedtime. Effective tonight. Effective," he added gravely, "for a week."

In the grove, this counts as a punishment of legendary severity. Peter was so relieved it wasn't him that he dropped the papaya, picked it up, dusted it, and walked beside it in shame the whole way home.

Mr. Bonobo carried the bunch of bananas to Peter's mother, who said it was very kind of him, and that Peter had been resting, which was one word for it. She put Peter to bed with a cup of chamomile-bark tea and a hot fig stone for his feet, and hung the little red vest before the fire to air, where it steamed gently and smelled faintly of adventure.

As for Benjamin, he was let out the following week a wiser bonobo, and went to bed before supper every evening of his sentence, like a toddler, while Peter sat outside the window and told the story with gestures.

The mouse lemur said "I told you so" to nobody in particular, and was not listened to, as usual.

And Farmer Grizzle spent the rest of the summer wondering three things: why his scarecrow had lost its vest and its shoes but gained an expression; why his cat would not come down from the tallest tree; and — this chiefly at supper — where all his green bananas had gone. But he was a gorilla, and gorillas nap at noon, and by one o'clock every day he had stopped caring, which is either wisdom or napping, and no scholar has ever settled the difference.
