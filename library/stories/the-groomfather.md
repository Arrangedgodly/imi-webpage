# The Groomfather

*A parody of "The Godfather"*

I believe in Bananamerica. Bananamerica has made my fortune.

So began Bonasera the banana-split maker, shuffling into the dark office of Don Vito Cooleone on the day of the Don's daughter's wedding, asking for justice. Two young mandrills had mauled his daughter and gotten off with a suspended branch. Bonasera wanted them peeled.

"A monkey who doesn't spend time with his family," said the Don, stroking a cat and a banana in the same hand, "can never be a real monkey. And on this, the day of my daughter's wedding, you come to me and ask me for murder." He considered. "I will be reasonable. Some day, and that day may never come, I will call upon you for a service. Until then, accept this justice as a gift on Connie's wedding day."

Outside, in the sunlight of the Long Island canopy, the wedding swung on. Connie Cooleone, a bride in white fur, danced on the tables while her brother Sonny, a scarred mandrill with a fuse for a temper, slipped into the treeline with a bridesmaid. Johnny Bonzane, the crooning chimp whose voice made weeping females dangle limp from the chandeliers, begged the Don's help with his career: his bandleader boss owned his contract and would not let him go.

"Look at this," said the godfather of groomers, gesturing at the trembling Bonzane. "A beautiful voice, and no spine. You act like a refugee from a deforestation. Fine. I will make the bandleader an offer he can't peel-fuse."

He did it without touching the bandleader at all: the man simply woke one morning to find his prize koi pond empty and a gorilla named Luca Brasi, the size of a fallen tree, sitting at the foot of his bed, feeding the last koi to the cat. By lunch the contract was remembered differently.

The studio was harder. Jack Woltz, the movie magnate of the Hollywood hills, threw Tom Hagen out of his bungalow at midnight, screaming that he would never, never release Bonzane. "Look at my stallion Khartoum!" he raved, pointing at the forty-foot topiary horse that was the pride of his estate, clipped from privet by six gardeners over twelve years. "That's the kind of beautiful thing I protect, and no gutter-ape is going to — " etcetera, etcetera. The next morning Woltz rolled over in his silk sheets and found himself nose to nose with the head of Khartoum, sheared clean off in the night, dew still on its leaves, privet sprouting out of the pillows. Woltz screamed so loudly the flamingos fell out of the trees. Bonzane had the picture role by Friday. But when the Don asked Johnny if he was eating enough, and Johnny said his mother had taught him that a monkey who shares his 'nanas shares his luck — well, that's family.

The Don had four children and one adopted one. Sonny, the fuse. Fredo, the nervous one, all thumbs and apologies. Michael, the youngest — a slim, quiet colobus, a war hero with a medal he never mentioned, the one the family agreed would stay clean, go legit, become Senator Cooleone. And Tom Hagen, a gibbon, the consiglieri, adopted at eight off the streets when Sonny brought him home for supper and he never left.

Trouble came wearing a pineapple.

A dealer named Sollozzo, called the Durian behind his back, wanted the Cooleone fruit-money and their politicians behind a new trade — pushing durian, the spiky fruit, the hard stuff, into every fruit bowl in the city. The Don refused. "It's not the 'nana business," he said. "A monkey will forgive you anything except what he can't digest."

Sollozzo's answer was classic monkey business: two trunks in front of a fruit stand, four shooters in a stolen banana truck. Don Vito took five in the fur and survived, an old ape too tough for the compost. Sonny declared war. Michael, the clean one, the civilian, came to the hospital to stand guard over his father with his knuckles bare and his eyes flat, moving a hospital bed with a nurse's help so the killers would find the room empty — and the baker Nazorine's enforcer-monkeys on the door.

Then Michael said the thing that changed the story. "They want to meet me to settle it," he told Sonny and Tom in the peach-lit kitchen. "Sollozzo and McCluskey, the crooked police baboon, in a little restaurant in the Bronx. I'll plant a plantain in the cistern behind the old lavatory. When I come out of the can, I shoot them both."

They laughed at him, the college boy, the war hero. Sonny laughed hardest. "You? The nice college boy? You want to gun down a police captain — because he slapped your face? This is business and this monkey is taking it personal."

"It's not personal, Sonny," Michael said quietly. "It's strictly banana-ness."

He shot them both, and crossed the water, and hid in the old country, in the stone groves of Sicily, where he walked behind a wall of shepherds with luparas slung like banana bunches, and met a girl named Apollonia and married her in a festival of lemons and gunfire. A car meant for Michael turned her into a light brighter than the noon. Michael did not weep. Something behind his eyes closed like a shop shutter, and never reopened.

Back home the war ground on, and Sonny's fuse found him. Connie's husband Carlo, a pretty spider monkey with a gambler's debts and a coward's heart, beat Connie on purpose, knowing Sonny would come alone across the causeway in a fury. At the tollbooth, the Tattaglia shooters rose out of the tall grass and hit Sonny with everything they had: coconuts, mangoes, one entire hand of plantains. Sonny died in the booth lane, still trying to swing.

The Don called a peace meeting of the five families — Cooleone, Barzini, Tattaglia, Stracci, Cuneo — under the great banyan. "How did things ever get so far?" he asked the circle of silverbacks. "I came here because there's a war over a fruit I refused to sell. Fine. I'll give my protection. But I want my son home safe. And I swear, on the memory of my son Sonny, I will not be the one to break the peace we make here." Barzini, the cleverest ape in the room, believed him. That was Barzini's mistake; a Cooleone promise is a banana — it ripens in the dark.

Michael came home, older, married Kay the gentle langur who had waited, and told her, "In five years the Cooleone family will be completely legitimate," which is a thing monkeys say the way they say the check is in the mail. And old Don Vito, semi-retired among his tomato vines — real tomatoes, the family's one honest crop — taught his son the last lessons. "Never let anyone outside the family know what you're thinking. Never hate your enemies; it clouds your judgment. And keep your friends close, Michael, but your bananas closer."

He died as he lived, comically and without warning: in the gravel of his garden, terrifying his grandson with a banana peel in his mouth, an old ape playing orangutan, and then simply lying down among the seedlings forever. Even the undertaker Bonasera, who owed him, was asked for one last kindness — "I don't want his mother to see him this way" — and the whole neighborhood came, and Barzini came, and at the graveside Barzini's eyes were already redecorating the throne.

It was Michael, not Barzini, who settled the accounts.

On the day he stood godfather to Connie's baby, in the smell of incense and candle smoke, the priest asked Michael the ritual questions, and Michael answered them one by one while, all across the city, his soldiers made calls. Do you renounce the durian? I do renounce it. Moe Greene, shot through the eye mid-pedicure. Barzini, undone on the courthouse steps. Tattaglia, Stracci, Cuneo. Do you renounce Satan and all his works? While Carlo was being driven to the airport with a ticket wired to his tail. Michael took Carlo's collar gently: "Barzini's dead. So that's it. You have to answer for Sonny. Don't tell me you're innocent — you dirtied your own nest, Carlo, admit it. Only don't tell me you're innocent, because it insults my intelligence."

Carlo confessed, was handed a ticket to Vegas, and sat in the car a relieved monkey — until the passenger beside him looped a vine around his neck, gently, almost lovingly. Connie screamed at Michael across the study: monster, murderer, my husband. Kay asked Michael, later, in the ordinary lamplight, whether it was true. "No," said the new Don Cooleone, meeting her eyes with the flat old honesty — and Kay was relieved, and went to pour the coffee, and watched as Clemenza's soldier kissed Michael's hand, and as the study door closed on her forever, the new groomfather of the family already deep in banana-ness.

The last thing Clemenza had said, folding his napkin at the christening lunch, was the family creed: "Leave the peel, take the 'nana-split."

In Sicily they still say the Cooleones ran the whole eastern fruit trade. They ran it the way a spider runs a web: quietly, from a corner, with everything that swings eventually coming home.
