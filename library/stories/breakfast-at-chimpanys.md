# Breakfast at Chimpany's

*A parody of "Breakfast at Tiffany's"*

I am a writer, which is to say a gorilla with a typewriter, and in the autumn of my youth I lived in a brownstone on East Seventy-First Vine, where the fire escape bloomed with stolen orchids and the lady downstairs rang the buzzer at all hours because she had lost her key, her mind, or her 'nana man.

Her name was Holly Go-Lightly, and she was a chimpanzee of no fixed address and every fixed intention. She'd been a barefoot child in some tulip-belt barnyard, married at fourteen to a horse-doctor, which in our world means a zebra-instructor, and by eighteen she had landed in New York Colony like a seed spat from a great height — and grown, somehow, into the most beautiful weed in the garden.

I knew her principally through the window and the buzzer. "Fred!" she'd call — she called every large solemn male Fred, and I was large and solemn enough for three — "darling Fred, I have the mean reds."

"The mean reds?"

"Not scared, Fred. Scared is when you look down and realize the branch ends. The mean reds is when you're scared and you don't know why, and the whole canopy feels like it's closing in, and the only thing that helps is — well. Come downstairs, I'll show you."

What helped, it turned out, was Chimpany's. Chimpany's was the jewelry store on Fifth Vine-ue, all cool glass and quiet silver, where nothing screeched and nothing swung and a monkey could stand at the counter with a 'nana split and feel that nothing bad could ever reach her. Holly had no money for diamonds. What she wanted was to find, somewhere in that hush of pearls, a place with no fences and no names, the way a 'nana feels before anybody peels it.

"Wouldn't it be marvellous," she said, licking chocolate off her knuckles, "to have a little shop like this? Just cases and cases of nice things, and everybody who comes in has to whisper. I'd call it something swell. Not Holly's. I haven't got a real name, Fred. Not one that fits. Names tag you like a collar. The mean reds can't get you if you don't have a tag."

She paid her rent, more or less, by visiting a mob orangutan named Sally Topmato in the federal cage up the river. Fifty dollars a visit, cash, delivered to the powder room by way of the lawyer's office. Holly honestly believed she was just cheering up an old invalid. She'd come back and repeat Sally's little weather reports to the lawyer — "warm front coming, fog on the bay" — innocent as a baby bushbaby, and fifty dollars the richer, and off to swing through El Morocco's with a different millionaire each night.

And what nights. I went to one of her parties and barely survived it. The treehouse was wall to wall primates: a Brazilian diplomat-tamarin named José who loved Holly sincerely, which appalled her; Rusty Trawler, a fat golden-lion heir who hoped somebody would tell him what to do and nobody ever had; Mag Wildwood, a model stork-ibis-voiced glamour ape who moved in on the apartment upstairs and Rusty downstairs; and a landlady screaming in the doorway about the furniture, the floor, the fur.

"That's the apartment," Holly explained, stepping over a fallen guest. "Somebody has to be lying on the floor. Hello, darling, you're stepping on a count."

The parties were the froth. Underneath she was running — from the barnyard, from the horse-doctor, from the word *wife*. Then the horse-doctor himself arrived, huge and weathered, a soft-eyed old vet named Doc Golightly, come to take Holly home. He'd brought with him, without telling her, a crate of live poultry for the yard. He honestly thought she was still the child bride, still fond of jam on the porch.

"Fred," she told me after, twisting a peel into a knot, "I told him to take those birds back to the trees they came from. I am *not* Lulamae anymore. I told him, you must tell Fred — that's you, he thinks you're my brother, I let him — that I am a different animal. I'm not going to be anybody's chore."

The Doc looked at me a long time. "She's got no tail-hold," he said sadly, "and no roots neither. Take care of her, brother." Then he drove his truck of shrieking chickens back to wherever forgiveness lives.

Winter came. José, the tamarin, did the unthinkable: he offered Holly a ring and a name and a ranch full of little tamarins, and she almost said yes, because being somebody's tag is better than being nobody's, or so she was tired enough to think. She told the cracker-jack ring she wore — a toy from a prize box, the one thing she never took off — that it would have to resign in favor of a real one.

Then Sally Topmato's weather reports came back with handcuffs attached. The old ape had been moving banana-futures tips through Holly's pretty mouth for a year, and the federals wrapped up the whole syndicate at once. They wrapped up Holly too, at seven in the morning, while her hair was still wet.

I bailed her out with my book money. She stood in the doorway of the precinct with her fur askew and her dignity astonishing, and I told her about Sally Topmato's fifty-dollar forecasts, and I watched the last of her barnyard innocence fall off her like a coat.

"That's the trouble, Fred," she said quietly. "You get the mean reds because you know too much, and you keep the mean reds because you can't say it. I've got to go where the weather means weather. José is finished with me — a girl on the front page of the Gazette, his government would go bananas. I'm going to Brazil, darling. Everything matures earlier in Brazil."

She was packed in an hour, because after all she owned nothing but the cracker-jack ring and one suitcase of costumes. At the curb she stooped and picked up the stray bushbaby she'd been half-keeping — a tiny quarrelsome thing with dishwater eyes that she had refused to name, because naming is tagging, and tagging is belonging.

"We're a couple of no-name slobs," she told it, and got in the cab, and rolled up the window, and thirty feet down the block she stopped the driver.

Because it had begun to rain, hard, and the little bushbaby had jumped out — I never saw how — and was streaking along the gutter boxes, wet and blind and screaming, and Holly was out of the cab in her thin coat, running flat-footed through the puddles, screeching into the rain, and the bushbaby wedged itself in a crate and hissed like a teakettle, and she hauled it out by the scruff, furious at it, furious at herself, holding it against her throat.

"You, bushbaby," she said, soaking, mascara gone, hat ruined, "are a pill and a mistake and I don't want you, and you'd better know that about me. There. Now get under my collar where it's dry."

She looked up and found me under an awning, useless and rain-jeweled, holding her forgotten suitcase.

"Fred, darling," she said, "I do believe you're looking at a monkey who has just made a colossal mistake on purpose."

I have since learned — because she writes, on paper smelling of airplane fuel and tropical fruit — that Brazil is warm and the ranches are enormous and there is, in the whole city of Rio, exactly one Chimpany's, a little hush of silver and glass where a 'nana may ripen in peace. She signs the letters with the name the bushbaby finally gave her, which is the name she gave herself, which is not Holly and not Lulamae, but whatever it is, it fits.

She says the mean reds come less often now. She says you can't run from them anyway; you only end up running into yourself, and yourself is the only animal in the jungle that can bite you and kiss you in the same hour. Then she says the bushbaby — the beast is enormous now, and vain, and sleeps in the sink — sends its love to Uncle Fred.

And here I sit at my typewriter in the brownstone, a large solemn gorilla with a column to fill, wondering about the difference between a cage and a house, and a tag and a name, and finding that the answer, like Holly, is somewhere out there in the rain, warm under somebody's collar.
