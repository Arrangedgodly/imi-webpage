# The Emperor's New Groves

*A parody of "The Emperor's New Clothes"*

Long, long ago there lived an Emperor Baboon named Hondo, who was so exceedingly fond of new groves that he spent all his bananas on them. He had a grove for every hour of the day, and when the good monkeys of the capital said, "The Emperor is in his grove," everyone knew exactly what time it was. His council of ministers learned to schedule wars, harvests, and treaty-negotiations for the one hour a day in which Hondo happened to be between groves — which is to say, the empire was governed during tea.

Of the Emperor's grooming, of his processions, of his new groves — of these, and nothing else, the whole city talked. "He wore the Fig-Grove-Fancy this morning." "No, no, that was yesterday's Sunset Arbor." "I heard he has commissioned a grove of golden banana trees so rare that —" and here every voice dropped to a whisper, because nobody knew the end of that sentence yet.

One evening two rogues came to town: two sleek capuchins calling themselves Slide and Shuffle, Grove-Planters and Leaf-Spinners to the Crown. And they had heard a thing or two about Emperor Hondo.

"Your Majesty," said they, bowing very low, "we grow and weave the finest grove in all the world: golden banana trees whose leaves can be spun into robes of the rarest silk — so light a monkey could swing fifty branches and never feel them. And the cloth is so rare that it is invisible to anyone unfit for his post, or unusually dim."

"Ah," said the Emperor, who privately worried about both of those things. "That is precisely my kind of grove. What do you require?"

The capuchins asked for a trunkful of golden bananas, a crate of the finest liana silk, kapok stuffing, thorn-proof gloves, and — to keep the moths off — the Emperor's spare crown. Everything was delivered at once, and the swindlers' paws trembled only slightly with the effort of keeping straight faces.

Then they set up two empty looms in the royal treehouse and pretended to plant and weave, clawing at nothing till late in the night.

"I should like to know how they are getting on," thought the Emperor — but he felt a little queer about the invisible-to-the-dim clause. So he sent his honest old minister, a mandrill with medals for grooming.

The poor old mandrill stared at the empty loom until his eyes watered. "Bless my scales, I can't see a thing!" thought he. "Am I unfit? Nobody must know."

"Does not Your Excellency admire the pattern?" asked Slide, holding up nothing at all.

"Admire it! Er — describe it to me once more," the minister managed, "that I may admire it more precisely."

"Certainly," said Shuffle, delighted. "Blossoms of spun gold upon a ground of living green, with hovering hummingbirds, and fig-leles at every border."

"Fig-what?"

"Fig-leles. A very rare pattern. Very rare indeed."

"Magnificent!" said the poor mandrill, sweating freely. "Such a green! Those hummingbirds! Those — fig-leles!" And he memorized every word the swindlers said, to repeat to the Emperor — who was so charmed by "fig-leles" that he promoted the mandrill on the spot, which was, everyone agreed afterwards, exactly the wrong lesson.

The Emperor next sent a high official of the court: a colobus with impeccable manners and, as it turned out, no eyesight whatsoever. He felt the empty air between his paws and pronounced it "a weave of the very highest order — soft yet firm, with a sheen." Afterwards the two officials met on a branch and compared notes.

"Do you see it?" whispered the mandrill.

"Of course I see it," said the colobus, lying.

"As do I," said the mandrill, lying better. And they parted, each privately relieved, each privately certain the other was dim.

At last the Emperor went himself, with the whole court trailing behind. He looked at the empty looms and went cold to the very tip of his tail.

"Am I unfit to be Emperor?" thought he. "That would be the most dreadful thing that could happen to me."

"Will not His Majesty carry the pattern in his heart?" murmured Slide. "Everyone else does."

"Hmm! Magnificent! Splendid!" announced the Emperor, loudly, to the enormous relief of everyone present. "I shall wear them at the Grand Grooming Gala!"

The capuchins asked for, and got, a second trunkful of golden bananas "for the royal trimmings," and worked all the next night by candle-fruit light, snipping the air with empty scissors — and before dawn they slipped away with the bananas, the silk, the kapok, and the spare crown, giggling all the way down the hill to the next kingdom.

Gala morning. The chamberlains dressed the Emperor in his magnificent new robes — which is to say, in nothing at all — while Slide and Shuffle's farewell instructions were read aloud from a leaf.

"Do they hang well from the shoulder?" asked the Emperor, turning before the mirror, and shivered a little, which he attributed to the material's famous lightness.

"How they swing with you, sire! Light as leaf-silk!" cried the chamberlain, lifting an invisible hem — and here is a thing worth knowing: an invisible hem must be held EXACTLY, and the chamberlain had practiced all morning with a piece of air.

Then out came the Emperor into the gala, walking proudly beneath a canopy of woven palms, and the officers of the troop marched behind him, solemnly carrying an invisible bunch of golden bananas — for none of them dared admit they were carrying air. The Master of Ceremonies announced, in a voice that cracked only twice, "Behold the robes of the Golden Grove!" and the whole canopy burst into applause.

"God bless the Emperor's new groves!" cried the crowds from every branch. "Look at his robes! Look at his train of golden bananas! Look at that FIG-LELE border!" — for everybody had heard about the fig-leles by now, and nobody wanted the neighbors to think them dim, or unfit. Two old uncles nearly came to blows over whose view of the invisible train had been the finer.

And then a little voice came up from the low branches, where a tiny macaque sat on her mother's shoulders.

"But the Emperor hasn't got anything on!"

"Gracious goodness, hear the child!" said her father; and one monkey whispered it to the next, exactly as the child had said it. "He hasn't got anything on! The Emperor's bare! The Emperor's bare-bottomed!" And the whisper swung from branch to branch like wildfire.

"I am NOT dim," said a dowager tamarin to her sister, and then, in a very small voice, "but neither, apparently, is the child." And one by one, all along the gala route, the crowd began — very gently at first, and then all at once — to laugh.

The Emperor knew perfectly well they were right; and he shivered again, and this time he knew it was not the material. But he thought, "I must go through with it." So he drew himself up more proudly than ever, and the officers marched behind, carrying the invisible golden bananas with even greater dignity than before.

That night, alone on his top branch, Emperor Hondo held an inquiry. Of the golden bananas, the silk, the kapok, and his spare crown there was no sign; Slide and Shuffle were by then two kingdoms away, selling an invisible orchard. So the Emperor sent for the tiny macaque instead.

"Child," he said, "you are hereby appointed Royal Truthteller, at one banana a week, and your only duty is to go on saying exactly what you see."

"Is that hard?" asked the child.

"Apparently," sighed the Emperor, "it is the hardest work in the empire."

And the groves? Not so much as a shoot came up — but no monkey in the capital ever said so out loud. The Emperor had new groves for every hour of the day; the only trouble was that nobody, ever, could see the bananas. Though after that day, whenever anyone asked where the Emperor was, the answer was always the same, and for the first time in years always true: "The Emperor is in his grove — the one real one, in plain sight, where he can actually be found."
