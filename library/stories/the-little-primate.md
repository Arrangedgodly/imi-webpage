# The Little Primate

*A parody of "The Little Prince"*

Once when I was six I read that pythons swallow their prey whole, without chewing, and then cannot move for six months. I drew a python with a tapir inside it, so everybody would know to be careful. Grown-ups took one look and said, "Why have you drawn a hat?" Grown-ups never understand anything by themselves, and it is exhausting for little monkeys to keep explaining. So I gave up drawing and became a pilot, and I have flown ever since over the great green canopy of the world.

Something went wrong with my hopper over the Great Sandwash — the one desert in the middle of everything, the one place with nothing to swing from — and I set her down harder than I meant to, alone, with water enough for a week.

At daybreak I was woken by a funny little voice.

"Please," it said, "draw me a loris."

Here was the strangest little primate, hardly bigger than a guenon's thumb, gold as a ripe banana, peering up with great serious eyes. A monkey in the middle of a desert, a thousand trees from anywhere.

"Draw me a loris," he repeated, "to comb my fur. Where I come from, it is very small."

"Where do you come from, little primate?"

"That is a very big question," he said, and watched the sunrise. "From up there. From Branch B-612."

Branch B-612 is a treetop island that drifts through the sky, so small you can only see it with a spyglass, if you know where to point it. On it were three volcanoes, a jungle the size of a bath mat, and one proud banana blossom he loved. He sheltered her under a glass dome at night and pulled up the strangler-fig sprouts each morning before they could crack his branch in two. Still the blossom sighed and preened and said, "Let the hornets come with their stings! I have my four thorns." And because she was a little vain, and he a little young, he had taken the migrating parrots and gone.

He told me about his journey, one branch at a time, in the evenings while I worked on the engine.

There was the Branch of the King, who commanded him to be his prime mate, and was delighted to be obeyed, because that made him a king. "I judge myself," the king admitted, "and it is much harder than judging others. But then I pardon myself, and everything is in order." There was the Branch of the Conceited One, who desired only applause — "Clap, then! Salute! No, no — I cannot salute myself, you must do it for me." To him every other monkey in the jungle was an admirer, which was restful, since he never had to meet any. There was the Branch of the Tippler, who sat before a tower of gourds of fermented sap. "Why do you drink?" — "To forget that I am ashamed." — "Ashamed of what?" — "Of drinking." And the little primate left that branch feeling quite low about grown-ups. There was the Branch of the Business Monkey, who counted all the bananas in the world, wrote the number on a leaf, and owned the leaf — "I am a serious monkey, me; I have no time for combs" — and the little primate couldn't see the difference between him and a drunkard, except the paperwork.

But there was one who was different: the smallest, loneliest branch of all, where a night monkey kept the lamp lit on a branch that spun so fast its whole day lasted a minute. "The orders haven't changed," said the lamplighter. "Good. The tragedy of old age is when the orders change." Light it, put it out; light it, put it out — he never once rested, and never once complained, because he was thinking of something besides himself. "He is the only one who isn't ridiculous," the little primate told me. "He could have been a friend."

And there was the Branch of the Geographer, an old gray uakari surrounded by maps of places he had never once visited. "I don't explore. I am too important. What is your branch like — does it have mountains? Volcanoes? Good. And blossoms?" — "Oh, yes, the most beautiful blossom—" — "We don't record blossoms," said the geographer. "They are ephemeral." The little primate felt his whole heart go hollow. His blossom was ephemeral, and he had left her behind with four thorns and a glass dome and his silly quarrel. "Where would you advise me to visit?" he asked, and the geographer said, "The canopy of the world. It has a good reputation," and that is how he came to the ground, to the desert, to me.

The first creature he met on Earth was a snake, a gold ring lying in the dust.

"Whomever I bite, I send back to the earth they came from," said the snake. "But you are innocent, you come from a star. I am sorry for you, little primate, so gentle on this grain of sand." And the snake coiled around his own ankle like a bracelet and slid away, which is how snakes say goodbye.

Then came the blossoms. He climbed the first ridge of the canopy and found himself in a garden of five thousand banana blossoms, every one of them hers. "I thought I was rich with a flower unique in all the world," he said. "All I own is an ordinary blossom and three volcanoes the size of my foot." And he laid down in the grass and cried, which is the only sensible thing to do at such a moment, whatever the grown-ups say.

On my third day in the desert he found a bush baby dozing in a hollow gourd.

"Groom me, please," said the bush baby. "I am looking for friends. What does 'tame' mean?"

"It means to form ties," said the little primate.

"I hunt at night and sleep by day, and you itch in a desert. Nothing about you tempts me — you are only one little primate, like a hundred thousand little primates. But if you groom me, my fur and your fingers shall need each other. You will be the only little primate in the world to me, and I shall be the only bush baby in the world to you."

So he groomed her, little by little, at the same hour each day. "Come at four," she said, "and from three o'clock I shall begin to be happy." And she taught him her last lesson: "You become responsible, forever, for what you have groomed. Men buy everything ready-made from the bazaar, but there is no bazaar anywhere where one can buy a friend, so monkeys have no friends anymore. Groom one. What is essential is invisible to the eye. One sees clearly only with the heart — and, on bad days, through clean fur."

Now he understood the garden of five thousand blossoms, too, and told me so. "I have one blossom, and she is more than all five thousand, because I watered her, and glassed her, and quarreled with her, and listened to her complain and boast, and even to her silence. Because she is my blossom. No bazaar sells that either."

On the eighth day my engine was mended. We had found a well in the desert at dawn — he insisted, and he was right; what makes a desert beautiful is that somewhere it hides a well — and the water was sweet as a festival. "The stars are wells too," he said, "and on my branch I shall have a well that laughs."

That evening the migrating parrots came down around him like a rain of red leaves.

"Don't come and watch," he said gently. "It will look too much like a monkey falling, and that is not what it is. It is too heavy, a body; a body is a sad old crate to leave behind on the sand. Look at the canopy instead. I shall be on Branch B-612, with my blossom under her dome, laughing at the hornets. And when you look at the stars, since I shall be laughing on one of them, it will be as if all the stars are laughing for you."

When I looked again he was gone, softly, the way a leaf lets go.

I have flown over that desert many times since, and when I look up at the great green canopy at night, I no longer hear silence. Somewhere, someplace, some monkey is grooming some monkey — and the whole jungle sounds to me like five hundred million little grooming combs, softly ticking, and all the stars laugh.

Draw me a branch with a star on it, if you ever pass that way. Look after the blossoms. And be patient with the grown-ups: they were all little primates once, even the ones in hats.
