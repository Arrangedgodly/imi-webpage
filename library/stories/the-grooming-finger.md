# The Grooming Finger

*A parody of "The Moving Finger"*

Jerry Burrows, a spider monkey with one mended shoulder from a gliding accident and a doctor's orders to be quiet somewhere with trees, took a house in the little canopy town of Lymforest and brought his sister Joanna to keep him out of trouble. Joanna was a glamorous young howler who considered "quiet" a form of insult, and within a day of arriving they had received their first letter.

It was typed, ill-spelled, and smelled faintly of the sender.

*YOU ARE NOT WELCOME. I NO ALL ABOUT YOUR FUR. IT IS MANGY UNDERNEATH. GO HOME.*

"The typing alone is a confession," said Joanna, holding it at arm's length. "No self-respecting secretary did this. And what does it mean, *mangy underneath*?"

"It means," said Jerry, "that we are now residents of Lymforest. Congratulations. Everyone gets one."

Everyone, it appeared, had. The village ran on anonymous letters the way other villages ran on gossip: fuel of the poorest grade, but abundant. There was poison in the post for the parson (a gentle langur who grieved visibly over each envelope), for the doctor, for the chemist's wife, for Elsie Holland, the golden-baboon governess, and — fatally — for Mrs. Simyangton, wife of the solicitor, who received hers one morning, took her tea to her room, and died of poison before lunch. Beside her, a note in her own hand: *I can't go on.*

The coroner said suicide, as coroners do when a monkey's own paw has held the cup. But that same afternoon a little marmoset named Agnes, the Simyangtons' parlourmaid, went missing — and was found next day in the linen chest, strangled with the clothes-line she had gone out to peg.

A village that will read an anonymous letter aloud to six neighbours will not strangle a marmoset over one, and Lymforest, to its credit, went collectively pale. It was at roughly this point that an elderly langur lady with soft silver fur and eyes like two patient blackberries called upon the Burrowses. She was staying with the parson, and her name was Miss Maple.

"Of Nana Mead," she said, accepting a chair and a small cherry brandy with the confidence of long practice. "My friend the parson is quite prostrated. These letters, you see — I have met them before. They breed, Mr. Burrows, like fleas. One ignorant scratcher writes a dozen, and then a dozen idle malcontents write one each for the fun of it, and soon the whole village is mangy underneath, wherever you look. But the *first* letter is not written for fun." She sipped. "It is written for a purpose. Find the letter with a purpose in it, and you have your murderer."

The police, meanwhile, arrested the obvious suspect: Aimée Griffon, the doctor's sister, a large energetic female who baked like a howler in June and had been visibly feeding her own temper for years. She confessed, tearfully and furiously, to *one* letter — an ugly note she had sent to Elsie Holland out of sheer clogged fury — and to nothing else, which was inconvenient, as she had not in fact done anything else.

"She is a nuisance, not a criminal," said Miss Maple. "Whereas the author of all this is a monkey who has committed the one crime anonymous letters are *for*."

"Which is?" asked Joanna.

"Manufactering the wrong explanation, my dear, at the right moment. Mrs. Simyangton did not write that note, and did not take that poison by choice. Her husband gave it to her, and typed her a letter first to make the story stand up. Notice — I have noticed — that the letter addressed to poor Mrs. Simyangton has never actually been *produced*. The maid told a friend there'd been no post at all that morning worth naming. Which is why the maid is in the linen chest."

And then Miss Maple said the terrible quiet thing she had come to say. "Mr. Burrows — you are the only monkey in this village who has been attacked in the open, on your own doorstep. Whoever it is has marked you. Will you do exactly as I ask, and nothing more?"

Jerry, who had been doing precisely as he pleased since the age of three, agreed on the spot. This should have warned him.

He had one card of his own. A letter had appeared in the pocket of his own coat, hung in his own hall — *seen by no postman, met by no maid* — and he had shown it to only one monkey: Mr. Simyangton, the widower, a dry, upright simian with a beautiful profile and a gone look in the back of his eyes. So when Jerry was summoned, two evenings later, by a wire saying his sister was ill — and when that same evening Megan, the Simyangtons' gawky adolescent daughter (a young gorilla whom Joanna had recently taken in paw, groomed, and transformed into somebody who suspected she might be a person), was sent for urgently by her father — Jerry did not take the slow road.

He took the window of the Simyangtons' study, shoulder and all, and found the gas fire hissing full on in the sealed room, and Megan lying limp in the fumes, and Mr. Simyangton sitting at his desk waiting for her to finish dying.

"He'll be told it was her temper," said the solicitor dreamily, as Jerry smashed the sash and hauled Megan's dead weight toward the air. "The girl took her mother's death badly. There are letters to prove it. There are letters to prove *anything*, you know. That is the beauty of the modern post."

Behind Jerry, the door opened on the entire apparatus of the law, arranged by a small silver langur with the timing of a professional. "I do hope," said Miss Maple, stepping over the sill with a smell of sherry and worsted, "that I have done right. It is so difficult to know, arranging a thing beforehand. But he would have to finish, you see. All his letters were written to *finish* things."

Mr. Simyangton did not resist. They seldom do, once the gas is off and the arithmetic begins. He had loved his wife's money and his children's governess, in that order, and when his wife had declined to oblige him with a quiet divorce he had manufactured her suicide instead, and every letter for miles had been his smoke-screen — posted, when convenient, to himself. The girl Agnes he had killed because she had noticed there was no morning post at all. Megan he had decided to spare the trouble of inheriting.

"And the *motive*," said Jerry bitterly, to Miss Maple, some weeks after, "was Elsie Holland. Who has now, I suppose, married him in prison."

"No, dear. She is engaged to the doctor, who has been devoted to her all along, which shows how much attention murderers pay." Miss Maple settled into her chair at Nana Mead, where Joanna was writing to Jerry twice a week for no stated reason, and Megan was eating buns like a young gorilla determined to grow into her new fur. "Everyone in Lymforest now reads anonymous letters with great suspicion and aloud, which will keep them honest for a decade."

Jerry looked over at Megan, asleep in the sun, breathing. He thought about the letter that had said his fur was mangy underneath, and how it had been written by a monkey who was himself mangy all the way through.

"Tell me, Miss Maple. How does it end? The rhyme all this keeps running through my head — *the moving finger writes, and having writ, moves on.*"

"That is not the jungle version, Mr. Burrows," said Miss Maple, taking up her knitting. "In Nana Mead we say it rather differently:

*The grooming finger picks, and, having picked, moves on:*
*nor all thy pity, nor thy fine-tooth comb,*
*shall lure one single flea of it to return.*

Which means, my dear boy," she added, peacefully, "that the past does not scratch you. It only holds still while you scratch it. And *that*, thank goodness, is the only thing a monkey can neither post, forge, nor steal."

Jerry Burrows married Megan in the spring. The whole of Lymforest came, and nobody received a single letter about it, which everyone agreed was the nicest present of all.
