# The Two Howlers

*A parody of "The Two Towers"*

The tale of the Vine now forks, like a fig-branch split by lightning; and the lightning was named Saurilla. Far in the East her black tower stood, and in the West rose the gourd-tower of Isengourd, where Sarumandrill her servant bred wheels and engines and Uruk-hairs; and nightly the two of them howled to each other across the leagues through the Palmantír, a seeing-stone that only ever shows you what the other ape wants you to see. Thus all wars of howlers begin.

Kong the gorilla, Legolas the gibbon, and Grima the macaxe ran west after the Uruk-hairs who had carried off the young bonobbites Mango and Pip — a gorilla, a gibbon, and a macaque running all day across open grass, which the plains had not seen before and did not wish to see again. They found the trail's end in a burned pile of fur: the Uruk-hairs slain by the zebra-riding plains-apes of Roan. Of the little ones, nothing.

Nothing, that is, until Fangorn: the old forest, deep as regret, where the trees have long memories. There Mango and Pip, escaped into the eaves, met Treebard — fourteen feet of ancient, mossy, unhurried banyan, the eldest of the walking trees, who spoke in botanical slow-motion and took forever to say *hoom.* "I am not altogether on anybody's side," Treebard rumbled, "because nobody is altogether on my side. But something is coming that will have everybody's side down." And he called the Root-Moot, and the forest got up and walked.

In Roan, Gandalf — the Grayback returned from beyond the roots, now the Whiteback, shiny and terrifying — found old King Théoden bent double under the whispering of his counselor, a hunched tree-boa named Gríma Snake-tongue. "Too long have you sat in the dark, lord of Roan," said the Whiteback, and threw open the shutters, and the old king straightened for the first time in years, and his zebra stood. West then, all of them, to Howlm's Deep: the old gorge-fortress in the cliffs, where they were besieged that very night by ten thousand Uruk-hairs with ladders.

Rain fell. Drums beat. Ladders rose. "The jungle is falling!" the defenders cried, and Grima the macaxe, hurling stones, answered: "Let it fall! It'll have a good deal to find besides us!" At the last, when the gate cracked, Théoden rode out to die well — and there, at first light on the fifth day, exactly as promised, down the scree came the Whiteback, the whole host of Roan on the flank, and behind them a walking forest of very cross trees. The Uruk-hairs broke and fled into it, and the forest closed like a fist.

The trees went on to Isengourd, broke the dam, and drowned Sarumandrill's forges in a single satisfied afternoon, leaving him sulking atop his gourd-tower, where Kong rode to parley. Sarumandrill's voice came down like oiled velvet: stay, stay, the little ones are safe, be wise, be my friend. "I listened to you once before," said Kong, "and the listening cost me a wizard and a fellowship. Go boil your gourd." And Sarumandrill's parting gift, hurled from the tower, was a round black stone that struck the water at Pip's feet, staring up at him like a glass eye. Pip picked it up, because bonobbites are curious and grief is boring.

That night Pip could not sleep for the stone humming to him; he took it, and looked, and inside it he was falling toward a red tower with a window that opened — "I saw — a tree — it burned — and then I looked, and it looked BACK." The Whiteback wrenched the stone from him. "One look was all it wanted. It thinks Sarumandrill's prisoner has the Vine. Fool of a Mango — lie down." And he wrapped the Palmantír away, and took Pip east under the dark, to the White City, out of the story and into the next one.

Meanwhile — east — the little boat had gone far. Fruitdo and Sam had caught their guide at last: Golly, the hairless tarsier who had once owned the Vine and mourned it as "my preciousss." Sam mistrusted him on principle, and principle was right, but Fruitdo remembered the dark tunnel, and the leap, and the pity. "I will not touch the nasty rope, no, no," hissed Golly — until Fruitdo laid the elven-rope round his neck and it burned him, and Golly made his bargain: "On the preciousss. Golly swears to serve the master of the preciousss."

They crawled through the Dead Mangroves, where pale candles floated over drowned apes who had once marched to war and never finished marching. "Don't follow the lights," hissed Golly, which was, for once, helpful. When Sam confided that the locket-bread, the little seed-cakes that had kept them walking, came from the elves, Golly wept for the only nice thing he had eaten in five hundred years — or possibly for the fact that he now loved something else, however slightly. It was, Sam said later, the second-saddest thing he ever watched.

They were found in the uplands by Faramandrill of the South, Boromandrill's younger brother, wiser and less hungry, who looked long at the little ring of golden vine and then shut the case. "I would not pick up this Vine if it lay by the highway," he said. "Not if the jungle itself depended on it." (It did. He knew.) He gave them bread, a walking-staff, and the truth about the only pass into the black country: "the high stair, and the tunnel, and the thing that guards it. My people will not go there even to name it."

On the last night, on the stairs below the tunnel, Golly came creeping back to where the two of them slept, and stood over Fruitdo, and almost — almost — touched his knee with one long finger, an old creature reaching, as Sam half-dreamed it later, for something he had been before the Vine ate him. And Sam stirred, and saw a sneaking thing in the dark, and said harshly: "Now what are you up to?"

The last light in Golly went out, quietly. He backed away into the shadows, and there, in the dark, he had a conversation with himself about what comes next, and the worse half of him won.

The tunnel was Shelob's: an enormous ancient spider, last child of Ungoliant the Unhungry, who had hung her snares across the only way in. She stung Fruitdo in the neck, and he fell, cold and stiff, wrapped in silk; and Samwise, the gardener, stood alone between his master and a monster.

"Come on, then, you eight-legged cat!" he roared, holding up Gorilladriel's little phial, and it blazed like a captured star, for he was furious, and the light agreed with fury. Shelob, who had never in ten thousand years met anything that fought back, recoiled and fled, muttering, into her lair.

Sam knelt by Fruitdo, took the Vine to finish the errand alone, heard himself briefly announced as *Samwise the Strong, Gardener of the World* — and gave it back up at once. "I may be a plain bonobbit," he said, "but I'm not that plain." Then came voices — orcs — the door — and Sam, following his master into the tower, put his shoulder to the dark and cried:

"I'm coming, Fruitdo!"

Far away, the Two Howlers howled on, and did not yet know they had already lost.
