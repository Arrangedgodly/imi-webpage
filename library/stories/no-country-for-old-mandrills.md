# No Country for Old Mandrills

*A parody of "No Country for Old Men"*

Sheriff Ed Tom Bell, first. Ed Tom came from a line of lawkeeping mandrills and had been sheriff of this troop for thirty-one years, and in the old days a sheriff mostly untangled monkeys from their own bad judgment. He always figured when he got older God would come into his life some way or other. He was not angry about it. He just couldn't seem to find Him. What he mostly felt, recent, was old — and the country he came up in getting younger and meaner than any country he knew how to sheriff.

It began, as these things do, with a monkey out before light.

Llewelyn Moss, a mandrill with a face like a stained-glass window gone wrong, was hunting the wild fig groves beyond the troop's range when he came over a rise and found the trucks. Six of them in a wash below, doors hanging open, monkeys dead all around — a regular auction of failed monkey business. The last truck held the only survivor, a young capuchin with his legs full of holes.

"Moss," the capuchin rasped. "You wouldn't have no water about you?"

Moss gave him the last swallow from his gourd. Two miles on he found the last runner: dead in the grass, the satchel still under him, stuffed with banded notes — more money than a troop spends in ten lifetimes. Pure bananas, and every bill of it trouble.

He took the satchel home to his mate Carla Jean, hid it in the air vent, which is the first place trouble always looks, and — because he was hard-headed but not heartless — went back that night with water for the capuchin. Trouble was waiting. There was a transceiver hidden in with the money, and it was already moving.

It was called Anton Chiguerrilla.

A small capuchin who moved with an odd, unhurried calm, like a monkey following instructions nobody else could hear. He carried, of all things, a coconut-knocker — the long-handled tool grove-keepers use to bring down coconuts — and had discovered that pressed against a lock it opened the lock, and pressed against other things it opened those too. At the trailer park where Moss had been staying, he stood at the counter while the little old attendant monkey sweated, spun a coin, and said:

"What's the most you ever lost on a coin toss?"

"Sir?"

"Call it."

"Mister, I got to close."

"Yes, you do. You been putting it off your whole life. Call it, friendo."

The old monkey called it and won, and Chiguerrilla walked out into the noon with his knocker over his shoulder and nothing changed, which was somehow worse.

The men who owned the money — suits, cologne, no scars — hired it done, and when Chiguerrilla proved unmanageable, they hired Carson Mangabey to clean up after him, which is like trading a leopard for a smaller leopard with invoices. Mangabey was a grizzled freelance olive baboon who found Moss in a border hospital, standing over him while the drips went in. "You can't bargain with him," Mangabey said. "You especially. He told me to tell you that. Give me the money and I can get you terms, protection, maybe."

"You work for them."

"I work for whoever's alive on payday. You seen the papers you signed? The money, it isn't even theirs, and it surely ain't yours. You're carrying another monkey's trouble."

Moss sent him off with a flat look and a flatter no. "I'll make him a deal directly," he said. "Tell him I'm goin' to bring him somethin' all right. Tell him I've decided to make him a special project of mine."

Mangabey didn't get to report it. Chiguerrilla found him first, in a hotel with bad art, and Mangabey stood there in his socks going on about principles — "Do you have any idea how crazy you are?" — and Chiguerrilla listened all the way through, the way you listen to weather, and said only: "If the rule you followed brought you to this, of what use was the rule?" Then the line went dead somewhere, and when Moss rang the room that night, it was Chiguerrilla who answered, and offered his terms: the money and the man, and the woman would be let go. Moss told him what he thought of the terms and hung up on a dial tone that sounded satisfied.

Moss ran, and it did no good. He crawled through the ductwork of one motor lodge, satchel on his back — a monkey in the vents, his one home advantage — and still the shots came through the drywall. At another, on a hot dead street, he saw the pickup waiting and stepped out with the shotgun, and the two of them shot up half a block of jungle at midnight, and Moss took buckshot in the side and Chiguerrilla took it in the leg, and both limped off leaking into the dark. Moss hobbled to the bridge and pitched the satchel over the rail into the weeds for later, and there was, as it turned out, no later. At the Fig Palm Motor Lodge it ended the way it had been ending since the wash: off in the dark, without warning, before Sheriff Bell could get there with his hat in his hand. Bell stood at the curtain afterwards, jaw set, and understood, finally, that you can't stop what's coming. It ain't all waiting on you. That's vanity.

He'd said as much to his uncle Ellis, a one-eyed old gelada on a porch, who'd snorted. "What you got ain't nothin' new. This country's hard on people. Harder than anybody knows. You can't stop what's comin'. Ain't all waitin' on you. That's vanity." Bell said being told the same thing twice by two dead generations was itself a kind of country.

Chiguerrilla came for Carla Jean at her mother's funeral, walking beside her with the hat brim low, patient as arithmetic. Her mother, dying, had told him all about Loretta's baby and the family's business generally, monologuing through the hospice window at a total stranger, which Carla Jean said figured.

"You don't have to do this," she said.

"Yes, I do. Your husband had the chance to give the money back. He chose otherwise. Everything that comes now is the fruit of his choosing." He held up the coin. "Call it."

"No," said Carla Jean, standing very straight. "The coin don't have no say. It's just you." She would not call it, and he walked out of that house alone, checking his boots at the door, and what happened in it stayed in it.

At a crossing outside the city, Chiguerrilla's car met a truck that ran a red light, and the paramedics found him limping down the shoulder, the bone of one arm pushed through the skin, his face gone calm and terrible — a wounded animal that would not be caught. Two boys on bicycles watched him splint his own arm with a bed slat, and one of them went home that evening fifty dollars richer for a shirt, sworn to silence, terrified, and right to be. Then Chiguerrilla was gone into the mesquite, and that was the last anyone saw.

Sheriff Bell retired soon after and sat out evenings on the porch with his wife, trying to say what had changed, and mostly failing, because the true history of the world is a history of monkeys failing to say what changed.

Mostly he thought about a dream. In it he was a yearling again in the high passes, and his father rode past him through the snow with a hollow gourd of fire cradled against his chest, going on ahead to make a camp in all that dark and cold. He knew his father would be waiting for him there.

And then he woke up.
