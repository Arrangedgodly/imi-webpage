# Mosshowler

*A parody of "Mossflower"*

The monkeys of Mosshowler forest had one habit that couldn't be broken: not one of them could resist a feast. Which was why Bella of Barkhall, the blind silverback matriarch, was feeding a starving young mandrill in her hall beneath the roots while soldiers of the tyrant Kotir emptied the forest's larders, taking every banana, every fig, every last cocoa bean as "tax."

"I know that face," Bella said, turning her clouded eyes on the guest. "It's out of my grandmother's stories. You're of Mandrill's line."

"I'm nobody's line," said the mandrill. "I had a father's sword, and Tsarmina has it now, in pieces."

Tsarmina Greneyes, Queen of Kotir — a brindled snow-macaque with a crown she'd given herself and a temper she'd never hidden — ruled Mosshowler from her squat stone fort on the flooded mere. Her weasels and stoats, gone soft from easy bullying, emptied the forest's stores and called it order. When the wandering mandrill spoke against her, she had her blacksmith snap his father's sword to shards before his eyes and threw him in a cell to think about respect.

It should be said that Tsarmina had not inherited the throne so much as poisoned it. Her father Verdauga, the old king, had grown slow and cruel in his last seasons, but it was Tsarmina who mixed the deathcap into his evening fig, and Tsarmina who pointed at her gentle brother Gingivere and cried *treason* while the old king was still warm. Gingivere went into the cell beside the mandrill's, there to keep mice and his own conscience company, and the forest learned what kind of queen they had: the kind who files family under *loose ends*.

The cell had a visitor the first night.

"Pssst. Psst, stripey-face." It was a capuchin hanging by his tail from the window bars, upside down, juggling three plums he had no intention of eating and every intention of not having paid for. "Gonzo's the name. Prince of Peek-Thieves, terror of the treetops, winner of last year's Least Honest Monkey award. I've come to rescue you."

"There are guards."

"There *were* guards. I've hidden all their breakfasts. The pantry, the armoury, Lord Ashleg's sock drawer — breakfast's everywhere. They'll be busy finding it till noon."

Escaping Kotir turned out to be mostly a matter of holding on, which suited him fine.

At Barkhall, Bella heard their story, gathered the forest's secret council — the Corim, squirrels and otters and moles who met after dark under a flag of noon — and gave them the quest in the way old monkeys give quests: as though she were only remembering it aloud. There was a mountain in the east, a fire-mountain called Salamandrill-stron, where her kinsman Boar the Boisterous kept a forge where broken things became unbroken. "Mandrill's sword was forged first in the north," she said. "It can be forged again."

So the mandrill, Gonzo the thief, and Grumm the pangolin — a placid beast who spoke of himself in the third person and considered tunneling a personality — went east, while the young rebels of the Barkship Companies made war on Kotir's patrols, shooting plum-stones from the trees and vanishing like rumors.

The war at home was fought with newspapers and nerve. Chibb the robin, the Corim's spy, overran Kotir's rafters disguised as furniture and reported everything, including the queen's breakfast. When Tsarmina sent her own spy — Fortunata the fox, sweet-voiced, posing as a refugee healer — the forest fed her three weeks of excellent false gossip and sent her home with battle plans that ended, on close reading, in a swamp. Ashleg the fox, the queen's chief advisor, watched all this, did the arithmetic on his own pelt, and began leaving his resignation in increments: first his loyalty, then his hours, and finally, one night, his uniform.

The road east had its moments. There were the bats of Bat Mountpit, who spoke entirely in food-based puns and directed them up a blind gorge. There was the monstrous pike in the mere at Kotir's gate, best left unmet. There were three days in a lizard swamp arguing about the word "north." And at the top of the world, steaming in the dawn, there was Salamandrill-stron — and Boar the Boisterous, a gorilla like a hill that had decided to be weather.

He took the broken sword, read the maker's mark, and laughed like a rockslide.

"Luuuuke," he said, "made this blade. Luuuke the Warrior, your father, stripey-face. And his son has come to Salamandrill-stron asking for a blade! Ha! I've forged the shards into a blade that no monkey will ever break. Not Tsarmina, not the pike, not the mountain itself — and I'll prove it!"

He did prove it. Boar the Boisterous, alone against a horde of shipwrecked corsair baboons who chose that morning to raid the forge, held the gates of his mountain until sunset, roaring old battle-hymns, and made the beach remember him. The mandrill came down from the crater to find the fight already told in smoke and silence, and the corsairs' leader gone to tell whoever was left, and Boar's last fire still burning in the forge with the blade laid across it, finished. That night, sleeping in the crater's warmth, the mandrill dreamed of his father — Luuuke, half-shadow, standing in the fire-glow — and his father said only what forge-fathers say: *Good steel. Better son.* He woke with the dream's heat on his face and turned west with a mountain's silence in him.

The woodlanders had not been idle. Under the beavers' direction they had dug and dammed and diverted all season, arguing about it in the way of committees and tunneling about it in the way of moles. And in Kotir, the queen was coming apart. Tsarmina, who had drowned her father's voice in one murder, began hearing it in the walls — *sloppy, daughter, sloppy* — and stopped sleeping, and stopped listening, and ordered the mere drained to hunt rebels who were in fact already behind her, hauling shovels.

When the mandrill came home, the forest broke the banks of Kotir's mere and let the whole flood take the fort — the walls, the granaries, the throne, and, swimming furiously against history, Tsarmina Greneyes, who met the mandrill on the last dry turret with a stolen axe in her paws and her father's voice in her ears.

"You have no sword," she snarled — but he did, and the duel on the drowning tower was the stuff of songs. The songs leave out the part where Gonzo the Prince of Peek-Thieves, hiding in the thatch, dropped a beehive on her head at the crucial moment, but the woodlanders never do. They also leave out the water. The queen who had feared it all her life went into the flood she'd been promised, and the mere closed over Kotir like a book.

In spring they planted banana groves where Kotir's parade ground had been. Gingivere, innocent and mild, was let out of what remained of the cell and retired to grow strawberries, which suited him better than royalty ever had. Gonzo married Columbine of the Barkship Companies, stole the wedding ring twice, and returned it once. The Corim voted itself a permanent institution, mostly for the feasts. And the forest named the young mandrill Champion of Mosshowler.

He stayed a season, hung the sword on Bella's wall, and wandered off into legend, leaving behind a forest that never quite stopped feasting.

"Mark my words," said Gonzo at the farewell feast, "that ape's going to found an Abbey. They'll name it something obvious."
