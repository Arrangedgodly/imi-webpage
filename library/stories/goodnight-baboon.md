# Goodnight Baboon

*A parody of "Goodnight Moon"*

In the great green jungle, in the crook of a fig tree, there was a ripe yellow banana and a picture of three little monkeys jumping over a mango moon — and there was a little baboon who was not one bit sleepy.

"Not sleepy," said the little baboon. "Not even a little bit. Not even a banana-sized bit."

"Hush," said the quiet old lady baboon, who was rocking and rocking in the crook below him. She was whispering "hush."

The little baboon looked around the great green jungle, and he decided that if he could not sleep, he could at least say goodnight. Goodnight was nearly as good as sleeping, and it took much longer, which was the whole point.

"Goodnight jungle," he said. "Goodnight baboon."

He said it to himself, because there was no other baboon his size in the tree, so he was the baboon being goodnighted, and he found this very satisfying.

"Goodnight monkeys jumping over the mango moon," he said, pointing at the picture. "Goodnight fireflies, and goodnight red balloon."

There was, in fact, a red balloon caught in the highest branches, left over from the troop's festival of the full 'nana, and it nodded in the night air as if it agreed.

"Goodnight balloon," he added. "Goodnight festival. Goodnight confetti that the wind took home."

"Goodnight sloths," said the little baboon, "and goodnight moths. Goodnight creepers, and goodnight sleepers."

The sloths, of course, did not answer, because they were asleep, because sloths are the only creatures in the jungle better at lying still than a baboon at bedtime is at wiggling. The little baboon knew this for a fact, because he had once tried to out-still a sloth at naptime and lost in under a minute.

"Goodnight lianas," he tried. "Goodnight vines. Goodnight everybody's tails but mine."

"Hush," whispered the quiet old lady baboon, rocking and rocking. "It is bedtime in the troop."

"One more goodnight," said the little baboon. "Two more goodnights. A whole bunch of goodnights. A bunch is what bananas come in, so a bunch of goodnights is the proper amount."

And he looked some more. There were two little tamarins in the next tree wearing one mitten between them, which is how tamarins like their mittens. There were pairs of socks hung on a kapok branch to dry, and a pair of small monkeys inside the socks, which were not socks at all but monkeys still in their pajamas.

"Goodnight tamarins," said the little baboon. "Goodnight mittens. Goodnight socks, and goodnight monkeys in socks. Goodnight clocks — "

There was one clock, a cukoo-clockbird asleep in its own little hole in the trunk, and it did not cukoo, because even clockbirds know better than to cukoo at bedtime.

"Goodnight clockbird. Goodnight birds. Goodnight every last word that rhymes with birds."

"Hush," said the quiet old lady baboon, who was very patient, as quiet old lady baboons are.

The little baboon tried an experiment then. He said goodnight to everything twice, to see if twice took twice as long. It did not. Twice took the same amount of once, only quieter, which was not at all what he had planned, and he yawned a yawn he immediately pretended he had not yawned.

Something went bump on a far branch. The little baboon sat up, which is the correct first move of every goodnight-sayer. But it was only Uncle Fig turning over in his sleep, and the branch said sorry, and everything settled again.

"Goodnight Uncle Fig," whispered the little baboon. "Goodnight branch. Goodnight bumps everywhere, as long as that's all you are."

In the shadow of a hollow trunk, two heavy old gorillas sat on folding chairs, dozing with their chins on their fists, and the chairs held their breath.

"Goodnight gorillas," whispered the little baboon. "Goodnight chairs."

Somewhere below, the troop's aunties slept in a row like commas, and one uncle snored on the low branch — a big round snore, like a frog with opinions.

"Goodnight aunties," whispered the little baboon. "Goodnight the uncle's snore. Goodnight the frog the snore sounds like, whoever he is."

There was a comb made of fishbone and a brush made of seedpod, hung side by side on a twig for the morning grooming.

"Goodnight comb. Goodnight brush. Goodnight fur, every single hair. Goodnight the little bald patch nobody talks about," he added, because it was his, and it did not get goodnighted enough.

There was a wooden bowl with tomorrow's breakfast in it, mashed banana going brown and sweet in the dark like a small soft moon of its own.

"Goodnight bowl," said the little baboon. "Goodnight mash. Goodnight to tomorrow, which can wait its turn."

" Hush," said the quiet old lady baboon, rocking slower now.

"Goodnight little house," he said, for the troop's sleeping tree was their little house, with its hallways of branches and its doors of leaves. "Goodnight louse — "

There was a very small louse on a very small leaf, and the little baboon, being a kind baboon, did not eat him, because it was bedtime, and eating somebody at bedtime is rude.

He looked up, and he looked down, and he said goodnight to the house's floors, which in a jungle go on for some way.

"Goodnight canopy," he whispered. "Goodnight understory. Goodnight forest floor, where the beetles sweep up after everybody."

He said goodnight to the weather, too, in case it was listening.

"Goodnight rain, whenever you come. Goodnight wind, and goodnight the direction you're going. Goodnight clouds, and goodnight the gap in the clouds where the moon gets through."

"Goodnight nobody," said the little baboon, to an empty branch where a friend might have been.

Then he whispered it again, because "goodnight nobody" felt lonelier than he meant it to, so he added, "Goodnight somebody, too. Goodnight everybody," to make it even.

The moon came up over the canopy like a big pale banana nobody would ever pick. The stars had come out and were doing their jobs, and the little baboon counted the ones he could see, to see if there were more than yesterday. There were always more.

The little baboon's eyes were going heavy, but he had saved the best for last.

"Goodnight stars," he whispered. "Goodnight the stars I can count, and goodnight the stars I can't. Goodnight air. Goodnight fruit bats everywhere."

"Goodnight noises everywhere," breathed the quiet old lady baboon, rocking slower now, and slower, and slower.

And the jungle said it back, in its way: the frogs went plunk, the crickets went chrk-chrk, and far off one young howler, who had not yet learned that nighttime was for hushing, tested his roar — hoo-ooo-op — like thunder practicing quietly.

"Goodnight howlers everywhere," murmured the little baboon, nearly asleep. "Goodnight 'nanas. Goodnight bunch. Goodnight trooooo..."

He did not finish the word. The quiet old lady baboon stopped her rocking and listened. From up in the crook of the fig tree came one small, contented snore.

And so, in the great green jungle, there was a ripe yellow banana nobody ate, and a picture of three little monkeys jumping over a mango moon, and a little baboon fast asleep with his tail curled around him like a question mark that had finally found its answer.

Goodnight little baboon.

Goodnight troop.
