# Howlerstör

*A parody of "Horrorstör"*

HOWLERSTÖR sat at the edge of the Bramblefield shopping savanna like a giant, patient banyan tree — which, structurally, it was: concrete buttress roots, skylights like dappled leaves, and a logo in that cheerful yellow font that means business. Inside, everything was jungle-modern. The NAPPER branch hammock. The GROOMING STATION KLOMP. The SWINGER Mk II tire swing, now with reinforced hemp.

> **NAPPER** — branch hammock, sustainably hallucinated hemp. *Sleep like you live in a tree, because you do.* 12 figs. Assembly required. Assembly not recommended after dark.

Amy, a capuchin who had worked the floor for fourteen months, two roommates, and one slowly evaporating dream of art school, could assemble a Mangabey wardrobe in her sleep. Corporate called this "empowerment."

"Sales are down in Grooming Solutions," announced Basil, her manager, at morning huddle, brandishing a clipboard. "Corporate is sending a consultant. Until then: nobody mentions the incidents."

The incidents. Amy's fur prickled.

It had started small, the way monkey business does. Displays rearranged overnight, always into spirals. The Swinger Mk IIs swinging at two a.m. in a locked showroom with the air conditioning off. The mirrors inside the Mangabey wardrobe fogging up even when nobody was breathing near them. A customer wrote in to say she'd been locked in Grooming for an hour and could not remember her own address; Corporate had sent her a coupon. And yesterday, every checkout screen displaying the same message in cheerful yellow: IS SOMEONE THERE?

Ruth Anne, who had worked retail since approximately the Pleistocene, wrote it all down in a little book she kept in her apron. "Corporate says it's a software patch."

"Software doesn't smell," said Matt, the baboon from Stock, who read ghost pamphlets on his breaks. "That tree in the showroom — the big display banyan? It smells like wet fur."

Amy hadn't wanted to agree. But the display banyan did smell like wet fur, and once, passing it, she had heard it breathing. Not speaker-breathing. Lung-breathing.

> **GROOMING STATION KLOMP** — self-grooming workstation, four combs, one mirror. *Because you deserve you.* 39 figs. Mirrors sold separately. Mirrors not recommended.

> **SWINGER Mk II** — tire swing, reinforced hemp, load-rated for two adults and one regret. *Swing into savings.* Do not use at night. Do not use if it swings first.

So of course they volunteered for overnight inventory. Corporate insisted someone catch the vandals, and Basil insisted it not be him. That left Amy, Ruth Anne, and Matt, with flashlights, clipboards, and the specific silence of a store after hours — which is not silence at all, but the sound of a hundred thousand figs' worth of merchandise, thinking.

By midnight the intercom clicked on. Nothing came out of it but breathing, slow and labored, and then — faintly — a lullaby. Ruth Anne went white under her grey fur.

"My grandmother sang that," she whispered. "That's an Old Grove cradle song. Nobody's sung it since they paved the ancestral roost."

Nobody said the obvious. Everybody knew the story: Howlerstör's banyan was the last of the Old Grove, and Old Peeler, the grove-keeper, had gone up it swinging his walking stick the day the concrete trucks came, and had never come down. The pamphlets Matt read all agreed on one thing, which was that it was worse than whatever Matt had read the week before.

At 1:14 a.m., the store rearranged itself.

They heard it happening — the soft scrape of the GROOMING STATION KLOMP sliding, the polite tink of shelving, like a shop that had decided to redecorate. When they rounded the end cap, the showrooms stretched away wrong, aisles multiplying into the dark, and in the AS-IS section stood furniture Amy had never seen in any catalog. Low, curved couches. Woven of fur.

The mirror at the end of the aisle had a message in it, written from the inside: GROOOM US.

"Right," said Matt, voice cracking. "We're leaving."

The lights went out. The banyan's breathing got loud enough to rattle the price tags. And Matt was gone — simply gone, the way a fig is gone when the tree swallows it back.

They found the hole in the display tree, a knothole breathing warm air, and heard Matt somewhere behind the bark, not far away, not near enough, saying something about spirals in a voice that had been sanded down at the edges. Amy did the bravest thing she'd ever done on minimum wage: she reached into the knothole past her elbow. Something groomed her arm, gently, horribly, and she hauled Matt out by his collar, and they ran — Ruth Anne, Amy, one baboon, and a clipboard — through aisles that folded behind them like a closing hand, past the mirror, which now said THANK YOU COME AGAIN, and out the loading doors into the honest dawn.

> **AS-IS** — clearance furniture. All sales final. *Some sales are more final than others.*

There were questions afterward, the way there are. Corporate's consultant never materialized, having developed a sudden and permanent interest in a different time zone. A maintenance crew came to inspect the banyan, found no knothole anywhere, billed the store for four hours, and left in silence by lunch. Basil filed the whole night under "vandals, extremely committed." Ruth Anne took her apron book home, and Amy heard she'd started it with the words THIS STORE IS ON A GRAVE, which was not technically accurate, since the grove was not under the store.

The grove, as far as anyone could tell, was the store. Or at least the store had been built around it, politely, the way you build a porch around a tree you're afraid to cut.

The store reopened in nine days. There was a sale. There is always a sale.

Basil put up a small brass plaque by the entrance — IN MEMORY OF THE OLD GROVE — and the incidents slowed after that, Amy noticed. The banyan in the showroom smells of green leaves now, most days. The mirrors stay clean. The intercom plays only the corporate jingle, which is arguably worse. Only the AS-IS section keeps one low, curved couch that nobody remembers ordering, woven of something soft and brown and warm, priced at one fig, forever sold out.

> **THE OLD GROVE COLLECTION** — one of a kind. *Cannot be ordered. Can order you.*

Amy still works there. Rent being what it is. She worked days, then evenings, then overnights again, because somebody has to, and because Matt — who remembers the knothole, mostly, and will not shop the AS-IS section on principle — says the store is calm when she's in it. Ruth Anne says the same thing, only she says it while looking at the banyan.

But when a customer asks her to assemble the Mangabey wardrobe, she does it in the aisle, in the open, in the light. She asks them to stay and watch. She makes a joke of it. She has never once said the real reason, which is that some furniture is assembled, and some furniture assembles you, and she would like witnesses either way.

Some things, she tells them, require assembly.

Some things, you should never build at all.
