# The Three Little Twigs

*A parody of "The Three Little Pigs"*

Once upon a time, a mother spider monkey looked at her three sons — all grown, and none of them gone — and said, "You've swung long enough in my nest. Off you go and build treehouses of your own. And remember: out there, something is always hungry."

She kissed each of them on the head and gave each a parting gift. To the first she gave her old hammock. To the second, a fruit basket woven of golden straw-colored grass. To the third, the youngest, she gave a coil of good rope and a look that meant she knew exactly which son would use it.

The first little twig, who was lazy, built his house in a single day, of whatever fallen twigs he could grab — a heap of them, piled in the fork of a cecropia tree. He hung his mother's hammock between two crooked sticks, painted a sign that read "LEAF ME ALONE," and lay in it eating bananas.

The second little twig, who was lazy but ambitious, took two days and wove his walls of great green banana leaves. Waterproof. Stylish. He put the fruit basket on display in the window, where it could be admired from three trees away, and he lay in the shade of it eating bananas.

The third little twig worked all season. He braided his frame of living branches, wove lianas tight and tighter, planted a thornvine round the trunk, and tested every knot twice with his mother's rope. He kept a store of rain water and a barrel of tree sap. When his brothers asked why, he said, "Weather," and went back to work.

"You'll work yourself into a knot," laughed his brothers. "Come swing with us!"

"Better a knot than a gobble," said the third little twig, tugging a liana.

Now in that jungle there lived the Big Bad Boa, who had a long body, a longer appetite, and the shortest temper. One morning his stomach rumbled like distant thunder, and he smelt monkey on the wind. Monkeys, in his opinion, were the one food that came with entertainment, because they squeaked so on the way down.

He came to the cecropia tree and coiled at the bottom of it. "Little twig! Little twig! Let me come up!"

"Not by the tuft of my curly little tail!" sang the first twig from his window of twigs, thinking himself very safe, thinking of nothing at all.

"Then I'll squeeze and I'll sway and I'll shake your tree down!" And the Big Bad Boa wrapped himself round and round that trunk, and squeezed, and swayed, and shook — and all the twigs came rattling down like hail. The sign that said "LEAF ME ALONE" was the last to fall. The first little twig shot out the far side with his mother's hammock around his neck like a scarf, and swung for his life, all the way to his brother's banana-leaf lodge.

The boa licked his lips and followed, unhurried, the way a landslide is unhurried. "Little twigs! Little twigs! Let me come up!"

"Not by the tufts of our curly little tails!"

"Then I'll squeeze and I'll sway and I'll shake your tree down!" He wrapped, he squeezed, he swayed — and the banana leaves tore loose one by one and fluttered down like green confetti. The second house was confetti. The fruit basket sailed off the branch and bonked the boa neatly on the nose, which he took personally. The two little twigs swung away, hearts hammering, to their brother's banyan.

"Little brother! Let us in! There's a boa!"

The third little twig let down his liana elevator, hauled them up, and set them on the strongest beam, where they shivered in a row.

"You were right," they said, in one voice. "About the knots."

"Knots now, thanks later," said the third little twig, and handed them each a spare liana. "Hold something."

The Big Bad Boa arrived. "Little twigs! Little twigs! Let me in!"

"Not by the tufts of our curly little tails!" they shouted together — and this time, holding their lianas, two of the three voices did not even wobble.

"Then I'll squeeze and I'll sway and I'll shake your tree down!" The boa wrapped that banyan round and round, and squeezed with all his might, and swayed till his own scales rattled, and shook till the ripe fruit dropped — and the braided branches creaked, and held, and held, and held. Not one knot gave way.

The boa rested, gasping, with his dignity somewhere below him. Then he tried climbing the trunk — but the thornvine pricked his nose and his pride, and he let go in a hurry and fell into a bush, which also had opinions.

So he tried cleverness. He draped himself over a branch, arranged his coils into what he believed was a friendly shape, and called up in a syrupy voice: "Good morning! House inspection! I am from the... Jungle House Association. I shall need to come inside to inspect your... house things."

The third little twig looked down at fifteen feet of glistening house inspector. "Certainly," he said pleasantly. "Just sign the guest book." And he lowered the guest book, and a quill, on his mother's rope.

The boa stared at the quill. Boas have no paws. The quill lay in the grass, mocking him.

"No?" said the third little twig. "Then we'll do the chimney inspection instead."

"Very well!" hissed the boa, delighted, for this was the plan all along. "I'll come down the hollow chimney branch and sizzle you three for supper!" For the third little twig, you see, had carved a cozy hollow chute through the great branch — and beneath the chute, just that morning, he had set a rain barrel brimming with icy water and sticky tree sap.

The Big Bad Boa lowered himself down, down, down the hollow branch — and plunged snout-first into the barrel. Sap! Cold water! He thrashed and whipped and spat, tied himself into a triple fisherman's knot trying to get free, and at last had to beg the little twigs to untie him, which is not a sentence boas enjoy saying.

They did — mostly — and watched him slither off sideways, soggy, sticky, and knotted, muttering that from now on he was strictly an egg-and-iguana boa.

"Should we have untied him all the way?" asked the first little twig.

"He'll hold that knot for a month yet," said the third little twig. "Sap sets like a promise."

After that, the two elder brothers rebuilt their houses good and braided, knot upon knot, and their brother walked the branch lines checking every one, rope over his shoulder, exactly like their mother. The sign in the cecropia tree now reads "LEAF ME ALONE (BUT KNOCK FIRST)," and the three little twigs swung and feasted on bananas in the banyan all the seasons of their lives.

And it is said that in that jungle, to this very day, whenever a certain boa hears the words "double knot," he ties himself up out of pure habit — and that he has never once, not even to himself, used the words "house inspection" again.
