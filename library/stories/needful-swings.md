# Needful Swings

*A parody of "Needful Things"*

The monkey who opened the shop in Canopy Rock that October was an aye-aye — a gaunt, silvery primate with enormous night-eyes and a middle finger so long it seemed to arrive five minutes before he did. His name was Mr. Gaunt, and the hand-lettered sign over his door read:

NEEDFUL SWINGS
A Little of Everything...
And the Thing You Need Most

Inside: swings, ropes, lianas, perches — and on small pedestals, the thing you had wanted all your life. Brian Rusk, twelve, found a mint 1956 card of Bananarama Bob, the greatest swinger who ever lived. Polly Chalmers, who ran the weaving hollow, found the itzya, a little charm said to melt the ache in her grooming shoulder. And old Buster Keeton, the town selectman, found a ledger in which THE PEELS — the missing funds, the missing funds! — were miraculously balanced.

Mr. Gaunt's prices were odd. "Not money, mostly," he said, showing Brian the card with a smile like a crack in a jar. "A little money. And a small favor. Put this rotten banana in Nettie Cobb's hammock. That's all. Just a prank, my boy. Monkey business. Harmless."

The pranks were harmless. That was the genius of them. A rotten banana here; itching powder in Wilma Jerzyck's fur-oil there; a telegram to each lady suggesting the other had said unforgivable things about her tail. By the time the Harvest Luncheon rolled around, Nettie and Wilma had worked themselves into the Great Rolling-Pin War of Canopy Rock — the two of them swinging from the luncheon rafters, rolling pins out, each dead certain the other had started it, both ending in the moat with their fur soaked and their dignity never recovered.

And Mr. Gaunt stood in his doorway, sipping the tantrums like fig juice.

He had others on the string. Brian Rusk ran three more errands before his conscience got the better of his collection, mailed the card back with a note reading USED. ONE OWNER. SANE., and took up whittling. Polly Chalmers, though she never quite felt proud of it, had done her own small favor first — a tarantula's moulted skin, planted in the baptismal font of the rectory with a note signed in the other clergyman's initials. The Reverend Mr. Rose and Father Brigham, pranked into believing each had called the other's sermons "banana oil" and now this, fought a holy war of casseroles and grudges that culminated in each publicly excommunicating the other from a religion only one of them could possibly be wrong about. And Buster Keeton, certain that THEM were auditing his ledgers, tore apart the selectmen's chamber hunting for Their listening vines, found none, and concluded They had gotten to the vines first. Buster bought a claw-hammer at Needful Swings ("a small favor — point it at the bank, Buster") and stood guard at the Fur-Trust with it, telling passersby, "It's THEM, Slim. It's THEM." Nobody in Canopy Rock was named Slim.

For deliveries and the heavier pranks, Mr. Gaunt took on a local boy with a scooter and no imagination — a ripple-furred juvenile named Acer, who did what he was told and asked for the money twice.

Sheriff Alan Pangborn saw the pattern before anyone, because seeing patterns was his one gift — he kept a corkboard with strings running prank to prank like a detective quilt — and because the thing he wanted most was sitting in the shop's back room where Mr. Gaunt kept it: a child's swing, worn oak, its rope gone soft from small hands.

"Your boy's," said Mr. Gaunt lovingly. "Todd's. He sat in it every evening, waiting for you to come home from work. You were always on duty. Well. I can sell it to you, Alan. I'll even throw in the evenings."

"That's not a swing," Alan said slowly. "And you're not a shopkeeper."

"Prank him, Alan," Gaunt whispered. "Just one small prank on Danforth Keeton. He bought a little tool at my shop — did I mention I sell tools as well as toys? Point him somewhere useful. One little push."

Now Alan Pangborn had pushed a monkey once in his time — pushed him toward a cell — and had never liked the memory. He looked at the aye-aye's long finger, at his night-eyes, at the dusty swings ranked behind him like a chorus line of hanged things, and he began, quite calmly, to laugh.

"It's all papier-mâché," he said. "The card, the charm, the ledger, the swing. Spit and paint. You're a peddler, friend. A banana huckster. You've been running this grift since before there were monkeys to grift, and I'll bet it hasn't been fun for a thousand years."

Across town, at that very hour, Polly Chalmers stood at the millpond with the itzya in her fist — her shoulder had ached since dusk, and the ache had a smell to it somehow, like the inside of the shop — and on a whim she could not afterward explain, she pitched it out over the water. It floated a moment, like the spitball it was, and sank. Her shoulder stopped aching before it finished sinking. She walked home feeling lighter than she had in a year.

Mr. Gaunt's face changed — truly changed, like weather — and the shop changed with it: the swings sagged, the ropes went to cobweb, and Bananarama Bob curled at the edges into a bottle-cap. Somewhere overhead something popped like a balloon, and the sky over Canopy Rock went the color of an old bruise. The sign over the door now read, in letters that crawled: EVERYTHING MUST GO. ESPECIALLY YOU.

"Noon," hissed Mr. Gaunt. "They riot at noon, the town eats itself, and I take the pickings."

The noon riot came, sure enough — howling monkeys, thrown fruit, the Fur-Trust bank windows going in like sugar, Buster Keeton at the doors with his claw-hammer, guarding the vault from THEM. But Alan and Polly walked out into the middle of Main Branch hand in hand and stood there, unhurried, unafraid, watching, as if at a parade. And a troop that has one pair of unimpressed eyes to look at will shamefacedly calm itself, because monkeys are mimics above all. The rioters looked at the two of them standing in the street, and began, one by one, to put down their fruit, and to remember other monkeys' faces, and to feel like fools.

By one o'clock, Canopy Rock stood — battered, bemused, embarrassed, alive. The Reverend Mr. Rose and Father Brigham agreed to disagree about who had excommunicated whom first. Nettie and Wilma compared telegrams over the moat and discovered the same florist. Buster surrendered his hammer to Alan with the words "They'll be back, Slim," and took up macramé.

Mr. Gaunt's wicker runabout was a heap of kindling, and Mr. Gaunt himself was gone, yanked away east by something in the sky he did not once look up at, which is telling. Acer's scooter was found in a ditch, out of gas and out of interest.

Alan Pangborn went home and hugged his boys until they squawked.

The shop, when they finally tore it down, left nothing but a floor of dust and swings — rotten, every single one.
