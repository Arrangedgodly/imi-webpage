# The Island of Doctor Monkeau

*A parody of "The Island of Doctor Moreau"*

Edward Prenripe was eleven days adrift in a dinghy after the sinking of the *Lady Vain* when the schooner *Ipecacuanha* fished him out — and even half-dead of thirst, he wished it hadn't. Her captain was a drunk. Her cargo was crates of live animals, howling and reeking, destined for an uncharted island. And her only friendly face belonged to a red-haired, hollow-eyed macaque named Montgoromery, who traveled with a strange, hunched, misshapen servant that barked.

When Prenripe was put ashore on the island with the animals, Montgoromery shrugged and took him in. There was a walled enclosure. There was a compound of laboratories. And there was Doctor Monkeau — grey-muzzled, ice-calm, with a grip that could crack a coconut without expression.

"Prenripe," said Monkeau, "you will see things here that your mind will struggle to shelve. Do not shelve them as magic. What I do here is not magic. It is *surgery*."

The first night, Prenripe heard screaming from the enclosure — the screaming of something being un-made and re-made — and fled into the jungle. There he met them: the Beast Folk. A swamp-hyena that walked upright. A puma with disturbingly monkeyish hands. A hippo-thing in a woven belt. A lean leopard-spotted one who would not meet his eye. And around a great fire, they chanted, led by a grey, bent, hairless figure:

"Not to walk on all fours; that is the Law. Are we not Monkeys?"

"*We are not Men! We are not Men!*" roared the Beast Folk, in a roar that had rehearsed itself for years.

"Not to claw bark or root in the mud for grubs; that is the Law. Are we not Monkeys?"

"NOT MONKEYS! NOT MONKEYS!" they chanted, guiltily, each of them remembering the mud.

"Not to howl at the moon or browse on all fours; not to eat raw meat; not to squabble over bananas like beasts; that is the Law. Are we not Monkeys?"

"MONKEYS," they howled, and Prenripe ran.

Montgoromery's servant — the barking one, called M'langur — turned out to be one of them: a Beast Folk, five years transformed, with pointed ears and a loyalty that had outlived his making. It was Montgoromery who finally told the rest, over rum: Monkeau had been driven out of the academies of Londonzoa decades ago, his lectures picketed, his name struck from the rolls. Here, far from regulation and mercy, the doctor had pursued his genius — remaking animals, beast by beast, joint by joint, into monkeys. Into *people*. Or near enough to pass, in bad light, at a distance, to a liar.

"They revert," Montgoromery said, not looking at him. "Constantly. So he keeps them here, teaches them the Law, and every time one slips back, into the enclosure it goes. To the House of Grooming, he calls it." He laughed, badly. "They call it the House of Pain. Children's logic. It's correct."

Monkeau was unbothered by any of it. He walked Prenripe through the laboratory himself, past the bins of plaster and bone-growth paste, the racks of instruments boiling in vats, the thing under the sheet that was breathing.

"Pain is simply an instrument," he said, pinning Prenripe with that grey stare while, somewhere behind the wall, a fresh scream rose. "The beast howls. So I chase the beast out of it. First I take a hound, and I give it hands. Then a face. Then speech. It takes months; the body forgets; the body *wants* to go back; so I make it more painful to go back than to go forward. That is all pedagogy is, Prenripe, at bottom. The monkey is simply the more *rigorous* animal. I am not a monster; I am a tutor with a knife."

"Every one of them screams that you are a god," said Prenripe.

"To a worm in a fig," said Monkeau, "the monkey that plants the fig tree is a god. The worm is not wrong. It is merely ungrateful."

Then came the week it all came apart. First, the leopard-spotted one was caught with blood on his muzzle — a rabbit, raw, torn, which is the whole of the Law broken in one meal. The gong sounded, the dogs were loosed, and Prenripe ran with Monkeau and Montgoromery and half the Beast Folk through the canebrake after the Leopard Monkey, who fled beautifully, who was cornered against the rocks, and who, at the end, turned and looked at them — at *him*, Prenripe always insisted, at the only witness who was neither judge nor kin — with such a face that Prenripe cried out to spare him. Monkeau shot him through the chest, and the Beast Folk, converging, chanted over the body: "His is the House of Pain. His is the House of Pain," until Prenripe understood it was the closest thing they had to a prayer, and that they were glad, because as long as the Law devoured someone else, it was not coming for them.

Three nights later the puma-subject — a female, half puma, half monkey, the finest of the whole wretched line — tore her restraints and got out of the House of Pain with her half-finished face still bleeding. Monkeau went into the jungle after her with a tranquilizer gun, calm as a customs officer.

"She was my best work," he told them at the gate. "Ten years. She would have passed in daylight."

He did not come back. They found him at dawn in the fern-gully, red of claw, beyond tutoring, and Prenripe stood over him and felt — he wrote this down later, and struck it out, and wrote it again — *relief*, and knew himself for a Beast Folk too.

Montgoromery got magnificently drunk. He gave the brandy to M'langur and the Beast Folk, and the brandy to the Beast Folk is a thing Prenripe would not see twice; he fired his gun into the sky, shouted that he was through, opened all the compound gates, and went down among them singing, and M'langur went with him, and both were dead by midnight. The enclosure burned. The laboratories, the plaster bins, the instruments, the notes — everything that had made the Beast Folk — went up in one long fire whose light Prenripe watched from the ridge, cold, unable to decide whether it was a defeat or a mercy.

And Prenripe — alone with a hundred Beast Folk who were forgetting, day by day, week by week, how to be Monkeys — lived among them almost a year. The grey Sayer of the Law died first; and the Law, unspoken daily, unmended nightly, went to pieces in their hands like wet bark. One by one they went down: the hippo-thing on all fours and liked it; the swamp-hyena remembered the taste of the chase and told the others it was better than the Law, and was believed, being sincere. Only the Dog Monkey stayed true — a wretched, loyal creature who followed Prenripe, and fought for him, and died for him in the end against the hyena-swine's pack, which is the whole of the dog's religion and needs no chanting.

Last of all came the hyena-swine himself, huge now, gray-muzzled, the Law gone from his eyes entirely, to tell Prenripe that the island was his.

"I am a beast, and the Law is dead," said the hyena-swine. "There is no House of Pain now. There is no Pain-Maker. There is only me."

"So the tiger and the swine inherit it," said Prenripe — and because the hyena-swine was clever enough to know what he was thinking, Prenripe killed him with a stone-splitting mallet that night as he drank at the stream, and was not sorry, and did not sleep either.

He was picked up, eventually, by a passing ship, in a little dinghy, with the last of Montgoromery's rum and no story anyone would publish.

He lives quietly now, in rooms in Londonzoa, avoiding crowds. Every face in the street — macaque, mandrill, gibbon, chimp — he studies for a flash of the beast in it, some telltale swell of the jaw, some too-yellow eye. He has found he cannot reliably tell the difference anymore, and it does not trouble him the way it used to.

"Strange is the jungle, and strange her children," he writes, in the account nobody will print, "but the calm of the high canopy, where the stars come out, is also mine. Even a half-made monkey may look up."
