# A Groom with a View

*A parody of "A Room with a View"*

The Pension Bananatolini stood halfway up a fig-drenched hillside above the great brown river, and the signboard promised that every bough had a view. Lucy Honeyswing, nineteen, a young chimp of excellent Surrey grooming, parted the fronds of her bower and discovered that theirs looked directly into a culvert.

"This will not do at all," said her elder cousin, Miss Charlotte Barklett, snapping a parasol-leaf open with a click of disapproval.

"The lady apes require the view," boomed a voice from the breakfast bough, "and we do not."

The voice belonged to old Mr. Emerfurson, who was considered bad-mannered by the entire pension. He and his son George occupied the corner boughs — the ones with both river and sunrise.

"I am not a lady ape," said Miss Barklett, stiffening her ruff.

"You are a primate of sense, which is better," said Mr. Emerfurson. "Take our boughs. I have never met anyone who needed a view more."

And that was how the swap was made, and how Lucy came by a view, and how — though she did not suspect it yet — she came within grooming distance of her whole life.

The next day Lucy went alone to the great cool church of Santa Figgia, without her Baedeker-vine, which was in Charlotte's basket. Nothing in the jungle is so frightening as a perceptive young monkey without a Baedeker: no label tells her what to feel, and so she feels it. She wandered among the shadows and the frescoes, trembling into knowledge of large things — that the jungle is splendid, and that she, Lucy Honeyswing, had never once been alone inside it.

That evening, buying postcards in the piazza, she stood beside two fig-vendors quarreling over a pitch. One struck the other; the struck one staggered, bleeding a little, into Lucy's view; the carabinieri-baboons came leaping; and the blood, in the way of blood, found its way onto Lucy's fresh postcards. George Emerfurson appeared out of the crowd, took her paws, walked her to the parapet, and spoke of the view until she stopped shaking.

"Some muddle," said George. "The world's full of it. Old Mr. Ebert down there will be all right by supper, and ashamed by morning."

At the river's edge, he quietly dropped her ruined postcards over the parapet, leaf by leaf, and filled her paws with wild violets instead; which was George's whole theology in one gesture: throw out the stained pictures, keep the flowers.

The excursion to Figsole happened the following week, in a hired carriage driven by a grinning young male Phaethon and his girl Persephonea, who toothlessly presumed to call her "Lucy" — "the signorina is not a name," said Miss Barklett, "she is a visitor" — and there, among the hillside violets, Lucy wandered alone to the lip of a drop and fainted neatly into a fern. When she woke, George Emerfurson was crouched in the grass, watching her with dark, serious eyes.

"Is it the height?" he asked. "It is the height for me."

Then, before she could gather her wits or her tail, he ran two fingers gently through the fur at her shoulder — a single grooming stroke, warm and entirely uninvited.

"Miss Barklett!" Lucy cried, scandalized, and also something else besides, something that felt like going bananas in slow motion. Charlotte declared they must leave for Rome at once. "This sort of monkey business," she whispered, "undoes a young female's entire upbringing." Lucy wrote George a little note saying nothing had happened, which was true only in the way that lightning has not happened once it has struck. Charlotte intercepted the note, posted it herself, and added a paragraph; and the violets dried in Lucy's guidebook between the pages for Surrey, where she kept them for a year without admitting they were there.

Back in Surrey, at the family nest of Windy Canopy, Lucy got engaged to Cecil Vise — a gibbon of impeccable grip, lofty brow, and staggering self-regard. Cecil did not groom her so much as inspect her, clinically, as one inspects fruit for bruises. He read aloud to drawing rooms from the Classical Boughs, pausing to correct the authors; he liked the idea of Lucy hanging gracefully beside him at dinner parties, framed.

"A mate," Cecil announced, "is a picture, and I have hung you well."

Lucy's brother Freddy, who viewed Cecil as one views weather, took at once to the opposite of him. When Cecil, to show his largeness of spirit, let a cottage to the Emerfursons, Lucy went faint with dread. Freddy invited George to bathe in the Sacred Pond with him and the vicar, Mr. Beebe; and for one shining afternoon three naked apes of three ages shrieked and chased one another round the pond in the sunlight, dripping and glorious, until Cecil arrived with Lucy, collected the visitors' discarded clothes with tongs of disapproval, and led the party indoors to be respectable. Mr. Beebe, toweling, observed that it was a pity civilization had ever come down from the trees. Lucy observed nothing, loudly.

George appeared in the lane within the week, beside her in the Sacred Grove of figs, and before she could say "propriety" he had groomed the back of her neck, slowly, from shoulder to ear.

"You cannot groom me," Lucy flared, "and pretend nothing has happened!"

"I don't pretend," said George. "I never have. That is my whole trouble."

Lucy broke the engagement that very evening, which surprised nobody so much as Cecil, who had never once considered that a picture could climb down off the wall. "You could never groom me," she told him kindly, "because you have never in your life been glad to see anybody."

"Then it is broken," said Cecil, adjusting his cuffs, "and I am the injured party, which at least is a position of some dignity." And he shook her paw, and meant it, which was Cecil's redeeming feature: he was never anything but pleased with his own performance of it.

But Lucy, unengaged, was not yet unbound; she announced she would live for others and tour the world with two elderly spinsters of irreproachable boredom. She would not, she told everyone, including herself, marry George Emerfurson. It was at this point that the cab-driver gossip reached the right ears, and old Mr. Emerfurson, thundering gently in the cottage bower, finished it. Lucy sat very still, saying "I have been going bananas quietly for a year and calling it restraint," and the old ape leaned forward.

"My dear girl, can you not see? Passion should believe itself irresistible. It should forget caution, and see, and be seen. Groom him back. Groom him back before you grow old and respectable and full of the view from the wrong window."

So she did.

They spent their honeymoon, against every canon of Surrey taste, at the Pension Bananatolini — in the corner bough, with the river and the sunrise, the figs and the violets, the whole staggering view. Each morning George groomed Lucy's shoulder exactly where the first grooming stroke had fallen, and each morning Lucy announced that it was still a scandal, and each morning she leaned into it.

Miss Barklett, encountering them on the terrace, pronounced the marriage a disaster.

"It is," agreed Lucy happily, hanging by her tail out over the sunrise. "But it is a disaster with a view. That is the only kind worth having."
