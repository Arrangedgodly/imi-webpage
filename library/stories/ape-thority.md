# Ape-thority

*A parody of "Authority"*

On his first morning as acting director of the Southern Peach, John Chimpguez found a plant growing out of the assistant director's filing cabinet, a mouse skeleton in the ceiling tiles, and a live woman in quarantine who had been dead for a year.

He had been sent to fix an agency, not to understand one. That was the first mistake, and it was his mother's.

They called him Control. Everyone at Central got a one-word handle, and his was a family heirloom: his grandfather had been a spymaster in three dirty wars, his mother had toppled governments the way other macaques topple fruit stands, and Control — the disappointing grandson, the one with a conscience like a limp — had been handed the Southern Peach as a rehabilitation, or a leash, or a burial. With his family, the paperwork for all three was identical.

"The twelfth expedition is a failure," his handler told him, back at Central, in a room with no corners. "Four went in. One came out. Find out what happened to the other three. Find out what the one who came out *is*. And Control — do not go near the border."

"Do not go near the border," Control repeated, in the tone of a monkey writing a will.

The Southern Peach occupied a building behind a blacked-out government block: a sagging hive of aquariums, dead plants, and filing cabinets that reproduced in the night. The assistant director, Grace — a gorilla of institutional grief who had run the place for thirty years and regarded Control the way a cliff regards weather — did not shake his hand.

"You're the fifth director in two years," she said. "The last one went into Area X with the twelfth expedition and did not come back. The one before that retired to a facility with no sharp objects. Before you ask: no, I will not help you. I will obey you. Those are different services."

"And the one who came back?"

Grace looked at the plant in her filing cabinet, which had, Control noticed, flowered overnight, out of season, in the dark.

"Fourth floor quarantine," she said. "She's the bananaologist. Or she is what came back wearing the bananaologist. You'll want to be careful with her, Director. She's very calm, and calm is contagious."

Ghost Bird — that was what the file called her, because her first act upon leaving Area X had been to look at the recovery team and ask them what year it was, and her second had been to look at the sky "like she was checking a load-bearing wall" — sat in quarantine like a guest in a slow hotel. She looked like the bananaologist from the photographs: same fur, same amber eyes. But the photographs had a woman in them. Ghost Bird had a weather system.

"It's a tower," she said, on day one, when Control asked her to describe the tunnel.

"The team called it a tunnel."

"The team was wrong," said Ghost Bird, mildly. "The team called a lot of things wrong names right up until the names stopped mattering."

They had eleven sessions. Control had interrogated insurgents, defectors, and one finance minister, and he had never been interrogated back so gently. Ghost Bird did not lie. That was the terrible thing about her. She simply decided, day by day, question by question, what he was capable of surviving, and gave him that.

She told him about the border's shimmer, the lighthouse's journals, the crawler's letters, the psyschimpologist on the rocks. She told him about the surveyor counting bullets and the anthropologist becoming a margin note. And on the seventh day she told him about her husband, who had come back from the eleventh expedition as a copy of himself and died of what the copy was made of.

"I went in to find out what happened to him," she said. "Instead I found out what happened to the woman who went in to find out what happened to him. I'm not her, Control. I'm the answer to her."

"The answer," said Control, "to a question nobody at this agency has the clearance to ask."

"Yes," said Ghost Bird. "You're all holding a flashlight in a burning forest and asking each other for batteries."

Around them, the agency rotted in real time. In the basement archive, Whitby — the fragile, brilliant analyst nobody had promoted or fired in a decade — showed Control the reports he had never submitted: photographs of the Southern Peach's own stairwells where, in long exposures, words grew on the walls in flowering golden script, the same words the twelfth expedition had found descending the tower. Area X was not across the border. It was in the building, in the language, in the way the mission reports began to rhyme.

"They gave it a name, you know," Whitby whispered. "The ones before us. Not a place-name. A *terroir*. Like wine. Area X isn't an invasion, Director. It's a vintage. And the soil it grows in is us."

Central, which had one idea and kept it in a safe, sent Lowry.

Lowry was a cane, a trench coat, and a grievance with a heartbeat: a survivor of the first expedition, forty years ago, the only one who came back — came back wrong, came back furious, came back certain. He did not want the Southern Peach investigated. He wanted it loaded, aimed, and fired at the coast.

"Containment failed in year one," he told Control, tapping the cane. "What's left is velocity. We burn the border, we burn the towns, we burn the maps, and if a few thousand monkeys with glow-in-the-dark poetry in their basements have to burn with it, that's called *triage*, son."

"She's not an asset," said Control, who had stopped saying Ghost Bird's file name days ago. "She's a person."

"She's a *copy*," said Lowry, "and copies are drafts, and drafts," and here he smiled with all the warmth of a filing error, "get shredded."

They drugged Control on a Thursday. His own family signed the order; his mother's faction at Central had decided he had gone native, which in the Southern Peach meant *gone sane*. He came to in a parking garage with an empty dossier, a poisoned tea's aftertaste, and two facts that fit together like a trigger: Ghost Bird was being transferred at dawn to Lowry's facility, and the border, per the overnight seismographs, had moved.

It had moved *inland*.

The end of the Southern Peach was not an explosion. That is what the movies get wrong, Ghost Bird had told him once. It was quieter and worse: the shimmer coming up the coast road like a tide deciding, at its leisure, to visit. That night the plants in Grace's filing cabinet opened like hands; the words on the basement walls fruited; the moaning that had haunted Area X for decades was recorded, for the first time, in the building's own stairwell.

Grace found Control breaking Ghost Bird out of quarantine, and — thirty years of obedience collapsing like a bad scaffold — handed him the keys to the motor pool instead of the alarm.

"My director went in and didn't come back," she said. "My agency grew words. I have filed one report every working day for thirty years, and this is the only true one: go. Take her to the coast. Whatever the answer to her is, it's at the coast, and it is not at Central."

Lowry's transfer convoy never got its draft. Control and Ghost Bird went west through the evacuating towns with the shimmer at their backs, outrunning a tide that was in no hurry because tides are never in a hurry, while behind them the lights of the Southern Peach went out floor by floor, like a candle being eaten by an orchid.

At dawn they stopped on a headland. The sea was very calm. Somewhere out past the island, something moaned — long, rising, almost an ape, almost an answer.

"So," said Ghost Bird, watching the bright line of the border come up the road below, unhurried, ripening. "What do you want to be when the world grows over you, Control?"

"Useful," he said.

She nodded, as if he had passed something.

"Good," said Ghost Bird. "Hold on to that. The orchard is hiring."

They went down together toward the coast, two small monkeys walking into the jurisdiction of something older than agencies, and the Ape-thority of the Southern Peach ended — as all authority ends, in the end — not with a report, but with a season change.
