# Beauty and the Fleas

*A parody of "Beauty and the Beast"*

In a jungle town of ordinary monkeys lived a bonobo named Belle — beautiful, admittedly, but mostly peculiar, because she read.

"Look at her," sniffed the fruit-sellers. "Nose in a fig-leaf book again. Never seen a bonobo with her head in the clouds and her tail in a library."

"There she goes," sang the town, in the way of towns, "the same old girl, with her nose stuck in the same old book — she's a loner, she's a pest, and her prose is never brief!"

The only monkey in town unimpressed by Belle was Belle. The second was Gaston, a gelada with a mane like a thundercloud, impressed by nobody but himself.

"No monkey alive equals me!" he boomed, flexing. "Every last hair of me is perfect! It's not arrogance, Belle, if it's accurate."

"No," said Belle. "It's just fleas."

"I HAVE NO FLEAS," Gaston roared.

He had fleas. His sidekick, a scrawny macaque called LeFlea, counted them nightly and reported the totals like sports scores. ("Gaston up three this week! A comeback story!")

Gaston proposed in the town square, mid-ambush, with a wedding already scheduled in his own head. "Just picture it, Belle: a hunting lodge for two. My trophies on every wall. You, grooming my magnificent mane while I tell you about me."

"I'm not marrying you, Gaston," said Belle.

"Of course you are," said Gaston. "It's the only plot in town."

"Not," said Belle, "this town."

Meanwhile Belle's father, Maurice — an inventor whose latest machine peeled bananas and, occasionally, tables — got lost in the mist one evening and took shelter in an enormous abandoned fig-tree castle. There were cushions, a fire, a meal laid out as if waiting.

Then the owner came home.

The Beast was a gorilla the size of a thunderstorm, fangs in his frown, pelt rippling — for he was utterly, catastrophically infested. And the fleas, if you looked closely, were dressed.

"YOU PICKED MY BANANA," the Beast thundered from the top of the stair.

"It was the only tree in the mist—" stammered Maurice.

"Then you will rot in my dungeon!"

Belle went looking for her father and found him in a cell of woven vines. "Wait," she said, stepping from the shadows. "Take me instead."

Here is the castle's secret. The Beast had not always been a beast. He had been a vain young prince who refused shelter to an old bonobo one stormy night, and she had cursed him into a monster's shape, and every servant of his castle into fleas. In the great hall, under a glass dome, a single banana browned slowly, one black spot spreading like weather coming. When it blackened completely, the curse would seal forever.

Living in the Beast's right shoulder were:

Lumière, a firefly-flea who glowed. "Ah, bonjour, chérie! Welcome to ze castle! You are our first guest in — Cogsworth, how long?"

Cogsworth, a tick who ticked. "Ten years, four months, sixteen days — NOT one second of it my idea—"

Mrs. Potts, a teapot-brown flea, with Chip, her smallest, who lived in a chipped thimble. "Never mind them, dear. Nibble something."

"Do not be alarmed," said Lumière, as Belle began to itch, "but tonight — we perform."

That night the fleas threw her a banquet in her own fur, hundreds of them, a full orchestra of chitin: "Be our pest! Be our pest! Put our itchy service to the test! Tails in knots? We'll undo them! Be our PEST!"

She was given the finest room, and warned of only one rule: the West Hollow, the Beast's own chamber, was forbidden. Naturally she went at once. Inside: a shredded portrait of a handsome young silverback, and the banana under its glass, browning like a bruised sky. The Beast found her there and roared so terribly that she fled into the night — where the wild dogs found her, and would have ended the story, had the Beast not come tearing out of the dark after them. He fought them off, and collapsed, wounded.

"You came back," he said, amazed.

"Even fleas deserve better company than dogs," said Belle, and tended his cuts with fig leaves, and the Beast learned to hide his fangs when he laughed.

"There may be something there," observed Mrs. Potts, "that wasn't itching there before."

Then, one morning, the Beast flung open a door with his eyes shut — the bravest thing he had done since the dogs. "A gift," he said. "Don't thank me. I can't watch."

Belle stepped through, and stopped. Shelves. Galleries. Fig-leaf books beyond counting, floor to canopy, every book ever pressed, and a ladder on wheels.

"All of them?" she whispered.

"All of them. It's yours."

She read standing up, sideways, and once, memorably, upside down, and the castle went quiet in the good way, like a library with fur.

Then came the worst week. Maurice staggered home raving about beasts, and Gaston saw his opening: marry me, Belle, or your father goes to the howling madhouse. The Beast let her go. "He's your father," he said. "Go." He handed her the magic mirror, so she could look at the castle whenever she wished. "And take this. So you'll remember —" he paused, being brave — "the fleas."

And behind them, the last spot spread.

At home, Belle proved her father sane in one move: "Show me the Beast," she said to the mirror, and there he was, huge and wounded and alone — and the town saw the monster, and Gaston saw his solution.

"The Beast is mangy!" he bellowed to the mob he had assembled with free cider. "It's got FLEAS!"

"The castle," muttered LeFlea, deeply conflicted, "is on the far ridge."

"KILL THE BEAST! We don't like what we don't understand! In fact it scares us! AND THIS ONE HAS FLEAS!"

The mob stormed the gates with torches. The flea-servants fought magnificently — a thousand tiny champions in livery, biting ankles with true patriotism. Cogsworth led a tick brigade up eleven trouser legs simultaneously. The wardrobe swallowed a villager whole and spat him out dressed as a fruit-seller, deeply confused about his career. Chip, in his thimble, was loaded into a spoon and fired as artillery.

At the top of the tower Gaston found the Beast, sulking toward the end of his banana. "She'll never have a MONSTER—" he sneered, and swung his thorn-knife. But grief had made the Beast angry, and anger had made him strong, and he held Gaston out over the roof-edge by the throat — and then, at the last, set him gently down.

"Get out," said the Beast. "You're not worth the spot on my banana."

Gaston, being Gaston, struck once more as the Beast turned away — missed, overbalanced, and fell into the deep fig-bushes below, where he itched insufferably for the rest of his life, which served. LeFlea retired to a quiet career in accounting.

The Beast lay dying on the rooftop as the banana's last spot bloomed black. "You came back," he whispered.

"I love you," said Belle. "Fleas and all."

The banana cracked — and light. The monstrous shape dissolved into a tall, tidy prince of the silverbacks, and the fleas whirled up in a golden cloud and became monkeys again: a butler with a candlestick, a housekeeper with a teapot, and one very small boy with a chipped thimble, which he kept.

And as they danced in the restored hall, the whole castle sang:

"Tale as old as thyme,
Flea as old as rhyme,
Beauty and the Beast — and, yes,
his fleas."
