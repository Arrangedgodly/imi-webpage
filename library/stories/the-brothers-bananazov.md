# The Brothers Bananazov

*A parody of "The Brothers Karamazov"*

Alexei Fyodorovich Bananazov was the third son of Fyodor Pavlovich Bananazov, a landowner well known in our district on account of his tragic and obscure death, which I shall relate in due course.

Fyodor Pavlovich was, in monkey terms, a disaster: a grasping, lecherous old bonobo with a pile of bananas and no dignity, three dead or fled wives, and three sons discarded in childhood to be raised by strangers.

Now the sons were grown and returning, and the whole jungle could smell the storm coming.

The eldest, Dmitri, was a magnificent, reckless ape with a taste for nectar, betrothed to the proud, beautiful Katerina, and madly in lust with Grushenka, a voluptuous young monkey with a tail that could start wars — and his father wanted her too.

"Three thousand bananas of my inheritance!" Dmitri thundered. "MY bananas, held back by that depraved old father — who now squanders them on perfume for MY Grushenka! Why is such a monkey allowed to go on drinking and stinking and ruining his sons?"

"Because," said the second brother, Ivan, coolly peeling a banana with terrible elegance, "there is no law without, only law within. I have written a most interesting article about it."

Ivan was the intellectual — lean, brilliant, tormented — and had said the most famous thing he ever said:

"If God does not exist, everything is permitted. If there is no immortal soul, then I am accountable to no branch, no canopy, no sky — only to my own appetite."

The youngest was Alyosha, the gentle, glowing novice whom every monkey in the book loves, who loved the earth, the trees, the sinners, and especially children. His elder at the monastery was Father Zosima, a wizened old gorilla so holy that moths landed on him by preference.

The whole family, absurdly, gathered at the monastery to settle the inheritance — and old Fyodor, taking holy objects for napkins, made such a spectacle of himself that Zosima suddenly rose, walked to Dmitri, and bowed to the ground before him. The troop gasped.

"He bowed to the shame of it," Alyosha understood. "To the great suffering in store for that monkey — for the one who would be asked to carry the heaviest branch of all."

"I have no peace," Dmitri told Alyosha afterward, gripping his own fur. "Here the banana is trapped in the peel — beautiful and terrible! It's the devil and God fighting, and the battlefield is the heart of a Bananazov."

Zosima sent Alyosha out of the monastery with one command: "Go, my son. Go love the children. Go be a monkey among monkeys."

And that same night Zosima died — whereupon his body, instead of performing the fragrant miracles the troop expected, began to smell like any dead ape's, which scandalized the monastery and tested every monkey in it.

Ivan, meanwhile, had one more thing to say to Alyosha: a poem. "The Grand Inquisitor."

"In Seville," said Ivan, "Christ returns — quietly, gently, in the jungle heat. The troops recognize Him; He heals a blind lemur; the cardinal, the Grand Inquisitor, has Him arrested. In the prison cell that night, the old cardinal comes to Him and says:

"'Why hast Thou come to hinder us? We have taken Thy work and corrected it. We have taken from the monkeys their terrible freedom and given them bread, miracle, and authority — happiness for the little ones, not that savage choice of Thine. And tomorrow I shall burn Thee, and the people will heap the coals themselves. Not one will stand up for Thee.'

"And Christ — who has not said one word through all of it — rises, and kisses the old Inquisitor on the bloodless, wrinkled brow. The Inquisitor shudders, opens the door: 'Go, and come no more.' And the Prisoner goes."

"That's... rather a lot," said Alyosha.

"Yes," said Ivan, "and you've just kissed me, so let's go drink and eat. I love your sticky little banana-scented love too, you know. It's just not enough. I don't reject God, Alyosha — I am returning my ticket."

The storm broke on a Thursday.

Dmitri, believing Grushenka had gone to his father's nest at midnight, crept into the grove with a brass pestle in his sleeve, beat the old male nearly senseless, and fled — arrested hours later, bloody-pawed, with three thousand bananas in a bag around his neck, crying, "What old male? I killed no one! But oh — I WANTED to! With every hair!"

The trouble was that somebody had finished the job.

Fyodor was found at dawn, skull crushed, his strongbox of "Grushenka bananas" missing. Dmitri had motive, blood, and the bananas.

But first — Grushenka. When she heard Dmitri was taken, something broke open in her vanity-hardened heart, and she came to him in the cell, and fed him, and stayed. And she told Alyosha her fable of the onion: "There was a wicked old female who died and was dropped in the lake of fire. Her guardian angel thought of one good deed: she'd once given an onion to a beggar. God said — take the onion, hold it out to her, pull her up. She grabbed it, and the other sinners in the lake grabbed her fur to climb out too, and she kicked them off — MINE, it's MY onion! — and the onion broke. That's me, Alyosha. I'd break the onion."

"I would not," said Alyosha. "And you would not either."

The trial was a circus. Katerina, loving and hating Dmitri past all reason, produced the "mathematical" evidence — a letter in which Dmitri had written, drunk, "I may kill my father tonight" — and sweet Grushenka, sobbing in the gallery, was called a man-eater by three separate lawyers in one afternoon.

Ivan went quietly, magnificently insane in the witness box.

"I know who killed him!" said Ivan, gray-faced. "I taught him! I said everything is permitted, and he wrote it down. I have a witness! He is sitting there — there — by the window!"

There was no one by the window. (Though Ivan, the night before, had entertained a shabby, smell-of-apricots visitor in his own drawing room — a seedy gentleman ape who kept insisting he was real, and that he existed only because Ivan could not believe in the devil without believing in God: "You are me," Ivan told him. "Myself. My fever. The me that does not exist." "Delightful," said the devil, crossing his legs.)

And the actual murderer sat quietly in the third row.

Smerdyakov — the epileptic servant-lemur, Fyodor's unacknowledged fourth son, who had cooked the meals and believed every word Ivan ever wrote. He had taken the pestle, the strongbox, and the logic. "You taught me everything is permitted," he told Ivan, "and I merely obeyed. You are the real murderer. I only held the stick." Then he went home and hanged himself in the cellar.

Dmitri was convicted — wrongly, everyone privately agreed, but the troop needed a story — and sentenced to twenty years in Siberia. He accepted it with the strange radiant relief of a passionate monkey handed, at last, a cross to carry:

"Very well! I did not kill him — but I AM guilty, guilty before all monkeys for everything, and so I will suffer and by suffering be purified! A monkey can rise from the depths by one act of love!"

And Alyosha — with Ivan's madness and Dmitri's chains tangling in his gentle heart — went out to the twelve schoolboys on the hillside, at the funeral of little Ilyusha, the schoolboy whose father had been shamed and struck by old Fyodor, and who had died, like all the innocents in this book, asking whether it was fair. They buried him by the great stone, and the boys — who had beaten Ilyusha and loved him too late — brought flowers, and one brought a dog.

"I found Zhuchka again," the boy announced. "Ilyusha's dog. I'll bring him tomorrow."

And Alyosha spoke the words the whole book had been climbing toward:

"Let us go, hand in hand, from this stone. We shall rise again! There is nothing higher, stronger, more wholesome in life than a good memory, especially one from childhood, from the little ones. And if only one good memory remains in our hearts — that alone may save us all our days. And whatever happens to us in life, let us never bury the Ilyusha inside us alive.

"And now let's go to his memorial pancakes! Pancakes and bananas, little monkeys — and we shall all climb — ever upward!"

And the boys raised their caps and shouted: "Hurrah for Bananazov!"

And the great canopy rustled, as if in blessing, over them all.
