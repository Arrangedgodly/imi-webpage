# Coldheart Canopy

*A parody of "Coldheart Canyon"*

Todd Pickett had been the Pride of the Jungle Circuit for eleven years — the swingingest action chimp in pictures, famous for falling out of planes into rivers and out of rivers into the arms of whatever beautiful monkey was that season's cover story — and then, in one summer, he turned forty-one and made *Primate Park III*, and Hollywood decided it had noticed.

"They're saying you look tired, sweetheart," said his agent, Maxine, a macaque with a telephone grown into her ear. "Nobody's saying *old*. They're saying *tired.* So we're going to get you a little vacation from your face."

"It's fur," said Todd. "You mean a vacation from my fur."

"A refreshment," said Maxine. "A small fur-lift. They take the pelt from behind the shoulders and re-drape it; Hollywood's been doing it since the twenties. You'll convalesce somewhere private."

The surgery went wrong the way surgeries go wrong in stories: quietly, then all at once. A patch behind his ear went septic; the re-draped pelt sat like a rug on a wet floor; and Todd Pickett, who could not be photographed, could not be seen, could not be *perceived*, vanished from Cedars-Sinai between midnight and the morning papers.

"Where am I going?" he asked, in the back of the car, hood up, glasses on.

"Somewhere that isn't on maps," said Maxine. "The last real secret in this town."

It was called Coldheart Canyon, and the maps were right not to know it. There was no road. The navigation gave up at Sunset; Todd's driver, a silent baboon, took a firebreak, a streambed, a gap between two mansions' backsides that should not have held a car — and suddenly the city was gone. Above: chaparral, hawks, silence. Below: a fold in the hills with an overgrown garden in it, and one house with a hundred green shutters, all closed.

A woman was waiting on the steps. A langur, silver and black, in trousers and a man's shirt, with a bob from another century, the face of a nineteen-year-old, and the eyes of a very old queen.

"Mr. Pickett," she said. "I'm Katya. This is my house. In this canyon everyone will know your name and nobody will ever say it. That is the arrangement. Do you understand?"

"Are you an actress?"

"I was," said Katya, "before you were born."

He did the math. It didn't work. He was Todd Pickett, and he let it not work, because letting things not work is a movie star's most-practiced skill.

The first night, he heard the party.

Down the garden, past a dry pool and a monkey-cage aviary full of sleeping birds, a ballroom was lit and loud — a band playing a foxtrot, laughter, glasses — and Todd, bored, healing, vain, followed the music and looked in through the shutters at the most beautiful party he had ever seen.

Everyone was young. He recognized half of them, and that was impossible, because the ones he recognized had been dead sixty years and were nonetheless exactly as they looked in the poster outside his grandfather's cinema: the swashbuckling gorilla in the admiral's coat, the whispering gibbon in the slinky gown, the comic orangutan twins, a whole menagerie of animal stars from the jungle pictures of the thirties, mingling with their martinis.

There was a chimp at the window when Todd finally stopped breathing. Young, pretty, dressed for a premiere in 1927, holding a glass of champagne that did not slosh.

"Aren't you coming in?" the chimp asked pleasantly. "The party's been waiting for you. It's been waiting for *everybody.*"

That was how Todd Pickett entered the party at Coldheart Canyon, and became, over the following nights, its newest regular — dancing till four, sleeping till noon, watching his septic patch heal pink and his tired pelt come back glossy as a two-year-old's. Nobody asked what he was. Nobody asked what *they* were.

It was Tammy Lauper who ruined it, in the sense that truth usually does the ruining.

Tammy was his greatest fan on earth — a capuchin schoolteacher with a website, a heart condition, and a shoebox apartment carpeted in Todd Pickett memorabilia — and when her idol vanished, she did what the studios had stopped doing: she looked. She found the driver. She found the streambed. She came over the ridge at dusk with an asthma inhaler and a camera, got as far as the dry pool before the birds began to scream, and hid in the aviary while the garden filled with partygoers who were not casting shadows.

She was still there at midnight when Todd came looking for her, and by then she had seen too much, and so had he, and there was nothing for it but the truth.

"There's a room," Todd said quietly. "At the top of the house. Katya showed me. You have to see it, and you have to not scream."

The room had no windows, and every inch of the walls and the whole vast ceiling was covered in painted tiles — thousands upon thousands, sixteenth-century, blues and golds and hunting reds — and the tiles were not ornamental. They were a *wilderness.* As you watched, the hunt moved: hounds through undergrowth, a white stag swimming a river, figures with bows in the leaves. The room was warm as summer, and it hummed, and Tammy said afterwards it felt like standing inside a held breath.

"They came from a count's palace in Italy," said Katya, from the doorway. She had the face of a girl and the voice of a museum. "A devil's bargain of a ceiling, the friars said; painted by a monster who taught the tiles to hold the world he painted. A man named Zeffer bought the count's palace stripped — to please me. He loved me, Mr. Pickett, in 1926, when I was the most photographed langur on earth and dying of it. He brought the tiles here on three ships, rebuilt this room around them, and we had our party, our endless party, and the room gives what it gives." She touched her young cheek with the backs of her fingers. "Youth. Vitality. Continuity. Every guest you see down there is alive enough to dance and dead enough to never leave, and I am neither, which is the joke of the century. Zeffer wanted to give me forever. He gave me *here.*"

"And Zeffer?" asked Tammy, because someone had to.

"The room takes as it gives," Katya said simply. "That is the arrangement in this canyon. Everything you love is charged to the room, and eventually the bill comes. The party has been running since 1926 and the bill is very, very long. Zeffer paid it." She looked up at the painted hunt, at the white stag at bay. "They will want Todd to stay, you know. He's new. The party *runs* on new. And the room has been so hungry lately. Why do you think you healed, Mr. Pickett? The room was fattening the guest list."

The party came for him that night — up the stairs, beautiful, pleasant, and utterly unarguable with, the admiral gorilla taking Todd's arm in a grip like a dock winch: "Don't fuss, son. Nobody *leaves* the party. Nobody ever leaves."

And Tammy Lauper, schoolteacher, capuchin, five feet of loyal, did the only important act of her life: she picked up a fire iron and she smashed a tile.

One tile. A six-inch square of the painted hunt.

The room screamed. Not with sound — with *absence*, like a jungle dropping a whole acre of birds at once — and every guest in the doorway stopped, swayed, and looked suddenly, terribly, tired. The lion yawned and lay down. And Katya looked up at the ruined patch of sky in the ceiling, sixty years of held breath finally let out of her face in slow motion, aging before their eyes into the magnificent, ruined, real old queen of a vanished Hollywood.

"It's all right," she said, and she was smiling, and it was the first true expression Todd had ever seen her wear. "It's all right, darlings. Party's over. Go on and *be tired.*"

They went. The ballroom emptied like a tide going out; the band played slower, softer, stopped; and the beautiful ghosts of Coldheart Canyon lay down in the garden they'd haunted so glamorously, and aged, in a single moonset, into rest.

Katya died at dawn, in the ballroom, in Todd's arms, ninety years old and famous again for one morning.

The canyon is on the maps now. There was no keeping it after that. The house sold and resold; the tiles went to the museum; the ballroom is a wedding venue. If you stand in the garden at dusk you can hear birds, real ones, and once — honeymooners swear it — a band playing a foxtrot, very far away, taking a bow.

Todd Pickett went back to work the following spring — scarred behind the ear, gray at the muzzle, a character actor now, and better at it than he ever was at swinging. He married Tammy Lauper, of all monkeys, which the papers found baffling, his agent found career suicide, and he found the only honest thing in his whole filmography.

"Forever's easy," he told her once, "in a room with no windows. Any monkey can be young forever. You have to be *alive* to be tired."

"Come on, kid," he added, taking his wife's paw. "We'll be late for our own party."
