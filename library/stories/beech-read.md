# Beech Read

*A parody of "Beach Read"*

January Andrews had written eleven romance novels about monkeys finding love in vineyards, ballrooms, and one memorable orchard, and she had believed every banana of them until the year her father died and her fiancé left and her muse filed for separation.

Now she was broke, blocked, and standing on the porch of her dead father's lake house in Beechnut Falls, Michigan, holding a key that worked and a scotch she'd found under the sink. The house smelled like mildew and secrets. The realtor had called it "a fixer." January called it "evidence."

"Nice night for it," said a voice from the porch next door.

She turned and beheld a miracle of hostility: a gorilla in a flannel shirt, unshaven by design, holding a book of postwar Polish poetry like a shield.

"Gus Everett," she said.

"January Andrews," he said, with the exact tone of a monkey who'd just found a tick.

They'd gone to college together. He'd been the literary one, the smug one, the one who'd said in a workshop, in front of everyone, that romance was "emotional fast food for primates who can't chew." She'd retaliated in her thesis reading with a monologue about how literary fiction was just sadness with a grant. They had not spoken in nine years. Both of them, apparently, had moved to the same lake.

"What are you doing in my father's county?" she asked.

"Living in poverty with dignity. You?"

"Blocked," she admitted, because the scotch was strong and the porch was dark.

"No," said Gus. "Not you. You're a machine. What's the new one called — *The Seven-Tree Stand*?"

"The machine is broken," said January. "It turns out you need to believe in love to sell it, and I have recently been shown the receipts."

There was a silence. Down the lake, a loon made a noise like a monkey being audited.

"Deadlines?" said Gus.

"Six weeks. You?"

"Four months and change," said Gus, staring at his poetry like it owed him money. "I've written one paragraph in five months and my publisher is building a cage."

And that is when January Andrews, heartbroken romance novelist, said the thing that would ruin and rearrange her summer:

"Wanna trade? You write a happily ever after. I write something bleak and literary where everybody dies of irony. We take each other on research trips. Loser confesses their genre is harder."

Gus Everett looked at her for a long moment. "You're on," he said. "Fast food."

"Grant sadness."

---

Her research trips were his idea of warfare. January planned meet-cutes: the farmers' market bang-into-each-other banana collision, the stuck-in-a-treehouse-during-a-storm, the enemies-at-a-wedding. Gus attended each like an anthropologist at a ritual he intended to debunk, taking notes.

"So the premise," he said, at the market, having deliberately dropped a zucchini, "is that two strangers fight over produce and this is erotic."

"The premise is that the fight is a *cover story*," said January. "Both of them are lonely and this is the socially acceptable way to get the other monkey's attention. It's a mating display, Gus. Your people call it 'modern dance.'"

"You're describing a rom-com. My editor wants a *literary* romance. No tropes. Trophies, possibly. Grief."

"Then pay attention," she said, "because I'm about to teach you the biggest trope of all: two monkeys who insult each other every day until the insults are the only thing keeping either of them warm."

Gus wrote that down. Later she would find the notebook page and it would be underlined three times.

His field trips were worse. He took her to meet Shirl, a placid elderly chimp in a cardigan who had survived the Squirrel Wood Collective, a notorious cult of nut-hoarders who believed winter was a metaphor and storing food was a sin. Shirl told her story plainly, terribly, and then served banana bread, and on the drive home January found she had written four pages on a napkin.

"How do you do this?" she demanded. "How do you walk around with all this in you and still function?"

"Functioning is my genre," said Gus. "Also, I don't. My mom died when I was nineteen. My dad became a person I had to manage. I write bleak books because bleak books are the only place my family fits." He pulled into her gravel drive and looked at her. "Your turn. What's in the lake house?"

"My father," said January, "is in the lake house. Figuratively. He had a woman here. Twenty years of summers. He died before I could ask a single question, and I'm living inside the evidence, and every morning I sit down to write love and out comes him."

They sat in the truck with the engine ticking. It was, January would note later, the first honest conversation of her career.

"Okay, grant sadness," she said. "New terms. I'll write the bleak book — about a marriage that survives a lie. And you write the romance — the one where the lonely monkey gets the warm one. No posturing."

"Deal," said Gus, and shook her hand, and held it one beat past the standard interval, which under the terms of her own genre was practically a proposal.

---

The summer went like that: shared dinners, shared deadlines, the slow horror of compatibility. Gus fixed her porch rail. January taught him the meet-cute. He laughed at her jokes with his whole chest, like something shaking loose. By August they had both stopped pretending the arrangement was professional, and one warm night on the dock, mid-argument about second acts, one of them — the record is contested — kissed the other, and the trope of enemies-to-lovers claimed two more casualties.

Then September came, and with it the truth.

She found it in the lake house, in a box of her father's things Gus had carried down from the attic: a photograph. Her father, younger, on this dock — with a familiar cardigan-wearing chimp. Shirl. Shirl of the cult, Shirl of the banana bread. And behind them, unmistakably, in the background, thirty years younger in flannel: Gus.

"You knew him," she said, on the porch, holding the photo. "You knew my father. You've known all summer whose house this was — I *told* you about the affair — and you knew *her*, and you let me do that interview without a word, and you — you watched me grieve him wrong, in your truck, and said *nothing*."

"Yes," said Gus, because he was constitutionally incapable of lying to her, which was the whole problem and the whole point. "Because Shirl asked me to. Because your father asked me, years ago, when I was a kid cutting their grass and he caught me reading on their dock and talked to me like a person — because the story is his to hand you, not mine. January, your mother knew. They stayed. It's not a betrayal story. It's a marriage story. I couldn't say that to you. You'd have burned it."

"Maybe I needed to burn it!"

"Maybe," said Gus. "But I couldn't be the match."

She went inside. He didn't follow, which was his genre. She cried for an hour and then, because she was still a professional, sat down and typed until dawn, and in the morning she drove to Shirl's, alone, and had the conversation her father had owed her, and came home with banana bread and both books finished in her chest.

Theirs, anyway. Gus was still staring at chapter one of the romance, which by his own admission was "the hardest thing I've ever written, including the funeral."

"January," he said, the night they swapped manuscripts on the porch, per the terms. "Before you read mine. The bleak book you wrote — the marriage that survives a lie — it's the best thing either of us has ever done, and I include you in that sentence on purpose. And my romance — the lonely monkey and the warm one — it's not a trope. I didn't have to research it. I moved in next door to it."

January looked at him for a long time.

"You get one thing," she said. "It's called a *happy ending*, Gus. And you're going to have to show, not tell."

He showed.

---

The books came out a year apart and fought on the bestseller list like siblings. The critics called hers "surprisingly substantial" and his "surprisingly warm," and both of them kept those quotes framed in the lake house, which they fixed up that spring — her father's house, with its evidence and its history, two writers in residence, an unstandardized number of bananas in the bowl, and one dock where, most nights, the insults continue, warmly, forever.
