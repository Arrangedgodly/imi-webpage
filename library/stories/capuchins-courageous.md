# Capuchins Courageous

*A parody of "Captains Courageous"*

The fog came off the Grand Banks like wet wool, and somewhere in the middle of it, between New York and London-Canopy, went the S.S. Grand Tamarin — and over her rail went Harvey Cheene.

He was fifteen, only son of the monkey who owned ten thousand miles of the Great Jungle Railway, and, in his own opinion, the most important capuchin on the water. He had been demonstrating his importance over dinner — lighting a sugarcane cigarillo, refusing fruit, informing the table that his father could buy the ship and the ocean under it — when the rail, the fog, and a rogue wave conspired, and Harvey went into the Atlantic with a yelp nobody heard.

Manuel heard it. Manuel, a gentle brown Barbary macaque off the banana-schooner *We're Hair*, out of Gibboucester, was over the side in a flash, and hauled the boy — choking, half-drowned, fur ruined — onto a deck stacked with sixty thousand bananas.

"Eh, eh," said Manuel, patting him. "So. You are not drowned. That is good. You are only drowned a little."

"Take me back," Harvey gasped. "I'm Harvey Cheene. Do you have any idea what my father—"

"Your father," said a stump-tailed macaque with a face like weathered bark, "is three hundred miles west, going east at twenty knots, and we are one little schooner with a cargo of bananas, going nowhere but the fleet. I'm Disko Troop, master of this boat. You ship with us till the season's over, boy, and you work your passage like a monkey."

"I don't *work*! I'm worth a hundred of this whole boat!"

The crew — Long Jack; Tom Platt, who had once served on the war-canoe *Pennsylvmacaque* and would tell you so; Salters, who farmed a phrase the way other monkeys farmed figs; Disko's son Dan, who was Harvey's age; and little Penn, a grey marmoset preacher with far-away eyes — looked at the wet little figure with something between pity and appetite.

Manuel, gently and enormously, knocked him down.

Just once, and not hard; it was Manuel's whole theology in a single blow. "You were getting ready to lie," he explained sorrowfully, helping him up. "The sea does not care who your father is. The sea says: hold on, or let go."

The next morning, Harvey learned the first article of the fleet's constitution: aboard the *We're Hair* there were no passengers, only hands, and no free bananas. Disko presented him, with great formality, an itemized account of everything he had eaten since being fished out of the ocean, including, with an asterisk, the sugar in the coffee he had spilled swearing about it.

"I'll take it out of your wages," said Disko. "When you've earned any."

"Wages!" said Harvey. "Wages for what? And why is the boat called the *We're Hair*?"

"Because we're hair," said Dan, delighted, "and don't ask where."

For a week Harvey was useless and knew it, and the knowing was the beginning of his education. Then the fleet anchored on the Banks, and the dories went out, and Dan Troop taught him the rest: how to read a sky, how to run a line of bananas down a rolling deck, how to cut, trim, and pack the bunches in the hold before the spray touched them, how to salt down a catch and swear at a fog, how to take a trick at the wheel, and how to tell, by the way Disko's tail curled at the tiller, whether the weather meant to be kind. Tom Platt taught him the bearings of the *Pennsylvmacaque* and, incidentally, longitude. Long Jack taught him the names of every rope, twice. Salters taught him that a monkey who talked while others worked was fertilizer. And Penn, baiting a line beside him one flat silver morning, told him, in a voice like a distant bell, about the flood that had taken his wife and children, and how his mind, walking softly, had gone back for them and never quite caught up; and Harvey, who had never in his life been silent on purpose, was silent for a whole watch.

Something in the boy came out of its shell. The crew called it finding his sea-fur.

The season did what seasons do. There was fog that put the fleet in fear of itself, and one white night when Harvey and Dan, lost in a dory with a biscuit and a bell-buoy's opinion of where they weren't, fired off their one signal charge and were found at dawn by Disko, who had not slept and did not intend to mention it. There was a waterspout that walked by like a column of smoke with a temper. There was the great liner that bore down on their quarter one glassy afternoon, and Harvey, waving his arms on the fo'c'sle, hollered his own name up at her bridge — "Cheene! I'm Harvey Cheene! Tell my father!" — and heard, drifting down, the liner's skipper reply, "And I'm the Emperor of Brazil," as she sheered off. (But the skipper passed the word anyway, for a joke, and the joke went up the coast, and into a club car, and thence, by degrees, to a gray-furred magnate who had been grieving for a drowned son.)

And there was the night the Frenchmonkey went down — a dory stove in the dark, two hands lost — when nobody sang at the splitting of the catch, and Disko Troop took his hat off, and little Penn said the old words, and the bananas went on ripening in the hold regardless, which was the whole sermon.

"I guess I owe you my life," Harvey told Disko one evening at the wheel, "and I guess I was a snob, sir."

"You *was* a passenger," said Disko Troop. "Now you're a hand. That's all the difference there is, and it's all the difference in the world."

When at last the *We're Hair* raised Gibboucester, the crew was paid off at the settle-up table, coin by coin down the line, and Harvey earned his first pay — a boy's third of a half-share, a hundred and fifty dollars in real money, which he counted four times with his paw shaking. Manuel, paid beside him, gripped his shoulder with a paw like a friendly fathom of rope.

"You was a good boy on the boat," said Manuel. "Now you are a hand. So — eh, eh — remember Manuel, who pulled you out." And Harvey, who once would have tipped him a quarter, said, "I'll remember everything," and meant it, and did.

He dictated a telegram to Cheene Senior: PICKED UP BY BANANA SCHOONER WE'RE HAIR. AM ALIVE. AM ALSO DIFFERENT. WILL EXPLAIN.

The railroad magnate arrived on a chartered steamer, braced for a tantrum and a ransom demand; instead a brown, competent young capuchin with rope-calluses swung down the gangway, gripped his hand hard, and said, "How are you, Father? I've a lot to tell you, and not all of it flattering. And the first thing is — is Mother all right?"

"She has worn the bark off a mile of pacing-branch," said the magnate, who had a cold and had been crying into it. "You'll cable her yourself."

They dined aboard, all of them, magnate and crew, and Cheene Senior — who had bought half a jungle but never once hauled on a line with his son — listened to Disko Troop over the fig wine.

"He's got the making of a first-class banana-man," said Disko. "Give him a season or two. And give my Dan a desk on your railway, if you want to square the debt."

"Done," said the magnate, and it was.

Go down to Gibboucester today, and they will tell you the firm of Cheene & Troop runs the fleet and the freight both, and that neither partner signs until they have shaken on it — one hand a magnate's, and one a hand that grips.
