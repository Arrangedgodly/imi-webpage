# Dreamscratcher

*A parody of "Dreamcatcher"*

Every November the four of them climbed the same ridge to the same treehouse — a drafty shack in the crown of a dead oak called Hole-in-the-Wall — and ate the same bananas. Jonesy, Beaver, Henry, and Pete had been a troop since boyhood, ever since the day they hauled little Duddits Cavell out of a drainage pipe where the big jungle kids had stuffed him. ("You shouldn't oughta stuff a fella in a pipe," Beaver had observed, and the big jungle kids, meeting his eyes, had agreed in writing.) Duddits was a small, funny-faced capuchin who saw the world through the wrong end of a telescope, and nobody else had time for him. But he had repaid the four of them a thousand times over: from that day on, they could hear one another's thoughts, clear as a howler at dawn. One band, one frequency, one bunch, hanging together.

On the walk home they had made him a gift — a hoop of vine strung with shredded bark, a net for bad dreams. Duddits held it up to the light, delighted, and said, "I dream you."

They thought he had the words wrong. It would be twenty-five years before they understood he had them exactly right.

"SSDD," said Beaver on the first night at the lodge, dealing out the 'nanas like cards. Same Bananas, Different Day.

"I heard that," said Henry, who had not said it out loud.

"Everybody heard that," said Pete from the bunk above. "I heard his hearing of it. It was smug."

Then the blizzard came down like a dropped mattress, and out of it staggered a hunter named Rick McCarthy, red-faced and shivering. "Got lost," he chattered. "Lights in the sky. And Lord, I am itchy."

They put him to bed with a hot brick and a banana. His teeth chattered in radio code. "You don't understand," he told the ceiling, from deep inside a fever. "It came down in the woods. I only looked at it. Looking was my mistake." Then he asked whether anyone else smelled bananas, and everyone said no, because everyone was lying.

While the others went to flag down help, McCarthy sat bolt upright and sneezed a red weevil into the toilet — a fat, furious, gibbering thing that thrashed like a peeled 'nana in a fry pan and tried to climb out of the bowl.

"It wants the banana supply!" Beaver cried, slamming the seat and sitting on it. "It gets in the water, it gets in the trees, it gets in everything! Bite the peel, man, don't let it escape!"

Beaver trapped the weevil under a peanut-butter jar, heroic as any monkey in any story, and sat upon the lid with his legs crossed. In the long history of sieges, few garrisons have held a latrine with more dignity.

And that was when the light came. A great silver beam swept the forest, gathering up the deer and the parrots and the jar, weevil and all — and, before Jonesy could grab his tail, Beaver too, lifted gently into the sky like a banana joining a bunch.

"BEAV!" Jonesy screamed — into the storm, into the frequency. The frequency held static. In twenty-five years, it had never once held static.

Then something else stepped into Jonesy's mind the way a stranger steps into an empty office: Mr. Gray, a gibbon-shaped patch of smoke with terrible manners. *I'll borrow this body,* it said. *There is a silo in the north where the world's seed bananas sleep. I mean to plant a weevil in every one.*

Jonesy slammed the doors of his memory warehouse — every banana he had ever eaten, filed and shelved — and hung out a sign reading NO ADMITTANCE, ESPECIALLY GRAY GIBBONS. Mr. Gray prowled the aisles, opening drawers: banana bread recipes; the entire smoothie summer of '09; eleven years of grocery lists; the plots of every serial Pete had ever narrated out loud, twice. *Where is the silo?* it demanded.

Everything I know about silos, thought Jonesy, I keep in the room marked FARM STUFF, WHICH I DO NOT HAVE.

Mr. Gray took the body anyway and drove it north through the snow while Jonesy rode along inside, shouting directions to nowhere. At a roadside fruit stand it met Pete, hitching home through the storm. For one instant Pete saw Jonesy's face with somebody else behind it — *Jonesy?* — and Mr. Gray touched his brow, and Pete fell asleep standing up among the papayas. He woke three days later in a strawberry patch two hundred miles south, sunburned, famished, and unable to find a single thought on the frequency. It was the loneliest morning of his life. He spent most of it eating strawberries, which helped.

Miles away, Henry felt it happen: one friend gone up the light, one possessed, one snoring among strawberries. He had been picked up by the army, which had thrown a quarantine of rope and clipboards around the whole forest. It was run by Colonel Curtz, a scarred old silverback with a thousand-yard stare and a policy. "Contamination," said Curtz, "is cured by fire." He planned, in the calm way of silverbacks, to peel the county like a banana and burn the peel.

"You'll burn four troops' worth of jungle for one weevil?" said Henry.

"I'll burn two counties for half a weevil," said Curtz, and stamped a form to that effect.

But Henry bargained hard, because fifty miles south, in a shopping cart pushed by his tired old mother, lived Duddits. Duddits was dying of the slows. His muzzle had gone gray. His mother wheeled him along the beach path, and he watched the sky through the wrong end of his telescope, waiting for something he could not name.

"Give me two hours and one fast chimp," said Henry. And Colonel Curtz — who beneath the scar and the clipboard had once been somebody's juvenile too — looked at the map a long moment, and gave him three.

What happened next, no two witnesses tell the same way, and the witnesses include a colonel, two pilots, and a shopping cart. Some say a second light split the first. Some say the sky over the silo turned the yellow of a ripe bunch, and out of it came a small capuchin riding a shopping cart like a chariot, pushed downhill by Henry and an air-force chimp named Underhill, both hooting like the whole fourth of July.

Mr. Gray stood at the silo door with the last jarred weevil under one arm, and the door would not open, because Jonesy — locked in his own head — had eaten the key. (The key was a banana. Keys are for monkeys with no imagination.)

"I Duddits," said the capuchin, standing up in the cart on the last of his strength. "I the dreamscratcher."

And he caught Mr. Gray by his smoky scruff — the way he himself had once been caught by four friends in a pipe — and held on. The hoop came out of the cart, the hoop of vine and shredded bark, and it worked the way it had always worked: the bad dreams went into the net. Twenty-five years of them. And a gray gibbon of smoke went with them, dragged up and out, one little monkey swinging one gray goblin into the cold between the stars — where bad dreams hold together exactly as long as somebody keeps dreaming them, and nobody was dreaming Mr. Gray anymore except Duddits, and Duddits let go.

The weevil, unsupervised, sulked back into its jar and was later given a government job in quality control.

The silo was saved. Jonesy woke in a snowbank holding a perfect banana. Pete was fetched home from the strawberries, and the frequency came back on mid-laugh, which is the best way. And Beaver returned three days after that, deposited in a drift by a beam of light — sunburned, famished, and bursting with a story only four monkeys would ever believe, plus one that even they wouldn't.

They buried Duddits in the spring, on the ridge trail where five kids had once walked home with a hoop. Bad dreams keep away from that hill to this day.

The next November they climbed the ridge again. They set an extra banana on the windowsill for a capuchin gone up the light, and Mrs. Cavell sat in Beaver's old chair, and nobody mentioned the weevil jar.

"SSDD," said Henry softly.

"Same Bananas, Different Day," agreed Jonesy.

"But keep one eye on the sky," said Beaver, and passed the bunch.
