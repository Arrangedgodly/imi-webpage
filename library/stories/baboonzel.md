# Baboonzel

*A parody of "Rapunzel"*

Once there lived a young baboon couple, and when the wife was expecting, she grew cravings. Chief among them: rambutans. Not just any rambutans — the fat red ones in the walled garden of Dame Grothel, a gorilla sorceress so feared that even the wind went around her wall.

They tasted of honey and crime, and the wife wanted more. So night after night her husband climbed the wall — one rambutan the first night, three the second, a whole armful and a mouthful besides on the third — and on that third night, as his hand closed on the reddest fruit of all, the garden went dark, and Dame Grothel's eyes opened like two yellow lanterns.

"How dare you steal my rambutans," said the gorilla, "in my own garden?"

"Have mercy!" cried the baboon. "My wife saw your rambutans from her window, and now she cannot think of anything else!"

"Neither," said Dame Grothel, "could you, apparently. Rambutans are like that."

The sorceress's smile was worse than her scowl. "Take all the rambutans you like," she said. "In exchange, I shall have the child your wife carries. Fair is fair. It eats my fruit, I eat — no. I *raise* it. Raising is the word I meant."

The baboon agreed — he was holding the rambutans, and they smelled of honey, and the lantern eyes were very close. When the baby came, a little girl with a red-gold ruff, Dame Grothel appeared at the door, carried her off, and named her Baboonzel, after the tree. The parents stood in the doorway with their arms full of fruit and their arms empty of daughter, which is the shape of every bargain like that one.

To keep her from the whole troop, the sorceress shut her at the top of a kapok tower with no door and no stairs, only one high window. Baboonzel grew there, and her hair grew with her — a red-gold mane, twenty branches long, strong as a liana — and each evening Dame Grothel would call up:

**"Baboonzel, Baboonzel, let down your hair — it's grooming hour!"**

Then Baboonzel would unpin her mane, coil it out the window, and let it fall, and the great gorilla would climb it, grunt, settle in, and pick through her hair for lice she had brought herself — because that is how some gorillas say *I own you*.

It was, in fairness, an education of a kind. Dame Grothel taught her the names of poisons, the moods of weather, the grudges of forty years. What she did not teach her was anybody else's name.

"Who lives in that far copse?" Baboonzel asked once.

"Nobody," said Dame Grothel.

"What's that drumming sound at dusk?"

"Nobody drumming."

"Why won't you make a door?"

"Doors," said Dame Grothel, "are how you lose people."

And Baboonzel, who had never had anyone to lose, sang to the nobody instead — all day, to the copse and the drumming and the empty blue air, because a song with nowhere to go goes a long way.

Now it happened that a young howler prince, riding the outer branches on a border patrol, heard that song floating from the top of a tower with no door, and lost his heart, and lost his voice — which, for a howler, is saying everything. He came back the next evening, and the next, and watched from the leaves until he had learned the words of the world's most dangerous game. And one night, when the sorceress had gone, he gathered himself and called up in his best old-gorilla voice:

**"Baboonzel, Baboonzel, let down your hair — it's grooming hour!"**

Down came the mane, up went the prince, and Baboonzel could only stare — he was the first creature her own age she had ever seen. Also he was slightly tangled in her hair, which diminished his dignity and improved her opinion of him at once.

"Your song," he managed, "is the best thing I ever heard. I lost my howl for it."

"You climb better than she does," said Baboonzel, unwinding him. "You weigh nothing. She pulls like a hippo."

"Who?"

"The gorilla who owns the tower. And me. Mostly me."

He came the next night, and the next after. He brought her a spiral shell that hummed sea-noise, a beetle shell like a green jewel, and news — that there was a drumming troop over the ridge, and figs in season two valleys east, and that the sunset, viewed from a *different* window, was a different color entirely. She laughed at that as though he'd said something wonderful, because to her he had. And when he asked her, one moonlit night, to marry him, she said yes before he finished the sentence — she had finished it herself days earlier, several times, in different voices.

"I can't climb down," she said, patting her hair, which was wound around the window-bar like rigging. "It lives here."

"Then we'll make a ladder," said the prince. "Each night I'll bring a bundle of silk-grass. You braid it with liana into a rope. When the rope is long enough, you'll climb down like an ordinary baboon, and we'll never tell anyone the hair was involved."

So each night he brought a bundle, and each night Baboonzel wove — and she was diligent, and quick, and happy, which any prison-warder will tell you is deeply suspicious.

One evening, climbing, Dame Grothel grunted, "Heavy tonight, my pet. What have you been eating?"

"Nothing," said Baboonzel. "Only — why are you so much heavier to pull up than the young howler prince?"

The silence that followed had teeth in it.

"The howler prince," said Dame Grothel, very softly, "hears songs. The howler prince knows the words. MY PET HAS BEEN RECEIVING CALLERS."

She snipped the mane off close to the skull with a shell-knife, and it fell around Baboonzel like red-gold rain, twenty branches of it, and Baboonzel said, over and over, the most punished word in the jungle: "He'll come back. He'll come back. He'll come back."

"Yes," said Dame Grothel, coiling the shorn mane over one arm like a stole. "That part was easy to arrange." And she banished Baboonzel there and then, down the outside of the wall, into the dry wilderness beyond, and kept the hair for company — grooming it, by all accounts, nightly, and getting no thanks whatsoever.

That night, when the dark went soft with a young voice calling *grooming hour*, the tower window answered. Down came the severed braid, silver at the roots. Up climbed the prince, joy first, alarm second.

"Your song was clever," said Dame Grothel out of the dark. "But a cat cannot keep a bird from singing forever." And she let go of the braid — and the prince fell, down and down, past the tower's whole height, into the thorn trees below. The thorns caught at his eyes, and the world went white, and then dark. And Dame Grothel, peering after him, satisfied at last, said the thing she would say to the empty tower for years: "Nobody's up there now."

Blind, he wandered out of the jungle, into the dry savanna, howling for her, living on roots and grief. And Baboonzel — banished to that same wilderness — wandered too, with twin babies on her back, a boy and a girl with her red-gold ruff, searching for a voice heard once through a window. The wilderness was wide and flat and cruel to singers: no cover, no water worth the name, no echo. She ate what the babies let her eat, which was little, and she called into the dark each dusk, and each dusk the dark declined to answer.

Until one day the wind carried a howl.

She followed it until her feet bled, and found him at last — hollow-eyed and thorn-scarred, calling to no one, in a voice that had come back rough and broken and beautiful. He heard her weeping before he heard her steps — for he had gone blind, not deaf — and he reached up, and touched her face, and knew it the way you know a song.

And Baboonzel bent over him and groomed him as she had groomed no one since the tower — finding each thorn-scratch with her fingers, licking the milky sap from his eyes, drop by patient drop, while the twins watched from her back with enormous eyes. And the dark thinned, and the white cleared, and the first thing he ever saw was her, and the second and third things were the two small red-gold strangers gripping her fur.

"You found me," said the prince.

"You climbed," said Baboonzel. "Twice. Somebody had to groom you. You look like a thornbush."

"They have your ruff."

"They have your volume."

He laughed until the savanna rang. Then, the twins on his shoulders, he led her out of the wilderness to his father's mango kingdom, where there was weeping in the good way, and a wedding under the blossoms, and a door — a great, wide, famous door — which Baboonzel insisted on installing in the oldest tower of the palace, kept forever unlocked, in memory of every monkey who ever waited at a window.

And they lived happily, groomed often, and unprickled, ever after — which is more than can be said for Dame Grothel, who grooms a stolen braid to this very day, and cannot think why it never grooms her back.
