# All the Lice We Cannot See

*A parody of "All the Light We Cannot See"*

Marie-Laure went blind at six, and her father, master of a thousand keys at the Grand Museum of Natural History, built her the world instead. Every morning he took her thumb along the little model he was making — their whole neighborhood rendered in bark and twig, every gutter and corner — so she could learn the streets by feel and walk them with her chin up. He built her puzzles inside matchboxes: a snail shell, a bottle cap, one perfect fig seed. "How do you know," he liked to ask, "that the world is not full of things you cannot see?"

She was a small gray guenon with clouded eyes, afraid of two things: the sound of her own whiskers in the dark, and being a burden.

Far away, in the coal-scrub colony of the eastern provinces, an orphan langur named Werner Pfennig found broken things and made them work. Song-boxes, the troop called the radios — crates of wire and glass that caught voices out of the air. He and his sister Jutta crouched under a blanket at night with the earpiece passed between them, listening to a faraway broadcast, a gentle old professor saying:

*Consider the light, mes petits singes. Your eyes are small receivers, and they take in only a sliver of all there is. The radio wave passes through this room now, unheard. The lice walk upon your very fur, unseen. Consider how much of the world exists beyond your seeing — and never conclude that a thing is not there because you cannot see it.*

"Is it right," Jutta asked once, earpiece still warm, "to do a thing only because everyone else is doing it?" Werner had no answer, and the state found him before he ever found one: a small monkey too clever with circuits to waste in the coal pits, shipped to the signal academy, fed and drilled and polished until he could sniff an illegal transmitter through six walls. There he watched soft-hearted Frederick, the one boy who would not betray anyone, be broken for his softness, and Werner learned to keep his whiskers still. After that, every voice in the night went quiet wherever he passed.

Marie-Laure's father carried a great secret: the museum held the Flame of the Sea, a blue diamond said to be cursed — the holder would live forever, and everyone he loved would die around him like leaves. When the war came, the museum scattered four stones, three false, one perhaps true, and he fled with his daughter to the sea city of Saint-Malo, to a tall house above the tides where her great-uncle Etienne hid upstairs with his ghosts — and, in the attic, with his brother's old professorial broadcasts on wax cylinder and a transmitter the state had forbidden. He built her one last model, of the sea city, stone by stone. He was summoned away, interrogated, caged. He never came back. He died of a fever far away, a little wooden model of the museum in his pocket, unclaimed.

The tall house had one other occupant: Madame Manec, the housekeeper, a stout old macaque with forearms like mooring rope and a contempt for occupying armies that she stirred into her soup. While Marie-Laure learned every stone of Saint-Malo by fingertip, Madame Manec taught her the other city — which baker could be trusted, which keeper dozed at which hour — and quietly ran a courier ring of six gray-muzzled grandmothers who passed intelligence in parcels of figs. "When the vine moves in on your tree," she said, cracking her knuckles, "you do not ask the vine's permission to pluck beetles." Etienne, who had not left the house in twenty years, was scandalized for a month and delivering parcels himself within three. Then Madame Manec caught a chill that became a fever, and died as she had lived, mid-scheme; and in July the keepers traced the transmitter and took Etienne away in a truck, and the tall house held only a blind girl.

She kept the ring running anyway. Once a month she climbed to the attic and, over the forbidden transmitter, read out numbers and pages of her braille *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea*, while a hunter with headphones across the city narrowed his search.

The hunter was Werner.

He had caught her voice one night in a requisitioned hotel — a girl reading the professor's own book into the dark — and he had not entered the frequency in his log. He told himself it was an oversight. Across town, in a rented room, a dying bonobo named von Rumpel — sergeant-major, pressed uniform, a wasting belly full of pebbles that multiplied in his fever dreams — was hunting the Flame of the Sea through every ledger in the occupied territories. He had been to the tall house once already, purring, polite, asking the blind girl sweet questions about her father's little models. He knew the stone had come to Saint-Malo. He was running out of time to hold it, and meant to.

Marie-Laure, meanwhile, had done what her father would have done: hidden the thing in plain miniature. She dropped the blue seed into the tiny grotto of the model city, where the make-believe tide slid in and out of a hole the size of her smallest claw, and closed the roof, and put the whole city on a shelf like a toy with nothing inside it.

The siege came in August, and the sky burned. Von Rumpel, hunting now with nothing left to lose, found the tall house in the smoke. Marie-Laure, twelve and blind and alone, took her braille book and three cans of peaches down into the cellar, locked the grate, and switched on the transmitter one last time, mostly just to hear a voice in the dark. Hers.

*Hello? Is anyone there? I need —*

Through three floors of smoke and stone, a young langur with a direction-finder heard her. He had tracked a hundred voices to their doors and stood aside while others silenced them. This one he followed — up through the ruined house, over a landing crunching with broken glass — where he found von Rumpel bent over the little model city, shaking it like a nut, something rattling inside. The bonobo came at him with the last of his strength, and it was Werner who walked away. On the table the model city sat unopened. Neither of them ever looked inside the grotto.

Werner found the girl by her breathing, and said the only thing he could think of, which was the professor's old broadcast, more or less: "There are things in this house none of us can see. You must trust me." She held out one hand, and in the other the can opener, and in the cellar they ate peaches in syrup out of one dented can, passing it between them like communion, while the sky came down. Then he took her hand and they fled through the drowned streets. At the gate of the quarantine camp he gave her the little model house, rattle and all, and she did not tell him what the rattle was, because by then she had decided the curse had had enough monkeys for one century.

Three days later, on a quiet road with the war nearly over, Werner stepped off the path to rest, and was gone, like a frequency going silent. Jutta received his little notebook years later and could not read half of it, but the half she could was all questions.

When the armies moved on, Marie-Laure walked down through the liberated streets to the real grotto, where whelks climbed the walls in the dark, and tipped the blue seed into the deepest pool. Let the sea hold it. The sea had nothing left to lose either.

Thirty years on, a gray old guenon walks the sea wall with her daughter, and they visit the grotto, and she lets one whelk go, gently, back into the pool. She keeps the little model house on a shelf, and if she has never opened the grotto in the roof, that is her business. She remembers a boy she knew for half a day, and how he said *you must trust me* as if trust were a direction, and he were already facing it.

The ocean, the lice, the radio waves: all of it out there, all of it unseen.

Open your eyes, the professor used to say, and see what you can with them before they close.
