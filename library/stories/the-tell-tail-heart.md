# The Tell-Tail Heart

*A parody of "The Tell-Tale Heart"*

TRUE! — nervous — very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad? The disease had sharpened my senses — not destroyed — not dulled them. Above all was the sense of hearing acute. I heard all things in the heaven and in the earth. I heard many things in hell. I could hear a fruit fly land. How, then, am I mad? Hearken! and observe how healthily — how calmly — I can peel this whole story.

Once the idea entered my brain, it haunted me day and night. Object there was none. Passion there was none. I loved the old monkey. He had never wronged me. For his bananas I had no desire. I think it was his tail! Yes, it was this! He had the tail of a demon — a dull, flat, thumping tail, like a metronome nobody had asked for. Whenever it slapped the hollow branch, my blood ran cold; and so by degrees I made up my mind to take the old monkey's perch, and thus rid myself of the tail forever.

Now this is the point. You fancy me mad. Madmen know nothing. But you should have seen me. You should have seen how wisely I proceeded — with what caution — with what foresight — with what dissimulation I went to work upon the fig! I was never kinder to the old monkey than during the whole week before the deed. I groomed him for hours, picking through his fur like a barber with a grudge. And every night, about midnight, I opened his door — oh, so gently — and thrust in my head, with a dark lantern shut close, so that not a single peel of light leaked out. And every morning I called boldly, "How is the old boy this fine dawn? Did he dream of bananas?"

"Figs," he said. "Mountains of figs. And somewhere, a drum." He frowned into his figs. "Rude of it."

And every morning I agreed that it was rude, and handed him his breakfast, and bided.

For seven nights I conducted the ritual. It took me an hour to swing the door the width of a fig-leaf; another hour to slide my whole head within; and then — oh, then — I would slip the shutter of the lantern a hair's width, and let fall upon his tail a single ray, thin as the whisker of a fruit fly. And for seven nights the tail lay innocent — furled, closed, silent as a peel in a pantry. It is impossible to say how first the idea entered my brain; it is equally impossible to say how, night after night, the tail refused to cooperate. I began to take it personally. Never before that week had I felt the extent of my own powers — of my sagacity. I could scarcely contain my feelings of triumph.

Upon the eighth night I was more than usually cautious. To think that there I was, opening the door, little by little, and he not even to dream of my secret thoughts! I fairly chuckled at the idea; and perhaps he heard me, for he moved upon his perch suddenly. You may think that I drew back — but no. His bed was in the deep hollow of the fig branch, black as a monkey's Monday morning, and I kept pushing the door, steadily, steadily.

I had my head in, and was about to flip open the light, when my thumb slipped upon the tin fastening, and the lantern clicked. The old monkey sprang up. "Who's there?" I kept quite still. For a whole hour I did not move a muscle; and in that hour I did not hear him lie down. He was sitting up in the dark, listening — as I had listened, night after night, to the drum in his dreams.

And have I not told you that what you mistake for madness is but over-acuteness of the senses? — now, I say, there came to my ears a low, dull, quick sound, such as a tail makes upon a hollow branch. It was the thumping of his tail. It grew quicker and louder every instant. The old monkey's hour had come! With a loud yell I threw open the lantern and leaped to the perch. He shrieked once — once only — and startled so badly that he tumbled backward into the deep knot-hole of the branch; and I, in the wild audacity of perfect triumph, sealed the hole with planks and gum before you could say "fidgety." The tail thumped on beneath the boards, muffled but, I thought, merry. Hearken! I said to myself. It laughs to scorn. I flattered myself it laughed to scorn.

At dawn there came three peacekeeper macaques; a neighbor had heard a shriek in the night. I smiled — for what had I to fear? I bade them welcome. The shriek, I said, was my own, in a dream. The old monkey, I said, had gone down to the river country to visit a cousin of indifferent health. I took them all over the tree. I showed them his banana cache, his figs, his spare comb. I brought them chilled coconut milk and a bowl of nuts, and they chatted of the harvest, and of a young macaque who had swung through a window for love of a lady, and I laughed at everything they said — loudly, warmly, at length. In the enthusiasm of my confidence, I brought chairs into the hollow and seated them; and I seated myself — calmly, hospitably — directly upon the very boards.

And then the sound came again — low, dull, quick. I talked more quickly, more vehemently; but the noise steadily increased. It thumped like a rumba. I descanted upon the weather; the thump answered in three-four time. The peacekeepers chatted pleasantly and smiled. Was it possible they heard not? Almighty jungle! — no! They heard! — they suspected! — they knew! — they were making a mockery of my horror! — anything was better than this agony! — anything was more tolerable than this derision! I felt that I must scream or die! and now — again! — hark! louder! louder! louder! "Villains!" I shrieked, "dissemble no more! I admit the deed! — tear up the planks! — here, here! — it is the thumping of his hideous tail!"

The peacekeepers tore up the boards; and there sat the old monkey, blinking, mid-banana, his tail still drumming the wood. "Boy," he said, in a voice of the profoundest weariness, "it was you. Eight nights at my door, and grooming me like a fool all week. I thought you were dying of politeness."

They exiled me to the bachelor troop on the far ridge, where I live to this day; and on quiet nights I hear it still — thump, thump, thump — and I know that somewhere, everywhere, there is always a tail. Every midsummer the old monkey sends a banana to the ridge, no note with it. I hear it before I peel it. Thump. Thump. Thump.
