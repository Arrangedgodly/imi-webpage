# Macaque's Cradle

*A parody of "Cat's Cradle" by Kurt Vonnegut*

Call me Bonobo. My parents did, or did anyway while I was a junior bonobo, and the name stuck.

When I was a younger monkey, I set out to write a book about the day the jungle ended. I meant the famous day — the one everybody pretends to remember — and to write it I went looking for the children of the great chimp who ended it: Felix Hoopenicker, Nobel laureate, the father of lice-nine.

Of lice-nine, this much is true. One afternoon, asked what a flea trainer had to worry about, old Hoopenicker said, idly, the way he said everything: "What would happen if some lice were simply more patient than other lice?" — and then went off to find out. Lice-nine was a crystal of pure patience. One mote of it, touching any louse, taught that louse to hold still; a louse that holds still teaches its neighbors; and stillness spreads from fur to fur, from troop to troop, until every jungle on earth is one solid, shining, motionless comb of lice.

The fur goes stiff as wicker. The grooming stops. And a monkey without grooming, as any monkey will tell you, is nothing whatsoever.

Hoopenicker played with the idea the afternoon he made it, put it in a little jar, and forgot it, and the world spent the rest of its life paying for that afternoon.

His children traded the jar away like a peel. Angela, the tall one with the recorder, traded hers for the admiration of a rich husband. Frank, the middle one, traded his for a minor governorship on the island of San Lemonzo — he had wanted, at nine, nothing in the world but a set of model fig-canoes, and at twenty-six he had simply scaled up. Newt — the old man's youngest, a pygmy marmoset the size of a fig — wanted nothing, which is why he saw the most.

The day I met him, Newt was weaving a vine between his paws, cradle-fashion, holding it up to the light.

"See the monkey?" he said. "See the cradle?"

I looked at the tangle of vine, and I looked at him.

"There isn't any monkey, and there isn't any cradle," said Newt, and dropped it.

A Baboononist, which I became without ever quite deciding to, would say Newt and I and Mona and all the rest of us were one karass — a team of monkeys put on the same errand by a god too shy to issue memos. A karass has no rank and no noticeboards. It is opposed, in the architecture of the universe, by the granfalloon — which is any troop that shares hats: nations, lodges, fan clubs, the League of Groomed Gentlemen. If you want to know whether your group is a granfalloon, Baboon-on taught: shout "One! Two! Three!" and see if everyone says "Four!" in time. Then run.

San Lemonzo was ruled by a dying tyrant called Papa Macanzano, who kept the island poor and grateful, and the most beautiful monkey on the island was Mona Aamons Macaque, whom Papa intended, in his last tired way, to make his child bride. The island's other ornament was Baboon-on, a gentle sage outlawed by his own decree, whose religion, Baboononism, taught that the truth was too sad for monkeys and that we should live by the foma — the harmless untruths — that make you brave and kind and well-groomed and happy.

The joke of San Lemonzo, which every baby sucked in with its mother's milk, was that Baboon-on and the tyrant who outlawed him had been conspirators from the start. Baboon-on had looked at his island of weavers too wretched to be good, and McBanza Macanzano had looked at the same weavers, and together they had invented a heresy worth being fed for: the hooks, the bounties, the martyrdoms — dynamic tension, Baboon-on called it. "A religion without a tyrant to hate," he wrote, "is a hammock without a tree."

I saw the holy man's works at the House of Hope and Mangoes, the free hospital in the jungle where Julian Roost — millionaire, wastrel, and lately the only saint I have ever groomed — went to die for other people instead of himself, and found he rather liked it. Over the gate hung the island's most famous painting: a monkey's paws hopelessly tangled in a vine, and beneath it, in letters a foot high, SEE THE MONKEY? SEE THE CRADLE? Inside, the dying lay in rows under the fig trees, laughing. A visitor asked Roost how he kept their spirits up. "Lies," said Roost brightly. "Tested ones."

Papa Macanzano died of the mango sickness, and — tyrants to the last — took the little jar with him, by way of a final tantrum. He had meant to announce a successor. He announced a corpse instead. Frank, seeing a career in it, offered me the presidency of San Lemonzo on the palace steps, and Mona as part of the furniture, and I — may the vine forgive me — accepted both.

Mona and I were married for one hour. She was gravely polite about the whole arrangement, the way a sunrise is polite. Ninety minutes later an airplane came down on the palace roof, and the jar broke, and the mote of patience slid out with the rainwater, down to the river, down to the sea.

You cannot half-freeze the sea with stillness. Ask the sea.

Within the hour, the river ran like combed glass. Within the day, every troop on earth sat where it was caught, mid-groom, a thousand million monkeys combing the mirror. The hurricanes stood offshore like exhibits of themselves. Six thousand years of grooming ended between one ripple and the next.

Mona touched the crystal on purpose, the way you or I might choose to be done with waiting, and went as still as her island, with her paws folded. Newt and Angela and Frank and I sat in the stone tower of Papa's castle, above the silent ocean, the last soft monkeys alive, and made the only arrangement left to us: we carried the still ones up the mountain and burned them, every body on San Lemonzo, before the tide could reach them and carry the patience back out to sea. Angela fed the fires. Frank supervised, pointlessly. It was the only government San Lemonzo ever had that worked.

*Busy, busy, busy,* is what we Baboononists say when we contemplate how much monkey business is afoot in the universe.

At the end of the first week Baboon-on himself came out of the jungle, thin as a whisker, carrying a note he had just written:

"If I were a younger monkey," it said, "I would write a history of monkey stupidity."

He asked me if the job was taken. I told him it was — I had been writing it all my life without noticing.

He looked at me a long time. Then he put his thumb to his nose, laid the thumb gently on the crystal, and became still — a statue of a sage thumbing his nose at the whole combed world, and I hope you will agree it was the last joke of the last comedian.

Call me Bonobo. I am still writing the book.
