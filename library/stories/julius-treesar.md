# Julius Treesar

*A parody of "Julius Caesar"*

Julius Treesar was the mightiest climber the Troop of Rome-vine had ever produced: he had swung the Rubicon-vines in a day and conquered the Fig Fields, the Nut Gallery, and the Prickle Bushes of Gaul. On the morning of the Feast of the Ripe Fig, he paraded through the canopy while the troop roared his name. The tribunes Flavius and Marullus watched the cheering crowd with deep misgivings. "Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home? O you hard hearts, you cruel monkeys of Rome-vine! You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things! Go home — and take those wreaths off the fig trees; he is no fig tree's friend."

Nobody listened to the tribunes. Nobody ever has.

And a small wild-eyed tarsier leapt onto a stump and shrieked: "BEWARE THE IDES OF MANGO!"

"What monkey is that?" frowned Treesar.

"A soothsayer, my lord," shrugged his friend Mark Ban-tony.

"Beware the ides of WHAT?" muttered Treesar. "I don't even like mango." And he marched on.

That night Calpurnia woke him, trembling. "I dreamed your statue spouted a hundred juices — coconut milk and mango milk — while the monkeys of Rome-vine bathed their paws in it!"

"You dreamt of smoothies, dear. It means I shall refresh the people."

"Cowards die many times before their deaths; the valiant never taste of death but once. Stay home tomorrow, and you shall taste no death at all — only figs."

He might have stayed, had the capuchin Cassius — a monkey with a lean and hungry look ("Such monkeys are dangerous," his aide whispered) — not been at work for weeks on Brutus, the noblest bonobo in the troop and Treesar's dearest friend.

"Brutus," Cassius murmured, "I was born free as Treesar; so were you. We both have the best grooming in the troop and the finest tails — and he gets the top branch? The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, but in ourselves, that we are underlings. Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world like a Colossus-monkey, and we petty monkeys walk under his huge legs. This morning I offered to groom him and he waved me off with a flick of his tail. A flick, Brutus."

"He is my friend," said Brutus heavily. "He is not dangerous... yet."

"Then think of him as a serpent's egg — kill him in the shell."

And when Brutus still wavered, Cassius had leaf-scrolls forged in several different pawwritings and thrown through Brutus's window at night: "Brutus, thou sleep'st; awake, and see thyself! Speak, strike, redress! BRUTUS, THOU sleep'st; AWAKE!" Brutus read them by moonlight, took a turn round the branch, read them again, and the pigeonholes of his conscience gave out. At home, his wife Portia found him awake at that hour and would not be dismissed: "Dear my lord, make me acquainted with your cause of grief. I grant I am a female bonobo, but I can keep a secret — I have proven my constancy, giving myself a voluntary scratch upon the thigh and bearing it patiently." "You are my true and honorable wife," said Brutus, and meant it, and still did not tell her.

On the morning of the ides of Mango, Calpurnia would not let him leave the hollow — until the capuchin Decius arrived to fetch him, heard the dream, and smoothly took it apart. "This dream is all amiss interpreted! Your statue spouting juices in a hundred streams? It signifies that from you GREAT ROME-VINE shall suck reviving sap — you shall refresh the troop with smoothies! This by Calpurnia's dream is signified."

"Smoothies for the people," said Treesar, his tail curling with pride. "Why, that IS a good omen. Fetch my wreath."

In the marketplace, a schoolmaster-lemur named Artemidorus pressed a leaf-scroll into every passing paw, crying "Treesar! Read this scroll!" — inside was written the entire conspiracy, every name. Treesar waved it away grandly. "What touches us ourself shall be last served." Which turned out to be extremely literal.

On the Council Branch, the conspirators gathered with folded paws and false petitions. Metellus Cimber bowed low and made a long, crawling speech about repealing the banishment of his brother. Treesar interrupted him with the frostiest speech of his career: "I could be well moved if I were as you; but I am constant as the fig-star — the northernmost fruit in the sky, of whose true-fixed and resting quality there is no fellow in the firmament. Know, Cimber, thy soft petition shall not brush me."

"The ides of Mango are come, sir," said the soothsayer-tarsier, from the back.

"Ay, Treesar," said Cassius, softly, "but not gone."

Casca struck first — "Speak, hands, for me!" — and the daggers came out like fig-knives at harvest. Treesar fought like the great monkey he was, roaring, swinging three conspirators off the branch by their tails — until he saw the last blade, and the paw that held it.

"Et tu, Brute-chimp?" he whispered. "Then fall, Treesar."

And he fell at the base of the Council Branch — thirty-three cuts and one broken heart — while the conspirators marched off crying "Liberty! Tyranny is overthrown!" and Cassius, planning ahead, suggested an oath in blood. "Stoop, then, and wash your paws in it," said Brutus, refusing. "Let us be sacrificers, but not butchers. And no oath — an honest monkey needs none."

At the funeral, Brutus spoke first — "Not that I loved Treesar less, but that I loved the canopy more! Who is here so base that would be a bond-monkey?" — and the crowd cheered and voted him figs. Then Mark Ban-tony climbed up with Treesar's torn cloak and gave the famous speech.

"Friends, monkeys, troop-mates, lend me your ears. I come to bury Treesar, not to praise him. The mischief that monkeys do lives after them; the good is oft interred with their peels. The noble Brutus hath told you Treesar was ambitious — but Brutus is an honourable monkey. When that the poor have cried, Treesar hath wept. Ambition should be made of sterner stuff. You all did see that at the feast I thrice presented him a kingly crown of woven banana leaves, which he did thrice refuse. Was that ambition? Yet Brutus says he was ambitious, and Brutus is an honourable monkey. I am no orator, as Brutus is — I am a plain, blunt monkey that loved my friend. But were I Brutus, and Brutus Ban-tony, there were a Ban-tony would ruffle up your spirits! My heart is in the coffin there with Treesar, and I must pause till it come back to me."

"You are not wood, you are not stones, but MONKEYS!" cried Ban-tony, producing the will. "Hear the will of Treesar: TO EVERY TROOP-MATE OF ROME-VINE, SEVENTY-FIVE BANANAS FROM HIS PRIVATE STASH!"

The canopy exploded. "We'll burn the house of Brutus!" They raced off to burn things and pellet Cinna the poet with overripe fruit — "I am Cinna the POET!" he cried. "TEAR HIM FOR HIS BAD VERSES ANYWAY!" — such is the wisdom of crowds, and of critics.

Brutus and Cassius fled and raised an army, and the night before Philippi-vines they quarreled in the tent over fig-supplies and honesty — "You have condemned and noted Lucius Pella for taking bribes — I am a monkey that can raise no money by vile means!" — "A friendly eye could never see such faults!" — "A flatterer's would not, though they appeared as huge as the Fig Fields!" — and then, because it was midnight and they were frightened, they made it up over a bowl of figs. "There is a tide in the affairs of monkeys," said Brutus, "which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune. We'll take the flood now."

"A word, Brutus," said Cassius, gently. "Portia is dead. She took ill suddenly and — she is dead."

"Why, farewell, Portia. We must all die, sometime." And the two of them sat very still for a while, being Romans about it, and being bonobo and capuchin about it too.

Through the tent that night drifted Treesar's ghost. "Thy evil spirit, Brutus," it said. "Thou shalt see me at Philippi-vines." Brutus, to his enormous credit, said only, "Why, then, I shall see thee again," and asked it to stay for a fig, which ghosts cannot do.

The next day the battle went badly, as battles do. Cassius, believing all lost on his birthday of all days — "This is my birth-day; as this very day was Cassius born" — fell upon the very fig-knife that had pierced Treesar: "Treesar, thou art revenged, even with the knife that killed thee." And Brutus, seeing the day lost, ran upon his own blade, gasping: "Treesar, now be still. I killed not thee with half so good a will."

Over the fallen bonobo, Ban-tony spoke: "This was the noblest monkey of them all. His life was gentle, and the elements so mixed in him that Nature might stand up and say to all the world, 'THIS WAS A MONKEY!'" He paused. "Cry 'Havoc!' and let slip the monkeys of war — no, wait, we've done that bit. Go home. And the next time a tarsier tells you to beware the ides of anything — BEWARE."

To this day, every ides of Mango, the troop climbs the tallest branch, eats seventy-five bananas in Treesar's memory, reads no leaf-scrolls marked "urgent," and tries very hard not to stab anybody's prime mate in the shell.
