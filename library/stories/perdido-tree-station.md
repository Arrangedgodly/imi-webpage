# Perdido Tree Station

*A parody of "Perdido Street Station"*

Isaac dan der Grimnebanana was a fat, brilliant chimp of dangerous curiosity, and when the hooded figure sloshed into his lab high in the boughs of New Crobazoo, Isaac should have shown him the vine.

"I am told," the visitor rasped, "that you are the monkey who studies everything and finishes nothing. I am Yagharek. I was a flying colugo. Now I am a falling one."

Isaac saw it then: the gliding membrane between the visitor's limbs, cut away in clean punitive ribbons. Flightless by sentence. A crime with a windfall of gold attached.

"Make me fly again," Yagharek said, "and it is yours."

"Sir," said Isaac, going a little bananas the way he always did at the edge of the impossible, "flight is just falling with *enthusiasm*. I accept."

Isaac's obsession was crisis energy — the monstrous voltage packed into the instant of a change of state, the lightning hiding in the moment a banana goes from green to ripe. To study flight he collected anything that flew, and through a fixer named Lemuel, who knew every crooked branch in the city, he bought a fat, dozing caterpillar the color of an eclipse.

"Where did it come from?" Isaac asked.

"A dealer," said Lemuel, counting gold. "Don't ask which. He had five of them. You could only afford one."

It pupated. It hatched. It was a slake-moth.

The thing that came out of that chrysalis had wings like hypnotic stains, and it gazed at Isaac with the patience of a nightmare, and that night it got loose. Slake-moths do not eat fruit. They drink dreams. They unspool a monkey's mind through the proboscis and leave the body walking around ripe and blank, staring at bananas it can no longer imagine wanting.

And then it got worse, because the dealer's other four larvae had been sold as exotic pets to Mr. Motley, the crime lord of the Rib, a mandrill whose face was a riot of mismatched colors, every patch a different rumor of a different monkey he had been. Four chrysalises hatched in his collector's parlor. Four moths flew out his skylights. Within a month New Crobazoo had a plague of the sweetly vacant, and the Mandrill Parliament posted rewards, and Isaac — whose fault it entirely was — kept suspiciously quiet.

Quiet, but busy. His lover Lin, a spider-monkey sculptor of astonishing woven friezes, had taken a commission from Motley himself: a vast effigy of the crime lord, to be woven around one of his own beams. When the moth panic made dream-art scandalous, Motley decided Lin's sculpture was mockery, and held her hostage in his colored parlor. That settled Isaac's arithmetic. He had hatched the moths. He would unhatch them.

Recruiting help meant descending to the Scraphole Parliament — the council of Constructs, half-beast half-machine things stitched from salvage, who chattered in the junk-falls beneath the city and knew every wind current by smell. Their Speaker was an engine with a tapir's snout.

"The slaked monkeys bloom where the moths feed," the Speaker snorted, tapping a map of dots. "Your pests are territorial. Four hunt the Ribs and the Rookeries. One — yours, Professor, congratulations on your purchase — returns nightly to the same belfry like a connoisseur. Also, we Constructs would help push them off a branch for you, but many of us cannot climb, as we are sofas."

"You're hired," said Isaac.

He recruited the Weaver — a vast peacock spider that moved through the city's shadows like an editor through a manuscript, speaking in florid verse and coveting, above all things, scissors and drama.

"Aaah, ripening chaos, fruit of the bough," the Weaver sang. "You offer my eight eyes a *theatre*. Four moths, and bait of quality. I desire the last pair of scissors in your laboratory, and the good china, and the *ending* to be mine."

The bait was Isaac's own invention: dream-hooch, a liqueur of pressed nightmares fermented from the terror-sweat of the moths' own victims. The Weaver took a barrel of it to the Old Grind, a derelict milltower, and staged what it called *an evening in four acts*. Four times that night the dream-hooch bloomed in the dark like a lie told beautifully; four times a moth came lurching in on stained wings, drinking deep; and four times the Weaver dropped from the rafters and did something swift, professional, and absolutely final with its pedipalps. By dawn New Crobazoo had one slake-moth left, and the Weaver sat among the wreckage wearing Isaac's good scissors on a thread of silk like a medal.

"Your city is duller now," it said happily. "You're welcome."

The militia, meanwhile, was hunting a phantom — a conspiracy, a cult, anything but the truth — and Isaac let it. The last moth was the first: his moth, the connoisseur, and it would not leave the heart of the city. So Isaac went to the heart.

At Perdido Tree Station — the vast tangled interchange where every liane, vine-line, and rope-rail of New Crobazoo knotted together above the poisoned river — Isaac rigged his crisis engine and baited the air with the last cask of dream-hooch. When the moth came, lurching toward the vintage, Isaac threw the switch. The crisis engine bloomed, a field of pure ripening, and in the space between green and gold the moth hung stalled, wings wide, dying of the change it could not stop — and the Weaver, dropping by on the off-chance of an encore, did the final thing, professionally, with style.

That same night, while the militia took credit for the plague's end, the Weaver paid a social call on the Rib, for reasons it described as "aesthetic." Motley's parlor was found the next morning redecorated from wall to wall in knotted silk, door to rafters, with a gap cut precisely where a hostage had been. Lin walked home on her own feet. Her hands, Motley's enforcers had broken, so that she would never weave a mockery again. She held them cupped in front of her like two sleeping birds.

The city never knew. Isaac, disgraced but alive, climbed to the Station's crown at dawn, where Yagharek waited, hooded, hopeful. And Isaac, before handing over anything, finally asked the question he should have asked on day one.

"What did you do, Yagharek? For the sentence."

"Among my troop," Yagharek said slowly, "we have one law above all: every monkey owns their own choice, as the sky owns the wind. I took a choice from another. I stole it. The gravest theft my people know, and the only sentence that answers it: they took from me the choosing of the air."

"They cut your wings," said Isaac, "for taking another monkey's *yes*."

"For taking their no," said Yagharek. "It matters which."

Isaac sat down heavily on the crown of the Station. The gold was blood money; the science was done; and there was not going to be a machine that could hand a thief back the sky. "I can't make you what you were," he said at last. "That skin is gone; the sentence took it. And I won't finish your commission, Yagharek. Not for that crime. But Lin wove you something, before her hands were hurt — web and feather-wool, a glide-cape. It's hers to give, not mine, and she gives it. It isn't flight. It's falling with enthusiasm, and it's all the sky any of us is owed."

Yagharek took the cape in his ruined hands for a long time. "In my troop, we do not forgive," he said. "But we do recognize *style*."

He stepped off the crown of Perdido Tree Station into the open air, and for nine glorious seconds, the whole city saw a falling monkey who had talked gravity into a compromise.

Isaac went home to Lin and her splinted hands, and began, slowly, to learn to weave beside her, his clumsy chimp paws fouling every thread until they didn't. It was not, he would say later, crisis energy. It was just the power in the instant a monkey goes from guilty to trying. The strongest voltage there is, and the only one that ripens you instead of the fruit.
