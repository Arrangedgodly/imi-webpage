# A Clockwork Orangutan

*A parody of "A Clockwork Orange"*

"What's it going to be then, eh?"

There was me, that is Alex, and my three droogs, that is Pete, Georgie, and Dim — Dim being really dim — and we sat in the Korova Banana Bar on a malenky evening, making up our rassoodocks what to do with the night. The bar served milk-plus: milk with vellocet or synthemesc or drencrom in it, plus, in our fair jungle, a great dollop of mashed banana, which is horrorshow for sharpening a young orangutan up for a bit of the old ultra-climbing.

This is the story of a naughty young ape, and you must not imitate him, oh my brothers. It is a caution, like the old gorilla said: a monkey chooses. An orangutan with clockwork in him merely winds down.

We went swinging through the municipal jungle park in our headdresses, flat-furred and flat-toed and full of terrible music, and we found an old gibbon coming home from the public library with three books under his arm.

"Go on then, brother," I said kindly, taking the books. "'The Rhinoceros in the Jungle.' Wretched. 'The Miraculous Monkey of Buenos Aires.' Trash. And what is this one? 'A Clockwork Orange'? Now what could this be?"

I ripped — I shredded — pages and pages of it, snowing them over his head, while he went "What, what, what?" like a burping mole, and Dim held him by the ankles and shook the fruit out of his pockets. Then we let him have it, the old veck, poor thing, and off we itted for more fun. That was the night, my brothers.

We finished it at a house called HOME, all windows and lonely, where a lone she-chimp wrote books with a room full of cats. We tolchocked our way in to the sounds of her screaming for the millicents, and there on her desk was a manuscript, and the title of it — I swear, brothers — was *A Clockwork Orange*. "It's a book about the terrible fruit of our times," she wept, "about how young apes are made into machines!" And I laughed and laughed, because it seemed very funny to me then, and there was a great bust-up, and the cats went everywhere, and Dim cracked a milk bottle across my fuzzy orange gulliver while the sirens came singing.

That was the betrayal, oh my brothers. My own droog Dim, spilling me into the paws of the millicents, and all three of themhowling with laughter, before the millicents stuffed me into a zoo van full of hooligans. I was sentenced to fourteen stretches for the old ultra-violence, and the droogs I had led went free as fleas.

In Staja Number 84 I was model prisoner 6655321, growing bananas for the governor and reading the Bible in the chapel for the nice old chaplain — reading it chiefly, I confess, for the smitings. "You are becoming a machine, Alex," the chaplain warned me, "a good machine, but a machine. And mark this: goodness is chosen from within, son. When a monkey cannot choose, he ceases to be a monkey." I said yes yes yes, and I would have said anything, because the Minister of the Interior was coming to choose a prisoner for the Ludovico Technique, and I wanted out of my stretch more than I wanted my own tail.

"This monkey," the Minister announced before the flashing bulbs, a big silver-backed politician clapping me on the shoulder like a coconut ready, "will be the first subject of the Ludovico Technique. Before we are done he will be utterly good. He will be kind to old gibbons. He will respect books. He will not so much as steal a windfall mango. And he will do it all without choice, without will, without freedom — a true clockwork orangutan, wound up and pointed at goodness. And think of the prison bananas we shall save!"

They strapped me to a chair with wire. They clipped my eyelids open. They made me viddy film after film of ultra-violence — monkeys going ape on monkeys, troops gone bad, whole jungles on fire — while a tube fed me and a needle made me sick, and over it all played the great drums of Ludwig van, the Fifth, the Ninth, boom boom boom, gorgeous and terrible. After two weeks of it, brothers, I could not hear the Fifth without wanting to die. That was the filthy unfair part. They took Beethoven along with the badness, the way a monkey pulling one vine brings down the whole curtain of leaves.

Then the demonstration, before the great and the good. A rude, snarling chimp was set upon me, and I could not raise one finger to defend myself — I could only feel the sickness rising. Then they held out a windfall banana, ripe and yellow and sweet, and I retched at the very sight. That was the flaw in the technique, brothers: it could not tell the difference between choosing to be bad and choosing a banana.

"Am I just like some animal, then?" I cried. "Am I a doggie? A doggie trained to leap at goodness? Don't I get a choice? I want a choice!"

And the newspapers all clapped and said: OUR ORANGUTAN IS CURED.

I was not cured. I was a little machine wound up by politicians. They let me out of Staja with a ticket and a small parcel, and I walked home singing, and found my dear old mother and father sitting at supper with a lodger — a young chimp named Joe, sitting in my chair at my table, being to them the son I had never been. "You've made your bed," said my Pee, weeping, "and now you must lie in it, but the bed's let to Joe." I could not even fight Joe for the chair, brothers, because the sickness rose in me at the first clench of my fist. Choiceless, I wandered out into the rain.

The world I had beaten came back for me one at a time. The old gibbons at the library found me and would have had their revenge, and the sickness dropped me, and it was the millicents who saved me — and the millicents were Dim and Pete, in uniform now, uniforms and hats and clubs, and they drove me to the river and worked me over like in the old days. "This is for the she-champ at HOME, little Alex," they said. "The jungle doesn't forget." I crawled off and put my head in the river, I don't know how long.

It was the old gibbon's friends who found me, beaten and sick by the riverside, and carried me to a house full of books and banana trees growing up through the floor. The gibbon was F. Alexander, the writer, and he did not know my face — only my voice was famous to him out of his worst night — and he wept for me and called me a victim, "a poor victim of the modern jungle," and fed me broth. "It was a monkey of your sort," he said, wringing his paws, "who ruined my HOME and my life's work — the very book I was writing. But you, poor lamb, are proof of what they do in the prisons. You will help us shame the Minister, yes?"

But when he and his friends played the Ninth — boom boom boom — to soothe me, the conditioning roared up my throat, and I could not bear it, and I heard them outside the door whispering that a martyr would serve the cause better than a witness. So I climbed to the window and leapt out, brothers, to escape the glorious Ludwig van. So much for the modern jungle.

I did not die. I woke in a white zoo-hospital with every bone bandaged, and the Minister himself at my bedside, smiling for the cameras and feeding me grapes. "The technique has been reversed," he said. "A defect in the old treatment. You are free to choose again, my boy, entirely free — and I hope you will choose to be photographed with me at the election." And I felt it true, brothers: the sickness was gone; the banana tasted like a banana; the choice was mine again.

I sat by the river that evening, my fur going a deal grayer, watching my own son swing off into the trees with his own little droogs, shouting for ultra-this and ultra-that. Youth is like that, brothers. He thinks the choice is a cage. He will learn, as I learned, that a monkey who cannot choose is not good but only wound, and that goodness freely chosen is the only kind worth the word.

Youth must go, ah yes. But youth is only being in a hurry, and I was in a hurry no longer.

I was cured, all right.
