# The Grooming of the Troop

*A parody of "The Taming of the Shrew"*

First, the frame. A tinker-monkey named Christopher Sly lay drunk as a felled log outside an ale-fig house, until a passing lord and his hunting party found him. "O monstrous beast, how like a swine he lies!" cried the lord — and, struck by inspiration: "Bear him gently to my nest. When he wakes, surround him with servants and sweet music. Tell him he has been mad these fifteen years and dreamed he was a tinker."

Sly woke to soft boughs and attendants. "For pity's sake, a pot of small ale!"

"Would your lordship prefer a dish of early figs?"

"I am Christophero Sly. Call me not 'lordship.'"

"Alas, sir — that has been your madness these fifteen years."

Sly scratched himself, always a sign of deep thought. "Am I not Christopher Sly? Then I must be a lord, surely. Do I not cling? Do I not grunt in my sleep like a lord? Fetch me my wife, then."

"Sir," said a small page in a veil, trembling, "your wife has vowed not to approach your bed, for fear of relapse."

"A wise wife," said Sly, flattered. "Well then — fetch me a play, to pass the time between the small ale and my wife."

They brought him the following comedy.

In the fig-forests of Padua, old Baptista had two daughters, and everyone knew the arithmetic. Bianca, the younger, had a line of suitors stretching three branches. Katherina, the elder, had a reputation stretching three territories.

Katherina was the fiercest macaque in the province. She could out-argue a howler, out-arm-wrestle a drill, and once chased a suitor up a kapok tree and left him there a week with nothing but his regrets. Nobody would groom her. Nobody dared.

"Gentlemen," Baptista told Bianca's suitors — Gremio, a rich old monkey with more figs than teeth, and Hortensio, a sporty type — "I have made a rule: Bianca stays un-groomed and un-wed until her elder sister is mated first."

"A hard bargain," said Gremio. "It's like hanging a rotten fig before a bird and saying 'eat the hawk first.'"

Just then young Lucentio arrived from Pisa with his servant Tranio, saw Bianca, and instantly went ape. "Tranio, I burn! I freeze! I have fallen into a fig-press of love!"

"Then, sir, be bold," said Tranio. "Disguise yourself as a schoolmaster. Tutors are allowed near her."

So Lucentio became "Cambio," a Latin tutor, while Hortensio became "Litio," a music tutor. Bianca, no fool, chose Cambio and let Litio believe otherwise — "I must not always study lutes and figs; preferment goes by blood," she said sweetly, dismissing them both and sending a secret note to Cambio, because Bianca was the shrewdest monkey in Padua and let nobody print it on a poster.

Meanwhile Petruchio of Verona arrived — a young gorilla with a loud laugh and a large net worth — having heard exactly one fact about Katherina: she was rich.

"I come to wive it wealthily in Padua," he announced. "If wealthily, then happily in Padua! I am as peremptory as she is proud-minded, and where two raging fires meet, they consume the thing that feeds their fury."

He presented himself at Baptista's grove in his loudest stripes. "Good morrow, Kate — for that's your name, I hear."

"Well met and well come, if you've brought ear-plugs."

"And well found. I am told you giggle and leap about."

"Called me giggle?" Kate's tail lashed like a whip. "I'll giggle your ears off, coxcomb! Away, you three-inch fool!"

"O, slowly, slowly — sing sweeter, nightingale. Sweet Kate, I court you not with gifts but with compliments."

*Not with gifts, at least,* thought everyone else in the grove.

They fought splendidly for an hour, and Petruchio emerged beaming. "We have bargained well! She loves me beyond reason — told me so herself. Sign the contract, Baptista; I'm in haste for the wedding figs."

The wedding became a legend. Petruchio arrived late, wearing a cracked coconut-shell hat, old fig-sacks for clothes, and a rotten vine for a sword, behaving like a baboon escaped from a zoo — which, as Kate noted with a snarl, was *her* line. He swore at the priest, kissed the bride so loudly that the hornbills left the county, and carried her off before the figs were served, over her vigorous objection and one broken branch.

Then the taming began. No bananas arrived: "You will not groom before you eat? Then you fast. It is all for your love, Kate." No sleep: "This sleeping branch is too soft; I'll find you a firmer one." The new cap and gown he tore to shreds: "Silk! You'd look like a parrot." A tailor brought a gorgeous gown; Petruchio found imaginary faults with every seam until the tailor fled, and Kate, who had wanted that gown more than breath, learned that wanting things in front of Petruchio was how things were taken away. And so on, until Katherina — who had fought everything her whole life — found that fighting Petruchio's madness was even more exhausting than being furious.

On the road back to Baptista's, they met an old gentleman of Verona. "Good morrow, gentle mistress — where away, sirrah?" said Petruchio warmly, to the elderly bearded monkey.

"Young and lovely, is she not?" said Petruchio.

"Old and bearded, say you?" said Kate. "A husband's eyes see farther than the trees."

"Even so. And look — the moon shines now."

"The sun. It is the sun."

"It is the moon, or no visiting your father's grove. It shall be moon or star before I journey."

"Then be it moon," said Kate slowly, watching him, "or sun, or anything you please, and let us be on our way. The fig you call a moon is ripe either way."

"...Why, there's a wench! Come on, and kiss me, Kate."

Back in Padua, Lucentio had eloped with Bianca, and the three husbands made the classic wager: each sent for his wife; the bananas went to whichever came. Bianca sent word she was busy. The widow sent word she was busier.

Katherina dropped everything and came — and dragged the other two wives along by the scruff.

"Where did you learn such troop-spirit?" gasped Hortensio.

"From my mate," said Kate, and delivered the famous speech. "Thy husband is thy branch, thy life, thy keeper, thy back-scratcher in storm and in drought. Such duty as the subject owes the troop, even such a woman oweth to her mate — and why not grooms? Our arms are soft, and our climbing-fit is set for comfort, not for combat. But you great brutes have strength to climb the tallest figs, and we have strength to hold on. I am ashamed that monkeys are so simple, offering war where they should kneel for grooming peace."

"Say that again, slowly, to Bianca," said Petruchio, delighted.

"Come, kiss me, Kate. We have won the wager, and I'll be sworn the troop was never merrier."

And nobody in Padua — least of all Katherina — was ever entirely sure who had tamed whom.

Then the curtains shook, and the lord's men announced that the lord had a fever and must go to bed at once. They carried Christopher Sly back to the ale-house bench, where he woke at dawn owing for two broken pots.

"Gor," he told the hostess, rubbing his eyes, "I have had the finest dream. I was a lord, and there was a play — and now I know the secret of taming a shrew."

"Do you, then?" said his own wife, arms folded in the doorway, tapping a fig-branch.

"...The secret," said Sly, "is that nobody is ever entirely sure who tamed whom."

"That one," said his wife, "you may have learned from a lord."
