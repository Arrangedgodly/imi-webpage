# There's a Marmoset in My Closet!

*A parody of "There's a Wocket in My Pocket!"*

When the moon came up over our banyan treehouse, Mama would tuck me into my leaf hammock and say, "Goodnight, little one. Sleep tight. Don't let the bedbugs bite."

I never told her that the bedbugs were the least of it.

"Did you ever have the feeling," I asked her one night, "that there's a marmoset in your closet?"

Mama sighed the sigh of a mother who has answered this before. "There is a marmoset in your closet. He's been there since Tuesday."

She was right. There was a marmoset in my closet, and he is very nice. He is small and gold, and when he yawns it is like a tiny pink sunrise. Sometimes at night I hear him humming in there. It is a very small hum for a very small monkey.

But he is only the beginning, because if you look around our treehouse — and I have looked EVERYWHERE — you will find that it is full of creatures I cannot see in the daytime, and some of them are friendly, and some of them are NOT.

There's a bonobo in my cocoa. He likes the little marshmallows, and he never takes the last sip, which I think is polite.

There's a howler on my towel. Every morning at dawn he howls. I would complain, but he is mostly on key.

There's a baboon in the kitchen, sitting up with a spoon. It is the family spoon. He believes it is HIS spoon. We let him believe it, because have you SEEN his teeth?

There's a gibbon in the ribbon drawer. There's a mandrill on the windowsill — he sits so still, that mandrill on the sill. There's a gorilla in my villa, although between you and me, the gorilla had the villa first, so really I am the child in HIS gorilla.

There's a capuchin in my canteen, a macaque up in the attic (she causes all the static), and a chimp upon my blimp (the blimp is made of leaves and wishful thinking, but it floats anyway, mostly).

There's an orangutan in the fan. The fan is made of one large leaf and one tired orangutan, and when he waves it, everyone in the room gets exactly one breeze and a smell of oranges.

There's a uakari in our library. His face is bright red, which is embarrassing for everyone, because the library is where we keep our quiet, and every time he blushes, the books rattle.

There's a bushbaby in the gravy, which we have learned to announce before dinner guests arrive. There's a sifaka in my speaker-aka, and she only plays music she can dance sideways to, which is all of it. There's a colobus on the colonnade — he is very formal, that colobus, and if you do not greet the colonnade properly, he will not let you across.

And there's a proboscis who knows-is exactly where my nose-is, which is a strange comfort at three in the morning.

Some of them are friendly. Some are not-so-friendly. And some are neither — like the boring old loris in my storage. He talks and talks and talks about his collection of dusk. He has dusk from every evening since the rainy season. He bores us. He does not care that he bores us. I think that is his favorite part.

One night I decided to make a census. I took a candle — a firefly in a jar, technically — and went looking, room by room, and the treehouse being a tree, the rooms are branches, and the census took a while.

In the kitchen, the baboon was sitting exactly where the baboon always sits.

"Good evening," I said. "Are you enjoying the spoon?"

The baboon looked at me for a long, long moment. Then he looked at the spoon. Then he looked at me again.

"Yes," he said finally, and put the spoon in his cheek, where I gather he keeps the important things.

In the dining branch, the bonobo had his feet in my cocoa, which I had thought was MY cocoa, and which I now understood to be a timeshare.

"Do you ever sleep?" I asked him.

"I'm nocturnal by arrangement," he said. "Nobody else wanted the night shift, and the marshmallows needed supervision." And he passed me the cup, which I think is polite, and which I did not drink, which I think he knew.

By the cold shelf, where the figs keep, nothing lived. I noted this down: "The cold shelf: empty." Later I learned I was wrong. The cold shelf was full of the macaque's static. It just doesn't show up on a census.

But of ALL the creatures in the walls and the halls and the drawers and the floors, there is only ONE I did not like at all. Not one bit. Not one call.

Late at night, when the moon slides behind the leaves, a big hairy shadow-shape sits on the far dark wall of my room. It does not hum like the marmoset. It does not howl like the towel-howler. It breathes. Slowly. Great big rumbly breaths. And every night I pull my leaf blanket up to my eyeballs and whisper, "Mama? It's there again. The shadow. The big one. On the wall."

And every night, Mama gets up, crosses the room, and turns on the lamp.

And every night, the big hairy shadow-shape turns out to be Grandpa Gorilla, asleep sitting up, snoring like distant thunder, holding a bedtime story he came up to read me and forgot he was holding. Sometimes he is still holding a banana he came up to eat. The banana is always, always peeled. We do not know when he peels it. We do not ask.

"Oh," I say, every night. "It's only Grandpa."

"It is always only Grandpa," says Mama. "One day, my love, you will believe me BEFORE the lamp."

Well. Maybe. But one night last week the lamp wasn't even necessary, because something ELSE happened, something new, and I want it on the record that I was very brave about it, mostly.

I woke up and there were two enormous eyes glowing in the dark above my hammock. Not breathing like Grandpa. Not humming like the marmoset. Just — eyes. The size of dinner plates. Glowing. Yellow.

"MAMA," I said, in a whisper that broke every branch on the tree.

Mama came with the lamp, and the eyes turned out to be the tarsier, who had come down from the attic to visit the macaque, and whose head is honestly two eyes with a monkey attached. He is even weirder than the chimp on the blimp, which takes doing.

"Sorry," said the tarsier, politely. "I can't turn them off."

"It's quite all right," said Mama, in the voice she keeps for exactly this.

"I'll look at the floor," said the tarsier. And he did, all night, and in the morning the floor reported that it had never felt so seen.

Last night, the marmoset peeked out of the closet at bedtime, and I peeked back, and we regarded each other, one little monkey to another, in the moonlight. And I thought to myself:

There are SO many creatures in this treehouse that I cannot see, up in the rafters and down in the root, swinging in the curtains and dozing on the sill. And some of them are friendly, and some of them are grumpy, and one of them is a Grandpa, and one of them is mostly eyeballs.

But the one I like the best, of all the primates in my hall —

is the small gold one who hums in my closet.

Because the marmoset is a little monkey.

And he likes ME.

Goodnight, bonobo. Goodnight, howler. Goodnight, baboon — put down the spoon.

Goodnight, marmoset.

See you on Tuesday.
