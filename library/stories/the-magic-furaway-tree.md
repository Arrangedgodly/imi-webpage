# The Magic Furaway Tree

*A parody of "The Magic Faraway Tree"*

When Jo, Bessie, and Fig moved to their new jungle cottage at the edge of the Whispering Wood, the first thing they noticed was that the wood whispered. It went hush-hush all day, and if you listened hard, it sounded like come-up, come-up.

"It is not the wind," said Jo, who was the oldest and therefore an authority. "Everybody knows there's a Magic Furaway Tree — taller than thinking, with bark as soft as fur. And at the top, above the clouds, there are Lands. Different ones, all the time."

Next morning they went in, armed with bread, a flask of milk, and Fig. The hush-hush grew louder, then stopped altogether, and there it was: a tree taller than thinking, its bark soft as a baby monkey's shoulder. When Fig hugged it — and Fig hugged everything — it hugged back, half a heartbeat later.

They began to climb.

At the first fork, a small furious arm shot out of a hole and nipped Jo's ankle.

"PASS BY!" shrieked the Angry Marmoset. "CAN'T A MARMOSET EVEN SULK IN PEACE?"

They passed by. A little higher: "WARE THE WATER!" — SWISH-SPLASH! — a great wave of soapy flea-bath roared down the trunk, and they flattened against the bark while Dame Groomalot, three branches up, wrung out her washing and grumbled that nobody ever warned her.

"You never warn us either!" Bessie called, wringing her tail.

At the next branch they met a most peculiar monkey hung all over with kettles, which clanked when he moved and boomed when he spoke, for he was a little deaf.

"EH?" he boomed pleasantly. "COME FOR A KETTLE, HAVE YOU? GOOD LADS! TAKE TWO!"

"He's the Saucepan Macaque," said a soft voice from a big hole in the trunk. "He's been giving away kettles since before your mother was born. Nobody has ever successfully refused a kettle."

That was Silky — a silky sifaka, white-gold all over, with a little kitchen in the hollow of the tree, just taking a tray of Pop Bananas out of the oven.

"Careful," she said. "They go off."

They went off. The Pop Bananas popped the moment they were bitten — pop-pop-pop, all down your teeth, tasting of warm toffee and banana. And there were Toffee 'Nana Shocks, which grow bigger and bigger in your mouth until your eyes start out, so never bite them, only let them melt. Fig bit one. It was a full minute before anyone could hear him scream with enjoyment.

"Come and see Moon-Face," said Silky. "The Land's up today."

Moon-Face was an owl monkey with a face exactly like a full moon, living on the topmost branch beside the strangest thing of all: a hole in the middle of the tree, where a great spiral slide — the Slippery-Slip — wound from the top clean down to the roots.

"That's the way down," said Moon-Face, beaming. "We ride it on leaves. And that" — he pointed to the ladder of cloud climbing out of the treetop into a hole in the sky — "is the way up. Whatever Land is visiting sits on top of the cloud. But come down in time — the Lands slide away when they're done, and if you're on one when it goes—"

He didn't finish. Nobody needed him to.

The Land that day was the Land of Pick-What-You-Pick, and it was every monkey's dream let loose: booths and barrows and whole shops, everything free — figs, whistles, fur-ribbons, fizzy soursop, pineapple-shaped hats — and you simply picked what you wanted, and the shopkeepers thanked you.

They were all happily filling baskets — Fig especially, shoulder-deep in a barrel of toffees at the sweet-shop — when a great bell rang across the Land.

"LANDED LONG ENOUGH!" boomed the bell. "MOVING ON!"

The whole Land shivered. It began, very gently, to slide.

"FIG!" yelled Jo, and ran for the sweet-shop. He hauled his little brother from the barrel, toffees raining, Bessie grabbing them both by the scruff, and they ran across a country sliding off the edge of the world like a pulled tablecloth. They reached the cloud-hole just as it stretched — stretched — and leapt, all three together, and were caught by Silky and Moon-Face, who poured them straight onto fresh fig-leaves at the head of the Slippery-Slip.

WHEE! Down and around and along — forty turns, leaves hissing, kettles applauding as they passed the Saucepan Macaque — out the trapdoor at the bottom, plop, plop, plop, into the soft moss.

"WARE THE WATER!" warned Dame Groomalot, too late.

They came back the next day, because that is the whole trouble with the Furaway Tree: one Land is never enough. And this time, feeling bold, Jo called up the first hole on the way.

"Angry Marmoset! There's a new Land up. Want to come?"

A small fury appeared in the hole, very surprised to be asked. "I DON'T SULK IN COMPANY," he announced, and then came anyway, sulking beautifully about it, which he said was different.

The new Land was the Land of Dreams, and it was the softest place that has ever existed. Pillowed hills. Feather trees. A warm wind that smelled of bedtime. Rivers, if you could call them that, of warm milk. Everybody in the Land was asleep, smiling, tucked under bushes that tucked themselves.

"It's lovely," said Bessie, and yawned.

"That's how it gets you," said Moon-Face grimly, chewing a Toffee 'Nana Shock to stay sharp. "Nobody leaves the Land of Dreams awake. It doesn't want letting alone, it wants company. Whatever you do, don't sit down."

Fig sat down. The moment his bottom touched a pillow-hill his eyes closed and his face went slack and peaceful, and no shaking, tickling, or shouting made the smallest difference.

"He's not waking up," said Jo, doing all three.

"They never do, from inside," said Moon-Face. "And when the Land slides at dusk—" He didn't finish. He never finishes, which is the scariest kind of monkey.

"Then he must be woken from the outside," said Silky, and unwrapped a Pop Banana from her apron.

It took four of them holding Fig's nose shut, one Pop Banana, and the Angry Marmoset shouting "WAKE UP, YOU LAZY LUMP!" directly into each ear in turn — the longest sentences any of them had ever heard him manage — before Fig's eyes flew open and he sat up, saying the words that follow everybody out of the Land of Dreams: "five more minutes."

"NOT A MINUTE," bellowed the Angry Marmoset, and bit him, to save time.

They ran for the cloud-hole with the pillow-hills sighing after them — stay, stay — and got through it just as the bell tolled and the whole soft country slid off into the dark like a blanket being pulled off a bed by a very patient mother.

Down the Slippery-Slip, out the trapdoor, plop-plop-plop — and the Angry Marmoset rode it too, shrieking that he was NOT enjoying it, in the voice of a monkey enjoying something enormously.

"I suppose," he said gruffly, at the bottom, straightening his fur, "you'll be up again tomorrow."

"Every day," said Bessie.

"HMPH," he said. "Somebody will have to keep an eye on the little one."

They ran home through the Whispering Wood for bread, honey, and milk, with a Pop Banana each in their pockets — and one for a marmoset, who took it as if it were his by law, and ate it, pop-pop-pop, all the way home, and only smiled a very little.

And as they fell asleep, all three agreed: this was the finest jungle they had ever lived in, and tomorrow they would go up again.

"Toodle-oo!" they had called up the tree, waving.

"Toodle-oo!" the tree had whispered back. Which everybody knows is wood-talk for come-up, come-up.
