# Monkey

*A parody of "Junky"*

The question is frequently asked: why does a monkey go bananas? Zoo men give you psychology: the disturbed home troop, the father grooming in another tree, the branch that snapped under him in childhood. I can only tell you how it was with me. I did not choose the 'nana. The 'nana chose me, the way a vine chooses a trunk.

I first tasted junk 'nana in a fourth-floor nest on the Upper West Branch, in the rooms of a friend named Roy. Roy was a big unremarkable chimp with a steady paw and no politics. He kept his fruit in an old boot. The skin had gone from gold to freckle to a black so complete it looked like mourning, and the smell came off it like the inside of a rum barrel.

"Go easy," Roy said. "Half a peel, no more."

I have never in my life gone easy on anything.

The fruit had fermented in its own skin, sun-cured, and when I swallowed it the jungle, which had always been too loud, went courteously quiet. The fear drained out of my body like heat out of a stone. For the first time I felt right. Not happy — right. There is a difference, and the difference will hook you.

I will not bore you with the middle, except to say the middle is all there is. Junk 'nana is not a snack. It is a way of life. A monkey on the gold wakes thinking of it, scores it, eats it, sleeps, and wakes thinking of it. Everything in between is weather.

In those days I ran with the pads around Branch Street. There was Gibbon Slim, who swung nowhere without a paper bag; the Saki Kid, seventeen and already hollow-eyed; old Hogsbreath, an orang with arms like stevedores and no expression left at all. We sat on the floor of a cold-water nest waiting for the connection, and we talked about 'nanas the way monks talk about God — incessantly, and to no effect.

The connection was a mandrill everyone called Red, on account of his face, which was red the way a fire is a fire. You could see him coming three canopies off, a walking warning label. Red never hurried, Red never missed, and Red had one rule: no credit, no exceptions, no friendship.

"You know what I like about you monkeys?" Red said once, counting my fruit with quick fingers. "Nothing. That's business."

The 'nana merchant does not sell his fruit to the addict. He sells the addict to his fruit.

They say every addict has a monkey on his back. In our case it was tragically literal. Mine was a small gray shaver named Pence who had attached himself to me during a shipment gone wrong and refused to be shifted. He rode me through the parks and the alleys, pawing my shoulders whenever I slowed. "Get your own back," I told him. But that is the whole of addiction in four words: nobody gets their own back.

I kicked twice that winter, in the kicking pen behind a cousin's nest. Junk sickness is not dramatic; it is plumbing. Every pipe in the monkey opens at once and runs cold. Your bones itch from the inside. You would trade your tail for one warm hour, and there are always trades about. I lay on the boards watching the window go from black to gray to black, thinking about fruit with a concentration I never brought to love or work.

Both times I went back. Of course I went back. A monkey does not leave the 'nana; he takes leave of it, the way you take leave of a relative at a funeral — formally, and with every intention of seeing it again soon.

My mate left in the spring, which was sensible of her. She said living with an addict was like living with a ghost who ate all the fruit. I could not argue. I had become a registry of ripeness: black ones for now, freckled ones for the weekend, green ones held in reserve against the long sick. I dealt a little to keep the habit fed, which is like fighting a fire with gasoline delivered by the drum.

The heat came down in the fall. The Trappers raided the pads and took the Saki Kid in a net at the fruit market, and a monkey I knew sat in a bare cage giving up names like a fruit tree giving up seeds in wind. A wise monkey travels. I went south.

I wintered in the Scrub, a flat brown country where I planted one hundred dwarf 'nana trees and learned the oldest lesson of agriculture: the crop comes in spotted, and you eat the profits. The voles took a row, the crows took a row, and I took the rest standing in the field like a fool at his own funeral. When a neighboring planter asked how I expected to turn a profit, I told him I was farming a state of mind. He nodded slowly. In the Scrub, that explanation passes.

From there, the great fig city on the river. In its old quarter I copped from a smooth bonobo called Beamish, who kept a parrot trained to squawk "LAW" whenever a stranger climbed the stairs. That bird saved my freedom three times and my life once. Beamish sold short and talked long, mostly about a female in Lake Village he intended to marry, and the strange thing was that every week she was a different female. I never called him a liar. In the fruit business, hope is inventory.

But the river city went thin on me — shortages, cuts, skins full of nothing but regret. So I went on to the mountain capital, where fruit stalls sold the black-skinned gold openly over the counter, wrapped in waxed leaves, taxed and stamped. There is no jungle so dark it has not invented a form.

By then the junk had stopped working. That is the punchline they save for the last reel. Tolerance is the jungle's usury: a 'nana that once laid out a young chimp for a full day now barely took the gloss off my morning. I was eating to stay even, and even was an address I could no longer find.

In a cantina behind the cathedral I met an old howler with yellow eyes who did not howl. He told me about yagé.

"You have been eating the fruit," he said. "You should meet the vine. Yagé grows on none of your trees, señor. It grows on the Vine of Souls, in the green hell far to the south. One bite, and every monkey in the jungle speaks inside your skull. No wires. No calls. No middleman. Pure troop."

"Telepathy," I said.

"Communion," he said. "Which is the high you have been chasing all along. You kept going down for it, that's all. It was never down. It was over."

He was right, which I resent in anyone. I had spent my whole habit trying to get connected — to the troop, to the branch, to the warm animal hum of everybody at once in the same tree. The 'nana only promised it. The vine, they said, delivered.

So I went south again, to a river port in the hill country where the jungle stands up against the sky like a wall with no gate in it. I carried a rucksack, a machete, a tin of tea, and the name of a guide called Ignacio, who was three days late, which in that country is a form of punctuality.

While I waited I wrote letters I did not send and read the local skies like a monkey reading scales. Ferries came in heavy with fruit and went out empty. The Trappers would come eventually; extradition is the one treaty every jungle honors. I was not afraid. An addict stops fearing the future the way a fruit stops fearing the knife.

Ignacio arrived at dawn on the fourth day, barefoot and cheerful, carrying a rifle for the jaguars and no opinions about mine. "The vine is far," he said, "and the vine decides who finds it. You understand this?"

"I've been decided for before," I said.

"That," said Ignacio, "is the whole training."

We crossed the river at a run before the light came up, and the green closed behind us like water over a dropped coin.

People ask if I found it. The honest answer is that I am still looking, and the honest addendum is that looking is the habit now — the last one I carry, and the only one I would recommend. Somewhere north my ledger waits in a drawer and my scales rust in a shed. Somewhere south a banana grows that would make me one with all monkeys at last.

That first night I sat in the green dark and did not think about 'nanas, which is a full-time job. The jungle does not care whether I find it. That is the thing about the jungle: it was never hooked on me.
