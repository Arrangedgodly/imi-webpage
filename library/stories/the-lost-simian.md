# The Lost Simian

*A parody of "The Lost Symbol"*

The invitation arrived by carrier parrot: deliver a morning lecture at the United States Capitol, "an honor including airfare, five hundred dollars, and a fruit basket." Robert Langdong, the world's most famous simian symbologist, swung into Washington at dawn, straightened his tweed vest, and climbed to the Rotunda.

He never got to lecture.

On the floor of the Rotunda, in the upturned palm of the bronze Many-Armed Ape of State, sat the severed paw of Peter Solobon — Langdong's beloved mentor, a wise old mandrill of the Smithsonian canopy — the flesh tattooed with the letters SBB XIII and a tiny crown.

Langdong's tail went rigid. "Peter."

"His paw," corrected a voice like a dental drill, "and presumably the rest of him, is somewhere else. Congratulations, Professor. You've become a person of interest." Inoue Sato, director of the CIA's Office of Security, was a tarsier the size of a coconut with eyes like twin moons. She looked all the way up at Langdong. It was like being glared at by an owl with a grudge. She held up his invitation. "Forged, incidentally. Whoever wrote it knew your lecture fee and your weakness for fruit baskets. You rang the bell on your own trap."

Across town, in a basement lab of noetic science, Katherine Solobon — Peter's daughter, a brilliant bonobo who had spent years proving that monkey see, monkey do is a real, measurable force of nature — smelled smoke. Her laboratory was burning. Standing in the doorway, lit by the flames, was the man who had taken her father: a towering, hairless mandrill dyed head to tail in tattoos, from the double-headed eagle on his scalp to the ancient script coiling down his arms. On his bare chest waited one empty patch of skin.

"I am a masterpiece," Mal'akh told the burning doorway, "unfinished."

Her assistant Trish, a tarsier whose login was faster than gossip, went to check the fire panel and did not come back. Neither did the night guard, the lab fern, or the building's sense of humor. Katherine escaped with a hard drive in her waistband and one finding she still trusted: she had once measured a thought moving between two monkeys at twelve ounces of intent per second, and peer review had never quite forgiven her.

Langdong and Sato were still circling each other over the paw when the Architect of the Capitol intervened. Warren Bellamy was an aged colobus in an ascot who had survived nine administrations by never hurrying where cameras could see. He smuggled Langdong out under a coat, hid him in the Library of Congress Reading Room — the greatest treehouse of books in the jungle, where pursuit is impossible because everyone whispers — and breathed one sentence before melting into the stacks.

"The Pyramid is not treasure, Professor. It is a promise the Treeclimbers made the age of monkeys. Protect it, and ask fewer questions in hallways." An hour later he surrendered to Sato with such slowness that two of her agents retired watching him.

By midnight the three of them — Langdong, Katherine, and a CIA tail holding a flashlight like a grudge — had dug up the Solobon family banana cellar and found what Mal'akh wanted: the Simian Pyramid, a stone artifact of the Free and Accepted Treeclimbers, guarded by Peter for decades. It was wrapped in a dish towel embroidered WISDOM, which in the Solobon family counted as a security system. Mal'akh took it from them at coconut-point, along with Langdong's dignity, Katherine's spare hairbrush, and Katherine herself, because villains collect sets.

He brought them all to the House of the Banyan — the great stone temple on Sixteenth Street whose thirty-three columns stood like a petrified forest with a stricter dress code. Peter Solobon was already lashed to the altar, and Mal'akh stood above him, dipping a thorn in dye.

"The Lost Word," he thundered. "The ancients hid a word of power. Tonight it goes on my skin, and I become the winged primate of legend. Stop me and I'll tattoo you both with spoilers."

"You cannot steal the Word, Zachary," Peter said quietly. "I gave it to you the day you were born."

The name landed on the tattooed mandrill like weather. For that was the secret beneath the skin: Mal'akh was Zachary Solobon, Peter's own son, lost years ago to the Zoo Cage and reborn as a blank canvas with a grudge. The one bare patch on his chest was reserved, like a throne, for the Lost Word.

"Then carve it into me properly, Father," he said, "and hang your guilt in the gallery forever."

He submerged Langdong in the temple's ritual dunking tub to loosen his tongue. This was a tactical error. Langdong floated, faked drowning with the theatrical skill of a tenured professor, and surfaced shouting the solution he'd assembled underwater, because that is how symbologists think.

"The cube is wax! The pyramid's cipher points to the Mall — the ordered realm is Franklin's magic square, 'the House of the Jungle's first king' is the old Congress cage, and the coordinates run down the Monument! The Word sits at the top of the obelisk in plain sight, you shaved plum, where every monkey in Washington could see it if anyone ever looked up!"

Zachary lunged, missed, and went through the temple skylight. He was last seen lodged in a magnolia, masterfully exfoliated. The thorn, the dye, and the empty patch of chest went to the county landfill unfinished, which is the cruelest thing that can happen to a masterpiece. Peter Solobon, who had now lost his son twice — once to the streets, once to the sky — closed his eyes and said the prayer for polished souls. They fished Katherine out of the filling font, soggy, furious, and forgiving in alphabetical order.

At dawn Langdong stood at the tip of the great obelisk with the golden capstone in his paws. Rescued, bandaged, and remarkably calm for a mandrill who had spent the night in a ritual pantry, Peter Solobon read the tiny engraving: LAUS DEO.

"Praise be," Peter translated, "to the Banana Within." Then, because Langdong asked where the Word itself had been hiding all these years, Peter nodded at the monument's base. "In the cornerstone. One book, sealed there by the first president: the Jungle Bible. The Word was in the foundation the whole time — symbolically, and, as it turns out, literally."

Langdong laughed until his ribs ached. "A word that turns out to be a book, a book that turns out to be a mirror. The Lost Word was never lost, Peter — only forgotten. The ancient mysteries were never hidden. They're the oldest monkey truth there is: the golden banana is inside every primate. We just spend so much time grooming each other's shoulders, we never check our own paws."

Far below, the city bloomed into morning. Sato clicked her notepad shut and allowed that the evening had gone "bananas — but in a useful way." She also confessed she had never suspected Langdong of anything but typography; she had come for the security video, because "if that tape leaks, every conspiracy howler with a podcast will be up that monument with a chisel by Friday."

Katherine rebuilt her lab with a new sign above the door: THE MONKEY SEE INSTITUTE — BECAUSE MONKEY DO. And Langdong flew home with his honorarium, his fruit basket, and one spare banana, which he ate slowly at thirty thousand feet, feeling — as the ancients promised — positively divine.
