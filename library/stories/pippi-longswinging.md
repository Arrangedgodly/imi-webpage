# Pippi Longswinging

*A parody of "Pippi Longstocking"*

At the very edge of the little monkey town, just where the last back branch gave way to real jungle, stood an old overgrown fig tree, and in the fig tree a rickety treehouse, and in the treehouse a parcel of vines, and wound through every vine of it an old garden of a house called Villa Villevine. And in Villa Villevine lived the strongest little monkey in the world.

Her name was Pippi Longswinging. Her mother was in the Highest Canopy, as Pippi put it, watching her daughter through a knothole. Her father was Captain Ephraim Longswinging, king of a band of wild ocean-going baboons, who had been blown overboard in a storm and was certainly ruling some island somewhere, and would surely come back for her one day. Meanwhile Pippi lived entirely alone with her pets: a tapir, and a tiny little pet human named Mr. Nilsson, who wore a blue jacket and sat on her shoulder and had no tail at all, poor thing.

Pippi had no mother to tell her when to peel her bananas, and no father to tell her to hang up her stockings, so she never combed her fur, her tail stuck straight out behind her like a flagpole, and her two braids stuck straight out sideways, which is quite a trick and cannot be learned. She was strongest monkey in the world, and she did not go to school, and she slept with her feet on the pillow, and she had a whole chest of gold pieces, and she was exactly nine years old.

Next door, in the Proper Monkey Garden, lived Tammy and Adobo, two well-brushed little monkeys whose mother had told them a thousand times never, ever to go near Villa Villevine. So naturally, the very first thing they did was go near Villa Villevine.

Pippi was hanging by her tail from the gate, asleep.

"Is it true," whispered Tammy, "that you're the strongest monkey in the world?"

"Quite true," said Pippi, waking instantly and dropping neatly to her feet. "I'm the strongest monkey in the world. Would you like a banana pancake? Mr. Nilsson made too much batter again."

Being a thing-finder was Pippi's profession, and she took the children out that very afternoon to find things. "Things-finding is the finest business there is," she declared, marching them into the jungle. "You have to be sharp. Once I found a whole chewing gum. Another time I found a live crocodile, but he wasn't very lost, he was just sulking." She led them straight to a hollow tree, reached in past her elbow, and pulled out a shiny gold piece.

"See?" said Pippi. "Things are always hiding in hollow trees. It's practically a hollow-tree RULE."

Adobo, whose pockets had never before contained gold, decided on the spot that Pippi was the finest monkey alive.

It was Tammy who had the terrible idea. "Pippi, why don't you go to school? School starts again soon, and you'd learn pluttification."

"Plutti-what?" said Pippi.

"You know. Arithmetic. Two and seven and all that."

Pippi considered pluttification for two days, and on the third morning she appeared at the Proper Monkey School riding her tapir, hat on the tapir's head, Mr. Nilsson on her shoulder, tail flying like a banner. "I shall go to school at exactly eight o'clock," she announced, arriving at ten, "or thereabouts."

The teacher, a gentle old langur, did her best. "Now, Pippi, if seven monkeys are in a mango tree and two climb down, how many are left?"

"That depends," said Pippi. "Are any of them my friends? Because my friends would climb right back up. Once I had seven friends in a tree and they ALL climbed down, on account of a leopard, so your answer is wrong before we start."

"That's not how arithmetic works."

"No," Pippi agreed kindly, "that's how monkeys work."

Then came the drawing lesson, and Pippi drew on the floor, because the paper was too small, and the music lesson, when she sang every verse of a song she was inventing, which had forty-one. At last the teacher sat down beside her and said, gently, "Pippi dear, I think perhaps you'd be happier not coming to school. Some monkeys learn one way and some learn another."

Pippi's eyes filled with tears — the strongest monkey in the world, crying. "I understand, Teacher," she said, and shook the teacher's paw politely, and meant it. "But you'll be sorry when I turn out to be the smartest monkey in the world and can't count my own coconuts."

After that, Villa Villevine was school enough.

The trouble with being famous is that the Proper Monkey Ladies came to call. Pippi, wanting very much to do it right, invited them to a kaffeenana — a proper coffee party with cream puffs. The ladies arrived in their best bonnets and were served cream puffs by their hostess, who somersaulted from guest to guest rather than walking, which was already unsettling. Then one lady said Pippi's hair wanted brushing, another said her tail wanted dressing, and a third, the very largest, said that a monkey without manners would never grow into a proper grown-up monkey at all.

"Going ape is a stage you ought to be past, dear," she said.

"Going ape," said Pippi, "is a stage I intend to stay in FOREVER. Observe."

And she picked up a cream puff, took one delicate bite, balanced the rest on a spoon, and flung it straight across the table, where it struck the large lady in her large bonnet with a sound like a soft little bell.

There was a moment of silence you could have hung from.

"That," said Pippi, "is called a cream-puff rally, and YOU'RE ALL PLAYING." The air filled with flying pastry. The ladies screamed and then, being proper only on the outside, screamed and laughed and flung back, and when the last cream puff was splattered on the last bonnet, they all sat down breathless among the wreckage, and the large lady said, "I haven't done that since I was a young monkey," and helped Pippi lick the bowl.

But Pippi's finest hour came with the circus.

The Great World Circus came to the monkey town, and everybody went — even Pippi, who paid for her ticket with a gold piece and got the whole front row. The ringmaster, a pompous macaque in a red coat, presented the roaring lions, the flying baboons, the elephant on the tightrope. Then he boomed: "AND NOW — the strongest animal in the world! STRONG ADOLPHUS, the terrible gorilla! Who among you dares to wrestle him?"

Nobody dared. Strong Adolphus flexed and pounded his chest, and the audience trembled, and the ringmaster laughed. "No monkey in this town is brave — or STUPID — enough!"

"Excuse me," said Pippi, stepping into the ring and handing Mr. Nilsson to Adobo, "but I am the strongest monkey in the world, and I have wrestled my papa's whole crew of baboons before breakfast, for exercise."

The crowd roared with laughter. Strong Adolphus grinned all over his huge gray face, took Pippi's small hand — and next second found himself flying through the air, landing in the lion's cage (the lions moved over politely), and coming back with his fur standing straight up.

"Best two of three?" said Pippi.

He tried again. He tried the Bear Hug, the Gorilla Grip, and the old Jungle Sweeper. Pippi pinned him flat, sat on him, and asked if he'd like a cream puff. The crowd went absolutely bananas — stamping, howling, swinging from the tent ropes. The ringmaster tore his red coat in despair, and Pippi marched around the ring with Strong Adolphus's gold belt over her shoulder and gave it right back. "Prizes are for people who need them," she said. "I already have a chest of gold. But thank you."

That evening the whole town followed her home, and Tammy and Adobo stayed for banana pancakes, and it was Adobo who asked, rather sadly, "Pippi, someday you'll be a grown-up proper monkey, won't you? And then it will all be over — the swinging and the wrestling and everything."

Pippi hung by her knees from the lintel of Villa Villevine, braids pointing at the ground, and thought it over seriously, the way she thought about everything.

"No," she said at last, with her whole enormous heart. "I shall be a monkey FOREVER. And when my papa the captain comes back on his great ship, and he says, 'Pippi, are you coming to be queen of the baboon island?' — then perhaps I shall go. But I shall tell the baboons about pluttification, and cream-puff rallies, and things-finding, and Tammy and Adobo. And then I shall come BACK here. Because Villa Villevine is my tree, and you two are my best monkeys, and Mr. Nilsson has just fallen in the pancake batter, and somebody strong has to get him out."

"Ooooh," said Mr. Nilsson, from the bowl, in tiny human.

"There," said the strongest monkey in the world, and flipped a pancake to the ceiling, and caught it. "Everything is going to be all right forever, or thereabouts."
