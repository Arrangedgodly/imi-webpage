# Chimpire Falls

*A parody of "Empire Falls"*

Every morning at six, Miles Roby unlocked the Chimpire Grill, fired up the griddle, and laid plantains across it in rows, like the keys of a piano he never learned to play. He had been flipping banana-burgers for twenty years. He was a chimp with long, thoughtful arms and a habit of staring out at the falls — the great churning Chimpire Falls that gave the town its name — as if the water might someday carry him somewhere else.

"You've got the look again," said Bea, the ancient waitress, pouring his coffee. "The look your mother used to get. The look of a monkey standing somewhere else."

His mother had waited tables in this same greasy hut, back when the Whiting Banana Mill ran three shifts and Main Street smelled of green bananas and money. She had scraped him into college on tips and claws, and then the cancer came, and she made him promise to come home and stay. "Just till I'm gone," she said, and then, being honest at the end: "Till after, too. You'll see why." He was nineteen. He had been keeping the promise for twenty years.

His father, Max Roby, had been the greatest unsuccessful acrobat in the jungle — a monkey who could somersault but not land — and his one contribution to the Grill was robbing the till twice, once on the way in and once on the way out, both times for bus fare to Florida.

His daughter Tick slid onto a counter stool after school and ordered nothing, the way she always did.

"Mom's marrying Walt," she said.

"I know."

"He calls me 'kiddo.' He owns a gym. It's called Pumping Primate."

"I know that too."

Janine had discovered the gym and Walt Comeau, the Silver-Furred Squirrel Monkey, at the same time, and it was never entirely clear which one she was engaged to. Walt posed in his own advertisements. He called Janine "my inspiration," which in Walt's language meant "my spotter."

Tick spent the rest of the afternoon doing what she always did: defending John Voss, the silent boy from the ruined end of town, whose grandfather hit him and whose teachers had stopped seeing him. When the other juveniles flicked peels at John, Tick moved stools. She thought nobody noticed. John Voss noticed.

That Friday, the summons came from the hill. Francine Whiting — the white-furred snow macaque who owned the mill, the bank, half of Main Street, and, in every way that mattered, the town itself — received Miles in her parlor, at her piano, which she played badly and unstoppably.

"Miles. You'll take the Grill when I'm done with it. Didn't I always say so?"

"You always said *someday*, Mrs. Whiting."

"And you always waited. Waiting isn't earning, Miles. You people quit. The mill quit, the town quit, and you quit — you just keep showing up, which is a different thing entirely."

"What do you mean, *you people*?"

Francine played a chord in honor of her dead husband, who had wanted to give Miles the Grill and had wanted several other things from Miles's mother besides. "I am not sentimental, Miles. The Grill goes to the bank with everything else. But do keep hoping. Hope is cheap, and you run on it, the way that dreadful fry-o-lator of yours runs on grease."

For twenty years Miles had been polite, because his mother taught him that politeness was how small monkeys survived big ones. He stood up anyway.

"Then I quit too," he said. "Consider this my notice. Two weeks. Like a professional."

"Where will you go? The island?" She smiled a thin, snowy smile. "The island's not going anywhere, Miles."

"Neither am I," he said. "That was always your favorite thing about me."

Walking out, he passed the pond where, at nine years old, sulking because Francine had humiliated his mother, he had put her cat in a sack with a stone and rowed out to drown it — and had untied the sack at the last moment, shaking, and watched the cat streak home wet. The cat had lived to a hateful old age. Francine had never mentioned it, not once in thirty years, which was how he knew she knew.

The following Tuesday, John Voss's grandfather went too far and did not get up, and the next morning John came to school with the old monkey's dart-gun in his book bag, and the geometry class became a hostage crisis before anyone had finished their warm-up problems.

The teacher froze. The class went ape. And Tick — who had moved stools all year for exactly this moment — set down her pencil, walked to the front row, and sat down across from John Voss like it was lunch.

"Nobody's leaving," she said. "But nobody's shooting either. You'd have started with me if you meant it. I'm the only one who was nice to you, and you know it, and you can't do it, because then there'd be nobody."

John Voss's chin went into the dart-gun, not the other way around. Father Marco, who had run in from the rectory on the strength of a bad sermon and a good heart, took one dart in the shoulder while taking the gun away, and was insufferably humble about it for the rest of his life. Miles arrived with the whole town, hugged Tick so hard she creaked, and did not let go until the sheriffs had driven off — and even then, only to shake John Voss's hand, which confused everyone, including John Voss.

The engagement party was at the Monkey Bar that Saturday, and Walt, deep into his third tray of banana daiquiris, was reenacting his own commercial for anyone still conscious. Then he got to the part about Janine's "before picture," and Janine laughed with her mouth only, the way you do when something hurts, and Miles set down his drink.

"Hey, Silver Fox."

Walt turned. Twenty years of flipping burgers builds shoulders a squirrel monkey should respect, and Walt had never respected anything. One punch, and the pride of Pumping Primate went down into the fruit tray.

"Come on, Ticker," Miles said, wiping his knuckles. "We're going to be late for a ferry."

The boat pulled out at dusk. Tick leaned on the rail beside him, her fur whipping sideways.

"Dad? What's on the island?"

"I don't know, sweetheart. That's the good part."

They were gone nine days. On the ninth, Bea reached him by ferry-phone: Francine Whiting had died at her piano, mid-chord, and her will had been read aloud at the Grill because her lawyer knew where the audience would be. She had left Miles the Grill, the mill grounds, and the pond with the cat in it — plus a sealed note that said, in full: *You didn't drown the cat, and I didn't drown your mother's spirit either. We were both crueler than that. It's yours now. Don't leave it to the bank; it sulks.*

Miles read it twice, laughed for the first time in twenty years, and caught the next ferry home. That was the thing he finally understood, holding the rail as the current took them back past the breakwater: the island had never been the point. The point was that now the Grill had him because he chose it, and no monkey — not even a dead one at a piano — had chosen it for him.

Behind the boat, Chimpire Falls went on falling, the way it always had. But that's the thing about falls, Miles thought — you only go over them if you stand still.
