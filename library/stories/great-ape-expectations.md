# Great Ape Expectations

*A parody of "Great Expectations"*

My father's family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip. So I called myself Pip, and I came to be called Pip — a young chimp of no fortune and no expectations, living with my shrewish older sister (married to Joe Gargery, the gentle black-furred smith of our troop) in the marsh country of the upper canopy.

My first most vivid and broad impression of things began one raw afternoon in the churchyard, when a terrified glimpse of a great hulking gorilla — ragged, shagged, manacled, a convicted escapee from the prison hulk — rose up from behind a gravestone and seized me by the throat.

"Hold your noise!" he growled, turning me upside down to empty the seeds from my pockets. "Keep still, you little devil, or I'll cut your throat! And bring me a file, and wittles — food, boy, food! A banana, if you have one. Bring them tomorrow, or I'll have your heart and liver roasted and et. There's a young ape hid with me, in comparison with which I am an angel!"

Terror is a wonderful organizer. I stole a file from Joe's forge, a lie from my sister's pantry, and a Christmas banana from the feast, and delivered them to the gorilla at dawn. As he filed his shackle, I watched him limp away toward the river — a fearsome creature to whom I had, unknowingly, given my first act of kindness. (The troops caught him that day, mud-wrestling his convict enemy — a scaly-tailed lemur named Compeyson.)

That Christmas, my sister was struck down by an unknown paw — and ever after wore a file slung round her neck, communicating by grooming slate. The reader may blame Orlick, Joe's sullen assistant, who had lured Joe away that day.

Years later, Miss Havisham of Satis Nest — the mad old orangutan who had stopped the clocks, never removed her tattered wedding veil, and let her one shoe rot on her foot at the exact hour her groom abandoned her — sent for a playmate. I was the unlucky candidate.

"Play," she commanded, gripping her cane, waxen and yellowed as an old candle, seated amid the ruins of a banquet gone to moss. "Play. I am not tired. I shall never tire. I have sickened at the sight of sunshine since that day. Broken banana bunches, every one — the feast that was never eaten. So look here, at a troop's celebration rotted to nothing. THIS is what love does."

And then I saw Estella — a sleek, cold-eyed young lemur, groomed to perfection, raised by Miss Havisham to break male hearts as a hobby.

"She is proud and insulting," I told Joe afterward, near tears. "She called me coarse. She called me common. She said I had great clumsy hands."

"Pip, old chimp," said Joe, hammering gently at his anvil, "lies is lies. Howsever they come, they didn't ought to come. She'd need a fine 'nan to call you coarse, and there ain't no such thing."

At Satis Nest I also fought a pale young gentleman in the ruined garden — a spindly spider monkey who challenged me to fisticuffs with tremendous ceremony and went down like a felled banana stalk, after which he shook my hand and beamed. This was Herbert Pocket, and no grudges were kept: when I later came to London, Herbert became my best friend, my roommate, and my conscience.

Because the expectations had arrived by then. Mr. Jaggers, the shrewd mandrill lawyer, came one gray afternoon: "I have instructions to inform you, Pip, that you have great expectations. You will come into a handsome property. You are to be removed from this troop and bred a gentleman. The donor's name is to remain a secret — one word more and the bond is void."

I assumed Miss Havisham. I assumed Estella was part of my inheritance. I was ashamed of Joe's sooty fur, ashamed of the forge, certain I was being groomed for Estella's hand — so much so that when Joe came up to London to visit me, awkward and huge in my fine chambers, and I was stiff and cold with him, he only said, with perfect gentleness, "Pip, dear old chap, you and me was ever friends, and ever will be," and went home. Jaggers's clerk, Wemmick — a brisk little marmoset living in a tiny castle with a toy cannon — warned me: "Portable property is the only property, Pip. Feelings is not portable." I did not listen.

I was wrong on every branch.

On my twenty-third birthday, in my London chambers, a visitor shuffled in — a great gray gorilla, thickened, scarred, seamed with age. He stared at me with an unbearable light in his eyes.

"I tried to get a look at you when you was a-sleeping," he said slowly. "I've come at last. I've wonderfully come. Look at me, Pip. Ain't you glad?"

"Stay!" I cried, shrinking back. "Keep off!"

"Have you forgot the marsh country? The churchyard? The young ape hid with me? Yes, you was a-smelling of it when I first see you. Have you forgot that there had been a banana in your pocket?"

O God! It was the gorilla. The convict. Abel Magwitch — my benefactor.

"Yes, Pip, dear boy," he said, gripping my hand with his knuckling fist. "I've made a gentleman on you! It was me, livin' rough in the bush of New South Wales, herdin' bananas with my own hands, every shilling earned for you. I said, 'That boy fed me when he starved. He shall be a gentleman.' I lived rough so you could live smooth. I worked for you, feared for you, worshiped you. And here you see me — your second father, Pip. Yours, from the day I et your banana in the marsh."

"But — I thought Miss Havisham—"

"Her?" He laughed like bark tearing. "I made her a fancy ornament for her nest once, when I was in trade with a scoundrel named Compeyson. It was Compeyson that jilted her. And it's Compeyson's shadow that's hunted me all my life — and it's Compeyson that hounds me now, for I'm banned on pain of death from ever returning. If I'm found, I swing from a rope instead of a branch."

The truth uprooted me. Magwitch's money — I couldn't touch it now. Estella — she'd married a brute of a drumming macaque. Miss Havisham — I went back and she sobbed, "What have I done!" as her moldy bridal furs caught flame from the hearth. I smothered the fire with my coat; we were both scarred.

One more monkey had not forgiven me: Orlick. He lured me to an abandoned sluice-house in the marshes, bound me to a stone, and stood over me with a hammer, raving about my sister and my pride — and it was Herbert, who had followed, who burst in and saved my life.

But Magwitch I learned to love. Herbert and I planned his escape by river — a steamer bound for the tropics, one dark night, all of us rowing. And in the night, at the last, another boat came alongside: Compeyson, hunting Magwitch for the reward. They went over the gunwale together, locked together like a nightmare, and only Magwitch came up. (Compeyson stayed down, and I have never since trusted still water.)

It did not save him. He was captured at the dock and condemned. I sat beside him in his cell and held his hand.

"Dear boy," he whispered, "you're never a-doubting me, are you? I've done everything smooth for you. Look at me. It ain't much to look at, but it's yours — it's been yours since the marsh."

"You have been with me, Magwitch, when no one else was," I said. "I will stay with you to the last."

And when the end came, I bent low: "Dear Magwitch, I will always remember you. Your daughter lives — she is a lady, she is beautiful, and I love her."

For he had a daughter, stolen by the law and scattered into the troop — and I alone knew where she had landed: cold-eyed, groomed to perfection, in the ruins of Satis Nest.

I fell into a fever after the trial, penniless and arrested for debt. And it was Joe — dear, vast, uncomplaining Joe — who came to London, paid my debts, nursed me night and day, and called me "Pip, dear old chap," as though I had never once been ashamed of him. When I woke humbler, he was gone home — to marry Biddy, the bright young female I'd been too grand to notice. I blessed them from afar.

For eleven years I worked abroad, honest at last. And one evening in the ruined garden of Satis Nest, in the light of the moon and the distant smoke of the old forge, I found a gray-cloaked figure — Estella, widowed, humbled, softened.

"I am greatly changed," she said, her cold eyes gone gentle. "I have been bent and broken, but — I hope — into a better shape. Suffering has peeled me, Pip. You have always held my hand when you could. Will you not hold it now?"

I took her hand. The mists of the marsh had long since dispersed, and I saw no shadow of another parting from her — only a chimp of great, and finally grateful, expectations.
