# The Louse and the Monkeycycle

*A parody of "The Mouse and the Motorcycle"*

Kit was a young rhesus monkey, road-tripped half to death, arriving with his parents at the Banana Grove Resort, the finest troop-hotel in the whole canopy. And riding in unseen on Kit's left shoulder came Rolo: a young louse, reckless and bored, who lived there with his mother and twenty-seven cousins.

A louse's life, if you have never had one, is mostly holding on. "The whole secret," Rolo's mother said daily, "is to be unseen. Unseen, unhurried, un-groomed. Groomed lice are ex-lice."

But Rolo didn't want to hold on. He wanted speed.

That night, while Kit slept in the guest hammock, Rolo crept down the shoulder to inspect the luggage. In the open travel-basket stood the most beautiful thing he had ever seen: a shiny red toy monkeycycle, with chrome handlebars and a saddle of real woven leather.

Rolo climbed the pedal, pulled himself onto the seat, gripped the bars, and — to feel brave — made the noise.

"Brr-r-r-r-rm," said Rolo.

And the monkeycycle moved.

Later, everyone would agree this was the marvel of it: the machine ran on the noise. Say it soft, it went slow. Say it loud, it went fast. Stop saying it, it stopped.

"WHEEEE — brr-r-r-R-RM!" cried Rolo, and roared up the nape of Kit's neck, around the Great Ear, and straight off the far side of the shoulder blade, where he tumbled into the Armpit Thicket and was not found until morning.

Kit woke with a start, felt the wriggle, and — instead of the traditional scratching — peered in and found a very small louse sitting up in the fur with a very large grin.

"You rode it," said Kit. "You rode my monkeycycle!"

"Brrm," said Rolo, by way of hello.

And that was the arrangement. Kit kept his secret, told no one, not even his journal, and at night, while Kit slept, Rolo rode the whole territory of him: up the arm highway, down the tail causeway, across the sleeping ribs like dunes in a red fur desert. But there were two laws. First, never venture into the Armpit Thicket, from which no louse returns without a combing. Second — and this they both knew without saying — stay clear of Aunt Groomer.

Aunt Groomer was Kit's great-aunt, a macaque of terrifying fastidiousness whose fingers went through fur like wind through grass. To Aunt Groomer, a louse was not a passenger. A louse was a snack.

The rules held all season — until the afternoon Rolo, showing off for his cousins, rode in daylight, wide open, up the wrong arm.

Aunt Groomer's fingers came whistling.

Rolo braked, bailed, and hid inside the monkeycycle's saddlebag while the world combed past — once, twice — and parted away. He shook for an hour. "Unseen, unhurried, un-groomed," he whispered. His mother was right. Mostly.

Kit, however, had seen the whole thing from the corner of one eye, and Kit was not pleased. He parked the monkeycycle high on the luggage shelf, out of louse reach, and said the worst thing he could think of: "I'm very disappointed in you."

The strictness lasted a week. Then came Grooming Fair Day, when the resort emptied every hammock, washed every sleeping-fur, and beat every cushion in the hotel — the one day of the season when the whole troop went mad with cleanliness. Kit's family stood in the grooming line, and their rumpled travel linens, folded on the counter, were swept up with everything else and sent, basket by basket, down the long vine to the wash-rocks below the falls.

The monkeycycle was parked on the folded linens.

Rolo saw it from Kit's shoulder — a speck of red riding a basket toward the thunder of the falls — and, unseen or not, he went. Down the vine he slid, past the hampers, onto the swaying basket-rim, and there was the monkeycycle, about to go over the rocks with the wash.

He leapt on. "BRR-R-R-RM!" And the two of them — one small louse, one small motorcycle — rode off the edge of the basket and onto the vine-line itself, the long sagging laundry-vine that ran from the resort down to the wash-rocks, taut and humming in the sun.

Halfway down, Rolo heard the basket hit the rocks below, and heard the laundress howler drop an entire load in astonishment. He did not look back. He rode the vine like a rope-bridge at a gallop, wind in his whiskers, down and down and then up the far slope, and shot through the window of the guest hammock-room and braked to a stop on the pillow, shaking.

Kit stared at him for a long time.

"You," he said finally, "rode the laundry line. In front of the laundress. In DAYLIGHT."

"I saved the —"

"You could have been combed! You could have been LAUNDERED!" And Kit picked up the monkeycycle and put it away and did not speak to Rolo for two days, which, for a boy who talks to a louse, is a very long time.

On the third night Rolo came and stood on his knuckle, and said, "I know the rules now. All three of them. The third one is: no errand is worth the whole troop knowing about lice."

Kit looked at him a long moment. "The third one," he said, "is that you're not a thing I own, like the cycle. You're a thing I'd miss." And he held out his arm for a ride home, which is louse for apology accepted.

Then Kit fell ill.

It began with chills, then a fever that made the little monkey talk to parrots who weren't there. The resort's healer, an ancient toque macaque with a satchel of smells, chewed a bitter grey cud and pronounced it fever-flower or nothing — three fresh petals, swallowed with water, before dawn.

"But I gave my last fever-flowers away," the healer said. "Has anyone seen my pouch?"

Everyone looked at everyone. It was Rolo who remembered: the fever-flowers had come packed in Father's shoulder fur on the last journey — three red petals still stuck fast in the deep fur of Father's back, a country away across the sleeping hammock-room.

"I know the road," said Rolo. "I've ridden it at night. I'll ride it now."

The daylight was against him — and the daylight was full of Aunt Groomer. So Rolo went the long way, by the tail causeway and the spine ridge, freezing in the shadow of every grooming hand, down Kit's arm, along the bridge of Mother's hand where she slept holding Kit's wrist, up the cliff of Father's shoulder, into fur that smelled of strange cities. He found the petals anchored like canoes. He bit the first one free, then the second, then the third, and dragged them — a sled-train of red — all the way back through the waking forest of fingers.

The healer found the petals on his mortar at dawn and assumed he was forgetful rather than miracle-adjacent. Kit chewed them, slept like a stone, and woke hungry, which everyone agreed was the best sign in jungle medicine.

At the end of the season, Kit's family packed to leave. Kit sat on the hammock rail and fit something over Rolo's head with great care: a thong, a chin-strap, and a shining yellow bottle-cap.

"A helmet," said Kit. "If you're going to ride, ride properly." And he carried the monkeycycle to the deepest, safest tangle of fur, behind the left ear, where even Aunt Groomer's fingers rarely wandered.

"It's yours now," Kit said. "Keep it in the saddlebag. Ride when I'm asleep — you have my permission forever. And Rolo —" he hesitated, being eleven, "— don't get groomed off."

"Unseen, unhurried, un-groomed," said Rolo, adjusting the helmet at an angle he felt was dashing. Then, unable to help himself: "But once in a while — brr-r-r-r-rm."

His mother, watching from the collar, did not quite smile. But she held on a little looser after that.

And every night after that, when the moon rose over the canopy, a very small motor went humming across a very small shoulder. It is the finest sound in the world, if you're small enough to hear it.
