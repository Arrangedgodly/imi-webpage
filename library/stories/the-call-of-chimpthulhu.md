# The Call of Chimpthulhu

*A parody of "The Call of Cthulhu"*

The most merciful thing in the world, I think, is the inability of the merely hairy mind to correlate all its banana contents. We live on a placid island of ignorance in the midst of black seas of infinity, and it was never meant that we should swing far. The sciences have hitherto harmed us little; but some day the piecing together of dissociated 'nana knowledge will open up such terrifying vistas of reality that we shall either go mad from the revelation or flee into the peace and safety of a new dark age — probably one with fewer bananas.

This is what my grand-uncle's papers taught me. Professor George Gammell Angell, Emeritus of Primate Languages, died after a nudge from an airborne winged thing that no zoo could identify. He had been, the coroner noted, walking briskly at the time, and afterward lay entirely still, which the coroner called "a transition." In his desk I found a locked trunk, and in the trunk three things: a bas-relief in clay, a sheaf of clippings, and a smell.

The bas-relief had been made by a young sculptor named Wilcox, who had dreamed it. He described the dream at length, in the manner of young sculptors: a great wet jungle-city of impossible angles, lianas thick as bridges, fruit that gleamed yellow in the wrong directions; and a voice chanting, from everywhere at once, *Chimpthulhu fhtagn.* His diary ran on: *February 28 — the city again. The peels curl like architecture. I have stopped sleeping. March 1 — there is a priest in it, or a fruit, or both. March 2 — called on Professor Angell, who asked me questions for two hours and then paid me, which frightened me more than the dream.*

And the dream had not been his alone. That spring, sensitive monkeys everywhere — poets, painters, thin aesthetic apes who wrote verses about the moon — dreamed the same city and woke sweating. My grand-uncle's notes showed he had written to every one of them, offering small fees for their dreams, and the replies were stacked and dated and indexed, because he was that kind of monkey. Two respondents had taken to sleeping in their own laundry baskets, for safety, and had to be talked out. An architect in the capital woke to find he had drawn the stairs in his own house plans sideways, and had them built that way before anyone could stop him; he lives in them still, sideways, and will not discuss it. A very serious gorilla professor assembled the reports and found the same refrain in most of them:

*Ph'nglui mglw'nafh Chimpthulhu Banalyeh wgah'nagl fh'nana-gn.*

Roughly translated: "In his house at Banalyeh, the great Chimpthulhu sleeps, dreaming of ripe ones."

And the date of the great dreaming, my grand-uncle had worked out, matched exactly — hour for hour — the night the sea shivered and every banana on the southern coast ripened at once. The newspapers printed this among the farm notices, where nobody reads.

There was worse in the trunk: a police report from the southern mangroves, where a swamp cult had been broken up — monkeys dancing around a monolith, led by a terrible old priest with three teeth. Inspector Legrasse's account ran eight pages, seven of which were the phrase "chanting, still chanting." The cultists could not say what they worshipped, exactly, except that it was old, and yellow, and patient. "They was waitin'," the old priest told the court, grinning with all three teeth. "The Great Old Monkeys come down from the stars when all the jungle was young. They taught the first monkeys to climb up and never come down again. Now they sleep in the deep green, dreamin' of bananas. When the stars come right, they swing again. Chimpthulhu'll wake, and there won't be no more climbin' — only swingin', forever, all together, one great bunch, one great hang."

The court released them for lack of anything sensible to charge them with; *menacing giggling*, the magistrate wrote, being no offense yet.

The clippings were almost worse: a cargo of plantains found adrift at sea, perfectly peeled and perfectly empty; a lighthouse keeper who swore the water lay yellow to the horizon for one night; a troupe of circus chimps who walked out of their tent at midnight in a line, in costume, and were found at dawn on the beach, facing out, waiting politely for something to arrive.

The last papers were a manuscript by a Norwegian sailor named Johansen, sole survivor of the bark *Alert*. His widow sent it to my grand-uncle with a note: "He talked of fruit in his sleep. Perhaps you can make something of it. I cannot." I read it standing up, by a window, with all the shutters open.

The *Alert*, Johansen wrote, had gone looking for an island said to have risen overnight, and found it: a coast-line of mingled mud, ooze, and weedy cyclopean boughs — nothing green upon it, only a geometry that hurt the eyes. A canopy built, he said, by somebody who had once had trees described to him over a very bad telephone. Stairs went sideways. A door was simultaneously a window. The crew mapped none of it twice, because it came out different each time, and there is no morale in a coastline that edits itself.

And above the quays of that dead city, one monstrous design repeated in carving: a figure at once ape and fruit and fungus — a swollen, mountainous chimpanzee whose head was a vast split banana, the peel curled back in lobes, from beneath which a hundred prehensile tails waved, slow and patient, like grass at the bottom of a warm sea; and behind its shoulders spread the webbed gliding-membranes of a flying squirrel, gone horribly to scale.

Two of the crew went ashore. Then the wind died, and something came out of the green door.

"If I say that my somewhat exaggerated imagination yielded simultaneous pictures of a chimpanzee, a fruit salad, and a barrel of monkeys," Johansen wrote, "I shall not be unfaithful to the facts. It was inches and it was miles. It was wet, and yellow in the creases. A mountain climbed. A mountain swung through the trees, gently — and each of my shipmates was taken up, softly, like a banana joining a bunch. I heard no screams. I heard *welcoming*. One look, and we steamed the *Alert* straight at it, and struck its midriff — and it spread like pulp, and flowed, and came together again behind us, laughing in color."

The *Alert* escaped with two aboard. Johansen died before anyone could ask him the questions. The manuscript ends mid-sentence.

I burned what I could. It did not matter. The cult will never die till the stars come right again. What has risen may sink, and what has sunk may rise. Latent under the world's canopy, the Great Old Monkeys wait, dreaming of the one great bunch.

*That is not dead which can eternal lie,*
*And with strange aeons, even death may peel.*

Why do I write this? Because I have looked into the shipping news. My heirs travel. And there are bananas in my cellar that ripen in the dark, too fast, all together, in unison — as if something were dreaming of them.
