# The Sun Also Rhesus

*A parody of "The Sun Also Rises"*

Robert Con was the wrestling champion of his old troop, a broad-chested mandrill with a face like a warning sign, and once, in a bower on the Left Branch of the Big Banyan, he had held Jake Branches down and groomed him possessively in front of everyone, and Jake had never quite forgiven him for it.

"I was wondering where the swellest climbing was," Con said now, in the Paris café. "A fellow wants to get away. Purify himself. Get in touch with the loam."

"You, Con," said Bill Gorton, "are a middle-aged literary mandrill, and you're hanging around the Big Banyan with a lot of bananas, and all you do is talk about getting in touch with the loam."

Jake said nothing. He was a rhesus who had come out of the Great Troop War with a mangled tail — he could still climb, after a fashion, but he could no longer swing, and a rhesus who cannot swing keeps very quiet about what else he cannot do.

Lady Brett Ashley could do all of it. She was a bonobo with a vine of flowers wound round her brow, and she moved through the canopy like weather. She sat down at their table, called the waiter "chimp," sent him for champagne figs, and looked at Jake in a way that made the whole café go quiet.

"We'd have had a fine time together," Brett said softly.

"Yes," Jake said. "Isn't it pretty to peel so."

One night they dined with a count — an old, courtly macaque with real title-fur, who opened the champagne figs himself, and watched the two of them not looking at each other, and said, without any unkindness at all:

"Enjoy it. You are all a lost generation, you know. I have been lost myself, twice, and it is survivable, but you people are doing it on purpose."

"That's the count," Brett said fondly. "Say it again, chimp. It sounds better with the accent."

In July they all went down to Pamplona Alta for the fiesta of San Firmin', where the peccaries run the gorge and the little capuchin wrestlers wait for them with capes of beaten bark. Brett arrived with her fiancé Mike, a shaggy gibbering baron who owed bananas in four groves and mentioned it charmingly. Con arrived uninvited, following Brett. Bill arrived with Jake, and they went fishing up in the hills above the Irati mist, where the river ran cold over stones and nobody had to want anything.

"Listen, Jake," Bill said, laying out figs and cold eggs on a flat rock. "You're an expatriate. You've lost touch with the loam. You hang around cafés taking it on the chin, and it isn't funny anymore. A monkey should root around in his own undergrowth a little. Irony and pity. Say it with me."

"Irony and pity," said Jake, and cast his line, and for one whole day it was the truest sentence in the jungle. They kept every fig they needed and no more, and the worms they dug were bait, and it was as close to happy as either of them ever planned to get.

Then they came down to Pamplona Alta, and the fiesta went bananas.

It began with a rocket in the square, and after the rocket nobody in the town was ever altogether sober again. For seven days and seven nights the peccaries ran the gorge each morning and nobody slept, and the wine-sap came out of the fig skins in the public square, and you could not throw a peel without hitting a drummer. They danced in the ring under the big bough, great circles of strangers holding tails, and Brett danced with them all, brow-vine shining, and was elected to the town in an afternoon.

At six on the first morning Jake ran with the crowd, ahead of the peccaries, for the sake of the old feeling: the drumming of hooves coming up through the wood, the whole shaking floor of the jungle, and himself above it on the fence-limb, close enough to smell the bristles — a fine clean fear with no war in it. It was about the only thing all year that had come at him honestly.

Jake, who knew the capes and the bark and the rules, was made an honorary elder of the wrestling troop. Old Montoya, who kept the ring-grove and judged every monkey in the world by a single word — *aficion*, which means having your whole heart in the bough — handed Jake the great collar of the order, and meant it. "Either a monkey has aficion or he has not," said Montoya, tapping his chest. "The crowd has money; the ring has aficion. You have some, señor. Do not lose it — it is the only thing the fiesta cannot sell you back." It pleased Jake more than anything in years.

Then Jake introduced Brett to Pedro Romero.

Romero was nineteen, a capuchin of the old classical school, and he wrestled the big peccary boars the way water goes over stones — no waste, no fear, straight lines that curved. Brett watched him once and stopped sleeping entirely.

"Don't do it," Jake said. It was the one thing he had left to give anybody, the introduction, and it was being eaten in front of him.

He gave it anyway. That was the worst of it; he handed it over himself, and Montoya saw him with Romero at the café, and caught his eye, and held up the great collar a moment, questioningly — and did not offer it again; and after that Montoya would not look at Jake, and Jake could not climb high enough in the evening to feel fine about it.

Meanwhile the hotel went rotten. "Look, chimp," Mike told Con that same night, "why don't you swing off? Why do you follow Brett around like a fruit bat? You're not wanted. You're a bloody peacock of a mandrill and you've never once wanted anything you couldn't sulk about."

Con went, finally, after the morning the whole thing broke. Romero had been seen going up Brett's stair; Con went up after him; and afterward Con sat in the café with a split lip and three wrecked knuckles, and wept — actually wept — because he had flattened Romero against the wall, and Mike, and Jake too when Jake came in, and it had not made him one hour happy. Romero stood up straight again with the blood on his muzzle, the old classical school to the last, and would not stay down; and that was the thing Con could not forgive: that he had won everything and gotten nothing.

Brett stayed, and took a room over Romero's grooming shed, and washed the wine out of his fur with her own hands — and then, because she was forty-one and he was nineteen and she would not be the thing that ruined the old classical school, she let him go and sent for Jake.

They rode out together in the big open carriage of the canopy railway, Brett's brow bare now, the vine gone, sitting very close.

"I'm not going to be a legend, chimp," she said. "I'm done going ape. I'm going back to Mike. He's a dreadful hangs-about, but then so am I, and it's fairer that way." She looked out at the gorge where the fiesta was still drumming. "You know it makes one rather ashamed. We could have had such a damned good swing together."

"Yes," Jake said, and the great fig trees went by, and the sun kept climbing whether anybody wanted anything or not. "Isn't it pretty to peel so."
