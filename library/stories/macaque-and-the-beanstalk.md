# Macaque and the Beanstalk

*A parody of "Jack and the Beanstalk"*

At the raggedy edge of the jungle lived a small macaque named — well, Macaque — and his mother, in a lean-to of broken branches and optimism. They were so poor that dinner was a shared banana peel, and dessert was believing in tomorrow.

Their last possession of value was the family cow, Milky, who gave warm milk and unimpressed looks. Milky had opinions. Milky kept them to herself, but you could feel them, like weather.

"No macaque climbs anywhere on an empty stomach," said Mother one morning, handing Macaque the lead rope. "Take Milky to market. Sell her. Buy bread. And do not — Macaque, look at me — do not come home with anything ridiculous."

On the path, Macaque met a grinning old gibbon with a sack.

"Good morning, young macaque! Where off to on this fine day?"

"To sell our cow."

"Sell her to me! I'll offer you five — beans."

"Beans," said Macaque, "are ridiculous."

"Beans," said the gibbon, drawing them out like five green stars, "of MAGIC. Cooking beans, climbing beans, wishing beans, beans of chance — but THESE, young sir, are beans of vertical destiny. Plant them by moonrise, and by morning you'll have a stairway to the sky itself."

And this is the shameful part: Macaque shook on it.

His mother's reaction was loud, long, and included the word "beans" eleven times. Then she flung them out the window, and Macaque slept in the woodshed, on principle, which was the only furniture they had left.

But beans keep their own appointments. By morning a beanstalk stood outside their door — thick as a rhino, ribbed like a ladder, leaves like sails, climbing through the clouds and out of sight.

Macaque looked up. Macaque gripped the stalk. Macaque, being Macaque, climbed.

Above the clouds lay sunlight, a golden road, and at the end of it a castle grown into the biggest fig tree Macaque had ever seen. A gorilla the size of a landslide was thumping up the steps.

Macaque did what small monkeys do best: he made himself little, and slipped inside.

In the kitchen stood the giant's wife, a mountain of a lady with an apron and a kind, tired face. She took one look at him and sighed.

"Another little monkey," she said, and that is how Macaque learned he was not the first.

"Out!" she whispered as the floor began to shake. "Under the fruit bowl! Quick!"

The door boomed open, and Gorlo sniffed the air.

"FEE-FI-FO-FUM!" he roared. "I smell the peel of a macaque young one! Be he alive or be he dead, I'll butter my breakfast with banana-bread!"

"You smell your own lunch, dear," said his wife, sliding him a platter of figs. "Sit. Eat."

The giant ate a fig tree's worth of figs, counted his money twice — a bag of golden bananas that chimed like temple bells — and fell asleep snoring like distant thunder. Macaque's legs carried him out the door with the bag before his conscience could file an objection, and if his conscience said anything, the snoring drowned it out.

He slid down the beanstalk like rain down a gutter and poured the golden bananas into his mother's lap. One, sold at market, bought bread, butter, a new roof, and a year of not being scolded.

The roof was the best part, his mother said. The roof and the quiet.

But Macaque was Macaque, so up he went again.

This time he hid in the warming pot, and what he saw was this: the giant's wife set a plain brown hen on the table and said, "Lay." And the hen laid a golden banana.

"LAY!" Gorlo would boom at breakfast, and the hen would go bok-BANANA — gold as sunrise, every morning, forever, and Gorlo had never once said thank you. The hen and Macaque exchanged a look through the pot lid. It contained an entire conversation.

That hen came down the beanstalk in Macaque's arms that very evening, of her own free will, and every morning after she laid one golden banana before breakfast, and one more on Sundays, "for the nest egg," she explained, and nobody argued with a hen that produced gold.

On the second visit Macaque also saw the counting house, where Gorlo kept his gold in piles he could sit on, and sat on them, and suspected them, and counted them twice more in case they had plotted overnight. Macaque thought of his mother doing sums in the dark by feel, and his conscience filed another objection, this time in triplicate, and was again outvoted, though it made a note of every banana.

The third time, hidden in the cupboard, Macaque saw the most beautiful thing yet: a tiny golden marmoset with a harp, who played so sweetly the fire stopped crackling to listen.

"My treasure," the giant would murmur, stroking her head with one enormous finger. "Sing, Harpy."

Harpy sang — but she sang, Macaque noticed, of doors. Of roads. Of anywhere but a table in a cloud. So when the giant slept, Macaque lifted her gently down, and Harpy, no fool, came willingly, harp and all. They were almost to the door when she could not help herself.

"MASTER!" she shrieked, in ringing, golden tones. "MASTER! SMALL FINGERS! MACAAAQUE!"

The castle erupted. Down the beanstalk Macaque flew, Harpy clinging to his collar and playing, in her terror, a fugue. Gorlo came after him like an avalanche with a grudge, and the whole stalk swung and groaned under his weight like a treeful of opinionated howlers.

"MOTHER!" Macaque bellowed, hitting the ground. "THE AXE!"

His mother was already halfway across the yard with it. She swung like she had waited all her life for the excuse, and she had, and both of them knew it. The stalk groaned, tipped —

"TIMBER!" said Harpy, who had always wanted to say it.

Down came the beanstalk, and down came Gorlo, with a crash that shook every fig from every tree in the jungle — which is why, old monkeys say, figs still all drop at once in autumn.

As for Gorlo, he picked himself up, dusted himself off, and — being a giant, and therefore hard to hurt and easy to embarrass — stomped off grumbling toward the far mountains, where the clouds hang low enough to climb into. On windy nights, they say, you can still hear him sniffing "fee-fi-fo-fum" and finding only plum.

The giant's wife came down the following spring for tea, on a ladder of her own knitting. She brought a basket of cloud-figs, pronounced the new roof "adequate," patted Milky (bought back from the gibbon at twice the price, and still unimpressed), told Macaque that the fourth husband she'd really wanted was a shopkeeper, and went home early because giants wilt in the lowlands.

Macaque, his mother, Milky, the hen, and Harpy lived richly and musically thereafter. The hen funded the household, Harpy scored it, and Milky supervised from the shade with the air of one who had predicted everything. To her credit, she never once said "I told you so" — she had told him nothing, having said nothing, which is how Milky won every argument she was ever in.

And if his mother still scolded him every Tuesday, out of pure habit — well, some beans are magic, and some are just beans, and the trick, as the gibbon never said, is knowing which ones you shook on.
