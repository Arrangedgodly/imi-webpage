# Middlebranch

*A parody of "Middlemarch"*

Miss Dorothea Bough was one of those rare young apes who are given to grand causes, and who occasionally bite off more banana than they can peel.

She lived at Freshitt Hall with her sister Celia and her uncle Mr. Brooke, a good-natured old gibbon whose mind had been wandering so long it had forgotten to leave a forwarding address. Dorothea longed for a great purpose. What she got, instead, was Mr. Casaubon.

Mr. Casaubon was a dry, grey orangutan of fifty-two, a scholar of Lowick Branch engaged upon a monumental work called A Key to All Banana Genealogies, which would trace every banana in the world back to the First Bunch in the Garden. He proposed to Dorothea beneath the fig trees, in the manner of a monkey dictating a footnote.

"Miss Bough," he began, "I have long wished to make you an offer of my — that is, of my library. My life's work, I mean. The Key."

"He is a walking encyclopedia," Dorothea told Celia, rapturous. "The most learned ape in England!"

"He is a fossil," said Celia, who was fond of Dorothea and of earrings, in that order.

They married, and honeymooned in Rome, where Dorothea discovered two things: first, that Mr. Casaubon's great work was a vast bog in which every banana thrown sank without a ripple — he had been drafting "a fair copy of the first chapter" for eleven years; and second, that his cousin, a bright-eyed young capuchin named Will Ladislaw, looked at her as if she were a sunrise.

"Do you know, Mrs. Casaubon," Will said one evening, "your husband's Key wants a beginning. He has notes on notes on notes, and no fruit at the bottom of them."

"That is a cruel thing to say of anyone's tree," said Dorothea. But she thought of it at night, in the vast cold nest, and wept.

Meanwhile, in Middlebranch itself, a young chimp doctor named Tertius Lydgate had come to town with a mission: to discover the vital principle of ripening — why a banana turns from green to gold — and to build a great Fever Hospital for sick apes of every troop. "The way to do good science," he declared, "is to study the banana at the bedside."

Lydgate was a chimp of noble instincts. He was also, as events would show, a chimp who could not say no to a pretty face. Rosamond Vincy, the vain little marmoset pearl of Middlebranch society, set her claws on him with the skill of a lifetime's practice, and before he understood his position, Lydgate found himself engaged, then married, then in debt — a chimp hung in a beautiful trap lined with rose-colored fur.

"I never said I could afford silk hammocks," poor Lydgate groaned, when the bills came home to roost.

"You said we would live like apes of position," said Rosamond, who had ordered the hammocks in four colors, "and I have merely helped us keep our word."

Elsewhere in town, old Mr. Featherstone died as he had lived — horribly — leaving two wills and a roomful of relations who had flattered him for twenty years on credit. The second will, the generous one, could have been burned; Mary Garth, the plain, steady young macaque he had bullied as a nurse, was left alone with it and the old ape's croaking instruction to throw it in the fire. She refused. She would not lie for anybody, living or dying, which cost her nothing and cost Fred Vincy everything. Fred, Rosamond's brother, gambled his way out of his inheritance and into the patient heart of Mary Garth, who had loved him since they swung from adjacent branches — and who loved him too much to marry him until he was steady, which is Middlebranch society in a coconut-shell. Between the two of them stood Farebrother, the moth-collecting vicar, who loved Mary himself with a quiet hopeless thoroughness, and who, catching Fred idling toward the billiard-room of his relapse, put a claw on the young ape's shoulder and delivered the only sermon that ever took: "Mind, Fred, if it comes to moths or Mary, I know which collection I'd rather tend." He united them afterward with his own paws, and went home and labeled three new moths, which he named, in Latin, after nobody at all.

Mr. Brooke, for his part, stood for Parliament as an independent, on a platform of improving everything in general, and was greeted by the free apes of Middlebranch with a hail of rotten bananas so sincere that he retired the same evening to write a pamphlet about it.

Mr. Casaubon died of a heart broken by the thought that his Key would be left unfinished, and that Will might finish it. He had extracted from Dorothea, in his last dark week, a dreadful promise — "if anything should happen to me, you will finish my work" — and died half-suspicious of her, entirely suspicious of his cousin, and one chapter short of a fair copy. Then his lawyer read the codicil: if Dorothea married Will Ladislaw, she should forfeit everything.

Dorothea, in her black mourning fur, grieved for a monkey she had failed, in a direction she could not name, until one grey evening at Lowick she came upon Will and Rosamond Vincy with their heads close together, and went home certain that the world was mud all the way down.

She went back the next day anyway — to Rosamond, of all apes — and found her weeping, for Lydgate was ruined: tarred by his benefactor Bulstrode's scandal (an old moneylender's past dug up, a sick stranger dead under Bulstrode's roof, all Middlebranch baying), a thousand bananas in debt, and his hospital half-built. And Rosamond, in the strange honesty of misery, told Dorothea the truth of everything — including Will, who had spoken to her only of his hopelessness, and of Dorothea, whom he loved.

"He thinks of nobody else," said Rosamond. "It is you, always you."

"I will not be warned against my own heart," Dorothea told Will, when she had found him — and gave up the fortune. Middlebranch gasped. Middlebranch gossiped. Middlebranch, in due course, got used to it, for Middlebranch always does.

Lydgate never found the vital principle of ripening. He moved to a fashionable grove, became a rich ape's doctor, and died at fifty of a broken purpose, remembered as a fur-greased comfort rather than a chimp of science. Rosamond remarried magnificently. Fred, with Mary's paw on the tiller, became steady keeper of the Stone Branch groves, and Mary wrote a little book about her troop which everybody said was very good, and which nobody read. And Dorothea — who had dreamed of mapping the whole banana tree of knowledge — spent her life on quiet, unfamous boughs: funding Lydgate's hospital, helping the poor apes of Middlebranch, and loving Will with a love that grew as fruit does, silently and out of sight.

Her full nature, as I have said, spent itself in channels which had no great name upon the branch. The growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric grooming; and that things are not so ill with the apes of Middlebranch as they might have been, is half owing to those who lived faithfully a hidden life, and rest in unvisited nests.
