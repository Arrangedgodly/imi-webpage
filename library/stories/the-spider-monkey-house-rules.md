# The Spider Monkey House Rules

*A parody of "The Cider House Rules"*

Here at St. Figs Foundling Bough, in the low jungle of the north, the babies arrived two ways: left in the night basket hanging from the gate of the great banyan, or carried in the arms of a desperate mother climbing the hill in the dark. Dr. Wilbur Larch, the old spider monkey who ran the place, helped with both.

"I have been asked to do the Lord's work," he liked to say, "and occasionally, when the jungle insists, the Devil's. Here at St. Figs's we do what is needed, and let the board argue with the need." Then he would take his fermented marula juice up to the reading platform and sleep like a monkey with no debts, which he had plenty of.

The board suspected the marula. Every year a stern delegation of mandrills climbed the hill, sniffed the dispensary, and asked Dr. Larch to account for his methods.

"Show me the alternative," Larch said, "and I'll account for it. Until then, the books balance: babies in, mothers alive, nobody turned away."

The books did balance, mostly because Nurse Edna and Nurse Angela balanced them, and because Homer Wells fetched whatever was missing. Homer was the boy nobody chose. Returned once for being too quiet, once for being too strange, and once for clinging — after which Dr. Larch declared him unadoptable, which at St. Figs's was a kind of promotion. "Homer," said Larch, "you belong here. Now make yourself useful. Watch and learn."

Homer watched and learned. At fourteen he caught his first baby, a girl with a full head of fur, and Larch let him name her. He was still light-headed with pride a week later. He learned which mothers wanted to talk and which wanted the door held open. But when Larch sat him down and explained the other work — the work with two names — Homer put his hands over his ears.

"I'll deliver every baby in this jungle," he said. "That part I'll do. The other part I won't."

"I'm not asking you to," said Larch. "I'm asking you to know how. Knowledge isn't a leash, Homer. It's a branch. A monkey with a branch can climb anywhere, including nowhere."

"I don't want the branch."

"Then you'll meet some mother, someday, who is already hanging by one arm," said Larch, "and you'll be the only monkey in the tree who knows where to grab her. Decide then. Not now. Now you're fourteen and the whole jungle is a question with no wrong answers."

The rest of Homer's education arrived with the harvest, in the person of Wally and Candy, young monkeys from Canopy View, the fig orchard up the ridge. Wally was a champion leaper with a dare in his heart; he went off to the Great Jungle War to show off for his grandfather, was shot down over the Black Water Gorge, and came home with his legs and his tail gone quiet forever. Candy stayed with him out of love, and then out of guilt, and then out of a love that had guilt in it, the way figs have seeds.

Homer went to work at Canopy View picking fruit, and he loved Candy, and Candy loved him back at a careful distance, and one evening at the dock she laid it out plainly.

"He needs me," she said. "And you're the one I want. That's the whole arithmetic, and I'm sorry it's terrible."

"Every day I wait is a day I don't mind waiting," said Homer, "which worries me more than the waiting does."

There was a boy called Angel at Canopy View whom everyone agreed not to explain, and this, too, was a kind of family.

It was at Canopy View that Homer first read the Spider Monkey House Rules aloud.

The Spider Monkey House was the big open shed where the migrant picking troop lived during harvest — a traveling family of spider monkeys who followed the fruit north to south like a rumor. On the wall, nailed by the management, was a board with rules printed in Human, which no monkey in the shed could read. Homer, who could, stood on a crate one night and obliged.

"Rule one: no climbing on the roof. Rule two: no swinging after dark. Rule three: lights out at nine. Rule four: no fires inside—"

"Who wrote those?" asked Mr. Rose, the picking troop's leader, a lean old monkey with a voice like a door easing open.

"The management," said Homer.

"Management got paws?" said Mr. Rose. "Management ever slept here? Last cold snap, the only thing kept my family off the leopard's menu was climbing that roof and swinging off in the dark. Rules ought to be wrote by the monkeys that got to live by them."

Homer looked at rule two for a long moment. Then he pried the board off the wall and handed it to Mr. Rose, who used it, all winter, as a tray for sorting figs.

That same winter, Angel asked Homer the question every foundling keeps in his cheek pouch, and Homer — the only monkey at Canopy View who could read the records — climbed to the office, found the file, and read it to him. It was a single line. Angel listened, nodded once, and went back to work with something in his shoulders set free. Homer thought of Larch: knowledge is a branch.

Then Rose Rose came to him — Mr. Rose's daughter, young, pregnant, and with a face like the weather before rain.

"Who's the father?" Homer asked, gently, because the records habit was hard to shake.

Rose Rose looked at the sorting tray that had lately been the rules. "You're looking at his figs," she said.

Homer understood. He also understood something else: he was the only monkey in that whole orchard who knew where to grab her. He did for her what Dr. Larch had taught him — the work with two names, the Lord's and the Devil's — and he did it because she asked him to, and because he could, and that was the whole of Homer Wells's religion. It had taken him years to hear the sermon, and the sermon was one sentence long.

Mr. Rose came after her with the fig knife when he found out. Rose Rose, who had picked fruit beside him since she could walk, was faster. She opened his arm shoulder to wrist, dropped the knife, and was gone north by the next moon. Homer stitched Mr. Rose himself, and for a while neither of them spoke, and then Mr. Rose examined the stitching with something near pride.

"She cut me clean," he said. "I taught her that. Doc — she's gone. Don't look for her, and don't let anybody else. A rule's no good unless the monkey it's wrote for can live by it." He flexed the arm. "Write that on a board and nail it somewhere."

They telegraphed him in the spring: DR. LARCH PASSED IN HIS SLEEP. THE BOARD REGRETS. IN OTHER BUSINESS, WE HAVE APPOINTED HIS SUCCESSOR, A DR. FUZZY STONE.

Homer read it twice. Larch had forged a young doctor in the registry years ago — a paper monkey, a fiction, a son with a future — waiting for the day Homer came home. Fuzzy Stone was thirty-one years old and had Homer's handwriting.

He told Candy and Wally. Wally gripped his hand in both of the ones that still worked. Candy cried, which let Homer feel the arithmetic of her one last time, kindly, like a lamp carried to another room.

He went home. He walked up the hill past the fig trees he would never finish pruning, into the banyan, where the boys were already in their bunks. Nurse Edna hugged him; Nurse Angela checked his fur for ticks on principle. He made his rounds, and in the dispensary he found Larch's last bottle of marula, unopened, with a note tied to the neck: FOR MY SUCCESSOR, WHO NEEDS IT LESS THAN I DID.

Homer poured it out the window for the old man, corked the empty, and put it on the shelf like a diploma.

At the end of the night he stood in the dormitory door and said the words he had heard all his life, the words that were now his to say:

"Goodnight, you princes of the canopy, you kings of the whole green jungle."

And somewhere south of everything, a girl with a full head of fur — or a girl named for an angel, or a girl gone north — slept a little easier, not knowing why. And here at St. Figs's, one monkey to another, we're all foundlings.
