# Rosemary's Baboon

*A parody of "Rosemary's Baby"*

Rosemary and Guy Woodhouse were rhesus macaques, young, married, and finally — after a long wait-list — moving into the Bramford Banyan, the grandest old fig in the district. It had a bad reputation (the Tench sisters, who bananaibalized a lodger in '26; the sleeper troop that vanished up the trunk in '59), but it had high ceilings, southern light, and a spare hollow for a nursery.

"A bad tree with good branches," Guy said. He was an actor, tall for a macaque, currently auditioning for the lead in the troop theater's *Grapple*. "We take it."

Their neighbors were the Castavines, Minnie and Roman, an elderly marmoset couple from the hollow next door — chatty, generous, relentlessly nosy. On the first Thursday they brought a chocolate-mousse nest of leaves and berries.

"Tastes a little sharp," said Rosemary.

"That's the tanni-root," said Minnie. "Roman forages it special."

Before the mousse, there had been Terry. Terry Gino was the Castavines' lodger, a scrawny young galago with sad eyes and a brand-new charm of knotted grass. "They saved me," Terry told Rosemary on the stairs, showing it off. "A year ago I was a mess. No groom, no troop, nothing. Now look at me." Rosemary looked. A week later Terry went over the railing of the seventh branch, and the vet used the word "accident" twice in one sentence, which is once more than accidents need.

"Despondent," said Minnie, clasping her little paws. "You'll have your mousse, dear, won't you?"

That night Rosemary dreamed of a hot night in a deep dark jungle. She dreamed of the sea roaring in her ears, and fur, enormous fur, and a great red face leaning close — a baboon's face with old, amused eyes — and someone saying *my Rosemary, my sweetest vine*, while claws, very gentle all things considered, gripped her shoulders. She woke with scratches and a taste like a metal banana.

"It was just a dream," said Guy. He was asleep before the sun.

Two months later, Dr. Sapirsting confirmed it: Rosemary was pregnant. Guy, who had just been offered the juiciest part of the season after his rival conveniently went blind in both eyes, was overjoyed. "Everything's coming up bananas," he said.

Everything except Rosemary. She hurt in a way no pregnancy book mentioned. She ate soil and raw plantain. Minnie mixed her a daily tanni-root smoothie ("for the blood, dear") that tasted of chalk and pepper, and steered her, with the delicacy of a forklift, away from Dr. Hill and onto Dr. Sapirsting, Sapirsting having attended "all the difficult births in this tree." When Rosemary complained of the pain, Sapirsting patted her hand and said it was "a mother's privilege," and prescribed no vitamins, no magazines, and no radio dramas. "SILENCE," he wrote on the chart, for some reason in capital letters.

Hutch saw it first. Hutch — old Edward Hutchins, who had known Rosemary since she was a pink eared baby — took one look at her across the fruit market and put down his papaya.

"You look like a grape left in the sun, Ro. What are they feeding you? And who, exactly, is *they*?"

She told him a little. He promised to look into "the old Castavine circle," which he remembered from somewhere, and he was as good as his word: he filled a napkin with notes and a phone number. The night before he was to tell her everything, he ate at the wrong lunch counter and fell into a coma the vets called "unaccountable." He surfaced from it only to die, and what he left Rosemary, when his bits and pieces were read out, was a plain-covered book — *All of Them Bananas: Canopy Covens of the Last Century* — and a note in a wobbling claw: "The name is an anagram."

At a dinner with the Castavines' friends — a tarsier, a Japanese macaque with cameras around her neck, and a very smiley toque macaque — Rosemary finally had enough, sat down hard on her cushion, and screamed at all of them. The guests cooed. The tarsier gave her a pendant: a ball of dark root on a black thread. Everyone smiled with too many teeth.

The next week she fled to Dr. Hill in the next district and told him everything — the mousse, the smoothies, the book, the anagram, all of it. Dr. Hill listened beautifully. "You've been through a lot," he said warmly, and while her coat was still warm on the chair, he telephoned the Castavines. Guy drove her home. Minnie had cocoa waiting, and a second helping of reassurance, and a third.

After that, the seeds. Rosemary spread them on the kitchen slab and pushed them around half the night. ROMAN CASTAVINE. She got NOVA CRIME SANTA four separate times and burst into tears, and behind her, in the doorway, Roman said mildly:

"It's Latin, dear. Old families keep old names."

He took a clementine and went next door.

The baby came early, in June, while a storm combed the banyan. Rosemary heard the cry through the trunk walls — high, wrong, like a howler's bark played backward.

"Is it alright?" she begged. "Is it alright?"

They told her it had been born dead. Nobody believed it, and nobody had to; she was under the roots, and someone was still crying.

She was up in three nights, following the sound through the Castavines' hollow — and there was the whole odd crowd assembled around a black-draped bassinet, an upside-down tangle of lianas woven on the wall, and Minnie hushing the baby like a mango cart coming apart.

"What have you done to his eyes?" Rosemary cried, snatching up the bundle — and there he was, red-gold all over, with a little mane already coming in, claws like tiny crescents, and the amber, deep-set, ancient eyes.

"He has his father's eyes," said Roman warmly.

"Zoon lives!" breathed the tarsier, to no one in particular.

"He has his real father's eyes," said Roman, more warmly still.

"You're all crazy!" said Rosemary. "His father is Guy. Guy — tell them—"

"Ro," said Guy, wretched, from the corner. "They gave me a whole banana grove. And the part. Every season, forever."

"Zoon his father," said Roman. "Zoon the Horned Baboon. Zoon the Unpruned."

"Rock him," said Minnie. "You'll want to rock him."

"Shut up — you're in it, all of you, you and your smoothies and your tanni-root and your Latin—" But the baby had caught Rosemary's finger in his fist with a grip like a little vine, and looked up at her with those old, old eyes. She had planned a great deal, in three nights of following that crying — a paring claw in her cuff, a lecture, possibly an escape over the rooftops — and every word of it dissolved, because he was hers, whatever else he was.

"He's mine," she whispered. "Mine, and no smoothie touches him, and *you*—" the claw came out of her cuff and pointed at Minnie "—will knock."

And Rosemary — sobbing, furious, exhausted — sat down anyway, and rocked him.

The green bananas along the sill ripened all at once. Outside, dawn — or something — was coming up orange.
