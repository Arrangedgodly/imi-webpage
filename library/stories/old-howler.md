# Old Howler

*A parody of "Old Yeller"*

In the season I was fourteen, Papa left with the fruit drive and left me man of the family at Birdsong Creek. "Man of the family," he said, "means the figs get picked and your mama doesn't worry. A monkey can stand anything except a worried mama."

I was still figuring out how when the howler came.

He was an ugly howler. Big in the shoulders, loose in the joints, yellow as an old banana going sweet, a tail like a rope somebody had left out in the rain. He stole dried fruit, tipped the mealie basket, and grinned at me while he did it.

"Get," I told him. "Go on, you thieving pile of shed fur."

He did not get. Instead my little brother Arliss — who was five and loved anything with teeth — flung himself on the howler, and the howler rolled him and pinned him and licked his face till Arliss screamed with laughing. Arliss claimed him, the way Arliss claimed everything, including a hornet's nest once and a live crawdad twice.

"He came because of Arliss," Mama said. "Stayers always come because of somebody little."

Then a trader named Burn Sander-ape came through, looked at the howler dozing in our yard, and said, "That's my dog, as it happens. Wandered off at the crossing." My heart did a thing I didn't expect — it sank. Arliss howled louder than the howler. Burn Sander-ape looked around at Arliss, and at Mama's preserves cooling on the rack, and made the trade every monkey in that valley still tells about: the howler for a gourd of fig preserves and the pick of the next litter. "He's a rascal," he said, mounting up, "but he's a smart one. Feed him work, and he'll feed you back."

I still said "get" most mornings. Until the bear.

Arliss had a way of catching things, and at the creek that day he grabbed a black bear cub, the one creature a monkey must never grab. The she-bear came out of the canebrake like a hillside learning to run.

I couldn't have got there in time. Nobody could. Old Howler did. He came off the bank roaring — and a howler's roar, up close, is the sound of the end of the world clearing its throat. It seemed to lift the fur clean off that she-bear. She took one look at the raging yellow thing between her and her cub, and decided the creek had nothing she wanted.

After that I quit saying "get." A monkey can't help respecting a beast that roars a bear off his little brother.

He pulled his weight, too, most ways. He packed small loads on his back like a pack-llama with opinions. He found every fig tree I ever lost. And he had a voice on him that could peel bark — he'd howl at sunrise, howl at the moon, howl at a leaf turning over, and Mama said it was the cheapest alarm system in the county.

It was at the hog-marking that he saved me the second time, and me knowing it was my own fault besides. Our hogs ran half wild in the canebrake, and marking ears is a young monkey's job: catch a peccary pig, notch his ear, turn him loose. I got one penned in the brush thicket and was reaching for the notch-knife when the whole sounder came back through the cane for their own. I went up a sapling so thin it bent like a fishpole. Old Howler came roaring like a landslide, and the peccaries decided, as the bear had, that they'd heard enough — but one of them opened my leg on his way past, and another laid Old Howler's shoulder open to the bone.

I made it home on him as much as my own two feet. Mama sewed us both — me swearing lightly, Old Howler licking her hand the whole time like being sewn was a kindness being done him — and I lay up two weeks with the leg, and Old Howler lay up beside me, and between us we taught Arliss to play checkers, which he won at, being untroubled by strategy.

That was the year the fear-of-water sickness went around. A neighbor girl, Lisbeth, came through with a warning from the Sander-ape place: beasts going moon-mad, biting everything they met. "It's in the saliva. A bite is a death sentence, only slow." She'd also brought word that Burn Sander-ape's howler had gotten one of his she-dogs, and brought us a speckled pup from the litter, only we sent it home again — we weren't ready to hear that yet, and didn't guess how soon we would be.

With me lame and Mama worn, Lisbeth stayed on and helped pick the late figs, and proved to be the handiest creature at Birdsong Creek after Old Howler himself. She it was who was standing by the meat rack with Mama the night the moon-wolf came: gaunt, jaw swinging loose, eyes like wet stones, walking wrong — walking like something already dead that hadn't been told.

It went for them, and Old Howler went for it.

A howler's roar means a great deal until the other beast is already mad, and then it is teeth. Old Howler gave us time enough to beat the wolf off with the fire-poles, and he stood over us while it died, and he had bites on him. He had bites on him.

We penned him and hoped. Mama remembered the fire-poles hadn't broken his skin, and Lisbeth remembered maybe they had, and the not-knowing was the longest part. Every morning he was the same shaggy, food-thieving, Arliss-licking rascal, and we counted days like coins. I sat by the pen one whole afternoon and he put his big yellow head in my lap and sighed, and I told him about the fruit drive and the figs and how Papa would be proud of me, and he listened better than any troop-mate I ever had.

Then, one evening, he didn't come to the water gourd. He stood off from it, snarling at the water he'd drunk a thousand times — and the light in his eyes was not his.

Mama never had to say much. She just said, "Travis," and I nodded.

I took Papa's dart tube down from its peg, and the black-thorn dart, and I did what a man of the family does. The only mercy left was mine to give. I didn't miss. I want that written plain: I didn't miss, because missing would have been crueler, and Old Howler had earned better from me than cruel.

I didn't cry till later, and then I cried the way you cry for a prime mate, which is what he was, ugly or not.

Papa came home in the fall riding a young jungle pony, with a pouch of coin besides — man's wages, he called it. He looked at my face and didn't ask. He just sat down and said: "Life isn't fair, Travis. The Canopy never promised fair. But it's good. It's a heap better than fair, most days, if a monkey's got somebody that'll roar a bear off him."

In spring, Lisbeth — who by then had her own reasons to come by the place regular — brought us the speckled pup out of Old Howler's line, and the little wretch stole the smoked meat before he'd been in camp an hour. When he howled that first night, his father's voice came rolling out of that small body, like thunder learning its trade.

I let him stay. A family needs one beast like that. I know, because we'd had the best one there ever was.
