# The Climbs

*A parody of "The Chimes"*

There are not many monkeys — older monkeys, steady monkeys, monkeys of business and substance — who will tell you what the Climbs say. That is because the Climbs only speak plainly to those who are too little, too poor, or too old and tired to be prevented from listening.

The Climbs stood in the middle of the old town: an ancient tower of ladders and chains and vine-wound rungs, twelve storeys of swaying timber, older than the church and greener than the market, with a bell-beam at the top and a hundred little rungs below it that rang like bells whenever the wind came, or the monkeys came, which was more often. Every child in that town climbed a piece of it, and every child believed the rungs were alive, which was true.

At the foot of the Climbs, all day in all weathers, stood old Toby Veck — "Trotty" they called him, for the way his legs went when work was offered — a little gray macaque of sixty with a rented white apron and a ticket-porter's badge, who carried parcels up and down the frozen streets for whatever the givers thought fit, and thought himself lucky.

"I'm a poor monkey," Trotty was fond of saying, with a kind of hopeless comfort, "born poor, and likely to stay so. It's in the fur, I take it. But that's the order of things, and who am I to argue with the order?"

His one luxury, taken at the corner window of Mrs. Chickenstalker's shop, was a knuckle of boiled banana-peel — "and very good eating too," Trotty always insisted, "if you toast it" — and while he ate it he liked to look up at the rungs of the Climbs and wonder at the inscription under the great beam, which the moss had half eaten: something about how they rang for all who climb, and heard all who fell, and judged no creature's pocket.

Trotty had a daughter, Meg, a trim young vervet with a laugh like clean water, who walked six miles on New Year's Eve to bring him his dinner hot, and who was to be married on New Year's Day to Richard, an honest laboring monkey with hope still in him like sap in a young tree.

And on this particular New Year's Eve, as the frost came down hard enough to split the branches, three great monkeys from the upper canopy, who had been dining extremely well, passed Trotty on the stair and stopped to improve his mind.

The first was an Alderman, fat and folded, named Cute. "Here's a porter!" said the Alderman Cute. "Now, you monkeys below must not marry. You cannot afford to marry. Stop everything! No little monkeys, no cottages, no cookshops, no discontent. When a poor monkey marries, he sets up a nursery for next year's bread riot. It is our duty — yours, I mean — to stop everything."

The second, a lean economist named Filer, had figures in place of fur. "A porter monkey, Alderman, is a statistical impossibility. The town contains more legs than parcels; your daughter, ma'am," — to Meg, who had arrived with the hot dinner — "is a consumer of bananas that ought by rights to be exported. Your affection, young woman, is not an asset. It is a running cost."

The third said nothing at all, being a driver of monkeys, and drove Trotty off the very step of the Climbs he had stood under all his life.

And Trotty — who had that very day seen a poor out-country monkey, one Will Fern, hunted out of the market-square for being hungry in a public place, with his little orphan niece Lillian's hand in his — went home with Meg's dinner gone cold in its basket, and something worse than cold in his heart.

"Perhaps they're right," said Trotty to the dark. "Perhaps we *are* a bad kind of monkey, born bad, no good. Perhaps it's wrong of Meg to marry, and wrong of Richard to hope, and wrong of any of us to be climbing at all." And he looked up at the old rungs, half-eaten moss and all, and said, "Do you say that too? Do *you* say it?"

He had to know. So old Toby Veck, on the last night of the year, climbed the forbidden tower — up the frozen ladders, past the sleeping town, rung by rung by rung, every rung ringing faintly under his poor feet as if it counted him — to the top, to the bell-beam, where the wind was howling like a whole lost troop.

"Speak plainly tonight," said Trotty, "for I am old, and poor, and tired, and I can bear it."

And the Climbs spoke.

The ghosts of the tower came out of the ladders — a thousand little climbing spirits, infants' faces in the vines, elders' paws on the chains, all the monkeys who had ever gone up those rungs and been told at the top to come back down — and they took Trotty's year away from him, and gave him instead the year that was coming, if monkeys like the Alderman were believed.

He saw Meg married, hopeful, hungry. He saw Richard's hope dry out of him like sap in a drought, and Richard grow sullen, and take to drinking the spirit that is sold in the dark to monkeys who are cold. He saw Meg's baby — Trotty's own little climbing grandchild, whose first act in the world had been to clutch at fur, as all of us clutch, and climb — die in a bare garret one hard winter, because Aldermen had ruled that poor monkeys must not marry, and so had merely ruled that poor babies must not live. He saw Will Fern outlawed for the crime of being a laborer with no labor, and little Lillian learning that if the world calls you bad long enough, it saves time to agree with it. He saw Meg herself at last, gray and bowed, standing one midnight at the river's edge with her last living child, and Trotty shrieked and sprang to stop her, and could not, because — the Climbs told him this worst of all — Trotty himself was not there to stop her. In the year they were showing him, Toby Veck had already fallen from the tower, on just such a night as this, unwitnessed and unlamented, and nobody changed.

"Where is the monkey," cried Trotty to the ghosts, "who says we are born bad? Bring him here! Bring him up the ladders! Set any babe of the canopy at its mother's fur — it is climbing to *you!* It climbs to you! Every climbing child in this town is climbing to you, and you are letting it be told, from the top of the tower, that it climbs for nothing!"

"We are the Climbs," said the spirits, ringing faint and many. "We do not say what is in any monkey. We only say what the youngest climber says, which is nothing — it climbs. Hear us, Toby Veck: the poor are not a bad kind of monkey. They are your kind, climbing. And whoever hears the youngest climber climb, hears us."

The wind took the tower, the ladders bowed, the rungs roared — and Trotty fell.

He fell, and woke.

He woke at the bottom, in the frost-bright dawn of New Year's Day, in the old tower doorway, with all the rungs above him ringing out the year — not a knell, but a peal, a swinging, cracking, shouting peal, like the whole canopy cracking its knuckles at once for joy.

"Lord keep my memory green!" old Trotty cried, which was the last thing the Climbs had charged him to say. "I know their meaning now! The New Year is born climbing!"

And he ran — old, stiff, sixty, and quick, so quick — down into the town, and the town was already awake. Meg was at his door in her wedding ribbons, laughing at him for crying; Richard had work, having gone at dawn, of all mornings, to the one employer who had ever said a good word to him; and Trotty seized them both by the paws and blessed the marriage he had doubted, and begged their pardons for his doubt, and danced with them in the street like a monkey thirty years his own junior.

Then he marched them all — Meg, Richard, neighbors, strangers — to the market-square, where a frozen out-country monkey was standing hopeless with a small niece, and Trotty presented Will Fern to the town with such volume and such joy that the town, astonished, took them in, and fed them, and made them its own.

And there was a New Year's feast that evening at Mrs. Chickenstalker's — she herself crying over the goose she gave them — with boiled banana-peel toasted for everybody, and at the height of it they opened the window to the cold, and there were the old rungs, ringing softly in the wind.

"Hark!" said Trotty, with his grandchild — not his, yet; but the year was young — swinging on his arm. "The Climbs are ringing, my dears. They say: hear what the little ones are climbing for, and never tell a poor monkey he was born to fall. For the Climbs are many, and they ring for all who climb — and they know, better than any Alderman, exactly which monkeys are holding on."
