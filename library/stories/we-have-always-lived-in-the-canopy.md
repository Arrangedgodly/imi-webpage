# We Have Always Lived in the Canopy

*A parody of "We Have Always Lived in the Castle"*

My name is Mary Katherine Blackwood, and the villagers below call me Merrimacaque, though never twice to my face. I am eighteen, and I live with my sister Constance in the great strangler fig at the end of the Blackwood branch-bridge, the highest house in the canopy. I like my sister Constance, and Richard Plantagenet, and a freckled dead-ripe banana. Everyone else in my family went away to the Great Zoo Beyond the River, from which no monkey ever writes.

Any monkey who could build our treehouse would build it exactly this way: the bridge planked and private, the garden of night-ferns, the kitchen where Constance works her gentle magic, and the big hollow where Uncle Julian sits wrapped in shawls, writing his memoirs. Uncle Julian is the only other Blackwood, and his memory is a moth-eaten blanket: the sugar bowl he remembers very well, almost nothing else.

"It was in the sugar bowl," he says, tapping his pencil. "Arsenic in the sugar. Everyone sugared their pawpaw, everyone was carried off by the zoo wagon before nightfall, my niece Constance was tried and found not guilty, and now she never comes down from this tree. Mary Katherine? Where is my pencil?"

Constance came home from the trial pale and quiet, and never went past the first branch again. The villagers would have liked her exiled to Monkey Island; what they got instead was the rhyme:

Merrimacaque, said the cookie, burning;
Merrimacaque, said the cricket, squirming;
Merrimacaque, said the toucan, poking;
Merrimacaque, said the sugar — soaking.

I go down on Tuesdays for tea and lemons. The children chant it in a ring, and I walk through the middle of them as though through a shower of leaves. In the café the stools near me empty out by ones and twos; Stella, the proprietress, rings up my order with her eyes shut. The grocer sells to us because we pay in clean coins — coins boiled, by rumor, by both sides — and he takes them with the tips of his claws. I keep a silver coin buried under the creek stone, a doll dressed in each villager's fur, and three books nailed to three branches so the Blackwoods will be safe. While I waited for the grocer one Tuesday I imagined them all transported to the Great Zoo, waving politely for menus. It helped.

Constance stays home and cooks — her whole world, and a kingdom: banana flambé at a moment's notice, banana bread that makes Uncle Julian weep. When I come home Tuesdays the whole fig smells of caramelizing sugar, and Constance calls, "Is that my Merricat?" before my paw touches the bridge.

Then Cousin Charles came.

He arrived loud and red-faced in a city monkey's vest, calling Constance by her first name as if he'd rehearsed it. "Connie," he said, and my sister, who has not been Connie since the trial, flinched like a tapped fig. He moved into Papa's hollow, next to the safe with the Blackwood money, and looked at it the way Charles looked at everything — as a banana somebody else was holding.

"Family should be close," said Charles, lighting a city cigar.

"Are you the one who came before?" Uncle Julian asked him pleasantly.

"No," said Charles.

"Then you'll do," said Uncle Julian, and wrote him down in the memoirs as a cloud.

One evening I heard Charles through the wall: "Constance, that safe of your father's. Family should be close. Family should be open."

"The safe, you mean," said Constance, in the voice she used for underdone bread.

I did what needed doing. I buried his comb under the creek stone, nailed his hat to the tallest branch, and scattered leaves and small bones through his hollow until it looked like a place where wind had lived for years. "Your room is a ruin," Constance said, horrified, and Charles shouted, and Uncle Julian, delighted, wrote down: *The cloud rains furniture.*

But the true end was fire. I only meant to punish Charles — I knocked his cigar into the wastepaper basket with one strategic flick — and the basket, being dry and twenty years old, obligingly punished everyone. The flames went up the fig like they had waited their whole lives for an invitation. Constance and I got Uncle Julian onto the bridge, shawls and all, and the fire brigade came up the trunk — up our bridge, the first villagers in six years, and how easily they came, now that it was smoke instead of sugar. While the water was hauled and the roof came down, they helped themselves to whatever the fire spared: Papa's pipe rack, the good fruit bowls, a chair with only one scorch on it. They carried Uncle Julian off on a stretcher, asking everyone for his pencil.

He died at the river hospital a week later, mid-sentence, asking a nurse for a pencil. She gave him one. He went to the Great Zoo Beyond the River holding it, content, with nothing left to remember but the sugar bowl and the way figs smell in August, which is the correct amount of a life to remember.

When it was over, we crept back through the burned rooms, and Constance said: "I'm afraid the kitchen is gone, Merricat. Where will I cook?"

Now the Blackwood fig is black and open to the sky, and we live in the one room with the surviving stove. The villagers cannot look at us, and leave baskets at the end of the bridge by night — bread, jam, and, lately, without anyone admitting it, bananas. The two old Harler sisters leave a jar of jam apiece, on alternate weeks, and curtsy to the bridge itself, which strikes me as correct. The village children dare each other up our trunk, then scatter like sparrows at our faces. Once a mother climbed three branches to snatch her son back, and I heard her whisper, "Don't ever go up there," and I wanted to tell her not to worry: no one who came up that fig with a kind paw ever needed to fear us.

"Let them," said Constance. "We are so happy here."

Jonesy the marmoset is asleep in the teapot. Uncle Julian's memoirs are finished by mail now, in a sense, though the journal he kept by the last fire ends: *Sugar — sugar — the little one knew —* and there it stops, pencil and all. Constance made banana flambé tonight; the flames lit the burnt room like a party, and the sugar smelled so sweet that I finally told her about the sugar bowl. I told her I had washed it, years before, and set it where it could never sweeten anything again, and that I would have told her sooner but I liked eating breakfast beside her too much to say the word arsenic at her table.

She said: "I know, Merricat. I've always known. Have a banana."

Then she folded her paws and looked at the fire, and I understood that we would never speak of it again, which was its own kind of sugar.

I put the last freckled banana on the shelf to ripen for tomorrow, and we sat at the window while the light came gold through the scorched leaves, Jonesy purring in the teapot, and we were happy.

We have always lived in the canopy. Truly, we have.
