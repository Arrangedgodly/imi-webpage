# Eleanor Orangutan Is Completely Fine

*A parody of "Eleanor Oliphant Is Completely Fine"*

Eleanor Orangutan is completely fine.

She has her routine, you see. Monday to Friday she takes the 7:19 to her job in the design den of a fruit-label printer in Glasgow, where she draws bunches of grapes — the labels said grapes, never mind that no monkey in the city had eaten a grape since the borders closed — and she wears the same coat over the same fur, which is orange, but then everything about her is orange. There is also the matter of the pale seam running through the fur of her cheek, which she does not discuss, as she does not discuss any of it. Volatile orangutan, one of the lads at work called her once. She'd had to look up *volatile* at lunch, and then *orangutan*, which she learned was not Latin for *orange ape* but Malay for *person of the forest*, which she found she could not stop thinking about.

She buys two frozen banana pizzas on her way home and eats them both alone in her flat, listening to the couple above grooming. On Wednesdays she has her phone call with Mummy.

"Eleanor, my darling girl. Are you being a good little monkey?"

"Yes, Mummy."

"You're a fool, Eleanor. You're a disgrace. No monkey will ever love you. Only Mummy loves you. Only Mummy."

Eleanor is completely fine.

The design den had its seasons. In spring, the sales monkeys demanded grapes that looked "aspirational"; in autumn, grapes that looked "trustworthy." Eleanor drew the same eleven grapes either way, because grapes, she pointed out, do not have ambitions, and was told she did not understand the brief. She took to reading the dictionary at lunch instead, from A upward, which is why she knew, when it mattered, the words for almost everything except what was the matter.

It was Wednesday, the call still ringing in her ears, when the computer in the design den died, and they sent up the IT monkey, whose name was Raymond and who was a bonobo with terrible posture and a kind, unhurried way of typing with two fingers. "Bad case of monkey business in the hard drive," he said, and Eleanor said, "That seems unlikely, as monkeys are not native to hard drives," and he laughed, really laughed, as though she had said it on purpose, and it took her the rest of the afternoon to realize that she had.

On the walk home they came across an old monkey lying on the pavement, fallen, his shopping spilled, his little hat rolled to the gutter. Eleanor would have stepped over him — other monkeys' business was other monkeys' business — but Raymond had already crouched down, holding the old monkey's hand and telling him a joke while they waited for the ambulance. "Come on," Raymond said, looking up. "Give us your coat for his head, it's freezing." And Eleanor found she had knelt in the cold in her orange fur, and stayed.

The old monkey was Sammy, and once they had saved his life they were, apparently, obliged to visit him in hospital every Sunday. Eleanor objected to this on procedural grounds and turned up anyway, with grapes, having finally found some. Sammy had a shaggy old dog called Ben, who adopted Eleanor on the second visit and slept against her leg every Sunday after, and Sammy called her "hen," which nobody had ever called her, and told stories about the shipyards that were, on inspection, almost entirely about tea. Raymond's two kits came too sometimes, and called her Big Orange, and meant it kindly. Eleanor noticed she was starting to look forward to Sundays the way you look forward to a fig after a long climb, and she did not know what to file that under.

Then she saw the musician. Johnnie Howler, singing at the back of a pub with his band, Rhesus Pieces, sad-eyed with black fur, and Eleanor went home and thought: *him.* She spent her savings on a full grooming — de-loused, pomaded, her ragged fringe shaped into an actual hairstyle — and a red coat, and waited outside the venue in the rain. When the tour van came out, Johnnie Howler looked through her entirely, the way you look through weather. She went home, drank the fermented sap she kept for emergencies, and did not go to work, and was not fine, completely or otherwise.

It was Raymond who came and found her, and cleaned up the flat, and never once said anything about the state of it, or her.

"You're my friend," Eleanor said at last, trying the word like a strange fruit.

"Aye," said Raymond. "Obviously. Thick as thieves, us."

The next Sunday at Sammy's bedside, Raymond proposed the pub quiz, and Eleanor objected on procedural grounds and came anyway. Her head, it turned out, was a trophy cabinet: capital cities, flag fruits, the collective nouns for everything. When the quizmaster asked what one calls a gathering of orangutans, Eleanor said, "There is no standard term, as they are solitary, which is often misread as rudeness," and the Blue Room had its first winning round in living memory, and a stranger high-fived her, which she had seen done in films and had not previously known to occur in life.

He took her, over her procedural objections, to see Dr. Capuchin, a small gray therapist with whom Eleanor initially conducted negotiations like an arms treaty. It was Dr. Capuchin who made her say things out loud, one Wednesday at a time. That the seam on her cheek was a burn, from a fire, when Eleanor was small. That Mummy had been in the smoke with her cigarette and her laughter, and that little Marianne, Eleanor's sister, had not got out. That Mummy had been gone for years — that the Wednesday calls had been going into the dark, to nobody, or worse, to the nothing that Mummy had left behind in Eleanor's head, which kept the lines open all by itself. "You are not obliged," said Dr. Capuchin, "to believe everything a dead voice tells you." Eleanor wrote that on a leaf and taped it to the fridge, next to a drawing of Ben the dog.

The last Wednesday, she dialed the number one more time, and let it ring into the dark, and said, "Goodbye, Mummy," out loud. The line, of course, said nothing back. "Exactly," said Eleanor, and put the phone in the drawer. Then she ate a frozen banana pizza at her table — at the table, with a plate, which counts — and slept until morning without dreaming once.

That was the year everything was said out loud. She swims now, before work, at the pond with the other early orangutans. She has let Raymond teach her to sit still while the vet checks her annually, and to eat at a table, and to cry at films about other orangutans in other forests. When Sammy came home from hospital she walks Ben on Saturdays and lets Sammy win at cards, and last winter she went to the community ceilidh in the church hall, where Raymond's kits dragged her onto the floor and she danced badly and on purpose, orange and enormous under the lights, a person of the forest after all.

This morning she caught the 7:19 and a young monkey gave up his seat, and Eleanor looked at her reflection in the dark window going by: orange, ungainly, enormous, face honest and eyes clear, the seam on her cheek catching the light like a river on a map.

Eleanor Orangutan is completely fine. She is, actually, at last.
