# Where the Red Fur Grows

*A parody of "Where the Red Fern Grows"*

I was a young spider monkey of ten when the wanting first came on me — the wanting for treers. Not trained treers, but pups I could raise myself, from first wobble to first tree. I wanted two of them, red-furred and rough-coated, so bad I ached way down in my chest, the way you ache for a 'nana you can see but not reach.

"Pups cost," said Papa, which was all Papa ever said about it.

Mama said I'd lose interest. Grandpa, who ran the store at Tallyhoochee and believed in wanting as a way of life, said different. "The wanting," he said, "is the marrow of a young monkey. Feed it."

So I fed it. For two years I picked the neighbors' fleas at a copper apiece, hauled fruit baskets to the ferry landing, dug the smokehouse pit every moon, and dropped every copper in an old gourd-bank. Twenty-five dollars is a heap of fleas.

The day Grandpa's bazaar catalog came through, he circled the litter himself and sent the money order off in his own hand. I didn't sleep a wink for a week. Then word came the pups were at the Tallyhoochee depot, and I walked there and back before moonrise, thirty miles, with two warm bundles in a sack against my chest, whispering to them the whole way so they'd know my voice.

At the ferry landing there was an old beech, and cut deep in its bark were two names: DAN and ANN. I knew right off, the way you know a thing that has already been true a while: Old Dan and Little Ann.

From the first week, they were different, and I trained them different. I dragged a dried coonskin around the yard till my arms shook. Old Dan would sit at the foot of a sapling and sing up it till the leaves came off, whether anything was up there or not — you could set your evening by him. Little Ann wouldn't sing till she was sure. She'd circle, and nose, and think, and then sing once, soft, like a question she already knew the answer to.

Dan had grit. Give him a treed ringtail in the dark, and Dan sat at the foot of the tree and howled up the trunk till the bark peeled — would not quit, would not come home, not while that coon was up that tree. And Ann had brains. The old ringtails tried every trick in the wood on us. The water trick: coon swims downstream and comes out on the far bank smelling of nothing. Ann went into the creek after him with only her nose out, floating down like a leaf with a mission, and treed him two bends later. The fence-post trick, where a coon tucks himself into a hollow post and lets the world howl itself hoarse: Ann found him in ten minutes flat and then sat down and looked at Dan until he understood it was a hole, not a tree, and the shame of it improved him for life.

Between Dan's never-say-die and Ann's know-how, our little troop ate high on the fig branch, and Papa started saying my name proud at the fire, and Mama stopped saying I'd lose interest and started saving coon fat for the lamp.

Word got around, the way it does, and that was how we met the Pritchimp boys. Reuben and Rainie, from Riverbottom, mean as wet cats and twice as smug. They hung around Grandpa's store of a Saturday, and one of them — Reuben, the big one — heard about our dogs and laughed at them, and then bet us two silver coins that our pups could never tree the ghost coon of Riverbottom.

"There ain't no such coon," I said.

"There's a coon," said Rainie, "old as the river, smart as sin, and treed by nobody. He's been running Riverbottom since before your mama was a girl."

Well, we went. That night the wind smelled of storm, and the ghost coon led us through every blackberry thicket in the bottoms, doubled back on us twice, swam the slough, and finally went up the big dead sycamore like he was tired of the game. Dan sang him up it. Ann sat quiet at the roots, minding the trunk. And when I climbed up there with my blowpipe, I found the hollow empty and an old grey face watching me from the next tree over, amused.

I came down slow.

"Well?" said Reuben, ugly with winning. "Where is he?"

"Treed him," I said. "Then let him be. A coon that smart has earned his timber."

Reuben called me yellow, and swung his coon-hook at the sycamore to scare the old boy out, and I want to say the branch held. It didn't. It cracked like a rifle and dropped him straight into the slough, coon-hook, boots, and pride, and it took me and Rainie both to haul him out by his collar. He never thanked us. But he paid the two coins, and he never laughed at my pups again, and from that night on the old ghost coon ran free in Riverbottom, which was a bargain I'd make again.

The Troop Championship at Tallyhoochee was three nights of ringtail hunting and a little silver cup. Twenty-five teams, a judge at every corner, and Grandpa so puffed up I feared for him. Dan and Ann treed five the first night and four the second, and I sat between my two red monkeys in the firelight with my chest about to burst.

The last night came in with a wind that smelled of the end of the world. The other teams pulled out; our judge nearly called it; and in the middle of that black squall, Dan bayed and Ann answered from two trees apart, and the two of them ran a ringtail ragged through the rain and treed him at midnight, and held him treed, singing into the storm like they were the only two things in the world not afraid of it. When they handed me the silver cup and the gold, Grandpa cried into his beard, and Mama's kiss tasted of woodsmoke, and Dan and Ann ate figs out of the trophy itself.

It was on the way home from the championship that the mountain lion came.

He came out of the rocks for me, tawny and rolling, and I want to say I didn't scream, but I did. Before he reached me, two red shapes went past me like thrown coals. Old Dan hit him in the face. Little Ann hit his hamstring. There was a whirl of screaming fur that I jumped into swinging my coonskin jacket, and then it was over, the big cat crashing off downhill, and Dan stood swaying.

Something inside him had come loose. I carried him home, four miles of it, and he made it to the yard. He made it almost to dark.

We buried Old Dan on the hill above the house, under a little cedar, and I cut the turf for it myself, and I am not ashamed that my parents let me, because grief is a tree you have to let a young monkey climb himself.

And Little Ann stopped eating. Nobody could shame her out of it, and nobody tried very hard. She slipped away to the hill one evening and lay down on the fresh mound and would not get up, and on the seventh morning she was still, with her nose pointed where his shoulder would be. We laid her beside him.

That is a true and hard thing, and I would not write it down if it hadn't made what came after. For come spring, when I climbed the hill, I stopped dead. Over both mounds had grown a patch of the reddest fur I ever saw — soft as a mother's shoulder, rippling when the wind came up the valley.

Mama said it was the old tale. Only an angel monkey child can plant the red fur, and it grows only where something brave has slept. Where the red fur grows, the ground is sacred, and it never fades.

The championship gold moved us to town that fall, where the young ones could go to school. From the ferry I looked back once. On the hill the spot shone like a patch of evening that refused to set.

I am an old monkey now, and my grandchildren think my stories grow like jungle vines. But I believe it still: that up on that hill the red fur is growing on, red as Old Dan's back, soft as Little Ann's ear. And where the red fur grows, the ground is sacred.
