# Canopy Row

*A parody of "Cannery Row"*

Canopy Row in the Great Central Grove of the jungle is a long swaying shelf of boughs, a hum, a coo, a hanging cluster of lives. It is the ripe banana and the rotten spot, the hidden still and the morning fish-cart, the grooming parlor and the scientific frog — all swinging on the same three fig-limbs over the same bright river.

At the row's end stood Li Cheek's Banana & Grain. Li Cheek sold bananas, mealworms, wine of the fermented sort, and nearly anything that could fall off a cart — which was, in his philosophy, the natural condition of goods. His face did not move when he made a profit, and his face did not move when he made a loss, and this is as near to wisdom as commerce ever climbs.

Two boughs down, Doc ran Western Junglogical Supply: shelves of glow-beetles, tanks of sea-monkeys, a microscope, a shelf of poetry, and a Steinway of patience. Doc was a bonobo of enormous kindness, and it was said of him — by Mack, usually — that he was probably a credit to the entire order of primates.

Mack and the boys lived at the Palace Treehouse-Flophouse, which Mack had acquired from Li Cheek in exchange for a debt involving a truck of figs that had gone, in the night, entirely bad. Mack and the boys were widely suspected of monkey business, and correctly. "We will watch your store," Mack had explained at the signing, "like nine little watchdogs." Li Cheek's face did not move.

The population of the Row was completed by Madame Bear-Flag, who ran the grooming parlor of that name with an iron paw and a heart of the same material, softened only by hard luck stories and the laws of supply and demand; by William, her bartender, who yearned to better himself and asked monthly for a position upstairs, and was monthly told that he was too sensitive for the work, which was true, and a great relief to the customers; by Henri, a painter of no paintings who was building a boat in the tree-lot — a boat that had been nearly finished for nine years, since Henri had discovered that a finished boat is only a boat, while an unfinished one is a vocation; and by Frankie, a young chimp of no fixed talent, who hung about the laboratory because Doc had once said "hand me that probe," and Frankie had handed it, and for one shining moment had been of use. He had failed at everything since, but he had that.

Now it happened that Mack and the boys owed Doc more kindness than they could conveniently carry. Doc paid a fat banana-bite apiece for live tree-frogs, which he needed for his work, whatever his work was; nobody asked, because Doc's work was Doc's work. And Mack said: "Boys, we will catch Doc his frogs and throw him a birthday party. It is the least we can do, and I have checked, and it is."

They went up the river-valley in the borrowed truck, the whole crew, and it was a great morning. The frogs sat in the shallows like green opinions, and the boys waded in with sacks, and there was much falling over and loud advice, and by noon they had four hundred frogs and Hughie had a leech in a place leeches should not go. On the way home the truck boiled over, and they celebrated the frogs' capture beside the road with a jug of Li Cheek's finest, and by the time they rolled back onto the Row, the frogs numbered roughly two hundred and the party had already, in a sense, begun.

While Doc was away up the coast collecting sea-monkeys, they decorated the lab. Henri painted a mural of battle on the far wall. Madame Bear-Flag sent her grooming girls early, out of respect. The punch was sweet palm-wine in Doc's own specimen bowls.

It must be said that Doc, alone up the coast in the tide-pools, was having his own kind of party. He went out at low water into the great beds of eel-grass, where the little sea-things hunted each other with perfect appetite, and he looked up once at the shore, where the jungle hung over the water in its glassy morning, gorgeous, cruel, and utterly uninterested in anybody's birthday; and Doc filled his jars, and was quietly, scientifically glad, and drove home humming.

The party began gay and ended zoological. Somebody fed sugared grubs to the blowflies and they spun like planets. A honey badger came in through the skylight and had, in quick succession, the insect collection, the guest of honor's tail, and the fish. Frankie, who had brought a present he could not afford and then dropped it down the stair, stood in a corner and cried quietly, the only sober and honest primate present. Mack ended the evening hanging upside down from the lamp-bough, apologizing beautifully to a coat. It was, all agreed, more fun than a barrel of monkeys, which in Canopy Row is a standard unit of measurement.

Doc stood in the doorway a long time.

"Look at my floor," he said, which was worse than shouting.

"Now, Doc," Mack began.

"Look at my floor. Look at my beetles. Look at my fish, Mack. That fish was three years of early mornings." Doc took off his spectacles. "Get out, before I remember I'm a pacifist by conviction and a bonobo by breeding."

The boys went. But the jungle has its own etiquette, and it is possible to make amends — not by apologizing, which no Row monkey could survive, but by replacing, quietly, at the source. Over the following weeks, frogs began appearing in the lab tank in ones and twos, with no note. Henri repainted the mural into a landscape. Frankie brought a new glass bowl, mended with gum, and put it down and fled before anyone could thank him and ruin it. And Mack, who felt worse than he let on, let on.

The second party was thrown at the proper time, which is to say on Doc's birthday, with invitations engraved on banana leaves and delivered by marmosets of good standing. Li Cheek sent wine and a card that read, in effect, nothing. Madame Bear-Flag sent her girls early, out of respect; William came along behind them, out of hope, and was seated near the door where he could see everything and be ruined by none of it; and the Old Gibbon-Mandarin who walked the Row at dusk, and whom nobody ever dared to greet, passed by in the first dark, and that was accounted luck.

This time nobody fed the blowflies. The honey badger was invited, as a guest, and behaved with dignity, having been asked at last to something. The toasts were long and mostly true. Frankie, standing by the doorway, was handed a bowl by Doc himself, and drank from it, and did not drop it.

Late, when the punch was low, Doc read them the old poem of the Black Banana-Blossoms of Old Ind — how even the blossoms must fall and blacken and be done, and how nobody ever explains to the blossom, and the blossom does not ask. The lamp hissed. A girl from the Bear-Flag wiped her eyes with a napkin-leaf and then checked, professionally, that no one had seen her do it for free.

"What's good about it?" said Hazel the friend, meaning the poem, meaning the Row, meaning everything. "Nobody gets what they want."

"That's not it at all," said Doc gently. "It's that everybody's looking. The search itself is the thing. That's what the blossom does too, if you watch long enough."

"That Doc," said Mack, wiping his eyes with somebody else's tail. "He is one hell of a fellow."

Which was, on the whole, the truth.

Only the gopher, in his perfect burrow under the empty bough, waited for a wife who never came. He had surveyed the ground, provisioned the larder, dug a home so ideal that no lady gopher could refuse him — and no lady gopher ever came. He came out at evening and looked both ways. But that is the jungle too.
