# Howler's Moving Castle

*A parody of "Howl's Moving Castle"*

In the town of Market Chomping, Sophie Hatter was the eldest of three chimp sisters, which meant she was doomed. Everyone knows the eldest fails first and hardest. So Sophie stayed in the hat shop, trimming peel-caps and telling her hopes to the hats, because the hats never went anywhere either.

"Howler's banyan stepped on the lavender again last night," said her sister Lettie, who had been apprenticed to the bakery. Her other sister, Martha, had been apprenticed to a witch — except Lettie and Martha had quietly swapped places, as sisters will, each wanting the other's life better than her own. Nobody noticed but Sophie, who noticed everything and said nothing, because saying things hadn't worked for her yet.

Howler was the district wizard, vain and howler monkey to the bone. They said he ate young monkeys' hearts, leaving nothing but an empty banana skin where the romance used to be. Sophie disapproved, and polished hats.

Then the Witch of the Wilt came in.

The Witch was grand and terrible, her fur the gray-green of fruit left in the sun, and she took offense at Sophie's tone.

"You should not have spoken to me of pride, hat girl," she said.

"It wasn't pride," said Sophie. "It was a hat."

"Then wear this instead," said the Witch, and laid the curse on her like a shawl: old age, ninety years of it, creaky as a rotten rope-bridge, with a seal on her tongue so she couldn't tell a soul.

An elderly gorilla cannot stay in a hat shop. Sophie marched out, and that night she saw it: a castle of living banyan, a hundred prop-roots stomping like an offended spider, lit like a party nobody had invited her to.

"It's not stealing if you're ninety," Sophie told herself, and let herself in.

She found a hearth with a fire burning in it — and the fire had fur.

"I'm Calcifur," said the demon, a heart of orange wrapped in smoke, crosswise about it. "Fire demon, chained to this kettle by a bargain I can't say aloud. You're old. Old ones bargain. Break my contract and I'll break your curse. Shake on it?"

"Shake," said Sophie.

So Granny Sophie became the banyan's cleaning lady. There was Micha, a cheerful young spider monkey who fed Calcifur wood and failed his exams. There was a door with a dial that opened onto four different jungles. ("Which way round is the dial today?" "Positively." "Thank you.")

And there was Howler at breakfast, exquisite and wilted, draped over a chair like a fur coat with a grudge.

"I feel revolting," Howler announced, sinking.

"That's the Wilt," said Sophie, who had read the slime on the bathroom ceiling like a warning label. "There is no call for that much green in a bathroom."

"I never lie," said Howler, nose in the milk jug. "I merely arrange the truth for display. There is no point in living if I can't be beautiful, so I am going to my room to pine."

"Pine quietly," said Sophie. "The floor's wet."

It became, to her horror, a household. She talked to the hats she made to sell, and things she talked to began, slightly, to come alive — which was, though nobody had told her, because talking things into being was her own magic, and the Witch's little curse hadn't given her anything except the freedom to use it. A scarecrow of old banana rind, called Rind-head, took to hopping after her on its turnip legs. She talked to that, too.

A royal summons came, and Howler turned three shades of pale and refused to open it, because the King wanted him to hunt the Witch of the Wilt, and Howler's plans for that were all named "flee." So Sophie went to the palace instead, pretending to be Howler's aged mother, and lied to a king for a solid hour with such enjoyment that she frightened herself.

"Howler is a slitherer-outer," she told the King's council, "but his heart is in the right place, which is more than the Wilt can say." She also visited the old herb-woman Madam Penta-stem, who had been Howler's teacher, and who peered at Sophie over her spectacles and said, "You're a witch, dear. You've been enchanting things your whole life. Do keep at it." The Wilt took Penta-stem in her garden that same autumn; the old witch went with her secateurs swinging, and Howler wore black velvet about it for a month, which in Howler was grief of the deepest dye.

For the Witch of the Wilt was hunting Howler, and she had already caught Sullipean, the royal wizard sent against her, gardening himself out of a flowerbed in the Wilt. When she stormed the banyan and seized Calcifur in a fist of wrong fire, it was Granny Sophie, ninety and furious, who stood up under her.

"You've no power over me," said Sophie. "I've already had my worst day, my girl, and I swept up after it. And you've no idea what this demon is to this house, because you've never once kept anything warm."

"You don't know what he is," said the Witch.

"I know what a bargain is," said Sophie, "and I know this house." She reached into the fire and took out the small, beating thing Howler had bargained away as a young monkey — his heart — and gave it back to him, and Calcifur's chains fell off like old bandages, and the Witch of the Wilt, with nothing left to grab, came apart like a green morning burning off.

Howler sat up straighter than he had in years, heart in his two hands.

"A heart," said Calcifur, from the grate, in a voice suddenly ten feet tall, "is a heavy burden. I should know. I carried his, and I'm only small, and I was a falling star besides. He caught me in a jar when I came down and gave me a job. I've been warm ever since."

"I knew you were Sophie from the first evening," Howler admitted. "Your ears wiggle when you're cross. I'm a coward, you know. Vain, and slack, and I never trim my toenails."

"Yes," said Sophie — who was, unnoticed, no longer ninety — "but you're learning. Also, you're going to help Sullipean out of that flowerbed, and then you're going to repaint the bathroom."

They did dig Sullipean out, petunias and all. And Rind-head, who had followed Sophie through fire and flood, turned out to be a prince under all that rind — a kiss from an un-cursed girl unpeeled him — which explained his excellent posture. He went home to his kingdom offering Sophie a crown; she declined it on the grounds that she already had a household, a demon, and a hat shop, and a crown would only need dusting.

The banyan settled down between the hat shop and the garlic fields, prop-roots folded like a cat, because — as Howler said, pink-eared — it had simply never had anywhere it wanted to stay.

That night Calcifur, a free demon, chose to stay anyway, and burned high and gold, and cooked; and the hats in the window at Market Chomping seemed, to the customers, strangely wise about love.
