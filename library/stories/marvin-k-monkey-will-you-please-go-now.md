# Marvin K. Monkey Will You Please Go Now!

*A parody of "Marvin K. Mooney Will You Please Go Now!"*

The time had come. The time was now.

The Great Fig Feast was over. Every fig in the grand old fig tree had been eaten, flipped, juggled, mashed, discussed, and eaten again. The whole troop was moving on to the mango grove across the river. Everyone had packed their favorite sticks and said goodbye to their favorite branches.

Everyone except Marvin K. Monkey.

Marvin K. Monkey was hanging upside down in the best fig tree in the jungle, by one toe, nibbling the very last fig on the very last branch.

"Marvin," said Great-Uncle Gibbon, the oldest and wisest ape in the troop, quite nicely. "The time has come. The time is now. Marvin K. Monkey, will you please go now?"

"No," said Marvin. "I like it here."

Great-Uncle Gibbon counted to ten. Then he counted to ten bananas, which takes much longer.

"You can go on foot. You can go on feet. You can go on your hands, which is twice as neat."

"My feet are tired," said Marvin K. Monkey.

"Then you can go by vine! You can swing! You can soar! You can swing yourself right out the canopy door!"

"My vine is tired," said Marvin K. Monkey.

"Your vine is TIRED?"

"Very tired. It worked hard this year."

Great-Uncle Gibbon's eyebrows went up. "You can go by cow. You can take a cow now. A cow goes moo, and a cow knows how."

"The cow is tired," said Marvin.

"The cow is NOT tired," said Great-Uncle Gibbon. "The cow is FINE. You can go by Croc-o-Dial! He'll carry you a while! Just don't ask him to smile."

"The crocodile is tired."

"He is a crocodile! Crocodiles are NEVER tired! It is his whole JOB!"

By now half the troop had gathered below to watch, because there is nothing monkeys enjoy more than somebody else's argument. Cousin Colobus suggested a vine-rocket. The marmosets suggested going by flea, one at a time. And Great-Aunt Splay elbowed forward with her own ideas, which was unfortunate, because Great-Aunt Splay had many.

"You can go by balloon!" cried Great-Aunt Splay. "You can float to the grove like a small hairy moon!"

"I get balloon-sick," said Marvin K. Monkey.

"You can go by mail! We will stamp you and send you! You'll arrive with the letters, all bendy but well!"

"I do not fit in an envelope," said Marvin.

"You'd be surprised," said Great-Aunt Splay, who had once mailed herself to a wedding.

Great-Uncle Gibbon took a deep breath and smoothed his fur, which is what wise old apes do instead of shouting. "You can go on skis. Banana-peel skis. You can slide down the hill with the greatest of ease."

"Too slippery."

"You can go on a pogo stick. You can go on a log. You can go on a galloping hog. You can go in a Crunk-Crunk-Cart that runs on fig seeds and grumpiness. You can go on a Zumble-Zay! You could go TODAY! It's the swankiest way there is to go far. It's better than going by car. It's better than going by star."

"What's a star?" asked Marvin.

"Nobody knows," admitted Great-Uncle Gibbon. "But it's slower than a Zumble-Zay, and that's what matters."

"Are there figs where we're going?" asked Marvin K. Monkey.

"There are mangoes where we're going. Mangoes, mangoes, mangoes."

"Mangoes are just figs that gave up," said Marvin K. Monkey, and he went back to his nibbling.

The troop discussed this among themselves. "Rude," said one. "Accurate," said another, sadly, because everyone knew the mangoes were not as good.

Then Marvin began his delays. He had been saving them.

"First," he announced, "I must say goodbye to every branch." There were four hundred branches. He said goodbye to eleven of them and pronounced himself exhausted.

"I must fetch my good stick." His good stick was across the river, in the mango grove, where they were going. "Then it shall greet me there," said Great-Uncle Gibbon.

"I must wait for the right breeze."

"It is the same breeze," said Great-Uncle Gibbon. "It has been the same breeze since Tuesday."

"This one smells wrong."

Great-Uncle Gibbon finally lost his temper, which took him sixty years and four seconds.

"MARVIN K. MONKEY! I don't care HOW! Go by foot! Go by toe! Go by tail! Go by snail mail! Go by flute! Go by fruit! Go by goose! Go by caboose! GO GO GO! You can go on a bomm flubb, if only you'd go! Marvin K. Monkey, I don't care how. Marvin K. Monkey, will you please GO NOW?"

Marvin K. Monkey looked at the last fig. He looked at Great-Uncle Gibbon. He sighed the sigh of a monkey who knows when the figs are done.

"Fine," he said. "I'll go by cow."

And he got on the cow, and the cow said moo, and away they went, the whole troop following in a very fine parade — Colobus on the hog, Great-Aunt Splay in a wheelbarrow of her own devising, the marmosets in a conga line, and Great-Uncle Gibbon walking very slowly and very grandly at the back, the way generals do when they have won.

Halfway to the river they passed the Croc-o-Dial, who was lying in the shallows with his chin on a rock.

"Farewell, Croc-o-Dial," called Marvin from the cow. "I would have gone by you, but I'm told you're tired."

"I am never tired," said the Croc-o-Dial. "I am at rest. There is a difference, and the difference is that I could eat you at any time, but choose not to, as a courtesy."

"That's very kind," said Marvin.

"It is policy," said the Croc-o-Dial, and slid under the water to think about policy.

They had gone almost all the way to the river when a toucan came flapping up in a great state. Toucans are the jungle's telephones, and this one was ringing.

"BRRRING!" said the toucan. "Message for Great-Uncle Gibbon! BRRRING! The fig tree has ripened AGAIN! Young figs! Dozens! A whole second crop! BRRRING! Tell Marvin K. Monkey — he can come back now!"

Great-Uncle Gibbon closed his eyes. He counted to ten bananas. Then he turned the entire parade around.

"I didn't want to have to be that way to you, Marvin K. Monkey," he said. "I really didn't."

"That's all right," said Marvin K. Monkey, happily upside down on the cow. "There's no going back now."

"There is," said Great-Uncle Gibbon. "We are literally all going back right now. That is what is happening."

"I accept your apology," said Marvin.

"I have not apologized."

"The cow heard it," said Marvin. "Cows are honest."

And everyone went back and ate figs until midnight. Great-Aunt Splay mailed herself a congratulatory letter, from herself, to herself, care of the fig tree. The Croc-o-Dial declined to attend but sent a polite current. The marmosets made a poem about it, and recited it from the top of the tree with the new figs glowing behind them like little green lanterns:

*"The time will come, the time will be. The time is often now, you see. But figs are figs, and figs don't last — so going now is going fast."*

Marvin thought it the finest poem ever written, mostly because it had figs in it.

Great-Uncle Gibbon thought it was pretty good too, mostly because it was over. He kept a copy in his fur, and on hard days he read it and added a line of his own under his breath: *"And patience, dear nephew, is also a tree — it grows very slowly, but grow it must, like the fig, like the fig, like the fig — eventually."*

(The next year, when the figs were done, Marvin K. Monkey went immediately — by Croc-o-Dial, smiling all the while. The Croc-o-Dial did not smile back, of course. But those who know him say he came very close to something, and for a crocodile, that is practically a grin.)
