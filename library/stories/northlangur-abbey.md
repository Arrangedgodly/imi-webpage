# Northlangur Abbey

*A parody of "Northanger Abbey"*

No one who had ever seen Catherine Langur in her infancy would have supposed her born to be a heroine of romance. She was a plain little langur with a plain little coat, who preferred cricket to cultivation and rolling down hills to rolling out plots; her mother wished she could only be persuaded to wash her face, and her father, a clergyman-ape of very moderate fur, did not insist on her being accomplished. In short, she had nothing heroic about her but her appetite — and her appetite, as it turned out, was for horrid novels.

By seventeen she had consumed every dreadful shelf in the parlor: *The Midnight Grove*, *The Mysterious Hothouse*, and that unmatched masterpiece of terror, *The Gibbon's Ghost*, in which the wicked uncle imprisons a young female behind a waterfall for eleven chapters. Catherine emerged with her nerves perfectly primed and her expectations perfectly wrong, which is the ideal condition for visiting a spa town.

Her chance came when Mr. Allen, a good-humored old monkey, was ordered by his physician to take the waters at Lather for his gout, and Mrs. Allen invited Catherine along. Lather was the great grooming resort of the monkey world — pump rooms and nit-rooms, hot springs and hot gossip, promenades where every afternoon the whole troop sat combing one another and deciding who mattered.

At Lather, nothing happened, which Catherine found horrid in the wrong way. Then, in the lower rooms, she was discovered by a Miss Isabella Thorpe — a tall proboscis monkey with an inexhaustible nose for advantage, eldest of ten and a finished coquette besides. Isabella seized upon Catherine as a best friend with the speed of a frog's tongue.

"My dearest creature," breathed Isabella, "you have read *The Gibbon's Ghost*? I could not sleep for thinking of the waterfall. How faint the males of quality are in comparison! Real life has no waterfalls in it whatsoever."

While Isabella practiced her languishing, her brother John — a burly young proboscis who talked of nothing but his vine, a prodigy that could do eleven miles an hour when nobody was timing it — attached himself to Catherine with the air of a monkey doing her a favor worth several hundred 'nanas.

Rescue arrived in the shape of a clergyman's family: Miss Eleanor Tilney, a reserved, remarkably agreeable langur, and her brother Henry — a lively young monkey with rather remarkable eyes and a great talent for nonsense deployed with precision. Henry introduced himself by scolding Catherine's prose style in a make-believe manuscript of her own life: "Miss Langur has been asked to dance by a well-looking young ape, and has promised — this is an improvement! — but now, madam, describe the ball, the room, the fruit-cup, the partner's fur."

"I cannot," said Catherine, laughing. "I can only say I was pleased."

"Pleased! In a manuscript! Your publishers will riot. But no — see how I have misjudged you. A monkey, especially if she has the misfortune of knowing anything, should conceal it as well as she can. You know nothing at all, and are therefore the perfect heroine."

He danced with her, teased her, taught her that 'nanas, unlike beaux, are improved by spots — and Catherine, who had never met a mind that worked faster than a proboscis nose, was quietly, entirely gone.

Inevitably, the families collided: John Thorpe and James Morland, brothers-in-law-to-be by ambition, took a dislike to Henry Tilney in the pump room and reported him to Catherine as a driver of impertinent barouches and a subscriber to the French. It was all nonsense; but it introduced Catherine to Isabella's engagement to James, and to Isabella's instant, colossal dissatisfaction with it.

"A clergyman!" Isabella sighed to Catherine, with a look that meant the opposite of every word. "And no title at all. But what does it signify? Titles are nothing but fur-trim. I despise fur-trim." She then inquired, with studied carelessness, exactly how many fruit-trees stood on James's property.

Then came the summons every reader of horrid novels prays for: Eleanor invited Catherine to Northlangur Abbey, the family seat — an ancient, tumbledown abbey of hollow oaks and shut-up wings, presided over by General Tilney, a stately silverback of impeccable politeness and unnerving exactness. He asked Catherine the size of Mr. Allen's orchard with the tenderness of a dentist extracting a molar, and invited her north the moment her fortune-to-be appeared satisfactory.

Catherine, riding toward the abbey in the rain, saw it whole against the sky — gray boughs, gothic hollows, a torn shutter of bark over one high window — and her heart drummed with every page of *The Gibbon's Ghost*.

At the abbey she found a cabinet in her chamber, an old cedar cabinet too tall to see into, guarded by a spring-lock. It took her an hour of trembling and a stumbled candle to reach it. Inside lay — a discovery to freeze the blood — bundles of manuscripts! Manuscripts in an abandoned abbey! She snatched the topmost pages to the light and read, hair on end:

*Item, two pounds of 'nanas, bruised. Item, picked over by the laundry-maid. Item, ditto, ditto. Item, a comb, mended.*

It was a grooming inventory. She put it down, laughed till the bed shook, and felt, for one evening, almost cured of horrid expectations.

Almost. For the General's wife was dead — dead young, dead far from home in a distant grove — and the General never spoke of her, and forbade her old rooms to be entered, and Eleanor crossed the corridor past them with her eyes down. What, thought Catherine, savoring the puzzle like a green 'nana, could be simpler, darker, truer? He had wronged her. Perhaps worse. The abbey knew.

It was Henry who caught her at it, standing at his mother's door on the forbidden stair, and Henry who stopped her with the finest speech ever aimed at an over-read monkey:

"Miss Langur, if you can conjure up such atrocities in this quiet English canopy, remember the country and the age in which you live. We are a nation of farmers and fruit-grocers, governed by laws and habit, where the roads are patrolled and the mangos licensed. Deeds of horror cannot happen here. If you go on expecting a wicked silverback behind every hollow oak, you will meet only furniture. Consult your understanding, your sense of the probable — and then tell me, upon honor, whether your imagination has not been pampered into ridiculous alarm."

Catherine cried with mortification for the better part of a chapter. He was entirely right, and the abbey was only an old family tree with an old family's grief in it, and the only mystery in the house was a widower's cold heart. She went down to breakfast humbled — and two days later, without one word of explanation, the General turned her out of the house. Eleanor, weeping, put her in a chaise at seven in the morning with no companion, seventy miles of branch-road to her parents' farm, and the sudden conviction that she had outstayed her welcome by exactly one fortune.

The truth — which Catherine never learned for a month — was proboscis work. John Thorpe, finding Catherine not the heiress he'd invented, had told the General she was penniless as well as engaged to himself; and the General, having dangled her before his son like a fruit-crop, plucked her off when the crop proved rotten. Henry, hearing it, said what he thought of his father's behavior with great correctness and no obedience, and went home to Fullerton.

"He is absolutely off with Isabella," ran the letter that settled her. Isabella, having flirted Captain Frederick Tilney — Henry's brother — into appearance and out of intentions, had jilted James in everything but words, and now wrote Catherine breathless confidences begging her to "manage" her brother. Catherine finally understood her, wrote back with entire civility and entire frost, and mentioned the correspondence to Henry.

"Had I been in love with Isabella Thorpe," said Henry, watching Catherine fold the letter, "I should have been a very miserable monkey. But I am not." Pause. "The indispensable ingredient, I find, is liking somebody else instead."

Meanwhile Eleanor — the meek, shaded Eleanor — had married a young viscount of the velvetest marmosets, blazed into happiness and a barouche of her own; upon which the General discovered that the bridegroom's orchards were vast, his temper adored, and the marriage everything a prudent silverback could want. His humor improved with his prospects, exactly as humors do.

And so, with the General consenting for the sake of his own convenience, Henry came down to Fullerton, walked out with Catherine among the nut-trees, insulted her handwriting until she understood him, and asked her to be his prime mate.

I leave it to be settled by whomsoever it may concern, whether the tendency of this little tail be to recommend parental tyranny, or to reward filial disobedience — but I can assure the anxious reader that Catherine and Henry were married, the bells rang over All-Canopy church, Isabella's nose led her to London and a succession of ineligible males, and that at Northlangur Abbey, on quiet evenings, Catherine sometimes still hears a shutter knock in the wind, and smiles, and does not go to look.
