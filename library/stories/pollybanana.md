# Pollybanana

*A parody of "Pollyanna"*

The monkey who got off the river ferry wore a faded straw hat and carried all her belongings in a leaf-wrapped bundle. Her name was Pollybanana, and her fur was the exact yellow of a very ripe banana. Her father, a poor missionary monkey, had gone to live in the great fig tree in the sky, and so she was sent across the jungle to live with her Aunt Polly — a stern old mandrill with a face like a sunset, who lived in the finest fig grove on the hill and believed that Duty was a thing you did instead of fun.

Nancy, the young capuchin who did the chores, met her at the gate.

"Aunt Polly didn't send anybody to meet me?" asked Pollybanana.

"She didn't figure on you being a girl," said Nancy. "She wanted a boy to fetch and carry."

"Well," said Pollybanana brightly, "I can carry fetchingly. There's a thing my father taught me before he went to the big fig tree. It's a game. Would you like to know it?"

"There's no time for games here."

"This one takes no time at all," said Pollybanana. "The game is just this — to find something about everything to be glad about. No matter what 'tis. Once, the missionary barrel came and I'd asked for a doll, and inside were only crutches. I was awfully disappointed. Then my father said, 'Be glad — be glad you don't need them!' And I was. And that's the game."

"That," said Nancy, "is the queerest thing I ever heard."

But Nancy tried it. And it worked.

Aunt Polly, being Duty, installed Pollybanana in the little attic nest at the top of the grove — hot in the afternoons, plain as porridge, no curtains. Pollybanana was seen, that evening, dragging her trunk across the floor. "It's for the window," she explained to Nancy, when Nancy came up. "See? I've filled it with books, and now the window sill is covered with things to look at — and there!" — she flung it open — "there's the whole sky, and the river, and that tree with the light on it. There's such a lot of glad in a window, if you crowd it up right."

The game went with Pollybanana everywhere. Old Tom, the grumpy orangutan who lived in a dark den and hadn't smiled since the fig blight, got it next.

"I've been sorry for you a whole week," Pollybanana announced, "because I decided I was glad about you."

"Glad about me?" Old Tom thundered. "Why?"

"Because you have such a comfortable grump," said Pollybanana. "It must save you ever so much smiling." And she swung away before he could answer — though Nancy, watching, swore she saw the corner of his mouth twitch.

Mrs. Rain got it too — a monkey so fond of being ill that she planned her own memorial weekly. Pollybanana sat by her nest, praised her boiled banana-flower soup, and turned every groan around until Mrs. Rain, to her own astonishment, laughed. (Mrs. Rain afterward maintained she had been glad the soup was burnt, because if it hadn't been, she'd never have tasted how good the good soup was going to be next time.)

Then there was John Pendleton — the rich, fierce old silverback who lived alone in the great stone ficus behind the wall, and was rumored to keep his heart in a locked box under the fifth floorboard. Pollybanana got into his grove by accident (having discovered, in a way she could not be talked out of, that the wall was only there to be looked over), and Mr. Pendleton broke his leg trying to catch her before she fell into his lily pool. Pollybanana felt responsible for the leg and came daily to cheer it, and found that leg or no leg, nobody could stop a glad game once it got into a house.

"You shouldn't come here," the old silverback growled. "This is a den of a monkey who hates other monkeys. Do you know what's the matter with me, child? Years ago I wanted something I couldn't have, and I have hated the whole jungle ever since." He looked at her — looked a long time. "You favor her," he said abruptly. "Your mother. Whom I wanted, monkey that I was, before she chose your father. There's a rift in the clouds, child — if you look for it."

"I'll look," said Pollybanana, greatly solemn. "Rifts are my very favorite kind of hole."

She introduced him to Jimmy Bean, an orphan macaque nobody would take because he was "all hands and appetite," and Mr. Pendleton said no, and no, and then one day the whole jungle learned that the great stone ficus had a boy in it — and that the old silverback had sent for a swing to be strung from the high fig, and laughed so loud the parrots left for a week.

Then came the day Pollybanana fell.

She had been swinging the high line over the ravine to fetch a stray fruit, and the branch — old and treacherous — cracked under her weight. The troop vet bound her legs and said, quietly, that she might never brachiate again. Never swing.

Aunt Polly's grand grove grew very quiet. Pollybanana lay in her nest and did not play the game.

"I can't think of anything to be glad about," she whispered. "I've looked and looked. There isn't anything. If it was just about the walking, I could maybe. But never swinging — never swinging — and the game has gone clear out of me."

"It hasn't," said Nancy fiercely. "It's gone out of every monkey in this jungle, is what it's done, and we're putting it back." For word travels fast in a jungle, and the jungle had learned her game. Notes came, chewed into leaf-baskets, dropped down the chute beside her nest: We is glad it wasn't the ravine floor. We is glad you swung so high first. Mrs. Rain wrote that she was glad — actually, sincerely glad — for one whole day, about somebody else's legs. Jimmy Bean wrote: Mr. Pendleton says you made a den into a house, and the house is glad, and so is the old grump, which is me. Old Tom wrote only one line: I am glad you swung by. And at the bottom, in Aunt Polly's stiffest, most careful scratching: I am glad I kept you, child.

Pollybanana read it four times. Then, very slowly, she began to smile.

"I'm glad about the notes," she said. "I'm glad I have a whole troop to be glad about me. And — oh, Aunt Polly — in a way I'm almost glad I fell. I never knew, before, how many friends I had."

There was one more visitor — Dr. Chilton, whom Aunt Polly had not spoken to in twenty years, over some quarrel so old that neither could now say whose tail had been trodden on first. He came to look at her legs, and stayed to argue with the troop vet, and got Aunt Polly to carry the nest down the mountain and take Pollybanana to the city doctors, because there was a chance, he said, that never-swing could be mended. It took the two of them, at last, swinging side by side to carry the stretcher — and it took a quarrel twenty years long, Pollybanana observed, to make up properly; short quarrels never had the swing in them.

After that she got better, one branch at a time — first sitting up, then walking, then clutching a bough, and one shining morning, swinging — crookedly, wobbling, laughing — across the old fig tree while the whole troop went absolutely bananas below, and John Pendleton stood at the wall with Jimmy Bean on his shoulders, looking for the rift in the clouds and finding it.

Aunt Polly, who had declared the game nonsense from the first week, was seen teaching it, stiffly, to the visiting mandrills.

"For Duty," she explained, "is easier with gladness in it."

And if you should ever wander through that jungle, you will hear it still — the glad game, swinging from tree to tree, more fun than a barrel of monkeys, and twice as yellow.
