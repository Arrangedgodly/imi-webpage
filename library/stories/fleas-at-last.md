# Fleas at Last

*A parody of "Peace at Last"*

It was late, and the moon hung over the great fig tree like a lamp left on by mistake. Mr. Bearangutan — an enormous, shaggy, extremely tired orangutan — climbed into the big nest bed beside Mrs. Bearangutan, folded his hands on his belly, closed his eyes, and prepared to sleep.

Mrs. Bearangutan began to snore.

"GRAAAHHH — SHNORRRK — GRAAAHHH — SHNORRRK,"

went Mrs. Bearangutan, like a tree falling down in instalments.

Baby Bearangutan woke up, bounced across the mattress, and landed on Mr. Bearangutan's stomach with both feet.

"Play," announced Baby Bearangutan.

"It is sleepy time," rumbled Mr. Bearangutan. "Night-nights. Lie down and close your eyes."

"PLAY," said Baby Bearangutan, standing on his father's head.

"I will play the sleepy game," said Mr. Bearangutan, with his eyes shut. "The sleepy game is where everybody is quiet and still, and the winner is whoever is best at it."

"I WIN AT GAMES," roared Baby Bearangutan, and bounced twice, and climbed to the very top of the nest, and leapt — wheee — square onto Mr. Bearangutan's belly, which went OOF.

"Again," said Baby Bearangutan.

"Sleepy game," croaked Mr. Bearangutan, from somewhere near his own ribs.

And — nibble nibble — a flea on Mr. Bearangutan's shoulder woke up too, and began its night's work.

"Oh NO," said Mr. Bearangutan. "I can't stand THIS."

So Mr. Bearangutan got out of the big nest bed, carried Baby Bearangutan back to the little nest bed, tucked him in twice, and went to sleep in the big branch chair in the living room instead.

TICK-TOCK. TICK-TOCK.

Went the cocoa-nut clock. It had a little wooden monkey that popped out on the hour, but even between hours it kept up a steady, gravelly, smug TICK-TOCK, and — nibble nibble — the flea, who had followed, sampled Mr. Bearangutan's knee.

"Oh NO," said Mr. Bearangutan. "I can't stand THIS."

So Mr. Bearangutan went to the kitchen to sleep in the kitchen chair.

WUMMM. WUMMM. WUMMM.

Went the big cold-store, humming its jungle hum, keeping the fruit flies happy. And the rain barrel under the window went DRIP. DRIP. DRIP. And somewhere in the thatch, a gecko went "TCHAK. TCHAK. TCHAK," which is gecko for the same thing the clock means.

And — nibble nibble — went the flea, who had followed again, and was beginning to consider itself a travel companion.

"Oh NO," said Mr. Bearangutan. "I can't stand THIS."

So Mr. Bearangutan tried the pantry instead, among the hanging banana bunches, where it was dark and quiet and smelled comfortingly of tomorrow's breakfast. But the bananas were at that certain ripeness that whispers. "Eat us," they whispered. "We're ready. We're perfect. We won't be perfect tomorrow." And — nibble nibble — went the flea, whose timing, Mr. Bearangutan felt, was becoming personal.

"Oh NO," said Mr. Bearangutan. "I can't stand THIS."

So Mr. Bearangutan took his pillow and climbed out to the garden fork, where there was a hammock slung between two branches. Surely, surely, in the sweet green night air, a monkey could sleep.

"OWWW-WOOOO," went the night apes, far off in the dark.

"FLAP FLAP FLAP," went the fruit bats, directly overhead, in considerable numbers, on urgent fruit business.

"ZZZEEEEET," went ten thousand cicadas, who had all agreed, in a great cicada assembly, that tonight was their night.

And — nibble nibble — went the flea, who had followed, and settled on Mr. Bearangutan's elbow as if it had reserved a seat.

"Oh NO," said Mr. Bearangutan. "I can't stand THIS."

So Mr. Bearangutan took his pillow to the bathing pool below the garden fork, where the water lay smooth and black and cool, and he lay back in the shallows with only his face above the surface, like a very large, very tired island.

Surely, he thought, even a flea cannot find a wet orangutan.

But the pool, it turned out, was the frogs' parliament, and the frogs had been waiting all day for quorum. "BREKEKE," said the first frog. "BREKEKE-KEX," said the second, with feeling. And then forty more frogs gave their speeches, all at once, at volume, with amendments.

And — nibble nibble — went the flea, treading water, remarkably, straight for Mr. Bearangutan's chin. The flea was not going to let a little thing like a pond come between it and its life's work.

"Oh NO," said Mr. Bearangutan. "I can't stand THIS."

So Mr. Bearangutan went, dripping, to the old jeep at the bottom of the garden, folded his great self behind the wheel, and laid his head back on the headrest.

TICK. TINK. TICK.

Went the cooling engine, which was going to keep ticking until dawn, thank you, it was no trouble.

And on the roof of the jeep, right above Mr. Bearangutan's head, a lovesick owl landed and began its whole entire speech: "HOO. HOO-HOO. HOO. HOO. HOO-HOO. HOO." And then a second owl landed beside it and disagreed, loudly, at length, about everything.

And — nibble nibble — went the flea, on Mr. Bearangutan's left ear this time. The flea, it must be said, was having a wonderful night out.

"Oh NO," said Mr. Bearangutan. "I can't stand THIS."

So Mr. Bearangutan went back into the house, past the ticking cocoa-nut clock, past the humming cold-store, up past the sleeping Baby Bearangutan, and looked at the big nest bed. Mrs. Bearangutan had rolled over, and now, in the pale hour before dawn, she had gone quiet.

Quiet!

Mr. Bearangutan climbed in as carefully as a burglar. He arranged his pillow. He closed his eyes. The moon went down. The house went still.

And then — nibble nibble — went the flea, one last time, on the very tip of Mr. Bearangutan's nose.

Mr. Bearangutan opened one eye. He looked at his nose. And there, in the last of the moonlight, sat one very small, very tired flea — who had followed him through the nest bed, the branch chair, the kitchen, the garden, and the jeep, nibbling all night, and who was now, at long last, exhausted.

The flea yawned a flea-sized yawn. It curled up on the warm bridge of Mr. Bearangutan's nose. And it went to sleep.

"Well," whispered Mr. Bearangutan, too tired to be angry at anything so small and so sleepy. "At least one of us can."

And the great fig tree sighed in the wind, and the whole dark jungle lay down — the owls and the bats and the cicadas and the gecko and the clock and the cold-store, all at once, as if they had only ever been waiting for the moon to go.

And Mr. Bearangutan slept.

He slept for minutes. Whole minutes.

Because then the sun came up over the edge of the jungle, and with the very first sunlight, every howler monkey in ten miles drew the deepest breath of its career and announced the news:

"OOOOHHH. OOOOHHH. OOOOHHH."

And Baby Bearangutan bounced into the big bed and landed on Mr. Bearangutan's stomach with both feet.

"WAKE UP," announced Baby Bearangutan. "BREAKFAST."

Mrs. Bearangutan sat up, bright as a fresh fig. "You look terrible, dear," she said kindly. "Didn't you sleep?"

And Mr. Bearangutan looked at the sun, and at his family, and scratched — nibble nibble — at the little flea now stowing away, fast asleep, in his eyebrow.

"No," said Mr. Bearangutan.

And he got up to make the breakfast.
