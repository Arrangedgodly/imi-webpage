# The French Lemurtenant's Woman

*A parody of "The French Lieutenant's Woman"*

The great Cob of Lyme Rhesus lay along the tide like the tail of some fossil monkey, half in the sea and half in legend. Along it, of a morning, paraded the macaques of the town — retired colonels of the Fig Office, widows of the fig trade, young monkeys of independent means and dependent opinions. And at the far, wave-lashed end, quite still in the wind, stood a lemur in a dark cloak, staring out at the sea as though she fully intended, one day, to swim to Madagascar.

"That is the French Lemurtenant's Woman," said Ernestina Figman, with relish. "Tragedy." She pronounced it with a capital T.

Ernestina was nineteen, pretty as a wax fig, and possessed of a mind like her father's ledgers — everything entered, nothing risked. She teased Charles that she was "a slave to your every whim, monsieur," and blushed at her own daring; and Charles, who had read Darwin and certain Greek poets no gentleman admits to, felt in her presence a tenderness unmixed with any danger whatever, which he mistook for safety and she for happiness.

Her betrothed, Charles Simianson — thirty, handsome, heir-apparent to the fig emporium of her father, the great Figman of Figman's Fig Emporium — considered himself a monkey of the modern scientific sort. He hunted the shale below the Underbranch for the bones of the Missing Branch, and kept a display case of fossils as a gesture against the churchly macaques of his acquaintance. He was, in short, a Darwinist before breakfast and a gentleman all day, which is a difficult double act.

The lemur's story, as the whole troop knew it, was this: a shipwreck, a French lemurtenant, ruin, abandonment — he had gone home and married another — and now Sarah Lemurdruff walked the Cob in all weathers, waiting for a sail, employed out of charity by the pious old howler Mrs. Poultney and inspected out of spite. Mrs. Poultney's charity was governed by two principles only: that hell was dirt, and that the other hell was being talked about. Her interview with Sarah had been conducted with a spyglass.

"Have you sinned, Miss Lemurdruff?"

"I have sinned, ma'am — in thought and in deed."

Mrs. Poultney, who had hoped for a denial, engaged her on the spot; a sinner repented was worth three innocents on the propriety exchange.

"You should not stand here alone, Miss Lemurdruff," Charles told her one gale of a morning. "The troop says — "

"The troop says I am the French Lemurtenant's Woman," said Sarah, not turning. "The troop requires a story with its tea."

He met her again in the Underbranch, the wild jungle below the cliffs, where his fossils weathered out of the shale. She told him the wreck, the lemurtenant, the ruin, in a voice like the sea doing the talking. Her eyes were the eyes of a lemur — enormous, ringed, adapted by ten million years for the dark — and they looked at Charles as if he were the dark. Once, among the weathered monsters of the shale, he had a sensation the century itself had not yet caught up with: that the world was a drawing hung upside down, that the upright monkeys on the cliff-top were the true fossils, and the ancient beasts under his hammer the living reality — that everything was exactly the reverse of what it pretended. He had put the feeling away, as a gentleman files his doubts. Sarah Lemurdruff took it out and handed it back to him.

"I am engaged," he said, irrelevantly, nobly, foolishly.

"I know," said Sarah. "It is what makes you safe to tell."

He was not safe. He thought about her while measuring Ernestina's engagement figs; he dreamed of the Missing Branch and woke with its name on his lips. He laid it all before old Dr. Grogan, the wisest drill in the district, who heard him out over two pipes.

"She is a victim, sir," said Grogan at last, "of the age. This is a most categorizing century, Mr. Simianson. It has dressed its females in a dozen cages and taught them to build the doors themselves. Where you see one melancholy lemur, I see a monkey who has looked at the cages and declined."

"Then what does she want of me?"

"To be looked at," said Grogan, "by a monkey who has never once, in his life, been looked at — and to see what he does."

Then, one morning, a note in a small lemur paw: she had fled Mrs. Poultney's piety; she was in the little Full Fig Family Hotel in Extree; would he come — as a gentleman, as a scientist, as a friend?

Mrs. Poultney did not long survive the scandal of her companion's flight. Hell, for her, had never been sin; hell was being laughed at, and she was laughed at now — by the entire troop, from the Cob to the church door. She took to her couch of propriety and shortly expired upon it.

He came. He found her asleep in the narrow room under the eaves, the truth lying on the table between them like a fossil. And the truth, when she woke and told it, was this: there had never been any lemurtenant at all. The whole affair was a fiction she had worn like a cloak, so that she could walk out into the weather and be left alone by a troop that forgives a monkey anything except freedom.

Charles walked back to Lyme Rhesus with his century in pieces. He broke his engagement — Ernestina wept, the great Figman thundered, the town turned its collective monocle — and signed, in disgust at himself and everything, for the New World, where a fossil-hunting monkey could at least dig without being read.

Now the chronicler is supposed to step in and provide the ending, neatly, like a fig in tissue. But monkey tales are not fig trees trained along one wire; and a tale, like a lemur, goes where it likes by night. So I offer you both, and you may keep whichever fits your cage.

In the first ending, Charles went back to Ernestina after all, and the emporium, and grew sleek on figs and doubt. Sometimes at dusk he walked to the Cob; and he never once saw the dark figure at the far end, because by then he had taught himself not to.

In the second, he traced her, three years later, to the riverside house of a poet-painter, where she kept the ferns and her own counsel. She had left no address; she had declined, in fact, everything except the ferns; and it took Charles two years and a private inquiry-ape of indifferent morals to find the house at all. He came up the stairs rehearsing speeches; and behind a bank of ferns stood a small lemur girl with enormous eyes — his eyes, Charles's own, staring out of a face two feet high. She came down to him very quietly and looked at him with the eyes that see in the dark.

"You were never my tragedy, Mr. Simianson," she said. "You were my proof that a lie can be kinder than a cage. Good day."

Charles walked back along the Cob alone. The sea stared at him and said nothing, as the sea does. And far out past the last hump of driftwood, Madagascar lay on the horizon like a rumor of some other, freer branch of the whole order.
