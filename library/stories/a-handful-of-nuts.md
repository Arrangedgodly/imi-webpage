# A Handful of Nuts

*A parody of "A Handful of Dust"*

Of all the groves in the home counties of the Great Troop, none was less practical than Chestnut Abbey. It was a vast, drafty treehouse of forty rooms built by a Victorian mandrill architect in the Gothic Revival style — pointed gables of split cedar, buttressed branches, a chapel with a rood screen made of walnut — and it stood, gray and magnificent and utterly unheatable, in three hundred acres of buttercups and mud.

Tony Leaf loved it the way other monkeys love their own fur.

"The place was a disgrace when my grandfather inherited it," Tony would say, showing visitors the stairwell he was rebuilding from sketches of the original — cedar balusters, hand-carved, each one a fortnight's work. "It was all plum and duco and bright paint, all Victorian monkey business. I am restoring it. In time, Chestnut Abbey will be the perfect expression of the Leaf family, and my son's son will inherit it entire, and that, you see, is what a family is: a nut planted, and tended, and passed on."

His visitors said it was wonderful, and went away laughing, and Tony did not mind, because Tony did not know.

His wife Brenda did not laugh. Brenda Leaf had married Tony at twenty, full of the idea of being mistress of a great estate; ten years, one small son, and one leaking roof later, the estate had turned out to be a blue-rinse lodger in a garden full of nettles, and Brenda had taken, twice a week, to going up to the Big Canopy.

"You could come with me, Tony. There's a decent little flat we might rent — something with other monkeys in it."

"Whatever for? There's plenty to do here. I'm doing the balusters."

So Brenda went alone, and in the Big Canopy she met, at a dying party, a young monkey named John Beech, the idle son of a divorced mother, who had no charms whatever, no money, no occupation, and — this was his entire appeal — nowhere else to be.

"How super," said John Beech, when she mentioned she was bored. "I say, are you doing anything Thursday?"

And that was that: an affair nobody would have noticed in either monkey, made frantic and important by the lying. Brenda rented a little set above a fruiterer's, and told Tony she was taking a course in economics.

Meanwhile, at Chestnut, six-year-old John Andrew was having the ideal monkey childhood, kept at the end of the garden with Ben, the ancient one-eyed gorilla groom, riding his zebra Grinner — "No, Master John Andrew, not the tail, you does not ride by the tail."

Then, on the wettest Tuesday of a wet June, the keepers' boy left the paddock rail down.

Ben found him under the chestnut leaves at the edge of the paddock, still as a dropped plum. Grinner, the young zebra, who had never meant harm in his short flighty life, had lashed out once, playing, the way young animals do, and John Andrew had fallen wrong, and that was all.

They wired to Brenda in the Big Canopy. She was, at that exact hour, at a party, telling John Beech she loved him. The wire was brought in on a tray; she read it, and put her face in her hands.

For a week everyone said how wonderfully she bore it. Then her lawyer-brother came down.

"It's dreadful of me, Tony, but Brenda wants a divorce — a quiet one, a little hotel at Bright-nest, a detective, all arranged. And she'll need an allowance. The awkward part: she wants the North Wing kept up for her. The nut cellars, the cedar staircase, that wing."

"The North Wing," said Tony, feeling the tree pulled out from under him. "You want me to sell Chestnut to pay for her to live near John Beech?"

"Well, after John Andrew — she does rather feel she can't come back here."

Tony walked out to the paddock and stood a long time at the rail. He was not an angry monkey; that was the trouble. He simply gave, and gave, and agreed. In the end he said yes to everything — the divorce, Bright-nest, the allowance, the North Wing — and then, having signed his own tree away, he did the only surprising thing of his life.

"Dr. Messi-nut," he said to the monocled aye-aye who dined at his club, "you were saying at dinner you wanted a patron for an expedition."

Dr. Messi-nut had a project: a lost city of the Nutmeg Kingdom, somewhere up the River of Bitter Water in the far interior — avenues and foundations no living monkey had seen. Everything, he said, was there. He needed only a backer with nothing left to lose.

"Then I'm your monkey," said Tony. "I have nothing left to lose. It's wonderful how light the branches feel."

They sailed and portaged. Black water under them, screaming canopy above, Dr. Messi-nut at the bow explaining that the Nutmeg Kingdom had flourished when the home counties were seabed. Tony learned to eat the flyblown stores and sleep wet, and found — the shameful part — that he thought of Chestnut less and less, Brenda not at all, and was, for the first time in ten years, almost happy.

Three hundred miles upriver, at the first falls, the hired bearers melted into the forest like mist, taking the stores. At the second falls, Dr. Messi-nut went ahead in the canoe to scout the channel — "I shall return by dusk" — and Tony watched the little canoe go over the lip of the falls and did not see it again, and waited on the bank for dusk, and for many dusks after.

Then his stores ran out, and his fever came on, and Tony Leaf walked into the forest because the river said nothing and the trees said nothing, and somewhere in the green dark he lay down.

He woke in a mud hut, under a roof of leaves, beside a fire, and an old monkey was spooning gruel into him — an immensely aged howler with amber eyes and a long white ruff, dressed in a settler's rags.

"Good," said the old monkey. "You live. There is someone here I want you to meet. He has been waiting a long time for company."

He brought out, from a tin chest, held together by cord and worship, a set of books: thick, cloth-bound, cracked at the spines. "Dickens," said the old monkey, pronouncing it slowly, like a password. "The only books. The man who came before you read them to me. Then he died. That was nine years ago. Since then, no one has read them, and I have been very much alone."

"I — how do you do," said Tony. "I'm awfully grateful, but if you could show me the river channel, I'll be getting along —"

"You will read," said the old monkey, with great gentleness, "this evening, after the gruel."

So that evening, by firelight, Tony Leaf opened *The Pick-walnut Papers* and read aloud: "The first ray of light which illumines the gloom, and converts into a dazzling brilliancy that obscurity in which the earlier history of the public career of the immortal Pickwick would appear to be involved..." And the old howler leaned back and closed his eyes in bliss, toothless mouth working along with the words, and Tony read on, chapter after chapter, because it was very pleasant, really, to be read to — and because the old monkey had gone to the door of the hut and taken inside the only machete, and hidden it.

He tried, once, in the second month, to leave. The old monkey wept — actually wept, frightfully, dust-colored tears — and then stopped weeping and picked up a heavy root-cudgel and sat down between Tony and the door, and neither of them mentioned it again. After that it was simpler to read.

The evenings took their pattern. Tony read; the old howler listened; the fire ticked. They finished *Pick-walnut*, and began *Little Dor-ridge*, and Tony — who now could not have said whether Chestnut's roof had been cedar or cedar-shaved — found his voice going out to the convicts and clerks of the fog as it had never gone to a baluster. "Mind you," he said one night, pausing over *Bleak House* — "mind you, I don't say it's the equal of Gothic Revival. But it has its points."

"You see?" said the old monkey, rapturous. "Now — the Jarndyce case. Again. From the beginning."

Years passed. That is all one can say of them, because for Tony Leaf there were now only two seasons: the days of gruel and mosquitoes, and the evenings of Dickens.

Back in the Big Canopy, nobody wondered much. Brenda had her allowance and John Beech, who bored her; the North Wing stood locked; and at Tony's club, when anyone asked after Leaf, the club monkeys looked vague, and someone said "exploring," and someone else told a joke, and the nuts went round again.

And up the River of Bitter Water, by firelight, Tony Leaf read on and on into the great green dark — while somewhere behind his eyes, Chestnut Abbey went on standing, and the balusters went on being carved, forever, in a handful of dust he no longer remembered he had ever held.
