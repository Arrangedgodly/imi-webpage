# Swinging Murder

*A parody of "Sleeping Murder"*

Gwena Reed, newly married and newly arrived from the colonies, knew the house the moment she saw it. Hillside, in the seaside town of Palmouth: a tall old villa with a monkey-puzzle in the garden and a view of gray water. She stood on the steps with her groom, Giles, and said the thing one is not supposed to be able to say.

"I've lived here. I know the paper in the hall — vines, blue vines, up the staircase wall. There used to be a door through there, didn't there? Papered over. I know it."

Giles, a sensible gibbon, pointed out that she had never in her life been east of Gibraltar. Gwena, who was a colobus and could be stubborn as bark, pointed out that she had not said she had *been* here. She had said she *knew* it.

They bought the house. Newlyweds will.

The odd things came slowly, like ticks. Gwena chose the bedroom that was not the best one, and slept well in it — but she could not walk down the staircase at dusk. Standing on the fourth step from the bottom, looking down into the hall, she would see, without wanting it, a picture that was not a memory and would not leave: the front door open, wet leaves blown in on the mat, and a she-monkey lying on the floor with hands at her throat, and somebody bending over her. And when Gwena looked at the wall of the staircase she found herself thinking, *The blue vines are wrong. The pattern repeats too low.*

She took advice, which in Palmouth meant taking tea. The advisor was an elderly langur staying with the vicar — Miss Maple, of Nana Mead, silver of fur and quiet of eye — and it was Miss Maple who said, when Gwena had finished, "Don't chase it, my dear. Some things are sleeping for good reason. Go home. Buy a different house."

"Is that what you did at my age?"

"Certainly not," said Miss Maple, refilling her cup. "But I had a grandmother who warned me about stairs."

So they dug. Gwena was born in Palmouth; that much was in the registers. Her father, Major Kelvin Halliday, a vervet officer home from the wars, had lived at Hillside with his second wife when Gwena was three — and that second wife, Helen, had one day gone away with a lover and never written again. The major had grieved, drunk, deteriorated, taken Gwena to the colonies, and died — but not before saying, in hospital, to the nurse: *I strangled her. I know I did.*

"Or he dreamed it," said the nurse, produced at length, an old woolly macaque now. "He'd wake saying he'd killed his wife. But there was never a body, and no lover's name ever came, and no letter."

Letters came, though. Helen had written to her brother — her guardian, her older brother, Dr. Kennedy, an ancient capuchin still in practice, grey-muzzled and courteous — and Dr. Kennedy had a bundle of them, which he shared most kindly, most sadly, in his book-room that smelled of carbolic and old fruit.

"You must not blame the poor major too much," said Dr. Kennedy. "Helen was a wild young thing. She ran. She wrote me from everywhere." He passed Gwena a page. It was Helen's writing — flighty, affectionate, unfinished — and yet something in the letters, Miss Maple observed later, was oddly *composed* for a runaway. Letters that tell a story tend to be written by somebody constructing one.

There had been a servant in the house, Lily — and Lily, it turned out, had spent eighteen years telling her husband one thing: *there's summat wrong about the missus going. And about the doctor's boots.*

Lily was traced to a riverside town, and Lily wrote back, eagerly, that yes, she remembered it all, and could meet, and would tell everything about that morning, about the trunk in the hall, about the doctor carrying it out to the car alone and saying the missus had sent it on ahead — and Lily fixed a day, and took the train with her telling-all face on, and was met at the station by somebody answering to an advertisement in her husband's name, and was found in the woods near the line, strangled.

"Which is a great deal of strangling," said Miss Maple, "for a family that never had a body."

They got one. Under the hydrangea bed at Hillside — the bed the old gardener had always been told to leave dug but never planted, the bed that took the rain and grew nothing — the police lifted the shallow earth and found a small she-monkey's bones with a fracture of the skull, and twenty years of *she ran away with a lover* came quietly to an end.

Miss Maple laid it all out for them in the papered hall, at Gwena's request, on the fourth step from the bottom.

"Kelvin Halliday believed he had killed his wife, because he was drugged, and drugged into believing it — the doctor supplied the poppy-sap and the suggestions, and let the major do the accusing for him. You see, the person who could not endure Helen's leaving was never her husband. It was her brother. Dr. Kennedy had raised her, arranged her marriage, buried his own life in hers — and when Helen finally, truly arranged to go away with somebody who loved her, he struck her down here, at the foot of the stairs, where a three-year-old saw it from the banisters and forgot it for twenty years. Then he posted a fictional Helen abroad in fictional letters, lugged a trunk he told Lily was going on ahead, and buried his sister under the hydrangeas — sending one small kindness after her. He planted the bed. He could never quite let the flowers go."

"He was fond of her," said Gwena, sick at herself for saying it.

"He was *owned* by her," said Miss Maple. "It is not the same thing, though it wears the same coat. Lily's misfortune was a good memory and a train ticket. He typed that advertisement himself — he had typed Helen's letters for twenty years, and the typewriter is a patient animal; it betrays a monkey in the end."

They did not arrest Dr. Kennedy. Old, respected, gentle Dr. Kennedy arrested himself instead: he wrote Gwena a note in his precise hospital hand — *You should have left it alone. It was not wise of you. I am, as I told you long ago, completely sane. There is no pathology whatever.* — and, in her copy of his medical dictionary, marked a passage about hereditary madness in a family, for her benefit, before he came to Hillside on the last evening.

Gwena was ready. She had been ready, waiting in the dark hall with every lamp out, exactly as Miss Maple had arranged, on the fourth step — no; below the fourth step this time — and when the door opened and the figure crossed the hall for her in the dark, and the hands came out to finish what had been begun twenty years before, the lamps went on all at once, and Inspector Larch of the county constabulary stood up from behind the furniture, and the whole apparatus of the law closed over Dr. Kennedy like a net over an old grey fish.

He said only one thing, looking past them all at the staircase wall.

"Cover her face," said Dr. Kennedy. "Mine eyes are dazzled. She died young — and now she has died *twice*, and it is your fault."

"Take him out through the garden," said Miss Maple. "Not past the bed."

They sold Hillside in the spring, and told the new people about the hydrangeas. Gwena and Giles sailed for home before the sale was final, and Gwena slept the whole voyage, dreamlessly, for the first time since she was three years old.

"Sleeping murder," said Miss Maple, back in Nana Mead, to her nephew, who had come down for the weekend. "That is what I call it, dear. You must never wake it. But if somebody's little colobus *does* go and buy the house — well." She took up her knitting. "Then you go down by the next train, and you bring the police, and you put the lamps where the child can reach them. Because the past does not lie asleep forever. It only pretends."

On the fourth step from the bottom, if you go there now, the new people have put a light.

They say it never goes out.
