# Big Little Lice

*A parody of "Big Little Lies"*

Nobody in the Pirriwee Branch troop ever died of lice. Everybody knew that. And yet, when the letter came home in little Ziggy's backpack — A CASE OF HEAD LICE HAS BEEN DETECTED — the mothers of the fig tree behaved as though the Black Death had swung in on a vine.

It had started months earlier, at orientation day, when Madeline Macaque turned her ankle on a root and a stranger named Jane helped her up. That was all it took. Madeline — forty years old, three infants, one ex, and opinions that could strip bark — had taken Jane's side forever, whatever sides there were. Jane was new to the troop, young, with wary eyes and a small quiet son called Ziggy, and by the end of orientation day Ziggy had been accused of biting Amabella Klein, daughter of Renata, the richest monkey in the canopy (chief executive of Bananaple, Inc., and never mind whose banana it was originally).

"He bit me," said Amabella, with the gravity of a soap-opera heiress.

Ziggy, round-eyed, shook and shook his head.

The troop took sides the way troops do: instantly, and forever, and for reasons no one could remember by tea. Renata hired lawyers to write letters about birthday parties. Madeline photocopied the letters and laughed at them in the car park with the hand brake of her whole personality off. Celeste — the beautiful one, the silvery one, so lovely that other females forgot their sentences mid-groom — tried to stay neutral, which pleased no one, especially not her husband. Lovely Perry. Tall Perry, who remembered every child's name and every wife's birthday. Celeste fell out of trees, everyone knew it. Such a clumsy beauty. At home, in the privacy of their own hollow, Perry held her a little too hard on the bad days, and nobody ever saw, because bruises hide in dark fur.

Celeste told the troop counselor none of this, and then all of it, twice a month, in a small office that smelled of fig leaves.

"He's never sorry in the moment," she said, staring at her hands. "He's sorry after. He cries after. That's the part nobody warns you about — the crying is so convincing you start auditing your own memory of the shove." Dr. Reesa, a patient old langur, asked what frightened her most, and Celeste said: "That I love him. Do I get to say that out loud and still be one of the sensible ones?"

Meanwhile the weeks rolled toward the gala. Madeline, directing the troop's variety show ("Frozen Bananas: The Musical"), spent long nights in the drama shed with Joseph Sapajou, the theater director, a smooth-talking capuchin with a headset and a marriage of his own — and if Madeline's fur got a little rumpled in the telling of it, well. Nothing happened, she told Jane, twice, which is what monkeys say when something almost happened. Jane looked at her with those flat gray eyes and said, "Things that almost happen still count. I'm an expert." Madeline laughed, and then didn't.

This was the thing nobody knew: for months, someone on the river path had hissed at Jane as she jogged past with Ziggy on her back. Just one word. *Suffer.* A male voice, low, amused, gone before she could turn.

She had told Madeline once, after two glasses of plum wine, what had brought her to Pirriwee with a baby and no troop. A conference, six years ago. A handsome male in a linen shirt who called himself Saxon Bonks — "a name like that should be illegal, and now, to me, it is" — who'd talked to her like she was the last ripe thing in the world, and who had hurt her, in a hotel room, in ways she still ran from most mornings, Ziggy's small warm weight the only thing that ever ran with her. He'd laughed when he left. Laughed, and said the one word she could not scrub out.

Then the lice letter came, and the whispering found its level: *blame the new monkey. It's always the new monkey. She probably came from a troop with no grooming standards whatsoever.*

Jane sat alone at the school grooming circle, combing her son's fur, staring at the middle distance.

"You can sit with me," said Madeline, dropping onto the branch beside her with the grace of a dropped coconut. "I've had lice four times. My head is basically a bed-and-breakfast for them. Very popular. Booked out seasons in advance."

Jane laughed — the first laugh anyone in Pirriwee had heard from her — and something in the troop shifted half an inch toward kindness, which is the most a troop ever shifts.

The gala came in June: trivia night and the costume fundraiser, where every adult dressed as their favorite famous ape. Madeline went as a silverback ("typecasting," she said, "and I don't care"). Celeste's twins went as the chimps from the tea adverts. Renata bid fortunes at the auction out of pure spite and Madeline bid out of pure theater, and it was, by every account, a wonderful night right up until the end.

The questions came round to famous apes of history, and the emcee asked one about the old hotel scandal, and across the platform Jane — five glasses of plum wine deep, which for Jane was four too many — said, to nobody, in a small voice: "Saxon Bonks. That was his name. He said it like a joke."

Celeste's head turned. Very slowly. Perry's family name was Bonks; Perry's cousin... no. No, wait. Celeste counted back six years, and the linen shirt, and the conference, and the front of her mind went white and quiet while the back of it did arithmetic she did not want to finish.

That was when Perry saw Jane across the platform. Madeline watched his face do something complicated — recognition, delight, cruelty — and watched him move.

"Suffer," he said, silkily, close to Jane's ear. "Remember me?"

Jane's face went white beneath her fur.

Celeste stepped between them, and her voice came out in pieces. "It was you. The conference. Her — Ziggy is—" She couldn't finish, so the night finished it for her: Ziggy, six years old, with Perry's exact gray eyes.

"No," said Perry, and grabbed her arm — the bad arm, the secret arm, the arm she'd explained away for years with stories about branches and gravity — and behind them Bonnie Baboon, the tree-yoga teacher, the calmest ape in the entire canopy, who had spent a decade breathing through her nose and rising above it all, saw red for the first time in her life. She had been raised by a father who grabbed like that. She had spent ten years learning to breathe out the memory. It was exhaled now.

She gave Perry one great shove.

He fell through the branches like a dropped banana, and the great fig tree — solid, respectable, mortgage-worthy — caught him exactly wrong.

The six females stood in a ring around the silence. Somebody's beverage was still fizzing gently on the platform boards.

"He fell," said Celeste, to the police lemur, an hour later, in a voice like a locked door.

"He fell," agreed the others, in perfect unison, and not one of them ever took it back.

The lice letter came again the next year, as it always does, and nobody died of that either. Amabella, at last, named her biter — not Ziggy, never Ziggy, but Max, one of Celeste's quiet twins, who had learned at home that love and grabbing can get tangled. And there are itches no comb can reach, and the troop of Pirriwee Branch has learned to live with them. Big lice, little lies.

It all itches the same.
