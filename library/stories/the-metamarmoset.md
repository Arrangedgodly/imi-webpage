# The Metamarmoset

*A parody of "The Metamorphosis"*

As Gregor Samsa awoke one morning from uneasy dreams, he found himself transformed in his nest into a gigantic — no. Into a tiny, trivial, thumb-sized pygmy marmoset.

He lay on his hard little back and saw what he had been: a sturdy chimpanzee with powerful arms and a fine working coat — all reduced now to a tuft of orange fur no bigger than a banana. "What's happened to me?" he thought. It was no dream.

Gregor's job was that of a traveling banana-broker, and it was a bad one: up at the alarm, on the early vine by five, out to the plantations to push "Samsa's Superior Suckers" on plantain barons who never once asked whether he had eaten. "If I didn't hold back for my parents' sake, I'd have quit long ago — told the head of the firm exactly what I think of him, swung from his chandelier, and thrown bananas at his head."

But the troop's debt had to be paid. Five more years, and then — this was Gregor's secret plan — the debt would fall, and he would take the day of his release to announce, at last, the other secret: that Grete, his sister, was to go to the conservatory, to study the little jungle fiddle with proper masters. He had not told Grete. He meant to tell her on Christmas Eve. And so, that morning, Gregor the chimp turned traveling salesman lay in bed a marmoset the size of a hedgehog's sneeze, staring at the alarm clock ticking toward five-thirty, unable to reach it.

"Gregor!" his mother called. "The early vine! You'll miss your connection!"

"I'm just getting up!" he called back — but the words came out as the faint pip of a rubber duck. "That was the voice of an animal," said the chief clerk, who had already arrived, sent by the firm. "If it were possible to be ill, I tell you, Samsa has picked the most insolent morning of the year."

Gregor rolled himself out of bed — his whole body was a rolling, really, a ball of fur with intention — and struggled at the door. No arms. No fingers worth naming. He turned the key by running around inside his own turning, like a monkey in a wheel, and finally the door sprang open.

The chief clerk, the mother, the father: three chimpanzees looking down at a teacup-sized monkey on the carpet. There was a silence.

"Oh!" said the mother, and fell back upon the divan.

The chief clerk backed toward the stair. "The firm has been defrauded — to think I believed you were merely ill—"

"Sir!" squeaked Gregor. "Sir, I am small — it is temporary — I will be at work by eight—"

But the clerk was gone, down the stairs and out, and Gregor's father, trembling, seized the breakfast fruit, and a hail of pears and plantains followed, and one ripe banana struck Gregor's back and stuck there, in his fur, gluey and bruised, and no one dared to remove it.

From that day Gregor lived under the couch. His sister Grete — a young chimp who slept late and was, in fairness, the only one who came — brought him old figs and cheese and tree sap, which, to her surprise, he adored, and took away, with a wrinkle of her nose, the fresh fruit he could no longer stand. Fresh fruit had no flavor anymore. Only the overripe, the bruised, the fermented, the genuinely objectionable. She sat in the corner sometimes, telling him quietly about the family's finances: the father's small pension, the mother's sewing, the servant girl dismissed, a little capital nobody had known about, salted away through the years of his commissions. They would survive, said Grete — just — if everyone worked, and nobody spent anything on anyone. "You're comfortable under there?" she asked once. He was. It was the last sentence anyone addressed to him as Gregor. To the household, he became "it," a small gray problem in a small gray room.

He learned his new body in the evenings. He found he could run up the wall and across the ceiling, hanging upside down over the dusty chandelier, and it made him sad — a strange, tiny sadness — that nobody came to watch. Once, to keep from forgetting, he crawled across the picture he had clipped from a magazine: a chimpanzee lady in a stole of banana skins, gazing out from her gilt frame. His mother and his sister, clearing his room one afternoon to give him more crawling space, moved toward the picture, and Gregor, panicked, dropped onto it, clamping the glass under his little body. It was too much for his mother. She saw him, this thumb-sized thing spread over a picture like a stain, and fainted, and Grete flew out for the smelling salts, and the father, home early from his new job as a bank beadle in his tight blue uniform with the gold buttons, took the morning newspaper and hissed and drove Gregor bleeding back into the wall. It was the second great wound of the family, and afterward nobody fed him for a day, and the banana on his back rotted quietly, and itched, and no one dared to remove it.

Then the family took in lodgers: three bearded capuchins, polite and exacting, who ate at the family's table and required the finest bananas, and Gregor learned to keep flat against the wainscot when they napped. The family's own meals got thinner. Grete took a shop job and came home gray-faced; the mother sewed; the father sat the long evenings in his uniform, too tired even to take it off. At night now, they left the lamp burning all night in the front room, because who could say — the father said it, gruffly, into his newspaper — who could say what might happen next.

One evening Grete, who had been teaching herself the little jungle fiddle, played for the lodgers. It was so beautiful. Was he a marmoset, that music moved him so? He felt as if the way to a nourishment he had never known was opening before him. Was that the conservatory, still, somewhere in the world — was that Christmas Eve? He crept out, drawn by the fiddle, and the lodgers saw him and raised their arms.

"Gentlemen," they said, standing, cold as accountants, "this household has concealed a vermin. We hereby give notice, and we decline to pay a thing for the weeks we have lodged here."

In the uproar, the father pleaded, the mother wept into her apron, and Grete — Grete, who had fed him figs — stood up, thin as a vine, and wept too, and said the thing that could not be unsaid:

"We must get rid of it. You must try to get rid of the idea that it is Gregor. It is not Gregor. It is a marmoset. And if it *were* Gregor, he would have understood long ago that monkeys cannot live with such a creature, and he would have gone away on his own."

She was right. That was the terrible thing, the thing he understood with his whole small heart: she was right. A chimpanzee who could not provide for them was not a chimpanzee. He crawled back under the couch, into the dust, and thought of his family with tenderness and love, and agreed with everything, and wished them, without being able to say it, a life of fresh fruit. His head sank down of its own accord, and from his nostrils came the last faint squeak, at three o'clock in the morning.

"You don't need to bother about the marmoset any more, ma'am," the charwoman said cheerfully in the morning. "I've swept it out."

The lodgers were shown the door; the charwoman was dismissed; the family, strangely light, took the tram into the country to recover. And on the way, Mr. and Mrs. Samsa noticed, almost simultaneously, that their daughter had grown — her fur was glossy, her shoulders had filled out — and it was time, they agreed, to find her a fine young chimpanzee of her own. And, as if to confirm their new dreams and excellent intentions, she stretched her young body and plucked a banana from the sun-warmed branch.
