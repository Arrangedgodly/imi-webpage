# Gorillaline

*A parody of "Coraline"*

Gorillaline Jones had moved into the new flat a week before and already explored it sixteen times. She was small for a gorilla, tireless, and thoroughly bored, which is the most dangerous combination in any species.

"We're extremely busy primates with a catalogue to finish," said her mother, typing on a keyboard of vines. "Go count the windows again."

There were twenty-one windows and one door that opened on nothing: big, brown, bark-knotted, and sealed shut with a brick-hard plug of ancient tree sap.

The neighbors were odd. Miss Squeak and Miss Furrycible, retired thespian macaques down the garden, read Gorillaline's fortune in banana peels and frowned at what they saw.

"Danger," said Miss Squeak.

"You're a clever little gorilla," said Miss Furrycible, "but clever and lucky are different things. Take this." She handed Gorillaline a river stone with a hole worn through it. "Good for lost things, bad liars, and small doors."

Upstairs lived the crazy old monkey Mr. Bobo, training a pygmy-marmoset circus ("They are not ready. They are *restless*"). Gorillaline did not like his helpers. They called themselves jumping mice, but they were too long in the body and too clever in the eye, and one evening she heard them singing to each other in the wall, in tiny ragged voices:

*"We know where you sleep, little gorilla. We know what's behind the sap. She says you're nearly ready. She says you taste of berries."*

"Nobody asked you," Gorillaline told the wall, which is the correct response to rats, and did not mention it at dinner, which is the correct response to parents.

That night, she followed three small black shapes down the corridor and watched them slip through the sap-sealed door as if it were fog.

The next afternoon she pushed the sap herself, and it was soft as fruit skin. The door opened on a corridor just like her own, only warmer, smelling of ripe bananas and sun-warmed fur — and on the other side sat her Other Mother, who looked exactly like her real mother except taller, thinner, paper-white, with big black buttons for eyes.

"Hello, Gorillaline. We've been waiting."

The Other garden was planted in the shape of Gorillaline's own face — lips of red hibiscus, eyes of banana blossom — and the food was better, and the toys were better: wind-up fig-beetles, a brass dragon hoarding raisins. There was an Other Father too, who showed her the garden with his gloves on and his eyes down. He was funny and sad in equal parts, like a joke told by someone being watched.

"Do you like it?" he asked. "The garden? I just plant what she tells me. I wanted tigers. I got begonias." He lowered his voice to a mutter. "She made me. Remember that, if it helps. She *made* me."

"You could stay forever," said the Other Mother. "There's just one little thing." She held up a needle, black thread, and two buttons.

"No," said Gorillaline. "I like my own eyes, and grass, and my real mother even when she's busy. I'm going home."

The Other Mother's smile did not move, which was the frightening thing. "Run along, then."

The sap door shut behind her — and her parents were gone. Not dead; *taken*. A cold shape sat inside each of them like a snow-globe souvenir of two monkeys waving from a vacation they had never taken.

So Gorillaline went back. Of course she did. But the Other Mother was waiting, and this time there was no warm corridor: there was a small cold room behind a mirror, where Gorillaline was shut for a night that went on being night. In the mirror, the shapes of three children came and sat with her: a bushbaby, a spider-monkey, a baboon, silver and translucent as breath on glass.

"She's a beldam," said the smallest ghost. "She loves us till we're used up, then keeps us. She loves everything that way. But her pride is her weak spot. Bet her. Make it a game."

So Gorillaline stood in the drawing room of the Other house, fists on hips, and lied like a champion.

"A finding-game. If I find the souls of the three children and my parents, you let everyone go. If I lose, I stay, and you can sew the buttons on yourself. I'll even thread the needle."

The Other Mother agreed, because she had never once lost — and because she had never imagined, in a thousand years, that a child would lie to her with a straight face and a lucky stone in her pocket.

The game had three legs. In the garden, under a face made of flowers, Gorillaline dug with her bare hands and found the bushbaby's soul hidden in a thimble. She also found the Other Father, sunk to his waist in the flowerbed, gone pumpkiny and strange, planted there for failing to be charming.

"I was supposed to stop you," he mumbled, roots in his beard. "I can't even hold your ankle properly. I'm sorry. I was always sorry."

"I know," said Gorillaline, and pulled free, and did not look back, and looked back anyway.

In the theater, an Other Miss Squeak and an Other Miss Furrycible performed a trapeze act in an empty house forever, to an audience of seated coats, and wept as they smiled; the spider-monkey's soul was in a shell under the stage, among the tired dogs. In the bedroom, the dragon gave up the baboon's soul from its hollow belly with the sulky dignity of a toy being frisked.

Which left the mantel, the snow-globe, and Gorillaline's own parents, waving on their tiny loop. And behind her, very close, the Other Mother stopped pretending to be anybody's mother.

"Found them," said Gorillaline.

And when the Other Mother shrieked and came apart into grasping white fingers, Gorillaline ran — through the collapsing corridor, buttons raining, fig-beetles scattering, the walls unzipping into gray mist that the stone in her fist cut like a hole punched in fog — slammed the sap door on the last reaching finger, and snipped it off with her mother's pruning shears.

The finger became a small white grub that would not die. Gorillaline carried it, wrapped in a leaf with the black sap-door key tied alongside, past the singing rats — who went silent as she passed, for once — to the old dry geyser hole at the edge of the garden, and dropped it in — down and down, where nothing with fingers could ever climb back.

Her parents came home remembering nothing, complaining about the traffic. Miss Squeak and Miss Furrycible toasted her health with ginger ale and never once asked what had happened, which is how you know true ladies. The marmosets finally performed. Mr. Bobo wept.

That night Gorillaline dreamed of three children, warm-colored and loud and alive, who waved once from the top of a hill and were gone; and in the wall, briefly, the rats began a taunt and thought better of it. She had left the stone on the sill where they could see it.

And Gorillaline Jones, explorer, slept in her own bed, in her own fur, with her own eyes — which were brown, and clever, and above all not buttons.
