# Farewell, My Lemur

*A parody of "Farewell, My Lovely"*

It was one of the mixed blocks over on Canopy Avenue, halfway between the banyan groves where the money swung and the bamboo dives where the sap went bad. I was a chimp with a license and an office over a banana stand when eight feet of gorilla in a suit two seasons too small filled my doorway.

"You the peeper?" he rumbled. "The private monkey?"

"Marlowak," I said. "Chimp, licensed, and reasonably sober until five. What can I do for you, big fella?"

"Mallox," he said. "Moose Mallox. I just did eight years in the Zoo Cage. I'm looking for a little lemur named Velma. Red fur, black rings on her tail, sang at Florian's. She was my gal. I want to find my gal."

Florian's Foliage Club had gone to seed and baboons. The madam was a gin-pickled spider monkey named Jessie Floriand, with a housecoat, five empty bottles, and a memory she rented out by the question. From the doorway she looked like a lot of class. Up close she looked like something made up to be seen from a doorway. I traded her a bottle for a photograph — Velma was a lemur to make a bishop monkey kick a hole in a stained-glass treehouse — and the tip that Velma had "gone to England, or up a rich tree, honey, whichever's farther."

"Velma's a long climb, Mallox," I told the big guy outside. "Monkeys change branches. Forget her."

"Moose don't forget," said Moose, and didn't, which was the whole trouble.

The next day a fussy fellow named Marriott hired me as muscle. He wore cologne, cologne over cologne, talked like a monkey dictating his own eulogy, and paid in fifties from a roll that had seen more palms than a ferry rail. He wanted to buy back a stolen amber banana pendant — property, he said, of a society langur named Mrs. Grale. The swap went wrong in a fern gully at midnight: I got sapped with a sapwood club (a chimp can be sap-ed with sapwood, and I resented the craftsmanship), and Marriott ended up past caring. I woke up flat on my back counting fireflies and invoices.

A bright-eyed capuchin named Anne Riordoon found me there, loaded me into her roadster, and fed me sandwiches I hadn't subpoenaed. She was writing a thesis on the coastal sap trade, asked questions in footnotes, and kept turning up afterward the way luck does when you don't deserve it. I was, she said, footnote material. I've been called worse by better.

A desk baboon named Nulty let me poke around to keep me off his paperwork. The trail ran through the fortune-teller's parlor of Jules Amthor, "psychic consultant to the stars," a turbaned lemur who smelled of incense and bad credit. "The amber banana," Amthor purred, "is a question of the mind. Where is your mind, Mr. Marlowak? I'll read it." His gorilla, an understated slab named Second Planting, tried to loosen mine with the furniture. The reading was inconclusive. Second Planting drove me back to my office with the courtesy of a suitcase handle and left me there like a delivery.

After that, the Bay City constabulary invited me to a room with no clock, where the questions were friendly and the furniture wasn't. A sergeant with a jaw like a mailbox asked if I'd ever heard of excess enthusiasm. I said I'd been married to it for a fiscal year. They asked me to sign a confession written in crayon by some previous chump. I corrected the spelling; it seemed only fair. They wanted to know why a chimp kept sniffing around the sap boats. I told them a little bird had told me the amber bananas weren't jewelry at all — they were packed with powdered sap, the pendant was the shipment, and Marriott's midnight errand was inventory in gloves. They threw me into the night and told me to stay thrown.

The big time sat at the end of the trail: Laird Brunette, an elegant silverback who ran the Monte-banana-ito, a gambling barge anchored beyond the three-mile line where the law got seasick.

Brunette received me on the dark afterdeck, all white fur and quiet menace. "Ask me nothing, Marlowak, and I'll give you a rumor. Ask me something and I'll give you the tide."

"A name," I said. "Who fenced the amber banana?"

"Try a tall tree on the north side," said Brunette. "And take the long branch home, little chimp. Short ones break under gorillas." He didn't deny the boats. He never discussed cargo on principle, and the cargo, as far as I could tell, was wearing pendants.

They didn't break under Mallox, though. He found her first. I reached the Grale mansion in time to watch the tragedy play out in pantomime: Mrs. Grale, the glamorous langur with the emerald collar and the trembling smile — and Moose Mallox grinning like Sunday because after eight years he'd found his Velma, and Velma was rich, and Velma was married, and Velma, whatever she had been at Florian's, was not going back to a gorilla in a tight suit.

She bonked Mallox once with a bronze banana, the decorative kind mansions keep by their doors. He sat down slow, still grinning. By the time I reached the lawn she was gone over the wall, rings streaming little goodbyes, and the rich howler husband was waking up to learn that his wife's singing career had a set list she never mentioned.

"She was cute," Moose told me from the grass, gone soft, "like a fruit cup. Wasn't she, shamus?"

"Out of this world, big guy," I said, and helped him up.

The cops called it self-defense and lost the file on purpose. Nulty got the commendation for the sap boats, which was the last thing he ever got except older. He told me once, at closing time, that I'd make a fine cop if I'd only learn to duck. Old Grale died soon after — of his heart, or his accountant, the death notice didn't say — and the estate, the pendants, and the truth all went up a tree nobody could climb. I went back to my office over the banana stand, and for two years after, the only monkey who said the name Velma to me was a depressed gorilla at closing time. Anne Riordoon finished her thesis. It had a lovely paragraph about me. I checked.

Then, on vacation in the lemur archipelago, in an island cabaret, the lights came up on a torch singer with eyes like a pair of stolen sunflower seeds. Between sets she found my table and gave me the big, wet, dangerous lemur eyes — the kind a lemur gives the last mango on the tree.

"Hello, shamus," Velma said softly. "Buy a girl a fruit cup?"

"Sure," I said, and while I signaled the waiter she tipped out the back and onto the night boat, light as a rumor. The waiter told me she paid her tab weekly, in advance, in a different fur. Class will tell. So will a head start.

Some monkeys you catch. Some you just file. I watched the boat lights scatter on the water and lifted my glass to the rings on a departing tail.

"Farewell, my lovely," I said, to nobody, the way chimps in the mixed blocks do.
