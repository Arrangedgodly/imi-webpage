# As You Lice It

*A parody of "As You Like It"*

In the jungle kingdom, Duke Frederick — a baboon with a crown two sizes too tight — had shoved his elder brother Duke Senior off the throne branch and into exile. Duke Senior took it well. He and his loyal troop now lived in the deep Forest of Itchy-Arden, where, he told his lords, the cold wind did not bite so much as gnaw politely, and the fleas were at least honest fleas.

"Are not these woods more free from peril than the envious court?" he asked, scratching blissfully. "Here we feel not the penalty of Adam — though we do feel the ticks of Adam."

His daughter Rosalind was not with him. She had been kept at court as companion to Celia, Frederick's daughter, and the two cousins loved each other like sisters.

One afternoon they went to see the court wrestling match: Charles, the Duke's champion — a gorilla with a grip like a coconut press — against a callow young ape named Orlando. Charles had cracked three ribs that season, two of them belonging to referees.

"Let us look with our eyes half shut," said Celia, wincing, "in case."

Orlando, youngest son of the late Sir Rowland, was being robbed of his inheritance by his elder brother Oliver, who kept him worse than a zoo keeps a lorikeet. He fought Charles the way only a desperate monkey fights — unfairly, brilliantly, and on pure spite — and threw him.

"Try what rescue you can," the fallen gorilla told his second. "For my part, I am retiring."

Rosalind, delighted, took the necklace of coral berries from her own neck and gave it him. "Gentle sir, wear this for me." Celia added hers, and Orlando stood holding two necklaces and no words at all, which is the classic symptom.

But Duke Frederick knew the family name. "The son of my old enemy?" he said. "Get thee from our court." And a few days later, deciding Rosalind had grown too popular for a cousin with no throne, he banished her too.

"You, niece? You are your father's daughter."

"Your daughter and I would have been sisters either way," said Rosalind, "had you not been his brother."

"Then banish me as well," said Celia. "Where you go, I go. We shall travel like sisters — one destiny, two tails."

So Rosalind, the cleverest monkey in the jungle, smeared her fur with mud, bound up her tail, and became "Gibbonymede," a young male traveler; Celia became "Aliena," her sister. They bought a fig orchard on the forest's edge with the coral berries and settled in to wait for whatever came.

What came was trouble with a name: Orlando, fleeing Oliver's plots into Itchy-Arden with old Adam, a retainer so ancient his fur had gone green. Adam had offered his life savings — "I have five hundred crowns, master, by which I mean figs — take them, and let me serve you; I am old, but I can still smell an ambush" — and now lay starving under a tree while Orlando charged into the green dark muttering, "There's no food in this forest? Then the forest is wrong."

Starving and lovesick, Orlando then did the most embarrassing thing a monkey can do: he wrote poetry.

ROSALIND, he carved into every trunk for miles. FROM THE FIG TO THE FARTHEST BANANA, NO FRUIT SO RIPE AS ROSALIND. And, wincingly: "Whoever loved that loved not at first swing?"

Rosalind, walking the woods, found the trees more eloquent than any orchard had a right to be. Then she found Orlando himself, moping under a willow, not recognizing his own beloved under mud and bound tail. So she did the monkey thing: she groomed his feelings.

"I can cure this love," said Gibbonymede briskly. "Pretend I am Rosalind. Court me. Woo me. Give me all the nonsense you would give her, and I shall out-nonsense you until you are cured."

So Orlando came daily to the orchard and made love to a mud-smeared boy-monkey pretending to be Rosalind, while the real Rosalind took notes.

"How long would you have her, after you possessed her?" asked Gibbonymede.

"For ever and a day."

"Say 'a day' without the 'ever.' No, no, Orlando: men have died from time to time, and monkeys have worms and fleas, but not for love."

Nearby, the subplot ripened. A shepherdess named Phoebe had fallen for Gibbonymede — hook, line, and mud — while the shepherd Silvius trailed her, sighing.

"Sweet Phoebe, pity me!"

"I do — the way one pities a fly in fig-juice," said Phoebe, who had a heart like a January fig. "Now I do frown on thee with all my heart."

"Then 'tis well enough," said Silvius, brightening, "for a frown from thee beats a smile from anybody."

When Phoebe sent Gibbonymede a furious love letter — "If you will be my monkey, know that I am yours, and also that I hate you" — Rosalind read it aloud in a mincing voice and gave the messenger a message back: "Tell her this: sell when you can; you are not for all markets."

Touchstone the jester — a pygmy marmoset with a bell and a law degree — watched it all while courting a goatherd named Audrey, and melancholy Jaques, a howler who only howled sadly, watched Touchstone.

"I would fain see you in a fool's coat," Jaques told him, "for you have bettered the trade."

"I remember," said Touchstone, "when I was in love I broke my sword upon a stone and swore I would run it through my own heart for my lady's coldness. Fool, fool, quoth I — and so I was. The truest poetry is the most feigning."

It was Jaques, though, who delivered the famous speech over a supper of grubs and figs:

"All the world's a canopy, and all the monkeys and apes merely swingers. They have their exits and their entrances, and one monkey in his time plays many parts. At first the infant, mewling and clinging in its mother's fur; then the whining schoolboy, dragged to climbing-lesson; then the lover, sighing like a furnace and carving bad verse into bark; then the soldier, seeking the bubble reputation even in the howler's mouth; then the justice, full of ripe figs and wise saws; and last of all, second childishness and mere oblivion, sans fangs, sans grip, sans tail, sans everything."

"Merci," said Duke Senior. "You have earned your fig."

Then came Oliver, searching the forest — wearing a new leaf, literally, pinned to his chest. He had been ambushed in the deep woods by a leopardess and would have died there, had not the very brother he had wronged heard his cries, wrestled the beast off, and stood guard over him till dawn.

"I know not what came over me," said Oliver. "Kindness. It is terribly disorienting."

He handed Gibbonymede a bloodied fig-leaf to pass to Orlando, proof of the rescue — and at the sight of the blood, "Gibbonymede" swayed and fainted dead away.

"Why, how now, Ganymede!" cried Oliver. "You a man, and lack a man's heart?"

"I am in fact — recovering," said Rosalind weakly, from the ground. "Pray, tell no one."

Oliver took one look at Celia, and went completely bananas. They were married more or less on the spot.

The next day, "Gibbonymede" said: "Orlando, if you would see your Rosalind, look — here she is." And she washed off the mud.

Hymen, the wedding monkey, tied four tails together: Rosalind and Orlando, Celia and Oliver, Touchstone and his goatherd — and Phoebe was summarily handed to Silvius, who took her exactly as a monkey takes the sunny branch. Duke Frederick, meanwhile, had found religion, or at least a mango grove, and gave the dukedom back.

Only Jaques declined the party. "I'll stay in the woods," he said. "There's a melancholy monkey of a new sort down by the river, and I simply must go ape over him."

So ends the play — and if it please you, it pleases us all; for as the man-monkey said to his barber: lice, and be liced.
