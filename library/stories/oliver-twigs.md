# Oliver Twigs

*A parody of "Oliver Twist"*

Oliver Twigs was born in the Mudfog workhouse, in a jungle so gray and joyless that even the bananas were rationed — one small, green, sour 'nan per monkey per week, and be grateful for it.

The workhouse was run by the board, a committee of fat old baboons who were greatly astonished that the orphan monkeys kept having the audacity to be hungry.

"They like it, you know," said the board, stroking their jowls. "Spoiled, that's what they are. Weak digestion. That's the ticket."

The orphans were fed three bowls of thin gruel a day, one onion twice a week, and half a roll on Sundays. In consequence, one large, raw-boned young monkey rose from the bench, advanced to the master, bowl in paw, and said the words that shook the board to its jowls:

"Please, sir, I want some more."

"*More!*" The master went pale. "Twigs asked for *more!*"

"That boy," said the board, when the fur had settled, "will be hung." (He was not. He was, eventually, adopted — the one twist the board never saw coming.)

Oliver was sold for five pounds to an undertaker named Sowerberry, whose name means sour berry and whose house matched. Mr. Bumble, the beadle — a fat, choleric baboon in a cocked hat who had named Oliver himself (the alphabet was his system; the last orphan had been an S, so Oliver got a T — Twigs, because he was thin as one and twice as crooked) — delivered the paperwork. Oliver was beaten, starved, and made to sleep among the coffins. When another apprentice, Noah Claypole — a lubberly ape with a taste for other monkeys' grief — insulted his dead mother, Oliver flew at him like a wildcat, and ran for it that same night.

He walked seventy miles to Jungle London and collapsed at the feet of a strange young ape in a peaked cap, who danced around him and said:

"Hullo, my covey! What's the row? Git up, then — you're pale as a peel. Come and have a banana."

This was the Artful Codger, the flashiest young pickpocket in London, who fed Oliver and brought him to Fagin — an old, red-furred orang who kept a den of boy thieves, pulling trinkets from his fur each morning.

"Here, my dears," said Fagin, holding up a silver fruit-knife. "This is the prig — I mean, the *treasure* — we're to practice on. Codger, show the new boy how it's done."

Every morning the Codger and Charlie Bates played the merry game: Fagin prowled up and down with a watch-chain, a snuff-box, a brooch, and a fruit-knife planted about his person, and the boys crept after him filching things; if the old orang felt a paw, the game began again, and the winner got the biggest 'nan at supper. Oliver, innocent as a sapling, thought it was all a grand game — "here's a knife, now take it from my pocket, well done, here's a banana." He was happy. He was fed. He had no idea he was being trained as a thief.

Then came the morning when Fagin sent Oliver out with the Codger and Charlie to "work." Oliver watched in horror as the Artful Codger slipped his little paw into an elderly gentleman's coat pocket and drew out a handkerchief — and then Oliver ran, and everyone cried "Stop thief!" and chased *him*.

He was caught and dragged before the magistrate, who had already determined Oliver's guilt by the shape of his ears, and would have been sentenced on the spot had the victim — a kindly old ape named Mr. Brownlow — not looked into Oliver's face and said quietly:

"I do not believe this child is a thief. His face is honest. I will take him home."

And so Oliver Twigs, for the first time in his life, slept in a clean bed in a clean house, with a kind old ape who fed him soup and asked him to read books aloud, and Mr. Brownlow's friend Mr. Grimwig, an old orang who wore his wig twisted in revenge for nature's treatment of his fur and doubted everything on principle. "A likely tale," said Grimwig, when Oliver's history was told. "I'll eat my own tail, sir, if he hasn't run back to his thieving friends by Thursday."

It did not last. Fagin's gang — terrified the boy would inform on them — kidnapped him back, lured into the alley by Nancy, a poor young she-ape of the streets who wept while she did it, for Nancy had been a workhouse orphan herself. And Bill Sikes, a burglar with red-rimmed eyes and a temper like a charging gorilla, dragged Oliver along on a country house robbery. Oliver tried to warn the sleeping household, and Sikes put a pistol to his head.

"Repent of this," said a voice inside Oliver. "Better to die than be a thief."

He was shot, wounded, and left in a ditch. He crawled back to the very house they had robbed and collapsed at the door — where Mrs. Maylie and her beautiful adopted niece Rose took him in, wept over him, and nursed him back to health. "It was the boy who was shot," said the old surgeon, "but it is we who were robbed — of our senses."

Meanwhile, a shadow hung over Oliver's past. On her deathbed, an old workhouse nurse had confessed to stealing a gold locket from Oliver's dying mother — the locket that held the secret of his birth — and selling it to a smirking ape called Monks, who dropped it into the river. And this Monks — half-mad, red-eyed, chewed of fur — had paid Fagin for years to keep the orphan poor and lost. For Oliver was the legitimate heir to a fortune, and Monks, his half-brother, wanted it all.

Rose Maylie and Mr. Brownlow compared notes — and Nancy, who had overheard Monks and Fagin plotting in a dark den, risked her neck on a midnight meeting on the bridge to tell them everything. "I am chained to a terrible life," she said, "but I was once a little monkey like him, and no one spoke for me." It was Nancy's word that let Brownlow trap Monks and make him confess: the locket was fished from the river, the will was proved, the fortune split. Rose Maylie turned out to be Oliver's aunt — his dead mother's younger sister, all along.

Fagin was arrested with the whole gang and sentenced to the cage — and on his last night, Oliver visited the trembling old orang in his cell, and even Fagin wept. "My dears," he whispered to the empty air, "my dear, dear dears —" while outside the crowd howled for the old ape's bananas.

Sikes, goaded by what he had done to Nancy in the dark of that quiet street — a deed that could never be undone, and that even his own dog slunk away from — fled over the rooftops with a lasso of vine meant as his escape rope, accidentally looped it around his own neck, swung off the gable, and hanged himself. The jungle's own justice, delivered by accident; and the mob below lowered their sticks and went home silently.

Mr. Brownlow adopted Oliver as his son. And on the first morning in his new home, Oliver Twigs ate a full, ripe, golden banana at a proper breakfast table, in a house full of warmth, and looked out at the green canopy of Jungle London, and thought that this — this, at last — was home.

"Please, sir," he said to Mr. Brownlow, smiling, "I'd like some more."

And Mr. Brownlow laughed and gave him the whole bunch. Mr. Grimwig, watching, cut a lock of his own tail-fur and laid it on his plate. "I'll keep it there," he said, "in case of another improbability — and in this boy's life, sir, I expect one."
