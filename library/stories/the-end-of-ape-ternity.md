# The End of Ape-ternity

*A parody of "The End of Eternity" by Isaac Asimov*

In the Jungle of Ape-ternity there is no night, every branch has a number, and Andrew Howler-lan was a Technician.

Do not envy him. In Ape-ternity, a Technician is the monkey nobody grooms. His work is the little snip: Upwhen and Downwhen he climbs, through the great Tree of Time, pruning this century's branch of a bad fig-blight or that century's nasty little war, each Change kept small and sensible, so that Reality settles a shade safer and a shade duller, forever. He made his snips with cold skill and went home alone, and the other monkeys of the centuries let him, because there is no superstition so durable as the one about the monkey who touches the tree.

The Maul had chosen his life for him at fifteen. You stand under the coconut machine, it drops, and your Life-Trade is in the shell: Educator, Sociologist, Computer, Maintenance. Howler-lan's coconut had said COMPUTE, and the beetle-browed Elder Twissell had collected him, and that was that.

He had one secret, the sort of secret that keeps a monkey sane: he loved the Primitive Centuries — the wild unnumbered branches below the Tree's first root, before Ape-ternity ever grew, where nobody pruned anything. He collected their artifacts like relics: a paper kite, a cork, a grocery list in dead dialect, and one folded news-sheet with a market-stall advertisement circled in ink. He could read a Primitive grocery list in the original. In Ape-ternity, this was the equivalent of a scandalous hobby.

His posting was the 482nd, under Computer Finge, a broad soft ape who distrusted curiosity on principle. "You are an instrument, Howler-lan," Finge told him. "An instrument should not look at mirrors." It was Finge who sent him to observe a festival, one of the mild gorgeous ceremonies the 482nd was famous for — and there, amid the dancers, he met Noÿs.

She was a bonobo at that festival — the dancers that year wore mirrors, and every step threw light around the hall — and when she looked at Howler-lan, the Technician with the lonely fur, she looked at him as though grooming were only a question of time.

"I know you," she said. "You're the one who decides nothing is ever quite worth the risk."

He broke every rule for her. He walked with her in the mirrored halls. He told her — stiffly, at the threshold of her family's great branch-house, then less stiffly — what a monkey's life in the Tree was like: no weather, no risk, no night, everything decided a comfortable century in advance. She heard him out with her head tilted.

"You could have anything you wanted," she said, "if you wanted anything."

Then Finge ordered a Reality Change — a small one, a tidy one, filed as usual — and when the new Reality settled, Noÿs's family branch-house had never been built, and her class had never risen, and in the new world she would be mated by arrangement to a stranger before the season's end. Howler-lan checked the computation twice, and then, with a calm that frightened him, he hid her Upwhen, in the Forbidden Canopy of the Hidden Centuries, where no Eternal is permitted to climb, and while he hid her, he learned the thing Ape-ternity exists to keep unknown.

The truth was this: the little snips added up. Every sensible Change, each one so small — a blight removed, a tyrant re-taught, a reckless idea mislaid — trimmed away the reckless monkeys themselves, century by century, until in all the Jungle of Time there was no ape left who looked at the night sky and wanted it.

Howler-lan ran the computation himself, in a hollow of the Hidden Canopy, and the numbers came back the same every time.

The price of Ape-ternity was the stars.

"You are not the first to find it," said Noÿs quietly, when he confronted her with the Computapalms' worth of figures. "We have known for a hundred thousand years in the Hidden Centuries. That is why no one climbs down to you. And it is why I was sent. The circle must be broken."

He looked at her then with the coldest question a monkey in love can ask — *you were sent; how much of you was a mission?* — and his fist rose before he knew it. Noÿs did not flinch. She stood in the doorway of the little hidden hut with her arms open, as if to say: here is the fur, here is the truth, groom or tear. He put his fist away, and the question never entirely died, but it learned to sleep.

For there was a circle, and Howler-lan was inside it. The pudgy young cub Cooper — the one Twissell was schooling in the arithmetic of the Tree — was destined to be sent Downwhen to the Primitive Centuries, to teach the first monkeys the field arithmetic, and to become, under another name, the legendary founder of Ape-ternity. The founder was taught by Ape-ternity, which the founder built. A knot with no end. A snake swallowing its own tail forever, with all the sky going to waste above it.

"You can keep the knot," said Noÿs, "and prune, and be safe, and sit on this branch until the sun burns out — every century of you."

"And the other half of it?" said Howler-lan.

"Break the circle. Ape-ternity never exists. And the monkeys who come after — the risk-takers, the climbers, the reckless ones we have been snipping away for a hundred thousand years — they get the stars."

It was Twissell who found them at the last, with the cub Cooper already aboard the kettle, waiting to be sent Downwhen — and the old ape looked very small and very tired. Howler-lan understood, by then, the last turn of the knot: Twissell had chosen him for the Mall, all those years ago, because the circle required a monkey who loved the Primitives. He had taught Cooper the dead dialect himself. He had shown him the artifacts himself. He had circled the market-stall advertisement in ink himself, years before he knew why. There was no part of the knot he was not.

"Every Eternal wonders once," said Twissell, "whether the Tree is worth the forest. Most of us stop wondering. It is the ones who keep wondering who get left alone, Technician, and I am sorry it took you so few centuries."

Howler-lan took out the primitive advertisement he had prepared — the silly thing, the fruit-market tip, ALL THE TALK OF THE MARKET-STALL, the small hook of paper on which the whole knot depended — and weighed it against the sky.

"The circle must be broken," said Noÿs.

"Then let it be a banana," said Andrew Howler-lan, Technician, "and let it be split."

He handed the paper to Cooper, and sealed the kettle, and somewhere Downwhen, in a Primitive century with no number, Ape-ternity quietly never happened.

And in the new real jungle — the one where nothing is pruned and everything is at stake — a young ape looked up past the highest branch, at the cold bright scattering of the stars, and began, at last, to climb.
