# The Unbearable Lice-ness of Being

*A parody of "The Unbearable Lightness of Being"*

The idea of eternal return is a mysterious one, and Nietzsche used it to perplex philosophers: that every monkey life is lived again and again, that each grooming session and each banana repeats an infinite number of times. In the world of eternal return, every careless scratch becomes a burden too heavy to bear. But what if each life happens only once? Einmal ist keinmal, says the German proverb. What happens but once might as well not have happened at all. A monkey who lives only one life has nothing to weigh it against; he is weightless, furless, liceless — nothing but a rumor in the canopy.

Tomas, a young mandrill surgeon with a face like a stained-glass window, believed in exactly nothing. He lived in Primate City with his patients and his mistress Sabina, a gibbon painter of elegant betrayals who wore a bowler hat tilted over one brow. "Metaphors are dangerous," he told his students. "Metaphors are not to be trifled with. A single metaphor can give birth to love."

Then the metaphor arrived, borne on six coincidences. First, the chief surgeon's sciatica sent Tomas to the spa town where Tereza worked. Second, he arrived the day she had a shift off. Third, they met because she was called to cover for a sick friend at the hotel bar. Fourth, she stood under his window with a book — *Banana Karenina*, by Tails Tolstoy — waiting for a stranger. Fifth, that very evening he had no appointment and so sat in the bar. And sixth, she had a copy of Beethoven on the gramophone, which was Tomas's one hereditary weakness.

A monkey in a basket pushed down the river, he thought. Moses. Six coincidences in a row, and Tomas, who believed in nothing, found himself believing in something absurd. He took her home, and she ran her fingers through the fur of his chest, and it was decided.

Love did not stop Tomas from grooming other females. "Grooming and love are two different things," he insisted. "A monkey may groom a female he does not love, and love a female he does not groom." He kept his appointments in a little black book — one hundred and sixty-one grooming sessions — and after each one he came home to Tereza, who heard the whole jungle in his fur: other females' fingerprints, other females' claws, the perfume of crushed mango that was not hers.

Tereza had been raised by a mother who believed the body was a public disgrace. "What do you want with modesty?" her mother shrieked, grooming herself loudly before the whole troop. "You have nothing anybody hasn't seen!" — which is why, Tereza believed, she had come to worship the secret, the hidden, the one book in the closed basket floating down the river. Her whole life was a gesture of discretion against a loud mother.

Tereza had nightmares. She dreamed she was back at the zoo pool, surrounded by the other females, all of them singing happily, all grooming each other with terrible cheerful efficiency — and their fur was alive, and their fur was full of lice, and the lice were Tomas.

"Lice," said Sabina, when Tereza described the dream, "are the most unbearable burden of all."

"No," said Tereza. "It is not the lice. It is that everyone pretends there are no lice."

Tereza left him once — packed her basket and floated back down the river — and Tomas, weighing his lightness against the mountain of her absence, understood the German words Beethoven had set to music: *Es muss sein.* It must be. He quit his mistresses, followed her, and told himself that love which did not begin as a delirium would have to become one by stubbornness alone.

Then came the tanks, and then came the article. The Gray Troop rolled into Primate City in the spring, and the monkeys climbed to the tops of their climbing gyms to watch, and nobody knew whether to raise a fist or raise a tail. In that fever Tomas wrote a short letter to the weekly: was not the baboon who ordered the occupation like a son who has blinded his mother and then written an essay saying she ought to be glad? The essay was published, cut, mocked, reprinted by the occupation as proof of sedition, and one gray morning Tomas was summoned before the chief.

"Disavow it," said the chief. "Say you were young, that you did not know what you wrote. Sign the statement and you keep the scalpel."

"But I knew precisely what I wrote," said Tomas. "I only didn't know the consequences."

"Then you cannot remain a surgeon."

"Thank you," said Tomas, with a strange lightness. "I have been wanting to quit for years."

He became a fruit-polisher — a window-washer of the jungle, a mandrill who went from grove to grove polishing the bananas of shopkeepers who felt honored to be scrubbed by an ex-surgeon, and (this is the shameful arithmetic, oh confess it, Tomas) the work came with a steady stream of female customers. Tereza, learning the arithmetic again, dreamed once more of the pool. And so, when she said one evening, "Take me to the mountains," he agreed. Es muss sein.

Sabina had left before any of them. Her whole art was betrayal — she painted a beautiful lie over a crack — and "betrayal," she said, "means breaking ranks and vanishing into the canopy. Betrayal means leaving your troop. My grandfather abandoned the troop, my father abandoned the church, and I intend to betray every jungle I ever loved." She fled to Geneva, where she took a lover named Franz, a professor with glasses, gentle as a sapling, who worshipped her bowler hat without understanding it. Franz believed in the Grand March — that all monkeys marching for a cause, any cause, made a single beautiful column of progress — and he marched off to the borders of the Great Jungle to protest a war, and the column was photographed, and a photographer stepped on a land-mine, and Franz, staggering, was clubbed by the very baboons he had marched beside, and died in Geneva of a wound nobody could find. So marches the Grand March.

Sabina went on — to Paris, to New York, to a hut by the sea — betraying each jungle in turn until there was nothing left to betray, only the unbearable lightness of a gibbon with no troop, no lice, no burden at all. "My enemy is kitsch," she said, "not the monkeys." When she was old she wrote a will asking to be cremated and scattered to the wind: *Here lies Sabina, who did not want to lie under a stone.*

Franz's own epitaph read: *A return after long wanderings.*

And Tomas and Tereza went to the mountains, to a little village where the vines hung heavy and no one knew about the black book. He drove a delivery vine-truck; she raised Karenin, their pet guinea pig, and photographed the aging faces of the village monkeys. Their son wrote them furious letters from the city, calling his father a traitor for leaving the revolution, which neither of them minded at all. It was a life pared down to two mandrills' worth of weight.

Karenin, their pet guinea pig, had no lice at all. That was the trouble with Karenin, Tomas said. A monkey without a burden is lighter than air. He rises above the troop. He is free, and his freedom is unbearable, because nothing in the world can hold him down.

So Tereza began to groom him deliberately, every evening, parting the fur along his back with her fingers, and he groomed her, and they did not speak of other females or other jungles; and if the grooming turned up a louse or two, they laughed and pinched it, and that was the whole of their philosophy.

The heaviest of burdens — a coat full of shared lice — presses us down, pins us to the branch, crushes us. But in the poetry of a monkey's love for another monkey, the heavier the burden, the truer and deeper the grooming. Monkeys, being no more monkey business than love is, could not tell the difference.

They died together in the end, tails entwined, when their vine snapped high above the valley. The village monkeys buried them under one stone, and it is said that Tomas smiled — for what better time to die than now, when the weight had finally been lifted, and what better place than here, where Tereza was.

Einmal ist keinmal. What happens but once, says the proverb, might as well not have happened at all.

But Tereza, in her last hour of grooming, believed exactly the opposite: that everything happens once, and once is everything; that there is no second grooming, no return to the branch. And that is why, she thought, as she picked her mandrill clean, a monkey's life is like a sketch drawn without an eraser — a single line, never corrected, and therefore unbearably, weightily, lice-ly beautiful.
