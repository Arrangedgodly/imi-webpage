# The Peel

*A parody of "The Pearl" by John Steinbeck*

Kino awakened in the near dark of the nest. The stars still shone through the woven canopy, and the day had drawn only a little wash of green light into the opening of the world. He looked beside him at Juana and at Cocotito, their baby, sleeping in his little hanging box, and Juana's eyes opened too, for she could sleep and wake and see him at once, as mother monkeys can.

And because the morning was quiet, the Hoot of Family rose softly in Kino's chest — the rubbing of one monkey against another, the morning song of the troop.

Then the Hoot of Trouble came.

A scorpion had come down the rope of the hanging box, and its tail was up, and its tail was aimed. Kino held his breath, and Juana held Cocotito's arm, and the scorpion felt the danger and paused, and then it struck.

Cocotito screamed with pain in his small voice, and Kino's fist crashed down and flattened the scorpion, but the cry went out over the troop, and every monkey's fur stood up.

"Go to the doctor's enclosure," Juana whispered.

The doctor was a fat mandrill who lived behind stone walls, where he kept caged birds and served only the rich troop whose cages were golden. He examined Cocotito's swelling shoulder without touching him.

"Are they of the rich troop? Have they nuts? No? Then tell the tree-monkeys I have gone to the far grove." And his voice said, as plainly as a voice can, when do these tree-monkeys pay?

Kino struck the stone gate with his fist and split his knuckles, and the Hoot of Trouble beat in his chest like a war drum.

So Kino went to the Great Ridge, where no troop gathered, where the wild bananas grew higher than any monkey had ever climbed. And in the crown of the tallest tree, past the sour bunches and the green, his hand closed upon a banana so vast and so golden that it took both arms to hold it — the Peel of the World.

Word ran ahead of him down the ridge, as word does. The priest appeared at the nest, suddenly recalled to a forgotten flock: "I hope thou wilt remember to give thanks, my son, in the proper place." Kino, dazed, heard himself promising a wedding in the church — he and Juana, properly mated at last — and marveled at how quickly the Peel had begun to speak through him.

The doctor remembered a cure for scorpion stings. He came to the nest that evening, studied Cocotito's shoulder like a monkey studying a fig tree he means to own, and left "a tiny white powder" in a leaf. Within the hour Cocotito was convulsed and near death; within two, the doctor returned, gravely administered a second leaf, and the swelling went down.

"The poison has gone inward," said the doctor, packing his bag. "It will return. But he is safe now — from the scorpion, and from my own skill, heh heh. I will be paid when the Peel is sold."

Kino looked at him with cool old eyes, and the Hoot of Trouble growled behind the Hoot of Family.

In the dark that night, Kino spread his dreams before Juana. A wedding in the church. New bark-cloth for them all. A steel harpoon with a bone handle. A rifle — and here even Juana's breath caught, for a rifle in a tree-monkey's paw was a wish the whole troop would hear. And last and greatest: "Cocotito will go to school. He will read the signs and count the accounts, and we will not be cheated, for he will know — and through him we will know — and the dealers' faces will go stiff when he passes."

Word of the Peel had also reached the banana dealers of the town, and the dealers, as it happened, were all branches of one tree. The first one examined the Peel with a jeweler's glass and sighed. "A curiosity. A monster banana. They grow hollow, you know, on the wild ridge. I will give you a thousand pebbles."

"It is worth fifty trunk-loads," said Kino. "I will not sell at fifty thousand."

The second dealer called it brittle and offered three hundred. The third would not look twice for less than five hundred. Kino, with the troop watching from the door, said: "I will go to the far capital, and sell it there."

The dealers' faces went stiff. Kino's own brother, Juan Tomás, walked him home through the shadows. "You have defied the dealers, brother. You have placed a price on a thing that has no price — and now there is no troop between you and those who want it. Be careful of the dark."

The dark came to them twice. First a prowler, sliding into the nest to grope for the Peel, and Kino fought him bare-handed and came away cut to the fur. Second, the next night, two shadows — and when Juana held the light, Kino stood over a stranger who would not get up, and there was blood on Kino's knuckles that no rain would explain.

Juana knelt beside him with the Peel glowing against her belly, and she saw, as monkeys before her had seen, what the great golden thing would do.

"It will destroy us all," she said. "Even Cocotito. Let me throw it back into the ridge."

"No," said Kino. "I am a chimp. This is my chance. Hush."

Before dawn she took the Peel herself and crept toward the ridge to be rid of it; and Kino caught her at the path, and struck her across the cheek with the back of his paw, and she looked at him with wide flat eyes as he snarled and wrenched the Peel away — and on the path home Kino was set upon again, and this time the Hoot of Evil sang in his ears and he fought like a troop of one, and came away with the Peel and half his ear gone.

They came home to ashes. Their nest had been burned in their absence, and Kino's canoe — his father's canoe, the one inheritance of the poor — had its bottom stove in with a stone. It is terrible to burn a nest; it is past terrible to smash a canoe, for a monkey with no canoe begs from no ape, and starves from no ape either. Juan Tomás hid them one night, and Kino, holding Cocotito, said only: "Tonight we go north. To the capital. And whoever follows us will be sorry."

They fled by night into the stone groves, pursued by three trackers with a jackal-hound that sniffed along their trail like fate. All day the trackers followed, and all night, Kino and Juana and Cocotito climbing higher and higher into the stone, with the Peel nearly as heavy as the baby.

Toward evening they found a small cave beneath a cold waterfall, and Kino climbed above the trackers' camp in the dark, silent as bark, a heavy coconut in each fist. Below, the trackers sat by their little fire; the jackal-hound sniffed up toward the cave; and one tracker raised his blow-pipe toward the black mouth of the cave — because he had heard, or thought he heard, a baby's cry.

Kino leapt. There was a scuffle, a howl, a flash, the whirr of a dart into the cave — and a long silence.

And out of that silence, Kino and Juana walked down the mountain, side by side, Juana carrying her shawl-wrapped bundle stiffly before her, and Kino carrying the Peel. The trackers sprawled senseless beneath the coconuts and the rage of an ape with nothing left; and the wailing that had come from the cave, and stopped, was the most terrible music either of them had ever heard.

They walked back to the town through the stone groves in the green morning light, and the troop gathered in silence to watch them pass — for a monkey and his mate may be followed to the ridge and back, and the whole troop knows what they carry. Kino held out the great Peel to his mate.

"You throw it," he said hoarsely.

"No, you," said Juana. And she put it back in his hands.

And Kino drew back his arm and flung the Peel of the World with all his strength, and it spun golden through the green light and sank into the deep masses of the Great Ridge, where no monkey would ever find it again.

And the hoot of the Peel faded to a whisper and was gone.
