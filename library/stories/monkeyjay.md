# Monkeyjay

*A parody of "Mockingjay"*

Katnip Evergreen woke in a bunk eight levels underground, in a district the whole jungle believed was ash. District 13 had never burned — it had simply dug. Down there lived the pale baboons, surviving on recycled rainwater and bananas so strictly rationed that hoarding a bunch was treason, and smiling during drills was considered suspicious.

"My name is Katnip Evergreen," she recited at the ceiling, the way the medics had taught her. "I am seventeen. My home is District 12. My sister is Prim. I am. I have to keep being."

President Coin was a silver-furred baboon with a clipboard where her heart should have been. "The rebellion needs a symbol," she announced from the assembly platform, "and you are already wearing one."

On Katnip's shoulder sat the pin half the jungle had scratched onto its banana crates: a little fire-colored monkey, mid-swing — the Monkeyjay. The Capitol had bred the Monkeyjay line to eavesdrop on its own citizens, but the little mimics had done something unforgivable: they'd learned to sing the lies back, in harmony, until the lies fell apart laughing.

"I'll be your Monkeyjay," Katnip said. "When you promise to rescue the captured tributes. Peeta. The others. And one more thing. When this is over, I kill Snow myself."

Coin made a note. "Agreed." On the clipboard, in the margin, Katnip would later swear, was a second column.

The propos began. Plutarch Bananasbee, gamemaker turned rebel, painted golden flames around Katnip's eyes and filmed her in battle armor made of white bird-of-paradise feathers, shouting into the grapevine camera: "Fire is catching! If we burn, you burn with us!"

"Could she possibly say 'bananas' instead of 'burn'?" begged Effie Twigket. "It tests so much better in the fruit districts."

"Bananas are catching!" Katnip snarled instead, standing in the ashes of a hospital the Capitol had firebombed. The reel played in every cave and canopy. Three districts went on strike the next morning. The war had a voice.

So, unexpectedly, did the past. Plutarch's cameras caught Katnip idly singing the old hanging-tree song her father taught her — *are you, are you, coming to the tree* — and by week's end the districts were singing it in the orchards, in the mines, at funerals, which in District 12 had always been the same crowd. Coin banned it within the day as inflammatory. The ban was sung back at her, to the same tune. "That," said Plutarch, delighted, "is the single finest piece of propaganda I did not make."

At terrible cost, the rebels smuggled Peeta out of the Capitol Canopy. Katnip ran to his bunk, and Peeta — her Peeta, who could talk about bread the way other monkeys talked about the moon — looked up at her and tried to strangle her with both hands.

"The Capitol poisoned him," the doctors said gently. "Tracker-jay venom. Every memory of you, twisted backward. They've convinced him you're one of their mutts."

Katnip sat in the doorway for a long time. Then she went back to the war, because the Capitol Canopy still stood, and she still had arrows.

There were bright hours anyway, down in the gray warren. Johanna Mason, sharing a hospital shift, taught Katnip to fight while insulting her hair, and never once mentioned the Capitol's rain tank, and flinched whenever the pipes dripped, and called the pine-scented soap they gave her "the only mercy in this dump." And one impossible afternoon Finnick Odair — trident, grin, rope — married his Annie in a ceremony of sugar-cube sculptures, because in 13 you applied for celebrations in triplicate, and love, it turned out, still got the occasional permit approved. Katnip sang. Nobody banned it.

The rebellion took the city block by block, past pods of scalding sap and swarms of engineered mutt-monkeys with mango-green eyes. Katnip's Star Squad — Boggs at the front, steady as a tree; Finnick knotting and unknotting a rope the whole march; Peeta walking carefully on his leash of questions, playing *real or not real* one memory at a time — carried a holo-map that beeped. When it sang, you ran.

In the sewers under the Canopy, it sang and would not stop. The mutts came out of the dark with Finnick's name on their teeth. He fought until his rope ran out. Katnip did not see the end of it, because Boggs shoved her onward, and afterward there was no body to carry, which in the old language of the sea means the same as goodbye.

On a street of white stone, the holo sang, and a hovercraft dropped silver parachutes onto a pen of Capitol children. The parachutes — like the ones Katnip had watched drop candy into the arena, like the ones Prim had squealed over at eleven — burst in a bloom of sticky fire. The medics ran in. And among them, small and quick and much too close, Prim.

Katnip did not remember the next three days. "That's the kindness of the mind, sweetheart," Haymuddle said, pressing a gourd flask into her hands. "Sleep it off. Even bananas bruise."

The war ended with President Snow, the white-muzzled old capuchin, caged in his palace garden among the roses. The whole jungle gathered for his execution. And there, on the balcony, President Coin proposed something obscene: one final Hunger Games, with the Capitol's young ones as tributes. To remind them.

"I vote yes," said Katnip, loud enough for every grapevine to carry. "For Prim."

Coin lifted her hand to give the signal. Katnip nocked her one remaining arrow, drew, and let it fly — not at Snow, but at Coin, who fell with her clipboard still clutched to her chest, schedule and all. In the square below, in the middle of the roaring, Snow began to laugh — a wet, rattling, delighted laugh — and laughed until something in his old chest gave way, and nobody ever established which monkey killed him, or cared.

They sent Katnip home to District 12 instead of to trial, because there is not much a jungle can do with a monkey who has already lost everything it was protecting. Gale took a handsome post in District 2 and sent no messages, because the last thing either of them needed was to say out loud that a parachute trap had been his design once, pitched to the rebels and turned back on them all; they simply let the silence do the arithmetic. For a while she was a hollow tree. She did not swing. She did not sing. She barely ate, which for a monkey is the surrender flag itself.

Then one grey evening she found Prim's cat, Buttercup, a starving rag of fur prowling the ashes of the Victor's Village, and the two of them screamed at each other, cat and monkey, grief and grievance, until they ran out of voice, and then she fed him, and he stayed, and that was the armistice: no forgiveness, just two survivors agreeing to be furious in the same house.

Then one grey morning Peeta came back, carrying a sack of primrose seedlings, and planted them under her window one by one. They played a game to rebuild his poisoned memories.

"You're my favorite color," Peeta tried. "Ripe or not ripe?"

"Ripe," Katnip said.

"I love you," he said. "Ripe or not ripe?"

She looked at him a long moment — the boy with the bread, the boy the Capitol could twist but never replace.

"Ripe," said Katnip Evergreen. "Ripe."

And the games, at last, were over.
