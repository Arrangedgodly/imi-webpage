# The Simiarillion

*A parody of "The Silmarillion"*

In the beginning Ilúvabough, the Elder Ape, taught the Great Hooting to the Ape-noor, first singers, whose voices rose like light through leaves. But Melkong, greatest and loudest, wove in a rhythm of his own — all teeth and drum and no ear — and the discord spread, choir tripping over choir, until Ilúvabough raised a paw and the music stopped.

"Behold your song," said Ilúvabough. "It shall be the world."

And the Ape-noor saw that the world — Arborda, the little round orchard — was made of their very hooting; and that Melkong's noise had been folded in, gnarled into the roots, past picking out. Even mischief gets woven in; and some came down to live in it.

Those were the Val-apes, the Powers, and they gardened. But Melkong knocked over the two Great Lamps, laughed, and was howled off to the ends of the map; then the Powers built the West-grove, where the Two Trees grew, silver and gold, flowering in turns to make the day. Under that light the gibbons awoke by the star-water, first-born singers of the canopy. (Later came the second-born, mortal apes, who the gibbons insisted — for centuries, loudly — had been a mistake. The Powers said nothing, which in an elder ape is a lecture.)

Then came the delved-apes, hammered out in secret by a Power who could not wait for the second-born and then, caught at it, was granted custody on condition they sleep until the gibbons had had an ample head start. They were stocky, stubborn, and magnificent underground, and they would regard the Simiarills the way sensible monkeys regard a beehive full of gold: with respect, from a distance, pending plans.

Greatest of the craft-gibbons was Fëanana, who thought faster than he groomed. In their light he made three Simiarills: golden bananas of impossible luster, in which the light of both Trees was caught and ripened forever. They were the most beautiful things ever made by paws, and paws being paws, everyone wanted them.

Melkong wanted them worst. He crept back as a mist with Ungoliant the Unhungry Spider, who drank the Two Trees dry — silver and gold withering black — and while the grove wept, Melkong stabbed open Fëanana's vault and carried the Simiarills to Hangband, his iron fortress under three smoking peaks, nailed them into his crown, and sat admiring himself.

Then Fëanana swore the Oath, and it was a beauty: "Be he beast or bird, be he foe or friend, no bar, no branch, no river-wide shall keep these Simiarills from us!" And the clever-paws marched out of paradise, drowning their cousins' swan-nests in the first kinslaying — which the sea remembered longer than they did — and the Blessed Grove closed, and grief came into Arborda, and stayed. Fëanana's seven sons pursued the Oath the way other apes pursue a dropped fig: downhill, forever, with increasing damage.

There followed ages of siege, woe, and heroic bad decisions. One tale is told most, and one is told worst — on purpose, at bedtime, to frighten young apes into obedience. That one is Túrin.

Túrin Turambar, son of Húrin, was cursed in the cradle: Melkong, having chained his father to a mountain chair for the crime of defying him, resolved to demonstrate that the whole family was hopeless, and arranged Túrin's life as a proof. Túrin, a mighty ape with terrible luck and worse listening skills, fled one tragedy into the next — out of the hidden halls into banditry, out of banditry into a doomed forest kingdom, out of that into a marriage under a false name, every step taken proudly and every step wrong. Against him Melkong loosed Glaurung, the Father of Dragons — a vast, clever, armored lizard whose favorite weapon was not fire but the well-timed lie. Glaurung's lies ruined three kingdoms at conversational speed; the actual fire was mostly punctuation. At last Túrin killed him, and the dying dragon told him one more lie, which was true: the ape he had married was his sister, who then threw herself into a ravine, whereupon Túrin fell on his sword. Melkong, watching from his chair, was delighted; Húrin, chained, wept thirty years in one afternoon; and the elders finish the tale with the traditional moral: "The curse was Melkong's, but the sword-handling was Túrin's. Groom your pride, little ones."

After Túrin, joy was rationed. But one tale of light survived: Beren was a mortal jungle-ape, an outlaw with no plan. Lúthien was the gibbon-princess of the hidden grove, fairest singer of the Children of Ilúvabough — when she sang, the trees leaned closer, and even the wind sat down. They loved each other; and her father Thingol, the Gibbon-king, set an impossible bride-price: "Bring me a Simiarill from the crown of Melkong."

Beren laughed at that, alone in the dark. Then he went.

Together they came into Hangband, and Lúthien danced before Melkong's monstrous court: the wolves asleep, the gob-rillas asleep, Melkong himself down off his throne into a sleep like a century of dusk. Then Beren, small and steady, took a knife to the iron crown and cut one Simiarill free. But the blade snapped, a spark flew, and the spark took his hand — the hand of Beren, in the crown, in the dark.

The great wolf Carcharoth swallowed hand and banana together and went mad with the burning; the hunt that followed fills three cantos. Beren died; and Lúthien's grief was so beautiful that Mandos the Doomsman, judge of the dead, stopped and listened — the first time in the history of the world. She was offered the endless West, and refused it.

"Then I choose the shorter tale," she said, "with Beren, over the endless one without him."

It was granted: they came back, mortal both, to the green middle of the map; and among the tales of grief and ruin that come down to us, the memory of her beauty remains — the whole use of sorrow, if a tale is told rightly.

The rest you know in outline, and the outline is mostly smoke. The last great hidden city, Gondolin, was betrayed from within — Melkong's dragons toasted it at dawn — and out of the burning came Tuor of the golden fringe with his lady Idril, carrying the child Eärendil; and Eärendil the mariner, with a Simiarill on his brow, sailed where living apes may not, begged pardon for a world, and was set into the sky as the Evening Ape, and sails yet. The Powers came back in wrath and broke Hangband flat; Melkong was chained outside the walls of the world, where he pushes at the gate and finds it, to his fury, locked from the outside. The other Simiarills were lost, one to fire, one to sea, for such bananas do not submit to being owned twice. To the faithful was given Númenut, a green island; but its kings grew proud and sailed against the Evergreen, and the sea folded it away like a leaf — nine ships escaped. From that remnant came the last alliance of apes and gibbons, the hand of Saurilla, and the One Vine — but that tale is told elsewhere, and no elder ape tells two great tales in one sitting.

Here ends the Simiarillion. If it has seemed long, remember: it is told by the eldest apes, who had nothing else to groom.
