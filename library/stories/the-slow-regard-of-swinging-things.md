# The Slow Regard of Swinging Things

*A parody of "The Slow Regard of Silent Things"*

On the first day, Auri found a bent spoon.

This was not a small thing. The Undergrowth beneath the Caniversity was very old, and it held its belongings the way a proud grandmother holds her teacups, and a bent spoon lying face-down in the Long Sigh was a wrong thing in a wrong place. Auri picked it up with both small owl-monkey paws and listened to it.

"You are not ruined," she told it. "You are a moon on a stick that had an accident."

The spoon did not disagree. She carried it home to the Root Cellar, where her hammock hung between two pipes like a secret, and hung the spoon — whose name, it turned out, was Pindle — on a hook where it would catch the lamplight and be happy. A thing improperly placed, Auri knew, is a small scream. She tried very hard never to make the Undergrowth scream.

In the Fern Gallery she fed Foxen, her glow-beetle, one drop of balm from the amber bottle, because Foxen burned brighter when he was fed and dimmer when he was lonely, and there was no sense in a lonely light. The green glow spread along the walls, and Auri regarded the swinging fern shadows for a long while, slowly, because quick regard is no regard at all.

Most creatures regard too fast. That was their trouble. They went grabbing through the world like a monkey picking all the bananas green. But Auri had all the time not being used by anyone else, and she spent it carefully, the way you spend a thing that is precious and not yours.

She had three rules, unwritten, which is the only honest way to keep rules. First: never take, only tend. Second: a found thing must be given a home, or given away — never hoarded, because hoarding is just greed with dust on it. Third: some doors are shut on purpose, and it is not her business, and — this was the hard part, the part she practiced on the fourth day, standing in front of the little gray door at the bottom of the Crockery with her paws folded — it would stay shut no matter how politely it was asked, even if asking felt, some nights, like the only thing left in the world to do.

She curtsied to the little gray door instead, and left, and only shook a very small amount.

On the second day, she washed. The water in the Twins ran clean and cold and said the same thing over and over, which was restful of it. She washed her one blanket and her three treasures and her small self, toes last, because toes are a finishing course.

On the third day, she mended a little brass hinge that had sulked shut for thirty years and deserved, at last, to open. On the fourth she found a key with no lock and a lock with no key, and introduced them, and left them alone to get acquainted. They were married by autumn. She had not attended the ceremony, but she had sent a gift, which is better manners anyway.

On the fifth day, Auri made soap.

Soap was not a small thing either. It took the good ash, and the clean tallow she had been saving in the Downy — the room with the bed in it, the bed nobody slept in, kept perfectly, for nobody — and patience, and quiet, and the exact number of stirs, counted out loud so the pot would know she was serious. She poured it into a leaf mold and set it to harden, and while it hardened she sat with it, because a thing left alone to become itself should at least be supervised kindly.

On the sixth day, Auri admitted the true business of the week.

He was coming.

He did not come often, and he was tall in the wrong ways, all elbows and hunger and a light he carried without knowing he carried it. She called him her Peripatetic, a word from a fallen book, because it fit him the way Pindle's hook fit Pindle. He was coming, and everything — everything — must be ready.

A place at the table, but not the wobbly chair, because his elbows needed room, elbows being half of him. A cup that was not chipped, because he would not notice a chip and she would notice him not noticing, and it would sour the soup. The good soap she had made, wrapped in a leaf of wax, set to the left of the basin, because he was left-handed in his heart even when he forgot.

And then there was the other thing. The gift.

Auri walked the whole of her small kingdom looking for it. The Hollow Elephant — which was not an elephant, only a room that remembered one — offered a green glass bead, which was kind, and wrong. The Crockery offered a saucer, which was polite, and wronger. In the Fern Gallery, Foxen flared his brightest green as if to volunteer himself, and she had to explain, gently, that he was not a gift, he was a Foxen, which was a much more important employment. She was growing a little desperate by the evening, sitting in the Root Cellar with her tail wrapped around her feet like a patient muff, when Pindle caught the lamplight and swung it at her, gently, and she understood.

Not a treasure. Not a key or a bead or a made thing.

The Undergrowth had exactly one perfect gift, and it grew.

On the morning of the seventh day, Auri went up the Long Sigh, and came back down holding it in both paws: one banana, perfectly ripe, gold all the way to the smile of it, without one gray spot anywhere, which in the whole history of bananas was a statistical miracle. She had visited it every day for eleven days, and turned it once, and told it about the weather, and asked it please, and it had, and now it was exactly itself, all the way through, which is the rarest ripeness there is: being entirely what you are, all the way through, at the right moment, for someone else.

She set it on the clean cloth at his place. She adjusted it a quarter-turn, so its best side faced the chair. Then she sat in her hammock and regarded the banana, and the soap, and the light of Foxen pooling green and steady in the middle of everything like a held breath, and she was, quietly, in every particular, satisfied.

"Hello?" she asked the silence, politely.

The silence, being well brought up, said nothing. But it listened.

Footsteps, later, above; the trapdoor shifted its dust; a voice came down the stair, warm and entirely too loud for a tunnel, calling her name — and there was room in the room for his elbows, and soap for his hands, and gold for his hunger, all of it proper, all of it placed, everything ready and right.

"Coming," said Auri, and her whole small face went bright.

It was a good day. And all the swinging things were glad.
