# Rhesus and Ramona

*A parody of "Beezus and Ramona"*

Beatrice Chimpy was ten years old, and everyone in her troop on Klickitat Branch called her Rhesus. Not because of her scientific classification — because of Ramona. When Ramona was a baby, "Beatrice" had come out of her little sister's mouth as "Rhesus," and it had stuck, and now even the aunts used it. Rhesus had to admit it wasn't the worst nickname for a monkey. It was better than some.

But then, everything about Ramona was like that. She was four years old, she had brown fur and enormous determined eyes, and she was — there was no polite way to say it — a menace swinging through the household.

Take the bananas. That very morning, Mrs. Chimpy had set out a fresh bunch for breakfast, and every single banana in it had one bite taken out of it. One bite each.

"Ramona Chimpy," said her mother. "Why did you bite every banana in the bunch?"

"So nobody else can have any," said Ramona, reasonably.

"Ramona —" began her mother.

"It works," said Ramona. "Nobody wants them now."

Rhesus put down her banana-peel cereal in disgust. "She's going ape again," she muttered. "Completely bananas."

"Rhesus," said her mother mildly, "she's four."

Four! As if four explained everything. As if four were a license for monkey business of every kind.

There was also the matter of Ribs, the family dog, whom Ramona rode like a bronco, dressed in paper hats, and introduced to strangers as "my trusty steed." Ribs, a patient old soul, endured it all with the dignity of a dog who had long ago accepted that this was his life now.

But the bananas and the bronco-riding were nothing, nothing, compared to the library book.

Rhesus loved the library. She loved the cool quiet of it, the smell of paper, the long shelves where a bookish young monkey could climb up among the stories and be alone. That week she had checked out her favorite book in the world, a beautiful red one about art, and she had been keeping it on the high shelf of her closet, safe from small grasping hands.

She should have kept it higher.

She came in one afternoon to find Ramona lying on the floor with the red book open in front of her and a fat purple crayon in her fist. On every single page, in thick waxy strokes, Ramona had drawn bananas. Purple bananas. Purple bananas on the Mona Lisa. Purple bananas on the pyramids.

"What — are — you — DOING?" whispered Rhesus, in the tiny strangled voice of an older sister at the end of her rope.

"Making it better," said Ramona, without looking up.

Rhesus had to take the ruined book back to the librarian and explain. It was the most embarrassing moment of her ten years on the branch. She had used her allowance to pay the damage fine, which meant no fruit ices with her friend Marsha for a month, and Mrs. Chimpy had said Ramona was too little to be blamed properly, so it all landed on Rhesus, which seemed — Rhesus thought, staring at the ceiling of the sleeping nest that night — unjust.

Sometimes, a terrible thought whispered in her darkest hours: *I don't love my little sister.*

Then came the summer art class.

Mrs. Chimpy had enrolled Rhesus in a drawing class at the community grove, and Rhesus loved it — charcoal, sketchpads, the soft scratch of drawing, the feeling of being someone other than Ramona's big sister. For three whole weeks she worked on a charcoal portrait of the old banyan tree, its branches spreading like held hands. Her teacher said it showed real promise. Rhesus carried it home flat between two pieces of bark, excited as a monkey with a fresh peel.

And Ramona, waiting at the door, flung herself upon it.

Ramona only wanted, she explained later, to hug the picture because she loved Rhesus so much. But she hugged it with a banana in each hand. The charcoal banyan tree acquired two large smears and one perfect handprint, and Rhesus stood in the doorway holding the ruin of three weeks' work and felt something inside her go snap, like a dry twig.

"Ramona," she said, in a voice that scared even herself, "I. Hate. You."

Ramona's lip trembled. Mrs. Chimpy arrived at speed. There was a scene, and Ramona cried, and Rhesus cried too, which surprised her, because she hadn't meant to cry, only to make Ramona understand, for once, once, that a big monkey's things mattered.

"Sometimes," Rhesus wept into her mother's shoulder, "sometimes I just don't love her. Is that awful? Am I an awful monkey?"

"You're a sister monkey," said her mother. "That's different from awful. Ask your Aunt Beatrice."

Aunt Beatrice arrived, in fact, two weeks later — for Rhesus's tenth birthday.

The party began beautifully. There were party games in the lower branches, and Rhesus wore her best ribbon, and her friends Marsha and Jane Ellen came, and even Howie from down the grove brought his tame beetle. There was a birthday cake, a magnificent banana layer cake with ten candles, sitting on the shelf, waiting.

Ramona had been told, twice, not to touch the cake.

But Ramona had a secret plan to make the party better. She had taken her rubber monkey doll, Bankie, and hidden him in the middle of the birthday cake while nobody was looking, so that when Mother cut it, everyone would get a surprise! A wonderful surprise! A doll-in-the-cake surprise, which Ramona could see no flaw in whatsoever.

So when Mrs. Chimpy cut the first slice and lifted it out, the entire middle of the cake came out too — collapsed, cratered, with a rubber monkey's grinning face sitting in the wreckage and vanilla frosting dripping off his little rubber ears.

"OOOOH," went the party guests, in the delighted tone of guests who love disaster.

"BANKIE!" announced Ramona, proudly.

The troop stared. Rhesus stared at her frosting-ruined, doll-cratered birthday cake, at her ten candles still standing in the wreckage, at her sister beaming up at her from the wreckage.

And then — and Rhesus never forgot this part — Ramona's face began to crumple. "The surprise came out wrong," she said, her chin wobbling. "It was s'posed to be a GOOD surprise."

Rhesus waited for the going-bananas feeling. Waited to shriek. Waited to say "I hate you."

Instead she started to laugh. She couldn't help it. The laugh climbed out of her like a monkey up a drainpipe, and once the guests saw the rubber doll wearing frosting like a little hat, they were all laughing, and even Mrs. Chimpy was laughing, wiping her eyes.

And then Mrs. Chimpy brought out the second cake.

The second cake — chocolate, with banana-cream filling — had been hidden in the pantry all along. "I always make two," said her mother calmly. "I've known Ramona for four years, you know."

Aunt Beatrice, cutting birthday slices with a flourish, leaned close to Rhesus.

"Your grandmother used to make two cakes for your mother's birthdays," she said, "on account of me. I was the Ramona of my generation. Ask anyone."

"You?" said Rhesus. Aunt Beatrice was the most graceful, grown-up monkey Rhesus knew — employed, stylish, excellent at checkers.

"Bananas, every one of us, at that age," said Aunt Beatrice. "Your mother got to be the Rhesus. That's how sisters work, little one. The nuisance grows up. The big sister survives. And it all comes out even in the end, like a checker game."

That night, after the guests had gone, Rhesus lay in the sleeping nest and listened to Ramona's small breathing beside her. Ramona was asleep with one arm flung over Ribs, who had also been fed cake, extensively.

"Ramona," whispered Rhesus, just to test it. "Hey, Ramona."

Ramona opened one eye. "Wha."

"You owe me a library book and a charcoal banyan tree."

"I'm four," said Ramona, and went back to sleep.

Rhesus looked at the ceiling of the nest for a while. She didn't love Ramona every minute. She probably never would. But that evening, when everyone was laughing and her sister's frosting-sticky hand had found hers under the table and squeezed — that, Rhesus decided, had been love, caught red-handed, purple-crayoned, and covered in banana cream.

She'd take it. Nuisance and all.
