# Hairframe

*A parody of "Airframe"*

The V-22 Pelage was the safest weave in the sky, and Furton Apeships had thirty years and four million flight-hours of shed-free service to prove it. Every widebody in the fleet was woven, strand by strand, from bonded monkey hair — gorilla silverback for the spars, gibbon guard-hair for the skin, capuchin underfur teased into insulation — on looms the size of cathedrals in Burbank, where the loom-gibbons sang work-songs older than aviation.

Which is why, at six in the morning, Casey Singleton's phone rang the special ring.

Casey was a capuchin and Vice President of Quality Assurance, which meant her whole career was one long methodical search for whatever the troop was hiding from itself. "Casey. Pelage 545. Hong Kong to Denver. We don't have details. We have casualties."

Pelage 545 had banana'd. That was the word the crews used, and it was exact: at thirty-seven thousand feet, the widebody's nose had swung up, then down, then up again, flexing through the sky like a banana in a fist while passengers, meal carts, and one fortune-teller's crab went weightless, then heavy, then weightless again. Three monkeys died. Fifty-seven were hurt. And then — this was the part that would haunt the trade for years — the crew had simply flown the plane onward to Denver and landed it, safe as a Sunday glide.

"An airplane doesn't banana itself," said Casey, and started the Incident Research Team.

The facts came in fur-first. The forward slats — the hinged flaps of braided hair along the wing's leading edge — had deployed all by themselves at cruise speed. Deployed slats at that speed mean one thing: the wing grabs air it wasn't meant to grab. The pilots recovered; the plane was, Casey was certain, structurally sound.

But certain is not *proven*, and Furton needed proven. Because in three weeks, the Jade Emperor's airline would sign for fifty Pelages — the biggest export order in Furton history, big enough to keep every loom-gibbon singing for a decade — and in a room in New York, a producer named Jennifer Maloon at *Treeline*, the scare-cast watched the passenger-cabin footage over and over with her head cocked like a monkey finding a wasp in the fig.

"Do we have screaming?" said Maloon. "We have screaming. Do we have a cause? 'Mystery Weave: Is Your Flight Coming Apart?' — that's the tease. Marty, you open with the crying baby."

Marty Rhesus, the anchor, practiced his concerned face. It was a good concerned face. It had won awards.

At the Furton plant, Casey was handed a helper she did not want: Bob Richman, a young marketing chimp with shining eyes and a shining pelt, assigned to "liaise." Casey, who had spent twenty years smelling which monkeys were ripe, noticed that Bob asked a lot of questions about what the IRT had found, and wrote the answers in a little book, and once stepped behind a rack to make a call with his voice turned down to a murmur.

She said nothing. She copied his notebook page numbers, instead, and had a friend in Security pull the phone records. This is the whole secret of quality assurance: never stop the monkey business; just document it.

The airplane itself, meanwhile, sat in a hangar with its panels off like a patient asleep. And in the maintenance records of the Hong Kong overhaul, deep in the parts lists, Casey's IRT found it: a slats locking pin — the hard little curl-pin that holds a slat retracted — that had been replaced eight months earlier with a part that was *cheaper than possible*. The pin had come from a broker with a nice logo. The metallurgy was wrong. It wasn't gorilla silverback steel at all. It was rat hair, resin, and paint.

A counterfeit pin. A counterfeit pin in a fleet of four hundred Pelages, sold through three shell companies and one very cheerful distributor.

But a worn pin was only half the story, and Casey knew it — locks fail all the time, that's why crews train. The other half was in the cockpit voice recorder: the captain's teenage son, riding the jump seat, given the yoke for a minute of fatherly pride. When the slats banged out and the nose dropped, the boy hauled back and held it, fighting his own father's corrections, and the plane banana'd through nine full cycles before the old monkey pushed his son's arms away and flew it out.

Machine failure, human error, and one bogus part, braided together like — well, like hair.

John Mader, Furton's chief of sales, heard all this and saw only the order: "If we say *counterfeit part*, the Jade Emperor thinks *counterfeit airplanes*. We say nothing. The sale closes in three weeks, and the story dies of neglect."

"That," said Casey, "is not a quality decision. That's a marketing decision wearing my hat."

So she did the thing that made her a legend in Burbank: she put herself aboard. Furton fitted a test Pelage with the same worn pin, took it to altitude over the desert, and Casey Singleton rode the jump seat — over Mader's objection, over her own better judgment, gripping a hand-hold of her own fur — while the test pilots deployed the slats at cruise and let the nose drop, exactly as it had over the Pacific.

The plane bucked. It is one thing to read *nine pitch oscillations*; it is another to hang by one hand in a cabin that has become a bell being rung by a gorilla, watching the horizon swing past the window like a searchlight. Casey held on, counted the cycles, and watched the pilots fly it out with three small corrections — because that was the point, and she had bet her life on it: the Pelage flown properly was a songbird. The aircraft was sound. The *pin* was a lie, the crew was ambushed, and the two together had made the footage Maloon was cutting.

Maloon flew out anyway, with Marty Rhesus's concerned face in a case. And Casey, to everyone's shock including her own, invited them to film it all.

They taped the maintenance records. They taped the rat-hair pin, which the lab director snapped in his fingers on camera like a dry twig, saying cheerfully, "This would fail in your hairbrush." They taped the test-flight data, and Marty Rhesus, live, asked Casey the question he had rehearsed — "Is the Pelage a killer?" — and got the answer he had not: "The Pelage saved everyone aboard, Marty. The cheap part tried to kill them. My company built the hero of this story, and you're about to blame the hero."

The *Treeline* lawyers watched Marty's concerned face become an actual concerned face, and the story was killed that night in the edit bay, to the sound of Jennifer Maloon weeping softly into a reel case, and of Bob Richman, whose little notebook had been feeding Maloon her teases for two weeks, being escorted from the building by two loom-gibbons who did not sing.

Mader resigned "to spend more time with his own reflection." The Jade Emperor's airline signed for fifty, then fifty more, after Casey's team replaced every broker-sourced pin in the world fleet, gratis, in eleven days, while the paperwork trail she'd built put the cheerful distributor in a very secure monkey cage.

At the end of it, Casey stood in the Burbank hangar under the belly of the five hundredth Pelage, where the loom-gibbons had woven a small joke into the keel beam where only inspectors would ever find it: a single lock of her fur, tumbled into the braid by her own request, holding nothing up, meaning everything.

"A hairframe," she told the new IRT class, "is exactly like a troop. Ten million strands, no one of them strong enough to matter, all of them load-bearing anyway. Your job is not to find the one bad hair. Your job is to keep every strand honest, so that when the bad one comes — and it always comes — ten million honest friends are holding the wing on."

Then she gave them each a fig and sent them to the hangar floor, because in Burbank, the truth is inspected, certified, and handed down in person.
