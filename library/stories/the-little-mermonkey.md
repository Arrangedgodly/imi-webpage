# The Little Mermonkey

*A parody of "The Little Mermaid"*

Far out in the deep green sea, where the water is as clear as banana nectar, lived the Sea King, an old sea-ape with a liana trident and six mermonkey daughters.

They were all lovely, but the youngest was the loveliest — soft fur like sea-mist, eyes like ripe plantain, and the finest singing voice in the seven seas.

She was a quiet, dreamy little mermonkey. In her garden under the waves she let only one thing grow: a red flower shaped like a low-branching tree, around a marble figure of a handsome young monkey, whose cold ears she would groom as she dreamed of the world above.

"When you are fifteen," said her grandmother, "you may swim up and see it."

"But why must I wait?" asked the little mermonkey. "The sun shines up there, and the monkeys walk on two legs, and the trees are full of fruit that has never once been wet."

"Rules," said her grandmother, "are like currents: you may swim against them, but you will arrive tired."

Then her grandmother told her the great secret of the two worlds. "We live three hundred years, my darling, and then we become foam upon the sea. But the monkeys above have souls — little warm lights inside them — that rise up higher than any tree when their lives are done. And there is one old story that says: if a mermonkey should win the true love of a monkey of the land, and be wed, she may share that rising soul."

"And if a monkey of the land should love me back?" asked the little mermonkey.

"Then you would share his soul," said her grandmother — and, tightening the little one's fur with careful fingers, she added, "but monkeys of the land, my darling, are not always wise enough to know what they love."

The little mermonkey groomed the marble ears and said nothing. But that night she sang to the moonless water, and the water was never quite the same again.

She counted the seasons, and on her fifteenth birthday rose like a bubble through the blue — and surfaced beside a great ship, its rigging hung with lanterns.

On the deck, monkeys in golden collars celebrated the birthday of their young prince, a capuchin with kind eyes and a laugh like a waterfall. The little mermonkey sang from the dark water, and the prince leaned over the rail, wondering where such music came from. Fireworks burst above the masts; the sky rained gold; and she thought the world above was worth three hundred years.

Then the storm came; the masts cracked like dry twigs, and the ship sank. The little mermonkey dove through the wreck, caught the drowning prince, and held his head above the waves all night, until she could lay him on the warm sand by the cliff-top temple.

As the sun rose she sang once more and hid. A young monkey girl from the temple found the prince and woke him — and he believed it was she who had saved him.

Her sisters knew a broken heart when they saw one. "There is one who might help," they whispered, "if you are brave."

So she went, through whirlpools and grasping polyps, to the Moray Macaque, the sea witch, old and grey and smiling far too much.

"I know what you want," said the witch. "Legs, for a mermonkey. But hear the price. Every step shall prick you like thorns. You shall never swim home as you were. And you shall pay me your voice — the finest in the seven seas, which is the whole reason I shall take it." She tapped a shelf of seashells, each one humming faintly with somebody's song. "I do love to sing at parties."

"If I take a monkey's voice," said the little mermonkey, "what will you give me in its place?"

"Patience," said the witch. "Yes or no, dearie. My polyps are waiting, and they rarely get visitors."

"I will pay it," said the little mermonkey, for a heart that has decided cannot be turned.

The witch brewed the potion in a cup of coral and took the mermonkey's voice as she drank — the sound went spinning into the smallest shell on the shelf, which rattled, satisfied.

She climbed the shore by the prince's temple, drank, and felt a pain like a thousand thorns — and where her strong tail had been, there were two legs.

The prince found her on the sand and marveled at her. She could not speak, but she was gentle and light as a leaf, and he kept her near, calling her his little foundling. She danced for him though every step pricked, and followed him up the cliff paths, further from the sea. He gave her a silken hammock outside his door, and told her stories, and once he said, kindly and terribly: "You love me best of all, little foundling — for you have the truest heart I know." And she believed, for a while, that love and a wedding were the same kind of thing.

Each evening she climbed the cliff behind the temple and let the sea-spray groom her fur, because spray is the next best thing to fingers, and the sea was the only part of home she could keep.

But a prince, however kind, is not always wise. One day he announced his wedding — to the girl from the temple, "for it was she who saved my life."

The little mermonkey could not tell him the truth. She had no voice, and the truth had no tongue. She braided the bride's flowers with shaking fingers.

The wedding was held on a ship, with sails of woven reed and a hundred lanterns, and the little mermonkey danced on thorns, and everyone said the foundling danced beautifully, and nobody knew why her eyes shone so. The prince told his bride that she had the voice of an angel, and the little mermonkey smiled and held the lantern higher — for the angel's voice had been hers, and no one would ever know.

That night her sisters rose from the sea, fur shorn short, traded to the witch, bringing a knife. "Sister," they wept, "before the sun rises, strike the prince, and his blood will turn your feet back into a tail. One life, or the other. The witch was very clear."

The little mermonkey took the knife and stood a long while over the sleeping prince. Then she kissed his forehead and threw the knife far out into the sea, and she climbed the rail, looked once at the sky going pink, and slipped into the water.

But she did not turn to foam.

She felt herself rising, out of the water and up into the cool bright air, carried on the sea breeze. Out of her sadness and her mercy a good thing had grown, and good things do not sink. She had become a daughter of the wind, one of the unseen riders who swing through the mast-tops of ships.

By gentle deeds, the older winds told her, she might one day earn a true monkey soul — fit to climb the Tallest Tree of All, whose branches no one in the sea has ever seen. And when the winds find a child sharing its fruit with a hungry stranger, they say, her climb is a day the shorter.

Sailors still tell of a warm wind that smells faintly of bananas, that fills the sails of kind ships and nips the ears of crews who share their fruit. And when a monkey on a cliff-top feels the breeze run soft fingers through its fur, like the world's gentlest grooming, and shivers without knowing why —

that is the little mermonkey, saying hello.
