# The Tommyknucklers

*A parody of "The Tommyknockers"*

Late last night and the night before,
Tommyknucklers, Tommyknucklers,
knuckle-walking up my door.
I want to climb down, don't know if I can,
'cause I'm so scared of the Tommyknuckler man.

That was the rhyme the juves sang in Happy Hollow all that strange green autumn. Nobody taught it to them. It was simply there one morning, the way the dew was.

It began when Bobbi Anderson, a howler monkey who wrote adventure serials for the *Jungle Gazette*, went walking behind her fig tree and tripped over something that was not a root, not a stone, and certainly not a banana — though it curved like one. Thirty feet of it, metal-bright, faintly humming, buried in the dirt like the world's biggest fruit dropped by the world's biggest customer.

"Should've left it buried," her friend Jim Gardener said later. Gard was a poet with a fermented-sap problem and a fine, despairing howl. "When you find something that big in the ground, Bobbi, it's usually down there for a reason."

But Bobbi dug. She dug for weeks. She rigged lamps, hauled buckets, invented tools to dig with — because the nearer she came to the great silver banana, the more the whole troop of Happy Hollow began to know things no monkey ought to know.

Her marmoset, Peter, changed first: he stopped eating, stopped sleeping, and hummed along with the buried hum in perfect pitch. Then Bobbi built a peeler that peeled bananas by itself, a typewriter that took dictation from her dreams, a lantern that burned on nothing at all. Her tail went stiff and glittered faintly green, and she stopped noticing. Down in the village, Old Doc Mushroom's granddaughter repaired the town clock with a paper clip and a wish; Farmer Hackett's wife built a mirror that showed tomorrow's weather; every juve in the Hollow went sleepwalking toward the woods at night and came back with green thoughts and green eyes, knuckle-walking in their sleep.

The inventions all had one thing in common: they were hungry. Every lantern battery in Happy Hollow went dark that autumn, and every watch stopped, and every toy drum rolled itself at midnight, so that the ship's toys could glow.

Then the juves began to disappear — not all at once, and not the way anyone feared. Hilly Brown, ten years old and an amateur prestidigitator of some local renown, announced one afternoon that he would now perform his greatest illusion, the Vanishing Brother. He put his little brother David in a fruit crate, threw a cloth over it, said the magic words (which were, roughly, "hocus pomus"), and whipped the cloth away.

The crate was empty. It was empty all the way through, which crates are not supposed to be. The audience of juves applauded, then waited, then applauded again, and then stopped. Hilly went home and lay down and would not wake. David did not come back, and the crate, when they broke it up for kindling, was still empty in a way that made the hatchet stink of ozone.

His grandfather, old Ev Hillman, beat on Bobbi's door with his cane. "Your hole in the ground took my grandson!"

It was the only accusation anyone in Happy Hollow made out loud all that autumn. Bobbi dug faster.

Gardener arrived in the middle of all this, fresh off a bender and a worse party — he had recited a poem about sap at a hostess's dinner and watched her cry into the fruit bowl, and he had taken the next ferry home. It took him one dinner to see it: his oldest friend was turning into something with a radio where her heart should be, and the whole town was singing backup.

He was also, as it happened, the only monkey in the Hollow whom the buried thing could not reach. A surgeon had set a steel plate in Gard's skull the summer a fig fell on him, and the great silver banana's green signal broke against it like weather. Gard called the plate his antenna. His doctor called it a soup pot. Either way, when the ship broadcast, Gardener got nothing but the menu music.

"It's a door, Bobbi," he said. "Whatever's down there is using every monkey in the Hollow as a key."

"The word it gives me," Bobbi said dreamily, "is *tommyknuckler*. Old miners' gossip, Gard — things that knock inside the rock. Except we haven't got rock. We've got this." She gestured at the trench, at the towering curve that filled the woods with green.

That night the dream typewriter typed by itself, four lines, while Bobbi slept upright in her chair:

*Late last night and the night before, / Tommyknucklers, Tommyknucklers...*

Gard read it and felt the soup pot in his skull go cold. "The ship wrote the rhyme, Bobbi. It taught it to the juves before you ever started digging. It's been knocking to be let out since before you were born."

"Then it can knock," said Bobbi, and smiled with someone else's smile.

Gard went out and sat at the edge of the trench until dawn, howling down into it — long, despairing, and, for one hour that night, useful.

The night the ship woke, the whole of Happy Hollow heard it — a knocking from under the world, knuckle on hollow wood — and every changed monkey in town rose and walked into the trees, and the great silver banana began to rise out of its pit, trailing roots and mud and jungle, lit green from bow to stern like a lamp made of spring.

*It rises incomplete,* said the voice in every changed head at once. *It will come apart in the air. It needs the heart.*

The heart was in Bobbi's shed: a green-glowing banana of light, humming like a nest of bees — the battery of the buried thing. And Bobbi, dreaming, humming, hers no longer, had already begun to drag it toward the woods.

Gardener howled the great howl of his line, bit down on every fear he had ever nursed, and did the one heroic thing of his unremarkable life: he hooked the heart to Bobbi's pickup, hauled it howling through the trees — while the changed monkeys of Happy Hollow reached for the tailgate with green, forgiving eyes — and drove it straight off the bank into the salt pond.

*FIZZ,* said the world. And then, softly, *fzzz.* And then nothing at all.

The ship dropped back into its hole with a sound like a disappointed stomach. Every green light in Happy Hollow went out at once. The changed monkeys woke among the ferns with headaches and no memory of the month. And out over the orchard the air split with a pop, and David Brown fell eight feet into a hay net — hungry, blinking, unbruised, and unable to say where he had been except: "It was Sunday there. It was always Sunday."

Hilly woke the next noon, sat straight up in bed, and said, "Ta-da."

The great silver banana settled into its dirt and began, politely, to rot — or something very like rotting — until it was only a rumor and a smell. Gardener poured his last jug of fermented sap into the pond for the occasion, and Bobbi, wobbly but herself, sat beside him on the bank.

"Gard," she said, "the children's rhymes. Do you think they'll sing them still?"

"No," said Gardener. "Not a knock. Not a knuckle. Not a note."

But late some nights, they say, when the moon hangs over the Hollow, a young monkey will wake in her nest and hear, very far away and very far down, somebody knocking to be let out.

And she will pull the blanket over her head, and hug her tail, and sing:

Late last night and the night before...
