# David Copperpeel

*A parody of "David Copperfield" by Charles Dickens*

Whether I shall turn out to be the hero of my own life, or whether that honor shall be held by some other monkey with more bananas, these bark-pages must show. I was born on a Friday afternoon in the crook of the old fig at Blunderstone, clutching a banana in my tiny fist — a sign, the neighbors agreed, of great fortune, and a personal insult, as it turned out, to my aunt.

For Aunt Betsey Troopwood had marched through the jungle that very day, determined that her great-niece should bear her name, and when Peggotty — our shaggy orange nurse, an orangutan whose fur stood up like a thicket — announced that I was a boy, my aunt looked at me with such fury that I buried my face in my banana.

"A boy!" she cried. "A boy and a banana! Monkeys and mischief!" And she stalked off into the ferns without so much as grooming my mother's head.

For some years we were happy, my gentle mother and I, with Peggotty and her family, who lived in a great upturned canoe in the trees at Yarmouth, where little Em'ly gathered shells, and her cousin Ham was a young gorilla of tremendous tenderness, and Mrs. Gummidge was a widow who "knowed it was a-failing" whenever the wind came in. That household was built entirely of orphans — "they ain't no kin to me," said Mr. Peggotty, "but fur makes kin" — and Em'ly and I gathered shells on the sand and were briefly, perfectly, in love, as children are.

Then came Mr. Chillstone. He was a gray gelada with a cane and a granite stare, and he brought with him a sister equally gray — Miss Chillstone, a steel ape with a bag of little bells — and life became a fence to be kept behind.

"Trotwood," said he (for he called me by anything but my name), "firmness is the very bark of character. Remember, David: if the banana is green, the banana is eaten green."

Under this regime I bit through many a raw plantain — and, on my last morning at home, out of pure terror, I bit through Mr. Chillstone's paw as well. For this I was sent to Salem House wearing a placard: TAKE CARE OF HIM. HE BITES. There the baboon Creakle rapped my knuckles, and the splendid older chimp Steerforth bought all my bananas with promises and called me Daisy, and protected me, and slept through every lesson, and was adored by every ape in the school, including, I am sorry to say, me.

Then my poor mother faded — she and the baby that never cried — and I came home to a little tombstone under the fig, and Peggotty's fur was rough with crying for a month. I was sent, at ten years old, to the warehouse of Murdstone and Grinby's, where I washed banana stems from dawn to dusk and examined "M" and "G" on the corks.

It was there I lodged with Mr. Micawber, a spider monkey with a monocle and boundless hope. "Copperpeel!" he would cry, dangling by one arm. "Something will ripen! Of that I am confident. Annual 'nanas twenty, annual appetite twenty-two: misery. Annual 'nanas twenty, appetite eighteen: happiness. Mark the formula, my dear Copperpeel!" And when nothing ripened, Micawber was flung into the debtor's cage, and I resolved to run away to my aunt in Dover.

I sold my waistcoat to a long-tailed old clothes-ape, was robbed of my half-guinea by a tinker monkey with a furless tail, walked the ridge road till my paws bled, and arrived on my aunt's lawn ragged, weary, and banana-stained — just as she laid about her with a walking stick at the spider monkeys who dared cross it.

"Don't ride over my lawn!" she thundered, and then, catching sight of me, softened. "Good gracious, child, you're the Banana Boy himself. Mr. Dick, fetch a grooming comb. We shall clean him up and call him Trotwood."

Mr. Dick — a harmless grey chimp with a kite and a memorial that never got written, because the King's head would keep getting into it — flew kites with me and pronounced me sound; and Aunt Betsey adopted me on the spot, informing Mr. Chillstone, who came to protest, "I see a firm gray ape, sir. Firmness is your bark — get out of my garden."

Under her care I went to school in Canterbury, lodging with Mr. Wickfield, an elderly bonobo, and his daughter Agnes, a serene young ape who always seemed, when I looked up, to be pointing upward.

"I am much obliged to you, Agnes," I said one evening, "for always pointing up."

"The way is up, Troopwood," she said quietly. "The way has always been up."

There, too, in the office below, sat the clerk Uriah Heap — a greasy capuchin who wrung his paws and purred, "I'm a very 'umble 'nana, Copperpeel. We're all very 'umble, us 'nanas, waiting in our skins." His 'umbleness hung about him like a smell. At school I had also found Traddles, the jolliest skeleton in England, who drew skulls on every blank leaf, expected to be cheated of everything, and usually was.

I confess I did not attend to Agnes's pointing. For I had met Dora-bell, a marmoset with flowers in her fur, and I went perfectly bananas. "Oh, Dora!" I cried. "Be my prime mate!"

"Barkis is peelin'!" she laughed, which was the only proverb she knew, and we were chimpions together, though she could not keep the fruit-ledger and called the greengrocer "a wicked old mandrill." When we married, I found I had wed a child-wife: our housekeeping collapsed under two apes who could not cook; her lap-monkey Jip bit my slippers; and when I tried to teach her bookkeeping she tied the ledger's ribbon in her fur and cried. I learned — slowly, and too late to matter — to let Dora be Dora.

For meanwhile the past was ripening badly. Steerforth had come with me to Yarmouth — charming Mr. Peggotty, charming Ham, teaching Em'ly to dream of being a lady — and one morning Em'ly was gone. Run off. Steerforth's doing. "I'm a-going," said Mr. Peggotty, taking up his stick, "to seek my niece through the wured. I'll never leave off, and I'll never falter. Not one step shall I turn till I find my Em'ly." And he strode into the world with the sea-wind at his back, and Martha Endell, once lost herself, walking ahead of him through the low places where lost monkeys go.

Then came Heep's harvest. Uriah had wound himself into Mr. Wickfield's business, duped the old bonobo into signing what he could not read, set his 'umble eye on Agnes — and made my aunt's fortune vanish by forgery. It took the returned Micawber, hunting through Heep's books with the fury of a wronged creditor, to strip his schemes bare before the whole troop.

"Bah! You're a shark, Heap! A shark with a peeled conscience! A scorpion, sir — an eel in a banana-skin!"

"You're very violent, Copperpeel," said Heep, writhing. "But I'm 'umble. I was always 'umble." And so he was, ever after, in the debtors' cage, where he begged me to come and witness his 'umility.

The last of it came at Yarmouth, in a great storm. A wreck drove in under the canoe-house; a rope was thrown; and Ham went into the sea to save a stranger — and the sea took both. When the second body was brought ashore, it was Steerforth, lying on the sand with his pretty head upon his arm, as I had seen him sleep at school.

Dora went away, at last, to the warm southern orchards where the fruit is always ripe, and left me her little grooming comb, and one request: "Don't judge me. I was only a child-wife." And when my grief had softened, Martha found Em'ly and brought her home broken and forgiven; and the whole canoe-house troop — Mr. Peggotty, Em'ly, Mrs. Gummidge, Martha — sailed away on the emigrant ship to the far southern jungle, where Mr. Peggotty became a legend among the settlers, and Em'ly, doing well, never looked at the sea but once a year, and never told any ape why.

And Agnes was beside me, pointing upward still.

"Agnes," I said, "I have wandered far, and peeled badly, and been a very great fool. But I believe the hero of my own life, after all, was at my side the whole time."

And she, pointing upward, is beside me still.
