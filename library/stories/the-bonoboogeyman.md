# The Bonoboogeyman

*A parody of "The Boogeyman"*

Lester Biloba came into the office already wringing his tail like a dishrag, and Dr. Hooten — pipe, calm eyes, grooming diplomas behind him — said the thing doctors say: "Take your time, Mr. Biloba."

It was their fourth session. The first three, Lester had spent on the weather, the troop economy, and the weather. Today he sat down different — wrung out, and done wringing.

"I'll start with the pantry," Lester said. "It always starts with the pantry."

He was a big bonobo gone loose at the seams.

"First it was our little Andy. Two years old. Woke up screeching three nights running — *bad dreams, Daddy, bad dreams* — and every morning the pantry door was open. Not wide. An inch. Just an inch, and the smell of old bananas coming out of the dark like breath." He laughed a terrible little laugh. "Ruth blamed me — *Lester, you and your midnight snacks.* But I never left it open. That's the rule of any decent troop: keep the 'nana store shut. And every morning — an inch. I took to measuring the gap with a twig. Ruth said a twig that lies is a scary twig. Nobody laughed.

"The fourth night I didn't check. I plugged in a night light, the kind shaped like a firefly, and told myself that fixed it. All it fixed was giving the dark a shape to be. In the morning the crib was empty and the pantry door stood wide as a grin, with little bare footprints in the spilled peel dust, going in. Not coming out."

Dr. Hooten made a note. Lester watched him make it with sudden hatred.

"Did you call the zookeepers?" the doctor asked.

"Two of them. One wrote *pest problem* on a form and the other left a trap with peanut butter in it. It caught Ruth's patience. That was all it ever caught."

"Go on, Mr. Biloba."

"Ruth cried for a year and blamed me for two. So we moved. New tree, three towns over, fresh twigs, and a pantry I fitted with a new latch myself. The first morning — an inch. That's when I knew it wasn't the latch.

"Then it was Shirl. She was four. She wouldn't eat bananas anymore, not even fried, not even in bread. Said they *whispered* at her. Said she could hear them being peeled somewhere all night, neat and slow, one at a time, like something counting her down to zero. The night it happened she said, 'Daddy, the pantry's breathing.' I told her pantries don't breathe. I told her that, Doc. To her little face."

The tip of his tail had gone gray.

"And the zookeepers that time?"

"Looked in the pantry. Looked at me. One of them said, calm as a vet, 'Sir, bananas don't peel themselves.' And the door was an inch open behind him the whole time, and I watched him not look at it."

Dr. Hooten's pipe had gone out. He didn't mention it. He said, "Denny."

"Denny was six when it took him. And Denny saw it. Came screeching down the hall at midnight, and I held him and asked who, who was in the pantry, a stranger, a big monkey, *who* — and Denny looked up at me with his eyes rolling white and said:

"'It wasn't a monkey, Daddy. It was the Bonoboogeyman.'

"And Doc, I opened that door myself. I flung it wide and turned on the light, and there was nothing but our 'nanas — except every single banana was peeled. Every one. From the top down, neat as Sunday, the peels lying on the shelf in a little row like they'd been *arranged*. Sixty-one bananas, Doc. I counted. And the smell — old bananas, sickly sweet, like a pantry breathing out.

"That's when I moved the family out of that tree entirely."

"And in all those years," Dr. Hooten asked, "did you ever see it? Yourself?"

"Never," Lester said, and his voice came down to a thread. "I saw the pantry. I saw the inch. I saw the peels in their little row, neat as Sunday — and once, Doc, once: a shape in the gap of the door, filling it wall to wall, and one eye coming around slow, like a peel uncurling. And I ran. It let me run, and I've had thirty years to figure out why running felt like part of its plan."

Dr. Hooten relit the pipe with paws that were mostly steady. "You said Ruth left you."

"We went to a motel. Tree-house motel, three towns over. A room *without* a pantry — just a kitchenette and a cooler. That was the whole point. I locked the cooler, put the chair against the closet door, and lay there all night listening to my heart go like a drum solo.

"Around four I had to pee worse than any monkey in recorded history. I was gone under a minute.

"When I came back, the chair was moved. The closet door was open an inch. And the closet smelled like old bananas.

"I never opened that door. Not an inch, not ever. I sat against it till sunup while my boy across the room said, without waking, soft as a leaf turning over: 'Daddy, it's dark in here.' And in the morning Denny's bed was empty, and the closet stood wide, and the peels were arranged in a row.

"Ruth left me that August. Never said goodbye. I never blamed her. A father has one job — keep the pantry shut. And who'd believe the truth? They'd put a net over me.

"You believe me though, don't you, Doc? You've got a *believing* face."

Dr. Hooten said, kindly, that their hour was up. He had to step out for a moment, but Mr. Biloba should make an appointment with the girl at the desk. Same time next week.

Lester went out. There was no girl at the desk. There was no desk, really, once you looked — just a card table and a fern, and dust on both, years of it.

And coming back up the hall, he noticed three things in the order that would ruin the rest of his life.

The office door was now shut. Through it, faintly, came the smell of old bananas.

And the closet door at the end of the hall, the one marked STAFF ONLY, was open. Not wide.

An inch.

Lester stood there shaking while the light under the office door went out, and from behind the STAFF ONLY door, in a small, patient voice he knew by heart, Denny said:

"Daddy? It's still dark in here."

The office door opened. The doctor was grinning, and the grin was too wide, going on forever, rind-yellow, the grin of something that had never once been a monkey, and it said:

"I told you I'd be back. Silly bonobo. You should never have left the pantry. Three little 'nanas, Lester. I counted them off one inch at a time."

Lester Biloba ran, arms over his head, out of the building and into the rest of his life — and behind him, softly, unhurried, the STAFF ONLY door swung itself shut.

To an inch.
