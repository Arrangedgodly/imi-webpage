# Doctor Screech

*A parody of "Doctor Sleep"*

Danny Torrance was a howler monkey with a hollow leg and a problem: the screech wouldn't shut up.

When he was a boy it had burned bright and terrible — enough to wake an old hotel full of hungry guests. Grown, he drowned it the way his father had drowned everything, except where his father had used the bottle, Danny used the barrel: banana wine, jungle juice, anything with a fizz and a lie in it. One morning he woke in a mulberry bush two valleys off course, wearing one boot and somebody's tail-wrap, and made the monkey's lowest prayer: *Help me.*

He got it, too, in the form of the Frazier troop, who ran a hospice at the top of the mountain called the Resting Roost. They gave him a broom, a cot, and eventually a purpose, because old monkeys came there to finish, and Danny — sober, or near it — found that if he sat close and screeched soft, low, and lullaby-true, the dying dreamed of morning canopies instead of the dark.

The name came from the oldest monkey in the place, a blind capuchin named Emmeline who spent her last night dreaming out loud about a long train ride through sunny treetops. When the sun in her dream finally rose all the way, she patted Danny's paw and said, "You're the doctor of the last good dream, child. Doctor Screech." She finished before breakfast, smiling. The staff took up the name like a hymn. He let them.

Azzie the bushbaby helped. Azzie's ears were satellite dishes, and she knew which monkey was next. She'd appear on a sill, tail curled, and Danny would come running with his softest screech. *Sic transit,* he'd think, grooming a trembling paw. *There goes every monkey, someday, swinging off the last branch.*

Tuesdays he went to the noon meeting under the big fig — the Fellowship of the Frond — where they said the only magic words he'd ever trusted: *one banana at a time.*

Then Abra Stone's banana letter arrived.

It came written on the inside of his dreams, in letters of squashed yellow fruit: **ABRA SENDS HER LOVE.** Abra was nine, a howler from three valleys over, and her screech could carry across seven of them. She'd made a friend on the shine, the way some monkeys make friends through a knothole — and then her friend had been taken.

She showed him, in a dream that smelled like thunder: a caravan of branch-cars rolling through the high pass, and riding in them the True Knot, a troop of rogue macaques older than any troop had a right to be, led by a smiler named Rose the Flower. They fed on the sparkle — the last happy scent of young monkeys with the shine, sucked up like the steam off a fresh-peeled banana.

"They took Banana Brad," Abra said. "He's in a cage on their roof. They're saving him. They like them frightened, Dan. Fear seasons the banana." She shuddered. "I want to bite her, Dan. I want to bite that Flower lady right on her smug face."

"Hold that thought," Dan said. "But we're going to be smarter than that. I was raised by an angry hotel. I know how hungry things work."

First he taught her the cupboards. Every monkey with the shine needs cupboards, he told her, in the kitchen of the mind. Jars on every shelf. Scary things go in jars. Jars get labels.

"And if the Flower lady knocks?"

"She doesn't get a jar," Dan said. "She gets a whole pantry, and the pantry is a trap, and we set it together."

She knocked anyway, first. That was Rose's way — she liked to visit before she took a meal, the way some monsters send a fruit basket. Dan felt it happen from two valleys off: Abra's dream going sweet and wrong, a garden party with a lady in a flowered hat standing too close.

"Hello, little one," Rose said through the dream. "Such a bright baby. Rose only wants a smell."

"Then smell this," Abra said, and slammed the cupboard of her mind shut — every jar labeled, every shelf bolted, the whole pantry of her skull done up in chrome and padlocks, because Dan had taught her well. Rose recoiled all the way across the pass, holding her nose. Azzie woke every monkey in the Roost doing something between a laugh and an alarm.

"She knows us now," Dan said grimly, watching the dark from the porch. "So we move first."

At Mango Gap, they dug the trap overnight: a pit across the whole trail, filled with overripe fruit, and over that a screen of innocent palm fronds. When the True Knot's branch-cars came sniffing after the bait scent — Dan's own shine, left out like a picnic — the front wheels went in, then the back, then the entire ancient troop of them.

The SPLORTCH was heard in the next valley. The pit, it turned out, carried the Ripe Rot, the one fruit-sickness old macaques never survive. The Knot floundered, aged a hundred years in a second, and came apart into confused, exhausted old monkeys who surrendered to the first zookeeper they saw. Crow Uncle, Rose's second, came out of the pit waving a white tail-wrap and asking, with dignity, whether the zoo had pudding.

Rose the Flower alone floated free, thornless and smiling.

She came for Abra at the Overgrown Lookout — the ruined hotel on the mountain where Danny had been a boy, now a shell of rotted beams and empty ballrooms with a hive of quiet at the middle. She took Abra's paw at the door — got exactly that far — because Dan had taught the girl well: Abra shone like bait, and the pantry closed behind the Flower like a lid.

"Little screecher," Rose said to Abra, standing in the lobby of nothing. "All that sparkle. Come give Rose a smell."

"She's not alone," Dan said, stepping out, and behind him, faint as morning fog, the ruin itself seemed to lean in, because empty places get hungry too. "You want to feed, Rose? Feed here. This is the emptiest pantry in the whole jungle. Stay awhile. Stay forever."

The Lookout took her the way a sinkhole takes a fallen tree. The last they heard of Rose the Flower was a very small, very surprised sniff.

Banana Brad went home to his troop, chunky and undigested, sneezing blessings on his enemies.

There was one more piece of business, and Dan did it because leaving it undone is how old hungers come back. He and Abra, with Azzie riding shotgun, visited the Knot's abandoned camp in the high pass — the branch-cars parked askew, the little clocks all stopped at their feeding hour. There was nothing left to fight. The Ripe Rot had finished the argument. They unplugged the clocks anyway, out of respect for the sparkle, and Dan said the words he said over every monkey, even those: *sic transit.*

"Sic transit what?" Abra asked.

"Every monkey, someday," Dan said. "Even the ones who deserved it less. Even the ones who deserved it more."

Abra's folks let her visit the Roost on Sundays, where Azzie rode her shoulder like a fancy epaulet. Emmeline's bed had a new tenant by then, and on her first Sunday Abra sat close and Dan showed her the lullaby-screech, soft, low, and true.

"Does it always work?" Abra whispered.

"No," Dan said. "But it always matters."

That last night, Dan stood on the porch under the first stars, missing his father — the sick monkey who had loved him and lost to the bottle just the same.

"You were a mess, Pop," Dan told the sky. "But you were my mess. I turned out okay. The screech isn't for scaring anybody, you know. It's for finding who's lost."

Down the mountain, faint across the valleys, Abra howled goodnight.

Danny screeched back. It carried, as far as it needed to.
