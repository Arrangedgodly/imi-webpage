# The Knuckle Boys

*A parody of "The Nickel Boys"*

Elwood Curtis believed two things: that the speeches on his grandmother's cracked record were addressed to him personally, and that a monkey could keep his fur clean in a dirty world. The record was of the great peaceful silverback — the Chimpion, the grandmothers called him — and his voice came up scratchy and certain out of the grooves: *You are good. Be good, even when they are not. Especially then.* Elwood played it so often the grooves wore down into little canyons.

In Tallybananassee the zoo-born macaques ran everything worth running, and chimps rode at the back of the fruit carts. Elwood's grandmother saved figs against every hard winter and taught him correct posture and incorrect patience. When the branch college opened a free class for chimps, Elwood enrolled — first in line, books borrowed, tie made of braided vine — and on the opening morning he hitched a ride in a fig-cart that turned out, in the official view of things, to be stolen. Nobody asked Elwood's side of it. The judge asked if he was sorry, and Elwood said the monkey who owned that cart should be sorry, which the court took as a no. The sentence was Knuckle Academy for Wayward Young Monkeys, motto: *Physical, Moral, and Arboreal Development.* The reality was monkey business.

At Knuckle they sorted you at the gate. Boys with school-shaped futures became Graduates, who appeared in the letters sent to the state; the rest became the other kind, who appeared in the fields, the kitchen, and the laundry. Elwood, who could read three languages and write one worth reading, was made a Graduate, which meant he was counted and invisible in the same column. Every morning there was Chapel, where Director Spencertail preached the Academy's great tradition of uplift, and every afternoon there were the banana crates stenciled FOR THE ORPHANS — crates that went, every one, onto the Director's private cart while the boys' rations came green. Elwood did the arithmetic in his head for a week before he understood it: there were no orphans. There were only the boys who unloaded the crates.

And there was the little whitewashed shed at the edge of the yard that everyone called the Greenhouse, because boys went in wet-eyed and came out wilted for weeks. Inside, you were stretched over an iron cot-frame and given the strap until your howl ran out. Nobody came out of the Greenhouse talking about uplift.

Elwood wrote everything down in a school notebook, because the Chimpion had said the arc of the moral canopy bends, and Elwood believed it needed witnesses.

"Don't write it down," said Turner, who had been at Knuckle three years and had the whole score. "You keep your head below the canopy, you laugh at the right time, you give nobody your banana. That's the whole curriculum."

"The Chimpion never met Director Spencertail," Elwood said.

"The Chimpion," said Turner, "never had to eat the rations here."

They were an unlikely pair, the believer and the survivor, and Knuckle did what it could with both of them. Elwood was assigned to Community Service, which meant typing the Director's correspondence, and so learned the school's finances from the inside: figs bought at one price on paper and another in the world, a boiler replaced three times that never once worked, and the school's labor leased out by the crate-load with the wages going north to Tallybananassee while the boys went without. One letter in the file concerned a boy named Griff who had written to ask about his trust fund. The reply was two words long.

Then came the exhibition bout. The Director had arranged the boxing for visiting zoo-born patrons, and it was understood that the Knuckle boys would fall in the third round, gracefully, like well-trained fruit. Griff was matched against a patron's huge pet and would not go down — you could see him deciding it, round after round, keeping his feet out of pure obstinacy, settling some private account in public — and in the silent gym there was one crack, and Griff did not get up, and the school paper printed FLED. The patrons were delighted by the competition. The Director was delighted by the paper. Griff's cot was reassigned by breakfast.

Elwood wrote that down too.

"When you going to learn," Turner asked, not unkindly, "that the canopy doesn't bend on its own?"

"Somebody," Elwood said, "has to pull."

The state inspectors came in spring, three macaques with clipboards and a van. The Director walked them past the Greenhouse ("storage"), past the crates ("charity"), and into a chapel choir that had been rehearsed for a month. Elwood had the notebook page folded in his fist. He handed it to the youngest inspector coming out of the privy, which was the one place the Director did not supervise.

For his trouble he went to the Greenhouse twice. And that same night he was moved to the back gate with a cohort "for transfer" — which every boy knew meant the banana rows and no record at all.

"Transfer," said Turner. "It's the one subject they teach here better than anybody."

Turner was on the list too. Of course he was: he had been seen talking to Elwood, and at Knuckle friendship counted as conspiracy, which made it the only class Elwood ever finished with honors.

They went over the fence instead, into the rows, running bent under the broad leaves in the dark, with the school's truck lights swinging somewhere behind them.

"The drainage ditch past the third turning," Turner panted. "Then the county line. Keep climbing, Curtis. You're good at climbing."

"I'm good," said Elwood, "at everything they say we're not."

The crack came out of the dark behind them. Elwood went down into the ditch mud, and Turner dragged him, and dragged him, and then stopped, because it was no longer any use. Elwood looked up past the banana leaves at the little stars you could see between them, and it occurred to him, in the way of last thoughts, that the stars did not care, and that this was exactly why they were worth writing down.

"You're a good one, Turner," he said. "Climb for both of us."

On the far side of the fields, a young monkey came down out of the trees and walked into the next county wearing Elwood Curtis's name, because it seemed to him that the name deserved a body that was going somewhere, and his own did not.

It took him a while to learn to wear it. He answered slowly to Elwood. He wrote left-handed, because Elwood had. He kept his fur clean in a dirty world, which was the least of the two of them he could carry. And every few years, in the night, he mailed a page to some monkey with a printing press — the crates, the ledgers, the two words about Griff's trust fund, the arithmetic that never balanced — until the pages sat in drawers so long that when the story finally broke, decades on, there was nothing left to argue with but bones.

They came with ground-sniffing tamarins and dug up the rows behind the old Greenhouse and found them — dozens, unrecorded, unnamed. The Academy's alumni association, which had spent forty years meeting annually to remember the good old chapel, discovered it could not remember any of these boys at all, and said so in a statement.

An old monkey who had lived a long time as Elwood Curtis read the news, and then read it again, and went down to the dedication with his hat in his paws.

"My name is Turner," he told them, in front of everyone. "The monkey you're naming this for was my friend. He believed the canopy bends. It doesn't — not on its own. It bends because somebody pulls. He pulled. He's the one who ought to have made it out, and the only reason he didn't is that he stopped to hand a page to the one inspector he thought might read it."

He looked at the long row of small markers.

"I ran faster," he said. "That's all I ever did faster than him."

They hung a bronze bunch of bananas on the gate, with his name under it — ELWOOD CURTIS — and beneath that the line from the scratchy record, the one Elwood had always known was meant for him: *You are good.*

Turner, who was nobody's witness and had outlived everyone he had ever conspired with, stood in front of it with his hat off, in the rain, being good anyway. Even when they were not. Especially then.
