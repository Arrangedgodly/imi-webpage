# The Baboonstone

*A parody of "The Moonstone"*

Being the truth of the matter, so far as I understood it, extracted from the grooming-journal of Gabriel Betterbranch, butler.

I address myself to the reader who likes his banana with a worm in it — the reader who knows that when a household goes wrong, it is usually over a treasure.

At the storming of Bananapatan, Colonel Horncastle — a baboon of the worst manners and the worst luck — took by force a yellow diamond from the forehead of the four-handed Moon Monkey at Somnauth. The priests of that god, three tall langurs in turbans, followed the stone across the sea, on and on, disguised as jugglers, doing tricks with coconuts in market towns. They are following it yet; I did not envy the Colonel.

Dying, and out of spite — for spite was the only crop he ever raised — the Colonel left the stone to his niece, Miss Rachel Vervet, to be given her on her eighteenth birthday. And so it came into the house, that banana-yellow diamond the size of a plover's egg, and the trouble came with it.

Miss Rachel wore the Baboonstone at her birthday supper. I will swear she never took it off. There were guests: Mr. Franklin Bough, her cousin, back from the Continent with fine words; Mr. Godfrey Ablebranch, the philanthropist, beautiful as a Sunday and twice as smooth; and, that very afternoon, three travelling langurs with a tamarin child, who asked, very politely, to see the stone. They were turned away. That evening my daughter Penelope saw the boy loose a tamed moth through the window — a little spy with wings, sent to find where the stone slept. It found nothing, the shutters being English, but I record it as the first sign that the tall men did not mean to give up.

At supper, Mr. Franklin and Mr. Godfrey fell to arguing over a committee of young ladies devoted to bettering the mothers of the poor, or the poor of the mothers — I could not follow it then and cannot now. Mr. Godfrey called it the age of sisterhood; Mr. Franklin called it the age of sentimental figs; my lady laughed; Miss Rachel wore the diamond and looked, I confess, magnificent. That same afternoon, by my lady's orders, Miss Rachel's door had been painted fresh blue. Remember the paint. Everybody in this narrative keeps forgetting the paint, and the paint never once forgets anybody.

In the morning, the Baboonstone was gone.

The local officer, Superintendent Seagrabb, arrived with the certainty of his kind already buttoned on. It was the Indians; it was always the Indians; the Indians, he noted, had been seen in the neighborhood, being the only persons connected to the affair who had any reason, any cunning, or any idea where India was. He proposed to arrest them, then the maids, then, on reflection, to wait for breakfast. It was at this point that my lady sent to London, and there came to us Sergeant Cuff — a lean grey langur with a face like a closed fig and one passion in this world, which was orchids.

"I suspect everybody I meet, sir," said Sergeant Cuff, as calmly as asking after my health. "The odds against the innocent are considerable."

He found the smear of wet paint on Miss Rachel's door — made, by the look of it, after midnight, by the fur of somebody leaving her room. He found that our maid Rosanna — a poor quiet girl with one shoulder crooked from an old fall, who loved Mr. Franklin without one shred of hope, and had loved him since the moment he was kind to her on the sand-bank, kindness being the one currency the poor are never allowed to keep — had begged odd scraps of linen, and kept her door shut, and gone pale at his questions. And he noted that Miss Rachel behaved outrageously toward Mr. Franklin — as though, said Cuff, "she had a fact in her possession which cut her own heart the deepest."

Rosanna, before he could ask her why, drowned herself in the Shivering Sand — that hungry grey mud which swallows a thing and keeps it, and breathes, when the tide comes in, like something asleep. She left a letter, sewn inside a nightgown, and buried in the Sand, and got (by arrangement with a fisherwoman's boy) into my hands at last:

*"I loved him, Mr. Betterbranch, and love is not allowed to the likes of me. On the night in question I saw what I saw, and I hid what I hid, in the Sand, where the Shivering keeps it. Don't blame Mr. Franklin. Blame me, and the Shivering, and my shoulder, and the world."*

Then they dismissed him — my lady would not hear theories against her daughter, and Cuff, I believe, had one. He went away to his orchids, and told me at the gate: "Mark my words, Mr. Betterbranch. You have not heard the last of the paint on the nightshirt. When the case is ripe, it will drop."

It dropped.

Mr. Franklin, hunting the truth like a bloodhound with a library card, found that he had been dosed, without his knowledge, with laudanum — Mr. Candy the doctor having dropped it in his grog as a joke about medicine, a joke the doctor was too ill afterward to remember playing — and that a monkey does not sleepwalk on nothing. On the night in question Mr. Franklin had risen, taken the Baboonstone from Miss Rachel's room with his own two hands while dreaming he was returning it to the bankers in Amsterdam, and hid it in the old cabinet in the sitting-room.

Where Mr. Godfrey Ablebranch, that beautiful philanthropist, had found it, and kept it, and pledged it to a moneylender in the city — being privately and hopelessly in debt, having married his charities with other monkeys' money for years.

Miss Rachel had seen it happen. That was her fact, and it had cut her deepest: that her own love had taken the stone, and that she could tell no one without ruining him. She had let an innocent maid's name lie under suspicion for a season rather than say the words. I will not pronounce on that; I am a butler, not a bench of bishops. But I observed that when the truth came out, Miss Rachel forgave Mr. Franklin in about one minute, and he required somewhat longer to forgive himself, and they were married by the spring, and I gave the bride away, and my voice did not go once, whatever the household says to the contrary.

We reenacted the night with the doctor's laudanum, in the presence of witnesses, and Mr. Franklin, asleep on his feet, walked, took the paste diamond from the cabinet exactly as I have described, and swore in his sleep at the bankers of Amsterdam. Miss Rachel watched from the stair with her claws in the banister, and laughed and wept together, which in a young woman in love is one operation.

Of Mr. Godfrey Ablebranch it must be recorded that the moneylender told Mr. Bruff, the family lawyer, that a tall gentleman would redeem the pledge on a certain Thursday. And that the three langurs, who had never once left the county, were seen that Thursday at dusk, doing no juggling at all.

He was discovered in his room at the hotel, peacefully asleep forever; and the trail from his window ran, in a line of small pressed banana peels, all the way to the docks.

The tall men went home with their god's yellow eye. Years later a letter reached me from one of them, by way of a wandering naturalist, and I keep it in my journal, and beside it my copy of *Robinson Cruso-ape*, because when a thing like this happens I take up my book, and I begin to read, and I am better.

The letter says: the Moon Monkey of Somnauth has his stone again in his forehead, and at night, when the moon is yellow, the jungle says he grins.
