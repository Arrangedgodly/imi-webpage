# The Battle of Lice

*A parody of "The Battle of Life"*

Once upon a time, a troop of monkeys fought a battle. It was long famous in that country as the Battle of Lice, and it was fought — as every battle is — over grooming rights: who had the right to sit on the sunny branch and comb whom. It lasted an afternoon. The sunny branch went back into shade. And the vale became, in the fullness of years, a pleasant green orchard country, with a blossom-laden lane where the armies once scratched each other to ribbons, and nobody could remember which side anybody had been on.

In that vale, at a great old farmhouse called the Nutshell, there lived a doctor of philosophy — Doctor Jeddler, an ancient orangutan with a fringe of orange fur like a setting sun and a settled conviction that existence was a farce.

"A battle!" he would say, sweeping his arm at the orchard. "In my garden! Over a branch and a comb! My dear creatures, life is a jest — an enormous, itching jest — and the Battle of Lice is the proof of it. The whole world is my garden, my dears, and the whole world is the joke."

He had, to furnish the jest, two daughters: Grace and Marion, two young vervets, motherless from the cradle and mothered by each other, with their father's pet economy of a house — which is to say, hardly any servants and an abundance of habit. And furnishing it further, one Clemency Newcome: a great, odd, freckled, strong-armed young monkey, daughter of the vale, who had grown up in the family doing everything by the shortest possible road, and whose private convictions, if she had ever stated one, would have run: "Right through, without stopping, and never mind the gate."

Clemency had two accomplishments: she could laugh like a shutter in a gale, and she had an unaccountable habit of winking at monkeys without her own consent, which she called "my misfortune," and which was very fortunate for this story.

Also of the household — coming and going between the Nutshell and his studies — was young Albert Heathfield: an orphan, the doctor's ward, a grave, handsome young monkey studying medicine under the doctor's old master in the far city, who was to come of age, and take his degree, and return, all within the span of one great year.

And on the hill above the vale, at the great decayed house, lived the young Squire's heir, Michael Warden: handsome too, but ruined — a rover, a waster, banned by his guardians from his own roof-tree, packing his trunks for foreign parts with a light heart and a heavy debit — a monkey of twenty-four with all the world in his pocket and nothing in it.

Now the plain truth of the vale was this, and everybody in it knew, except the three most concerned. Albert Heathfield had loved Marion since she could reach his shoulder. Marion had loved Albert since before that. And Grace — quiet, elder, capable Grace, who kept the house and everyone in it — loved Albert too, with a love she had folded up small and put away in the deepest drawer of herself, and told no living creature, and least of all herself.

Doctor Jeddler, meanwhile, laughed at everything. "Marion and my boy Albert!" he chuckled, on the birthday that began our tale — for the great feast-day of the vale was the anniversary of the Battle of Lice, a holiday of cakes and bowls and garlands, on which the doctor always proposed the same toast: "Gentlemen, to the Battle — the wisest thing the world ever did, for it proved the world a jest, and jests need no minding."

That birthday was the brightest the Nutshell ever kept. It was also the last.

For Albert went away to the far city, engaged to Marion — betrothed with the whole vale as witness, and Marion's ribbon on his arm, and a year to wait. And into the gap of that year slid Michael Warden, on his white-shouldered vine, telling Marion, with a rover's plainness, that he was going away forever, ruined, alone, and that he loved her.

"Your sister," said Warden, watching her with his frank, ruined eyes, "loves him. I have eyes, Marion, and I was not always ruined. You could be free — tonight — and I could carry you to a wider world than this vale of jests. Say one word and the vine is at the gate."

"You are going away forever?" said Marion.

"Forever."

"Then take me where no one of this vale will ever see me again."

And so it was done. There was a cloaked figure at the gate in the last dark of the birthday's end; there was a vine waiting in the lane; there was Clemency Newcome, up at midnight with a colic-cure for Britain the odd-man, who saw from the orchard wall a young monkey in a dark cloak meet a gentleman and go away with him — and stood like a clod of the valley itself while the lane emptied.

By morning, Marion Jeddler was gone, and the vale knew what it knew. Doctor Jeddler's laughter stopped as if a hand had been laid on it. Albert came home at the year's end to a bride who had vanished with another monkey. Grace put her arms around her father and her sister's name — "She is not dead, papa; while she lives, we forgive her; while we forgive her, she is ours" — and never, by one flicker, let Albert see the folded love in the deepest drawer.

They might have gone on so forever — the doctor grave, Grace keeping the house, Albert faithful to a shadow — if the world's jest had kept its nerve. But here is what the vale did not know, locked in Clemency Newcome's honest breast for six whole years:

Marion had never gone away with Michael Warden at all.

She had gone *to be hidden by* him — carried no farther than a lonely cottage in a far fold of the hills, kept by Clemency's own married sister (for Clemency had by then married her Benjamin Britain, the odd-man, a gorilla of few words and great sturdiness, in a wedding of enormous cake) — and there Marion had lived under another name, at her own strict choice, all that time.

"Grace loves him," Marion had said to Warden on the last night, holding to the vine's rail with a grip that told him his answer. "She will never say it while there is a ring of mine between them. And he will never look at her while I am in his way, in the vale, forgiven or unforgiven. Take me out of his road, Michael. Tell no one — not even her — and some year, when I know they are happy, send for me."

Warden had obeyed her like a gentleman, which was, of all his ruined habits, the one that saved him. He went abroad alone, and honest, and worked, and — as ruined young monkeys sometimes will when they are given one true thing to carry — grew worth carrying.

Six birthdays of the Battle of Lice went by. And on the sixth, with the orchard in bloom and the vale at its cakes and bowls again, there came into the lane, riding slowly, weathered and broad and quiet, a monkey whom nobody knew — and behind him, cloaked, with her hood back at last, a lady vervet whom everybody knew.

They brought her into the Nutshell in the old holiday light. Grace saw her first — Grace, now Albert's wife these five years, with a small Albert asleep upstairs — and folded her in her arms before she could kneel; and Doctor Jeddler, old and orange and shaken, looked from one daughter to the other, at the child asleep overhead, at Albert, at the wife who had never once reproached him for his jesting — and something in the old orangutan's philosophy went down like a palm in a cyclone.

"I called it all a jest," said the doctor, "because of a battle in my orchard. But the jest is on me, my dears. There has been no jest in this house. There has been a battle in it, and my two girls have been the only soldiers who never struck a blow, and conquered everything. It is not a farce, Marion. It is earnest — earnest from the first branch to the last," and he wept as happily and grossly as any old ape in the canopy.

It fell to Clemency Newcome — sobbing, nodding, winking without consent — to give the tale away entire, which she did at length and out of order, with Britain confirming the load-bearing facts in threes: "True. All true. Cooked for her myself. Six year."

And Michael Warden, standing at the door among the garlands, was forgiven, and made welcome, and — seeing Marion again in the blossom-light, seven years steadfast in a hiding-place she chose for love of her sister — understood at last that the one true thing he had carried all that time was not a debt.

They say the vale kept that birthday till morning, and that the Battle of Lice was toasted, for the first time in memory, without one word about the battle — and that Clemency winked at every monkey present, by misfortune, and was not contradicted by anyone.
