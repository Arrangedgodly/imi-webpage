# Small Things Like Fleas

*A parody of "Small Things Like These"*

In the last week of November, in the year of the big frost, Bill Furlong rose before the light, as he did every morning of the year, and went down through the cold yard of his wood-and-charcoal yard in New Moss to light the stove and feed the two old ponies.

He was a broad, quiet vervet of forty-some years, with hands like tree roots and a way of listening that made other monkeys tell him everything. Other creatures did the talking; Furlong did the lifting, which was the nearest thing he had to a philosophy.

He had five daughters and a wife, Eileen, and a house that was never quiet and never warm enough. Coming up in the world, he had learned long ago, was not for the likes of him — but there was food, there was school, there was a fire banked in the grate, and that was more than his own mother had ever been promised.

The morning of the frost, he took the lorry across town to the convent, to fill the hoppers for the nuns. The Convent of the Perpetual Peeling stood high on its own ground above the river, grey and clean, with a school on one limb of the property and, behind a wall, the laundry, where — every monkey in New Moss knew and no monkey in New Moss said — they kept the girls.

The young ones called them the flea girls. Girls who had gotten into trouble, or gotten nowhere at all; girls whose families had looked at them one day and seen something crawling that could not be seen, and had sent them to the sisters to be scrubbed of it. *Deloused*, the parish called it, as if a heart could be combed like fur. The town had agreed, years back, not to mind. If you want to get on in life, Furlong's mother had told him once, there are things you have to ignore.

Furlong had gotten on. He had ignored a great deal.

The convent yard was empty, the outside door locked. He let himself round the side, past the hoppers, took a wrong turn, and found himself in a long grey room he had no business being in, where the steam rose off vats and the air was thick with lye, and thirty or forty young females in grey shifts worked in a silence so complete he could hear the fleas hop.

None of them looked up. That was the thing he could not put down after. They had all, at some hour, stopped expecting the door to open into anything.

He was backing out, cap in hand, when a small figure came the other way, carrying a scuttle — a young macaque, hardly more than a pup, with a belly six months gone under the shift, and a scratch of grey dust in her fur that no comb would ever get out.

"You're the charcoal man," she said. Not a question.

"I am, miss."

"Sarah," she said. She looked at the scuttle, and then at him, with a directness that no creature in this building was supposed to have left. "They've sent me for coal but there's no rush; they lock the yard door on me either way. I'm in for having a baby," she said, "and for asking where the last one went."

"Everyone in this town has a daughter," Sarah said. "That's what I can't get over." She lifted the scuttle, and something in her went back inside itself, like a paw withdrawn into a sleeve. "Anyway. Happy Christmas, charcoal man."

"Is it fleas they're combing off you?" Furlong heard himself ask. "Is that what they say it is?"

"That's what it says on the forms," said Sarah. And she went back through the steam.

---

The Reverend Mother found him in the yard before he could find the gate. She was a handsome elderly baboon with the manners of a bank and the eyes of a scalpel, and she was charming to him for ten full minutes — his daughters, were they, at Saint Brigid's? Such good reports. Such a man of the world as himself would understand that the laundry did quiet work for quiet sorrow, and sorrow, like fur, was best deloused in private.

"Times are changing, Mr. Furlong," she said, pressing a Christmas hamper on him, a turkey and a tin of biscuits, "and not all change is improvement. Some creatures," she said, glancing once toward the steam, "are clean by nature, and others are not, and it is kindness, in the end, to teach them which they are."

Furlong put the hamper in the lorry. His hands did it; the rest of him was elsewhere.

"Happy Christmas to you and yours," said the Reverend Mother, and the door of that charming voice closed like a gate.

---

He could not sleep that night, nor the next. He lay in the dark next to Eileen and thought about his own mother — a servant girl, seventeen and pregnant, in the same town, in the same frost, forty years before. There had been a place for her, too, behind a wall like that one. Instead she had been taken on by an old langur widow named Mrs. Wilson, who had said only, "Well, and what will we do with you, girl?" — and had gone on doing it, and had taught the boy Furlong his letters, and left him the money that bought the first lorry: one monkey, choosing not to ignore something.

"Bill," said Eileen from the dark, wide awake, because she was always awake when he was, "you're going to say it. I can hear you limbering up."

"There's a girl in that laundry," said Furlong, "no bigger than Niamh, due in the spring."

Eileen was silent a long time. The school was the sisters', the yard's best customers were the parish, and five daughters were five daughters.

"The nuns will take the business, the school places, the custom. There's not a monkey in this town will take our side."

"I know it."

"And you're going anyway."

He said nothing, which in Furlong was a speech.

"Then I'll air the bed in the back room," said Eileen, "and you'll take the hamper back. I'm not having that turkey in my house."

---

Christmas Eve, the frost hard as iron, Furlong drove up to the convent with the last delivery of the year. The Reverend Mother met him at the gate, warm as whiskey.

"Mr. Furlong! And is the family well?"

"They are, thank you." He set the hamper down on the step between them, turkey, biscuits, and all. "I've a message from my wife," he said. "She says we can't accept."

The smile stayed exactly where it was, which was worse than if it had gone.

"I've come for the girl Sarah," said Furlong. "The one that scuttles your coal."

"The girls in our care," said the Reverend Mother, each word ironed, "are exactly that. In our care. Deloused of their troubles and returned to the world clean. It is a work of charity, Mr. Furlong, and it is not a gate."

"It's a gate," said Furlong, "and I'm standing at it."

He stood there, a big, quiet, slow animal, and he did not move, and there was nothing the most charming baboon in the county could do about a charcoal man who would not be ignored on Christmas Eve, with half the parish singing carols up the road. A young sister was sent. A door went. A scuttle clattered.

Sarah came out into the frost with a bundle of nothing, squinting like a creature let up from underground.

"Are you coming?" said Furlong, and held the lorry door.

"Am I —" She looked back once at the steam, and then at him, and got in. She kept her paws flat on her knees the whole way down the hill, as if the lorry might be a trick that would wear off. She said, "You have daughters."

"Five."

"You're doing it because you have daughters."

"No," said Furlong, watching the road. "I'm doing it because I *had* a mother."

At the house, the windows were orange with firelight and the girls were arguing over the pudding. Eileen opened the door before he knocked, took one look, and said, "In by the fire, so; the bed's aired, there's broth on," and that was all the speech the occasion got. The smell of the house — smoke, bread, candle wax — came out at Sarah like weather from another planet, and she began to cry the way the laundry girls had not let themselves cry, loudly and without manners, while five small monkeys gathered at a safe distance, deeply impressed.

Later, when the girl was asleep and the house quiet, Furlong stood at the fire, banking it up for the night. Eileen came and stood beside him.

"They'll make it hard for us, Bill."

"They will."

"And you'd do it over."

Furlong thought of his mother, and of Mrs. Wilson saying *what will we do with you, girl*, and of the small things — fleas, really, in the world's fur, the biting small things you could ignore if you wanted to get on.

"I never once regretted," he said, "the things I didn't ignore."

The fire caught and held. Upstairs, in the aired back room, a girl of New Moss slept in a clean bed with a cat at her feet and no comb in the world with anything to do.
