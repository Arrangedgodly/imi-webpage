# Apetonement

*A parody of "Atonement"*

The play was Briony's idea, as everything was Briony's idea. *The Trials of Arabonobo*, written, directed, and starring Briony Tallis, a thirteen-year-old spider monkey with quick black eyes and a notebook, to be performed the day her big brother arrived. In the Tallis troop's great fig-tree compound that summer of 1935, the light fell green and gold through the leaves, and Briony felt the whole world arranged itself into scenes only she could see through.

That was the trouble with Briony. She did not see the world. She saw stories.

From an upstairs window she watched her elder sister Cecilia, down at the pool beneath the little waterfall, and Robbie, the housekeeper's son, a serious chimp whom the Tallises had put through Cambridge. Cecilia was cross about the priceless seed-pod vase her uncle had left; it had slipped, cracked in two in the water, and she had waded in after the pieces, cross and dripping while Robbie stood holding both halves like a monkey who had just been handed blame. Briony saw the scene from above and understood it completely — and completely wrong.

"I know what he's like," she whispered to herself.

Then came the letter. Robbie had written two drafts of a note to carry up to the house — one polite, one that was not polite at all, one that spoke of dreaming of the soft fur behind Cecilia's ears and of his "hungry little heart." Into the tangled branches of the canopy, Robbie's message went, and Briony, chosen as runner, plucked it down first and read every word. Her imagination caught fire like dry season grass.

"He's a maniac," she told her cousin Lola, a slender bonobo with scratches on her wrists and a talent for being believed. "I saw him. I know."

That evening, at the library in the hollow of the tree, Briony went looking for her brother's return and found her sister instead — Cecilia and Robbie against the shelves, in an embrace so still and fierce that to Briony it looked like an attack. Nobody saw her. She backed out, eyes wide, her notebook of a mind already writing the scene down wrongly.

Dinner that night was a disaster served hot. The heat sat on the table like a guest; the cousins' twins had been caught plotting to run away; the grown-ups passed the roast figs and mentioned the war the way one mentions a smell in the next room. And after supper the twins actually ran — two small humps of night, gone into the dark with fig-lanterns swinging behind them, the whole troop calling their names along the riverbank.

It was while the lanterns were still out that Lola was seized by a shadow at the old quarry fig and left sobbing. Briony did not even hesitate.

"I saw him," she said. "It was Robbie."

They took him away that night, still shouting, while Cecilia screamed at her family from the steps, and Briony went to her room feeling terribly, righteously important. To the constable's question she said it calmly, twice, and the second time she believed it. The twins were found asleep in a hollow at dawn, and Lola, wrapped in a shawl, said nothing about the chocolate-eyed guest at dinner, and nobody asked her twice.

The war came for Robbie by way of a prison troop and a bayonet. On the retreat from the northern canopies, footsore and fevered, his shin gone wrong under the fur, he marched with two corporals, Nettle and Mace, who argued about fig brandy and carried between them a sergeant's boots and a sergeant's maps and no sergeant. The roads were full of the broken troop: cartloads of grooming brushes, someone's gramophone playing in a ditch, a farmwife handing out water with her eyes shut. At Bray Dunes the sea came into view at last, gray and flat and crowded with ships, and the sky overhead put up its raptors, and Robbie put his troop behind a wall and told Nettle and Mace the only story he had. "I did it," he said, meaning the crime he had not done, "and she waited for me anyway. You'll get home. I'll come back to her." The word *back* was the whole of him, packed small.

He kept one letter in his breast pocket. *"Come back,"* it said, in Cecilia's hand, over and over. *"Come back to me."*

Briony's war was quieter and longer. She was a trainee nurse in London by then, her hair scraped back, her certainties scraped away, swabbing floors and feeding soldiers too young to be lying in the beds they were lying in. One night she sat holding the hand of a young French macaque, Luc, wounded in the head, who called her *petite* and told her, in two languages and half of each, about a farm with fig trees. She let him believe she was anyone he wanted. When the sisters weren't looking, she cried for him, and it was the first time she had cried for anybody but herself, and she understood — three and a half decades early — that this was the debt the other thing had been accruing.

That same year, before the visit, she went to a wedding: Lola, twenty and glossy, marrying the chocolate-eyed guest, Paul Marshall, grub-caterpillar fortune and all. Briony sat in the pew with the truth in her throat like a fig seed. You cannot accuse the groom at his own wedding. You cannot try a crime when the witness is married to the answer. She ate the cake instead.

"You're sorry?" Robbie said to her, years later, when she finally came to Cecilia's narrow flat in Balham — no. That is the version where I get to be forgiven, and we will come to the truth of it presently. In the version as I first wrote it, he said: "You, of all monkeys. Cecilia will never forgive you. But I'll hear it. Say it again."

"I'm so sorry," she said. "I was thirteen. I lied."

And upstairs, in a small rented room above the wartime racket, the two sisters were together at last, and Cecilia held out her hand, and said only, "Come back," and: "Write it all down. Put it right, Briony. Put it right for once."

Which brings me to the truth, which is this: I am Briony, an old spider monkey now, my memory thinning like fur on an elder's crown, and that reunion never happened. Robbie died on the beaches at Bray Dunes of a blood fever, waiting for a rescue that came one monkey too late. Cecilia died the same autumn, in the bombing of the Balham station. I made them live, in the only place I could, and I will not apologize for that. I gave them what I could.

How could a story that ends in two funerals give anybody hope? What sense of an ending was that? I am old. This is my last novel, the one I have been writing all my life.

Last week, on my birthday, I went back to the old fig compound. It is a hotel now, with apes in the pool beneath the waterfall and nobody's priceless vase in the case by the desk. A young cousin of the young cousins was married on the lawn-bough, and I sat in the front row in my best scarf, and I understood, sitting there, the last legal fact of the matter: everybody who could be held answerable is dead. Lola is gone; the grub-caterpillar magnate is gone; the cousins, the constable, the whole cast. I cannot be sued, and I cannot be forgiven, and the diagnosis says that soon I will not be able to be sorry on schedule either — the memory thins, and thins, until the story is all that's left holding the fur down flat. So the ending I wrote is the one that has to stand.

The trials of Arabonobo, ended happily at last. Read it kindly, whoever you are. The attempt was all.
