# The Colour of Macaque

*A parody of "The Colour of Magic"*

The world rides through the void on the back of the Great A'Macaque, a turtle — no, hang on. It is a very large monkey. Four vast elephants stand on its shoulders, and on their backs rests the Discworld, and on the Discworld, regrettably, rests Rincewind.

Rincewind was a wizard. Technically. He had failed his exams at Unseen University, but one of the Eight Great Spells of the Octavo — the Creator's own grimoire — had crawled into his head during a dare, refused all requests to leave, and made him magically unemployable and almost impossible to kill. The Spell liked it in there. It had a view.

This is why, when Death turned up — and for Rincewind he turned up the way bad weather does, often — he was always mildly put out.

"YOU," said Death, manifesting in the corner of the room with his scythe and his air of a skeleton at the end of a very long shift, "ARE APPALLINGLY DIFFICULT TO SCHEDULE."

"I do my best," said Rincewind. "Was there anything else?"

The story proper begins in the city of Ankh-Morpork, in the Broken Bongo, a tavern whose motto was "You Can't Beat It," largely because most of the clientele had tried. Into it came two figures. One was Rincewind, hired for two gold pieces as a guide. The other was the first tourist in the history of the Disc.

He was a cheerful little macaque from the Agatean Banana Empire, dressed in enormous spectacles, an even larger smile, and a straw hat, and his name was Twoflower. He had never met a danger he recognized, a bargain he could resist, or a custom he understood.

"Banh!" he said, meaning hi. "I am knowing that in your language I am about to be saying something very unfortunate!"

And behind him, on hundreds of little legs, came the Luggage: a chest of sapient pearwood with a homicidal temperament, filed somewhere between "luggage" and "litigation." It followed Twoflower everywhere. It ate things. Once, a thief. Twice, most of a door.

Twoflower had come bearing the Disc's most dangerous invention. Not a weapon. An idea.

"Is called," he announced proudly, "inn-sewer-ants-polyse."

Twoflower explained: you see, you give me money, and if anything bad happens, I give you more money back. The tavern's owner, a monkey of advanced larceny, listened with growing religious awe. A business where you were paid to simply promise things!

"Is very good against fire and flood," said Twoflower.

The proprietor looked at his firetrap of a tavern, recently insured for five times its value, and did the sums. That night the Broken Bongo burned down with tremendous efficiency. So, in the excitement, did most of Ankh-Morpork, which had been constructed over the centuries of dried wood, bad luck, and second thoughts. The fire brigade, by ancient tradition, looted on the way.

"MY CITY," wept the Patrician's aide, watching the glow.

"CHEER UP," said Death, passing through with a wheelbarrow. "IT COULD BE WORSE."

"How?"

"IT COULD BE YOURS."

And so the two of them fled — the tourist, snapping pictures with his picture box, in which a tiny imp painter each scene quickly and impressionistically, and had already been paid, and knew it; and the wizard, running, which was the one thing at which Rincewind had no equal on the Disc. The Luggage trotted behind, teeth gleaming where teeth should not be.

The countryside tried to kill them, as the countryside on the Disc does. There were trolls, made of stone and slow thoughts. There was a bridge with opinions. There was a bandit whose prophetic beggar companion kept announcing the future as "Millennium hand and shrimp! Fork it and stick! Bananas of the apocalypse!" — visions, he explained, of the dumplings of time.

And there was a temple.

It stood where no temple should, of stone the colour of a dead star, with eight sides, eight doors, eight angles everywhere you looked, because it belonged to Bel-Shamharoth, the Soul-Eater, whose very name sensible monkeys never spoke, and whose sacred number was the one after seven and before nine, which in these parts no monkey would say out loud for fear of being it.

"We're going to have to go in, aren't we," said Rincewind. It wasn't a question. On the Disc, an obvious exit is a mechanism for delivering you to the thing you were escaping.

Inside, the walls were carved with eyes, and the light was the colour of magic itself: bananarine, the eighth colour, visible only to wizards and cats — a sort of fluorescent yellow-red that the eye keeps sliding off, like the last banana in the bowl that nobody will admit is too ripe. Rincewind could see it. He had always wished he couldn't.

The Soul-Eater came for them out of the eighth angle, and it was terrible beyond the describing, and what saved them in the end was the one reliable law of the Discworld: that things imagined have weight. Twoflower, a native of an empire where ninety thousand household gods include several of luck, imagined so hard and so politely that the temple tore itself inside out with embarrassment, and the Luggage ate the door on the way past.

"Amazing," said Twoflower, looking at the rubble.

"Don't," said Rincewind, "say the number."

The next wonder was the Wyrmberg, a mountain so saturated with magic that it had turned upside down, point-down like athreatening conker, and over it flew dragons. This was possible because on the Wyrmberg, dragons exist precisely as far as you can imagine them, and not one inch further. Rincewind, whose imagination ran entirely to exits, could not get airborne. Twoflower imagined a splendid if technically confusing dragon and was briefly a great rider. The local dragonlords — a family whose father, Greicha, had died years ago but stayed on out of spite — were scandalized by foreigners with better imaginations, and war was narrowly averted by the arrival of Hrun the Barbarian.

Hrun was a silverback of magnificent thews and negligible vowels, carrying Kring, a talking sword with a mind of its own, or rather several minds, none of them restful.

"Ysss," said Kring. "Kill them all, I ssshall help."

"No," said everyone.

From the Wyrmberg, catastrophe delivered them — as the Disc delivers everything, eventually — to the sea, and the sea carried them to the Circumfence: the great net that girdles the Rim of the world, built by the scientists of Krull to catch whatever the waterfall sends over. They were rescued, at the last possible moment, by Tethys, a sea troll from an ocean in another universe entirely, who had been salvaging the Rim for years and had opinions about tourists.

In Krull, Rincewind and Twoflower were hailed as guests of honor, given baths, robes, and an excellent dinner, which on the Disc is the traditional preamble to a sacrifice. The Krullian astrozoologists had a Great Question, and a scheme for answering it, and the scheme was a great bronze flying machine, the Potent Voyager, built to be launched over the Edge with two expendable passengers aboard.

"You will have the honor," said the Arch-Astronomer, beaming, "of discovering the sex of the Great A'Macaque."

"I'm very happy for the Great A'Macaque," said Rincewind, "but is there an option involving stairs?"

There was not. And so it was that at moonrise, on the rim of the world, with the Rimbow glittering below in colors that included bananarine, the Potent Voyager was hurled off the Edge of the Disc, containing one tourist, one wizard, and one item of luggage with definite views about being thrown.

"LOOK ON THE BRIGHT SIDE," said Death, turning up alongside in mid-air, because of course he did. "YOU ARE MAKING HISTORY."

"I'm leaving it," screamed Rincewind. "There's a difference!"

"Butterflies," said Twoflower serenely, strapped in and delighted. "Butterflies in the sky! I will take many pictures!"

And down, down over the Edge they went, the whale-shaped shadow of the world's waterfall thundering behind them, the Potent Voyager gathering speed toward the stars, the Luggage paddling furiously to keep up, and the Spell in Rincewind's head, for the first time in years, beginning very softly to recite itself.

Somewhere beyond the rim of everything, the Great A'Macaque swam on, patient as geology, bearing its elephants, its Disc, its wizards, its tourists, its wars, wonders, and insurance policies, toward a destination known only to itself.

What happened next, no monkey on the Disc could tell you.

Though several, in the traditional manner, would gladly sell you insurance against it.
