# Ooh Goes There?

*A parody of "Who Goes There?"*

The trouble began, as trouble usually does, with something frozen that should have stayed that way.

Primate Station Nine squatted on the Antarctic ice like a dropped banana crate: thirty-seven monkeys, one radio, one ping-pong table, and one pilot named McRhesus, a red-bottomed macaque with a bad attitude and a worse chess game. He was losing to the assistant dog-handler — we called them dog-handlers though the dogs were husky-built spider monkeys in harness, the only troops in the world that would pull a sled — when the radio spat static.

"Attention. Attention. this is the North Station." The voice was breathless. "We have tracked an animal. It crossed the ice from the magnetic anomaly. It is — it is *wrong*. Kill it. Burn it. Do not let it touch your people. It —"

The message ended in Ookish, then in nothing.

"Explorers," said McRhesus, tipping the chessboard over. "Always melodrama."

Two hours later the animal arrived: a young langur, thin as a promise, loping flat-out across the ice with a sled team in pursuit — monkeys from the North Station, rifles and ropes. The langur collapsed inside our perimeter, shrieking one word over and over: "Ooh! Ooh! OOHHH!"

The sled team caught it in the dog-lines. There was a tangle, shots, and by the time McRhesus and the others got there with lanterns, the langur was dead and one of the harness spider monkeys lay beside it — torn open like a duffel bag, and *steaming*.

Not bleeding, understand. Steaming. As if something inside it had been left on the stove.

Dr. Copperfield, our physician mandrill, knelt over it. "That isn't a wound," he said slowly. "That's a — remodeling. There are shapes in here I don't have words for. And they're *warm*."

It was Blair, our baboon biologist, who said the thing everyone else was thinking not very well. He'd been down to the magnetic anomaly in a tractor that morning — they'd found a peel-shaped craft under the ice, older than the ice, and thawed its pilot out with thermite charges "like idiots," he said, "like absolute amateurs."

"You have to understand what it does," Blair said, and his voice climbed into the upper branches. "Every animal you see — it imitates. Perfectly. It eats a monkey and then it *is* that monkey. Fur, voice, memories, fondness for bananas, everything. You cannot tell. I cannot tell. That dead langur in the yard may have been the pilot, or may have been lunch. The monkey that killed it may be our friend. And now —" he laughed, high and unhappy — "now you have to decide, every breakfast, whether the chimp passing you the peanut butter is a chimp."

They sedated Blair for everyone's peace of mind, and he repaid us by wrecking the tractors and the radio, "so it can never reach the world," he said through the drugs. It was the sanest crazy thing any of us ever did.

We burned the remains in the furnace — Copperfield insisted, McRhesus lit it — and we told ourselves it was over. Then the killings started small: one dog-line monkey that didn't come to dinner. Then, hidden in the stores, we found a lump of writhing fur with two half-faces on it, one of them still smiling, and nobody smiled for a while after that.

McRhesus took command the way he did everything: rudely.

"Nobody goes anywhere alone," he said. "Nobody sleeps alone. Nobody eats anything they didn't watch being opened. And from now on, when you approach a colleague in a corridor, you call out — and they answer. Anything that won't answer isn't one of us."

"And what do we call?" asked Childs, the big mechanic gorilla, who had not stopped cleaning his rifle since the langur died.

"Ooh," said McRhesus. "Ooh goes there?"

It would be funny later, if there was a later. All that night the corridors of Station Nine echoed with it — *ooh goes there, ooh goes there* — a hundred paranoid monkeys challenging each other in the dark, like the world's worst bird sanctuary.

The test, when it came, came from Copperfield, and it was elegant. "Blood," he said. "Blood's alive, at the small scale. Its blood will defend itself. Ours just lies there feeling victimized." He drew a sample from every monkey in camp — thirty-six little dishes, thirty-six names on labels — then touched each with a heated copper wire while we all stood in the cold room, shoulder to shoulder, not looking at each other very much.

Dish after dish hissed. Dish after dish steamed, normal, boring, warm-blooded and cowardly. Then he touched the wire to the sample marked NORRIS — and the blood *scamped*. It leapt to the far side of the dish, it threw up pseudopods, it *hissed*, and everybody at once remembered that Norris the meteorologist chimp had had a heart attack that very afternoon, and that Copperfield had massaged his heart back to life with his bare paws, which had then gone very white under the fur.

Copperfield looked at his paws. He looked at Norris. He said, faintly, "I have been such an idiot," and his two arms each went a different direction.

There was a great deal of noise, and kerosene, and afterward nobody wanted breakfast.

Norris-thing burned for an hour, and while it burned it wore several faces at once, and one of them, unforgivably, was doing an impression of each of us. McRhesus watched it with his jaw set like a trap.

"It was building something," he said. "In the tool shed. A raft. An *aircraft*." He spat into the fire. "It wasn't going to winter over, mates. It was going home — and it was taking a souvenir case of us with it."

So we burned the aircraft too, and then — because Blair, sedated in his bunk, had been awfully quiet for two days — we checked the sedated Blair in his bunk, and found the bunk empty and the vent cover swinging.

That was this morning. This evening is now.

The station is burning. We had to — every corner of it might have been somebody's puppet. Childs and I fired the last wing together, and then the smoke ate the sky, and now it is just me and one other figure sitting against a fuel drum in the dark, sharing a bottle McRhesus had been saving "for the end of the world."

It looks like Childs. It is wearing Childs's coat. It drinks like Childs, wincing at the rawness of the spirit. But then, McRhesus thought, it would. It would imitate anything. That was the whole miserable trick.

The wind came off the ice, and the figure by the drum pulled the coat tighter and said, conversationally, "Well. Monkey. What do we do now?"

McRhesus looked at the fire, and the bottle, and the dark where the ice went on forever. He was so tired. They both were so tired. One of them had been tired since before the ice.

"Do now?" he said, and passed the bottle. "We sit here a while. And we watch each other."

Somewhere in the smoke, something went *ooh*.

Neither of them looked at the other. Neither of them dared.

"Ooh goes there?" said McRhesus softly, to no one, to everyone, to whatever the night had left him.

And the long Antarctic dark answered, in his own voice: "Ooh goes *you*, friend McRhesus. Ooh goes you."
