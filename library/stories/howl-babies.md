# Howl Babies

*A parody of "Owl Babies"*

Once there were three howl babies who lived in the hollow of a great fig tree with their Mother. One was named Sarah, and she was the biggest. One was named Percy, and he was the middle-sized one, thoughtful and careful, the sort of small monkey who checked a vine twice before trusting it with his whole body. And the third was named Bill, and he was the littlest, with fur that stuck up at the back like a startled tuft of grass and a howl much, much too big for him.

They lived high up in the greenest part of the jungle, where the fig tree held out its branches like a mother offering snacks, and the evening came warm and slow, smelling of ripe things and wet leaves.

One night the three howl babies woke up, and the hollow was quiet, and the branch where Mother always slept was empty.

"Where's Mummy?" asked Sarah.

"Oh, dear!" said Percy.

"I want my mummy!" howled Bill.

The jungle, far below, went on with its business. Something rustled. Something dripped. Somewhere a beetle ticked like a tiny clock.

"Sarah," said Percy, "where do you think Mummy has gone?"

Sarah thought hard, because she was the biggest and thinking was her responsibility. "I think she's gone to find 'nanas," she said. "Big yellow ones. Bendy ones. The kind that make your tummy happy."

"I think she's gone to find figs," said Percy. "Sweet purple figs. For our supper."

"I want my mummy!" howled Bill.

"Well," said Sarah, "she'll come back soon. She always comes back. Mummies always come back."

But deep down, in the small dark place where worry lives, all three of them wondered. The hollow felt bigger without her, and colder, and the noises of the jungle sounded a little too much like footsteps.

So they sat, and they thought, all three of them, in a row. And they waited.

A gecko crept along the bark below them, going about his evening.

"Have you seen our Mummy?" Bill called down.

The gecko looked up with eyes like wet black beads and blinked, once, slowly, which was not an answer at all.

"She doesn't know anything," said Percy.

"I want my mummy!" howled Bill.

"Shhh," said Sarah, but not unkindly. "Come here. Sit closer."

They sat closer. They sat tight. They sat so close they became one warm ball of fur and ears and tails, with Bill in the middle because the middle was the safest place and Bill needed it most.

Far away, across the valley, the great howler troops began their night song, roaring and whooping and hollering at the moon. It rolled over the treetops like jungle thunder.

"Should we sing too?" whispered Percy. "So Mummy can find us?"

So the three howl babies lifted their little faces and howled as loud as they could, which was not very loud, and mostly came out as "ooo-eee-ooo," like very small ghosts practicing. The jungle did not answer. But they felt a little braver, having made a noise.

"Close your eyes," said Sarah. "And wish. Wishes help Mummies come home."

They closed their eyes and they wished, each in their own way. Sarah wished in full sensible sentences. Percy wished politely, with a please at the beginning and a thank-you at the end, just in case. Bill wished the loudest, in great silent waves: *Mummy, Mummy, Mummy.*

They sat, and they wished, and they waited.

But Mummy didn't come.

Percy opened one eye. "Sarah?"

"Yes, Percy."

"She's been gone a long time."

"Yes," said Sarah. And for a moment the biggest howl baby's voice went small and wobbly, because even the biggest is still little, sometimes. "Yes. A very long time."

They tried to sit still, the way Mummy had told them. *Sit still and be safe and don't go wandering in the dark, my little fruits.* But the dark was getting darker, and the hollow was getting bigger, and Bill's lip was getting tremblier.

"I want my mummy!" he howled. "I want her NOW."

And this time Sarah and Percy howled too, all three together, one long wobbling "oooooo" that spilled out of the hollow and down through the branches and out into the listening night.

Then Sarah had an idea, and ideas were rare and precious at night.

"Let's look. Just to the end branch. Not down. Just to the edge."

Carefully, carefully, holding tails and hands and each other's fur, the three howl babies climbed out along the big branch until they reached its very tip. They looked below them. The leaves went down and down, branch under branch under branch, into shadows where the last light pooled like spilled juice.

Nothing came up.

Nobody climbed.

"Mummy?" Percy called, softly. "Mum-mee?"

Nothing.

They waited at the edge of everything, three small shapes against the big green dark, swaying a little because the branch swayed, and the jungle breathed in and the jungle breathed out.

"She should be back by now," whispered Sarah.

"Unless something—" began Percy, and then stopped, because some thoughts are doors you should not open at night.

Bill pressed his face into Sarah's shoulder. "I want my mummy," he said, in the smallest voice in the whole jungle. "I want my mummy, and I want my supper, and I want to go to sleep, and I want her HERE."

They huddled, and they closed their eyes again, and they wished harder than they had ever wished anything—

And all of a sudden, the branches shook.

Leaves flew. Vines bounced. There was a scrambling, a swinging, a whooshing of fur through the canopy, and out of the deep green jungle came Mummy, riding the branches like roads, with a bunch of ripe figs in one hand and a rope of bananas over her shoulder and her eyes bright and certain as two little moons.

"HOORAY!" shrieked Sarah.

"MUMMY!" cried Percy.

"I WANT MY MUMMY!" howled Bill, "AND HERE SHE IS!"

Mummy gathered all three of them into her arms at once, which takes great skill and even greater love, and held them so tight their fur squished.

"What's all this fuss?" she said, in her warm rumbly voice. "What noise! What howling! What a welcome!"

"You were GONE," said Sarah.

"You were gone such a long, long time," said Percy.

"And I was hungry," said Bill, "and worried, and hungry, and scared, and hungry."

"I was finding your supper," said Mummy, "and the fruit was far tonight, and the vines were slow. But I was always coming back. Little fruits," she said, touching each of their noses with one gentle finger, "I am always, always coming back."

The howl babies danced on the branch. They bounced. They flapped their arms. They swung by their tails and turned somersaults of pure joy, and Bill did his happy jig with the funny leg, and even sensible Sarah turned a cartwheel right over Percy's head.

Then they clambered onto Mummy's back, all three of them, holding on tight, and she carried them up to the hollow, and they ate figs until their tummies were round, and bananas until their cheeks were full, and the juice ran down their chins and nobody scolded them for it.

"Will you go away again?" asked Percy, sleepily.

"Only to bring back more," said Mummy.

"Not in the dark," said Bill.

"Never long in the dark," said Mummy. "And do you know what I heard, all the way across the valley? Three little voices, calling me home. Loudest of all, one very small one."

"That was me," said Bill, falling asleep mid-proud.

And Mummy laughed her low warm laugh, and gathered them in, and the three howl babies curled together in the deep safe hollow of the fig tree — Sarah, and Percy, and Bill — warm as toast, full of fruit, and nobody's mummy was gone anymore.

And the jungle went "ooo-eee-ooo" all night long, and it was only the wind.

Probably.
