# Bananadise Lost

*A parody of "Paradise Lost"*

Of Monkey's first disobedience, and the fruit
Of that forbidden banana whose mortal taste
Brought all our woe, with loss of Bananadise —
Sing, Muse, and justify the ways of Apes to Apes.

Satang, proudest of the arch-apes, he who first went bananas and broke troop discipline, was flung headlong and flaming from the Bananadisial sky, with his grumbling cronies, into the Scorch — a burnt crater of ash, thorn, and disappointing sunsets. Nine days they fell; nine days they lay bruised; on the tenth Satang stood, spat out a cinder, and spoke.

"Monkeys of the Fall," he said, "what though the troop be scattered? All is not lost: the unconquerable will, the study of revenge, immortal hate, and courage never to submit or cling."

Beelzebaboon, second in mischief, lifted a half-charred banana like a scepter. "All very rousing, sire. But there are no trees here."

"Is it better," said Satang, "to be a pampered pet of the Sky Ape, groomed on schedule in Bananadise, or to reign in the Scorch? **Better to reign in the Scorch than serve in Bananadise!** For the mind is its own tree, and in itself can make a heaven of the ashes."

But first, a flashback. The Sky Ape had proclaimed His Son regent of the whole canopy, and Satang — burning like dry grass — had gone through the shining troops gathering signatures for a grievance. One seraph alone stood up in the middle of the mutinous muster: Abdiel, the faithful, small, and completely unbothered.

"Unjustly thou depravest it with man's distinctions," said Abdiel, "which are not the Sky Ape's. Servitude? Thou art His creature; He made thy grip. Shalt thou give law to God? Thou hast thy freedom — the freedom to swing wrong, which is the only freedom you are interested in, and you may keep it."

"Traitor," hummed ten thousand apes.

"Faithful," said Abdiel, "among the faithless." And he turned alone, one small bright monkey against the whole dark tide, and swung home — the loneliest and best-run branch in the poem.

War followed in the Bananadisial sky. Satang's legions uprooted hills and hurled them; the loyal host answered with peaks. Then Satang's cleverest ape, a sulfurous engineer, invented artillery — which tells you everything — and the angels dug in. The war ended on day two, when the Son rode out alone in a blazing chariot, without raising a paw: the rebel squadrons saw him coming, panicked at their own shame, and threw themselves out of heaven sideways, hell-bent for the Scorch.

In the Scorch, they built Pandananaonium, a parliament of thorn and smoke — a hall so vast that the devils shrank themselves to fit in it, which Satang spun as "costuming," not "defeat." Moloch bellowed for open gorilla warfare — climb the sky again, bite the gates! But Satang chose guile: a passing fruit bat had told him of a green place below, Edanana, where the Sky Ape had planted a new pair of monkeys. And where monkeys are, temptation always finds a branch.

"Who will go past the flaming palm frond of the Archangutan Gabriel," he asked, "down into Edanana?"

Silence. Even devils hate errands. So Satang went himself, disguised as a young and unremarkable bat, to faint applause, like far-off rain.

In Edanana, Adamang and Evana, the first monkeys, lived at ease. One law only had they been given: "Of every tree in the garden eating freely, eat; but of the One Banana that hangs in the midst, touch not; for in the day that thou eatest, thou shalt surely itch."

Satang found them grooming at sunset and was, for a moment, almost sorry. Then he entered the sloth — subtlest and slowest beast of the field, who hangs where nobody suspects a hurry — and for three days the possessed sloth sidled toward Evana, humming questions.

"Good morrow, Empress of this happy world."

"Good morrow," said Evana, "though you are extremely slow."

"I am deliberate," the sloth agreed. "Tell me — which law forbids the One Banana?"

"The law of the Sky Ape: eat of it, and surely itch."

"**Thou shalt not surely itch.** He knows that in the day thou eatest, thine eyes shall be opened, and thou shalt be as gods, knowing ripe from rotten. Behold — I hang beside it daily. Am I not glorious?" He scratched himself. (Behind his eyes, Satang winced.)

The One Banana hung plump and yellow, luminous, like a small moon with a stem. Satang, thwarted once — the Archangutan Gabriel had caught the bat sulking near the tree and turned him back with a blush — tried a second road: he breathed a dream into Evana's sleep, so that she woke unsettled, with the taste of altitude and glory on her tongue, and lingered near the tree at the day's hottest hour. Adamang, meanwhile, had proposed that they work the grove apart that morning, she by the fig-walk, him by the spring — "for attention, sweet, is best in shifts." Evana demurred, feeling suddenly that apart was where trouble found monkeys; Adamang insisted, kindly, that no harm ever came of a little solitude; and so the morning was arranged exactly as the tempter needed it, which is how most mornings are.

Evana reasoned at length about knowledge and fairness, as one does when one has already decided. She picked it; it peeled like a whisper — stunningly sweet, a little green at the stem — and she carried the rest to Adamang.

He knew better; but she held it, and she was dear, and fondness has ever undone reasonable monkeys. He ate.

Immediately their fur went to mats. They knew at once that they were ungroomed, and — worse — that the other had noticed; so they sewed breechcloths of banana leaf and hid among the figs, where the Sky Ape, walking in the garden in the cool of the evening, found them instantly, figs being not that thick.

"Who told thee that thou wert rumpled?" asked the Sky Ape gently. "Hast thou eaten of the tree?"

"The sloth did egg me on," said Evana, "and my mate did share."

"The ape thou gavest me — she gave it me," said Adamang, blaming the whole chain of custody in one clause.

Sentence was given. The sloth should crawl forever, and be laughed at by children. Between the woman and the beast, enmity: "it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt nip her heel." And for the monkeys: thorns, sweat, and the heartache of knowing what a good banana tastes like when it is the wrong banana.

But sentence was not the last word. The Son came down, gentle as evening, and clothed the two shivering monkeys with coats of fig-skins — proper ones, with the fur side in — because judgment in this canopy has always traveled with tailoring.

Satang flew home in triumph to make a victory speech and found his tongue forked in transit, so that he hissed. It was the first laugh the Scorch had ever heard. Sin and Death, his dreadful relatives, smelled the news from far off and built a broad highway between the worlds — on the standard Scorch infrastructure plan: over budget, over schedule, and paved with intentions.

And in Edanana, before the first exiles, the gate was guarded and the wild world rolled to the horizon, dark and tall and unpicked. The Archangutan Michael took Adamang up a high hill and showed him everything to come: two brothers quarreling over fruit; a great flood and a floating crate; towers and tyrants; and, very far off, a small light in a low tree.

"What is that?" asked Adamang.

"Not yet," said Michael. "But it undoes all this."

The world was all before them, where to choose their place of rest. Adamang and Evana took each other's hand, and with wandering steps and slow, through Edanana took their solitary way — and after a while, being monkeys, they began to swing.
