# The Last Olymchimpian

*A parody of "The Last Olympian"*

The war began, as monkey wars often do, with two friends blowing up a cruise ship.

Percy Jackson and Beckendorf, son of Hephaestus, rode flying pegasi onto the *Princess Andromeda*, Kronos's floating horror-show of a war barge, with saddlebags full of explosive Greek fire. The sabotage worked. The escape didn't. Beckendorf stayed behind with the timers so the bombs would land on time, and he did not come back, and the sea carried Percy home to Manhattan carrying a grief the size of a battleship.

That was the spring the prophecy came due. A demigod's sixteenth birthday would decide the fate of Mount Olymchimpus — his birthday, as it turned out — so before the war could start, Percy stood on the bank of the River Slick in the Underworld, one of those rivers you don't visit unless you mean it. He meant it. He held his breath, and dunked.

The Slick burned like a whole troop's worth of bad history. It stripped away mortality and left something sharper: an ape who moved like a rumor, whose golden fur gleamed, whose only flaw — because there is always one flaw — sat at the tip of his tail, where the river hadn't quite reached.

"Your Achilles tail," said Nico, son of Hades, who'd arranged the dunk and looked appropriately guilty about it. "Nobody hits that spot. Nobody learns that spot. Not even Annabough."

"Not even Annabough," Percy agreed.

Getting the bath had cost a bargain. Nico wanted two things: his father's respect and the gods to keep their promises. So Percy swore an oath on the River Slick itself — every unclaimed kid counted, every ignored cabin built — and Nico, satisfied, went below to have a very old argument with a very rich uncle who lived underground.

"I'll get him to fight," Nico said. "He's been sulking for three thousand years, but he's also been *keeping score*, and the Titans owe him a trespassing bill the size of a mountain."

Meanwhile, Mount Olymchimpus — six hundred floors of the Empire State Building, home of the gods — stood nearly empty. The gods were off fighting the giant Typhon, the worst of all the storms that ever walked, and the Last Olymchimpian remaining to hold the fort was a small, plain goddess of the hearth who served Percy warm milk and quiet advice while Manhattan fell asleep around them.

"Understand, Percy," Hestia said, feeding a splinter to her hearth-fire, "the strongest thing in this war will not be a sword."

"Is it a banana?"

"It's a choice."

Then Kronos came, marching up Fifth Avenue with his army of monkey-mortals and dracaenae, scythe in hand, wearing the body of Luke like an ill-fitting rental tuxedo. The Battle of Manhattan: pegasi strafing from above, the Ares cabin finally getting the brawl of their dreams, hellhounds in the park, and Clarisse so furious she took on a drakon solo when nobody would take her chariot joke seriously.

When the drakon's venom cut Clarisse down, it was Silena, daughter of Aphrodite, who put on the armor, took up the spear, and led the charge — Silena, who had been the camp's spy all along, bullied by the enemy into betrayal one secret at a time, and chose to pay it back at the tip of the line. She fought like an Ares kid. She died like a hero. Even Clarisse forgave her, which for Clarisse was practically a marriage proposal.

And when the bridges were burning and the defenses nearly done, Nico collected: the pavement of Fifth Avenue split open like a fruit peel, and up came the armies of the dead, led by Hades himself in mourning armor, on the excellent legal grounds that Kronos had trespassed in his realm. The Ares cabin called it cheating. The dead called it Tuesday.

Percy fought through it all — untouchable, golden, and increasingly aware that untouchable isn't the same as unbeatable, because Kronos had time on his side. Literally. He could slow a moment until Percy's perfect sword stroke arrived a second late, then answer it. Ethan, son of the forgotten goddess Nemesis, swung at the Titan instead of at Percy — rebel to the last — and Kronos unmade him with a flick, for the crime of aiming at the wrong monkey. But not before Ethan had shown everyone the point: even in rented bodies, tyrants are held up by the loyalty they haven't earned.

The last stand happened on the top floor, in the throne room of the gods, where a monkey in golden armor faced down a Titan wearing his oldest enemy.

At the height of it, Kronos cheated: a time-slowed strike aimed straight at the tip of Percy's tail, the one spot the river had missed. Annabough saw it coming and did the only thing a wise monkey could do faster than think — she stepped in front. The blade took her shoulder instead of his tail, and Percy, watching her fall, stopped fighting like a swordsman and started fighting like weather.

That was the thing: Kronos needed Luke's body. Luke's mind held out just enough, and Annabough — bleeding, furious, one arm hanging, still clutching the knife Luke had given her years ago, when he was still a big-brother figure and not a rental tuxedo — staggered to her feet beside Percy. The knife had a magic rule: it would hurt its owner's flesh and no one else's.

Annabough couldn't use it. She couldn't.

"Family, Luke," she said, and threw it — not at the Titan, but to the one small corner of Luke still awake in there.

Luke caught it. And in the last free choice an ape ever gets, he drove it into his own Achilles spot, under his arm, exactly where his mother had once held him.

Kronos scattered like a sandcastle in the tide — a golden dust that sighed across the marble and was gone, all that time and malice collapsed into the space one monkey's choice takes up.

"I'm sorry," Luke said, and meant it, and stopped.

Afterward, the gods offered Percy godhood. Right there, on the spot, all-you-can-eat immortality, one throneship only.

And Percy, sixteen and golden and not entirely healed, said no — but he did have one condition: no more unrecognized children. No more cast-off demigod monkeys growing up alone and angry, exactly the recipe that had cooked Luke in the first place. Every child of every god, claimed at last. Even Hades' cabin got doors, which surprised everyone including the doors.

Rachel Elizabeth Dare walked into the oracle's attic and walked out with glowing green eyes and the next Great Prophecy already forming — a new crew, seven demigods, and trouble big enough to require a flying ship.

The hearth still glowed on the top floor. Hestia fed it another splinter, satisfied.

"You gave up immortality for your friends," she said.

"I gave it up because I like being a monkey," said Percy, "not an exhibit."

Down in the lobby, Annabough waited by the doors to the city. Camp Half-Primate needed rebuilding; the climbing wall needed un-scratching; the campfire needed new songs for the names that weren't coming back; and for once there was no prophecy due on Percy's birthday. Just a summer, and a girl, and a whole troop of survivors.

"Come on, Seaweed Brain," she said. "We're going to be late for the campfire."

And Percy Jackson, hero of Olymchimpus, holder of the river's blessing, holder of the last banana-poppy cake of the summer, followed her out the doors.
