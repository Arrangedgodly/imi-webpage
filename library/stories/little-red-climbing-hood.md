# Little Red Climbing Hood

*A parody of "Little Red Riding Hood"*

Once upon a time, in a bend of the great green jungle where the trees grew taller than gossip, there lived a sweet little she-monkey called Little Red Climbing Hood — for the red climbing harness her grandmother had braided her, the finest fit in the forest.

One morning her mother packed a basket. "Grandmother is not feeling well — the sneezes, poor dear. Take her this banana bread, this jar of papaya juice, and this pot of banana honey. Now listen: stick to the understory trail, keep your wits and your whistle about you, and go straight there. No detours. No matter what shiny things you see."

"And if there IS trouble?" asked Little Red Climbing Hood, buckling her red harness.

"Three sharp whistles, and the whole troop comes running. One whistle means 'hello.' Two means 'wait for me.' Three means trouble. That is what a troop is for."

"I promise," said Little Red Climbing Hood, and off she went.

She followed the trail till the sun came slanting through the fig leaves — where a jaguar lay along a branch, pretending very hard to be a shadow.

"Well, good morning, little climbing hood. Up early, with a full basket. Where might you be off to?"

Now, she had promised not to talk to strangers, but he spoke so politely that she forgot.

"To my grandmother's, sir — the third fig tree past the river, the one with the broken branch. She's ill, and I'm bringing banana bread."

"A dutiful child," said the jaguar, smoothing his whiskers. "And what fine ripe mangoes are those, just over there? Won't your poor sick grandmother want a sweet, sun-warm mango too? And the butterfly-orchids beside them! A nosegay for her bedside. Sick monkeys adore nosegays."

Well, it was all a lovely idea — mangoes AND orchids — and the moment she swung off the trail to gather them, the jaguar slid down the trunk, loped along his shortcut, and reached the third fig tree first.

Knock, knock.

"Lift the latch and walk in," called Grandmother. But before she could rise, the jaguar was upon her — and, being a manners-optional sort of beast, he tucked the old lady into the big fruit cupboard, pulled her nightcap over his ears, wrapped her shawl round his shoulders, tucked his tail beneath the blankets, and waited.

From inside the cupboard came a muffled, furious voice: "Who is that padding about my house? Whoever you are, you are wearing my best shawl, and I WILL be having words!"

The jaguar ignored her, having no interest in criticism.

Knock, knock.

"Lift the latch and walk in," rumbled the jaguar, in the rumblingest voice ever worn by a grandmother.

Little Red Climbing Hood climbed in — and stopped, with one foot still in the air.

"Why, Grandmother — what tufted ears you have grown!"

"The better to hear the ripest fruit drop, my dear."

"Grandmother — what great green eyes you have!"

"The better to spy the sweetest mangoes, my dear."

"And what a great big BLACK NOSE you have, Grandmother!"

"The better to sniff out the bread baking, my dear. Even when it is baking three trees away. Which it is. Presently."

"How strange," said Little Red Climbing Hood, edging one step closer to the door. "Grandmother never brags about her nose. Grandmother says bragging is for peacocks and uncles."

"Some grandmothers improve," said the shape in the bed, and its tail slipped out from under the blanket and curled up quickly, like a thief retracing its steps.

"And what a long spotted tail you have!"

"All the better to steady me on the branches, my dear."

"And Grandmother — what a tremendous mouthful of teeth you have!"

"ALL THE BETTER TO EAT YOU WITH, MY DEAR!"

He leapt — but Little Red Climbing Hood went straight up that trunk like a firework, and she whistled three sharp whistles, the jungle alarm, the one every baby monkey learns before it learns to giggle.

And here is a thing the jaguar had not considered: the jungle is never empty for long. One howler heard the three whistles and took up the cry, and the cry has no off switch. "ALARM!" bellowed the howler captain, in a voice that could be heard in the next valley and possibly the one after that. "ALARM AT THE THIRD FIG! ALL MONKEYS! THIS IS NOT A DRILL! WE DO NOT HAVE DRILLS! WE HAVE ALARMS!" And the alarm rolled through the canopy like thunder. Down came the howler troop, howling fit to wake the rain. Capuchins pelted him with figs, ripe and otherwise. Coatis jeered from the safe side of a thorn bush. A squadron of toucans circled overhead, giving commentary. "He's wobbling," said one. "He is definitely wobbling," agreed the other. "Ooh, direct hit, that fig had pulp." Old Mr. Gibbon swung in like a striped wrecking ball. And from inside the cupboard came Grandmother's voice, hammering on the door: "I knew that jaguar's mother! I'll be having words!"

The jaguar spun to snap at one monkey, lost his footing on the smooth bark, slid down the trunk, bounced off a branch, and splashed — flat as a fig leaf — into the river. He swam off downstream with his ears flat and his tail dripping, snarling to himself that from now on he was strictly a fish-and-turtle jaguar, and possibly a vegetarian.

An elderly sloth who had heard the alarm arrived three days later, studied the river, and said, "I heard something happened." Then he began the long climb home, in case anything else happened.

They let Grandmother out of the cupboard, and nothing worse had happened to her than a ruffled temper, which the banana honey soothed very nicely. She put her nightcap straight, straightened her shawl, and looked round at the wreckage of her front room.

"Did he at least eat the mangoes I paid for?" she asked.

"No, Grandmother. But we brought you these," said Little Red Climbing Hood, holding out her nosegay of butterfly-orchids, only slightly squashed.

"Hmph," said Grandmother, and put them in a gourd, because she was fond of her granddaughter even when she was cross.

Then the whole troop sat down to banana bread and warm mangoes, while Little Red Climbing Hood told the story with her tail standing straight up — the whistles, the eyes, the teeth, the SPLASH — and the troop roared at the splashing part, and the toucans corrected her on several technical points, and Grandmother hushed everyone by saying "I heard it all from inside the cupboard, and mine is the only accurate version."

"And I'll never leave the trail again," said Little Red Climbing Hood. "Or chat with jaguars. And I will always, always whistle three times when I'm frightened."

"Good," said Grandmother, settling her nightcap. "Because that is what a troop is for."

The next week they visited again, the whole lot of them, and Grandmother had nailed a sign beside her door. It read, in careful monkey print:

1. KNOCK.
2. NO JAGUARS.
3. THREE WHISTLES MEANS RUN, DEARS.

And they say that to this day, high in the fig trees of that jungle, you can hear a little voice practicing her alarm call — one whistle for hello, two for wait for me, and three, perfectly pitched, just in case.
