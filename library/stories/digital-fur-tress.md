# Digital Fur-tress

*A parody of "Digital Fortress"*

Susan Fletcher, lead cryptographer at the National Simian Agency, was brushing out her tail on a Saturday morning when Commander Strathmore's emergency parrot shrieked through her kitchen window: "Come in. Now. Bring a banana."

The crypto pit at Fort Meade was a concrete treehouse three stories deep, and at its heart, humming like a hive of caffeinated bees, stood BANANATRL — the Agency's three-million-processor code-breaker, a machine the public did not know existed and the taxpayers assumed was a very large juicer.

BANANATRL had been chewing one file for eighteen hours. Strong codes fell in minutes. The toughest in the world lasted a day. Eighteen hours meant one thing, and Strathmore — a barrel-chested silverback with the eyes of a monkey who had slept in the office so long he'd begun molting there — said it out loud.

"Digital Fur-tress. Ensei Tankadoo's algorithm. The macaque swears it's unbreakable. This morning he dropped dead on a plaza in Sapville."

"Heart attack?" Susan's ears flattened.

"A very convenient heart attack." Strathmore tapped the glass. Below them, technicians were locking the doors against every hacker on the planet, because inside the Agency's databank sat the world's banana markets, troop dossiers, and zoo feeding schedules — a golden bunch behind one rotten peel. If Digital Fur-tress were real, the peel had just come off. Tankadoo, born with bent fingers and cast out by his mother's troop, had sworn revenge on the Agency that used him and discarded him. Now he was auctioning the kill-code to the highest bidder — and his only clue circled a plaza in Spain on the finger of a dead monkey. Or so the dossier said. Strathmore had hired the crippled genius himself, then buried the fact so deep even his conscience needed a shovel.

"Send David," Susan said. David Becker, her fiancé, was a howler monkey professor of languages. He could haggle in eleven dialects and had once out-argued a customs macaque. He was also, Susan admitted, the only monkey she knew who looked good in both tweed and parachutes.

Becker landed in Sapville and was shown the plaza security footage first: Tankadoo collapsing in the sun, and with his last gesture raising one hand, fingers spread, waving something at a crowd that stepped around him the way one steps around a puddle. "He wanted witnesses," Becker said. "Instead he got tourists."

The tourists were the fog of a classic banana republic chase. The ring had passed from the dying Canadian tourist to a German salesman, from the German to his twin brother — identical, and identically rude — and from the twin to a teenage howler with an eyebrow piercing. Becker interviewed the first German in his hotel bar. "I do not have the ring," the salesman said, drinking with the tan line of a missing ring visible at the base of his finger. "That is a watch tan." "It's a ring tan, Herr Sock." "It is a large watch."

Behind Becker pruned a deaf assassin named Hulohot, disposing of witnesses like a gardener with a grudge and taking instructions through an earpiece he wore like a hearing aid of the damned. Becker finally cornered the teenager on a rooftop, where the kid agreed to hand over the ring for fifty thousand pesetas and a sincere apology from society.

"You're a professor?" the punk asked, handing it over. "Cool. I'm an influencer."

Back at Fort Meade, the pit had two problems. The first was the machine. The second was Greg Halberd — an in-house ex-hacker the Agency had hired on the keep-them-close principle, banned from DEF CON by name since the incident with the badge printer. Halberd had found a back door in the databank and started asking questions with his eyebrows, so security did the sensible thing and locked him in Node 3 with Susan, a chair, and a very American standoff.

"I didn't sell anyone out," Halberd said. "I found the back door and I was going to report it Monday."

"It's Saturday, Greg."

"I wanted the weekend to be wrong first."

"Monday," Susan repeated. "You were going to report the crime of the century on Monday."

"I had tickets Sunday."

It was while staring at him that Susan saw what Tankadoo's tracer had been yelling all along: the mysterious partner bidding up the auction, signing himself N. DAKOTA, was nobody. NDAKOTA was an anagram of TANKADO. The macaque had been his own accomplice, his own rival bidder, his own entire gang.

"Same letters," she said softly, "different order. The oldest trick in the bunch."

The auction clock on Strathmore's console ticked past eleven million. Somewhere in the dark of the net, three cartels, two governments, and a dorm room in Palo Alto were bidding on the keys to the world's fruit.

By midnight the pit was going bananas in the literal sense. The head of systems security — an orangutan universally addressed as Jabba, for an unfortunate resemblance and a fortunate surname — held court over the melting firewall like a landlord at a bonfire. "Hear that?" he said as another filter sagged. "That's every hacker in the world unzipping his backpack. By dawn they'll have our banana ledgers, our troop dossiers, and — this is the part that hurts — our karaoke rankings."

BANANATRL's cores were overheating; the filters were melting; the databank's firewall was peeling open layer by layer, and the world's hackers were pouring through like ticks at a troop reunion. It was Susan — locked in the commander's office with the dying machine and a printout of Tankadoo's riddle — who saw the rest.

"Quis custodiet ipsos custodes," she read. "Who will groom the groomers. He wasn't taunting us, Commander. He was educating us." She held up Tankadoo's inscription: element after element, prime after prime, all of them pointing at the oldest joke in nuclear physics. "Two hundred thirty-five. Two hundred thirty-eight. The difference between the two uraniums. The kill-code is three. It was always three — as plain as the three sides of a banana: stem, peel, and squish."

"Type it," Strathmore said quietly. Then, because he was a silverback with a conscience after all: "Susan, I let it in. I wanted a back door. I wanted to hold every secret in the world in one fist. Type it anyway."

She typed 3. BANANATRL shuddered, sighed, and farted out a cloud of blue smoke that smelled, unmistakably, of burnt banana bread. The firewall sealed. The databank held. Somewhere in the pit, six hundred cryptographers cheered and immediately pretended they hadn't been crying.

Halberd was cleared, promoted sideways, and given the badge printer as a trophy. In Sapville, Hulohot cornered Becker at the plaza and was instantly swarmed by the punk's subscribers, who mistook an assassin for content. He is now a meme and a resident of a federal greenhouse.

Strathmore resigned the next morning, honorably, to a grove in Maine where he planned to count coconuts and think about what he'd done.

Susan flew to Sapville, where Becker was waiting on the plaza with the ring, a sunburn, and a punk protégé who now had a merchandising deal. Under the orange trees, she slipped the ring onto her own finger and kissed him.

"Who will groom the groomers?" Becker asked, quoting the riddle.

"Simple," said Susan, picking a louse off his shoulder. "We take turns."
