# Twiglight

*A parody of "Twilight"*

I'd never given much thought to how I would fall. But falling in the place of someone I loved seemed like a noble way to drop.

That was Bella Swing, seventeen, newly transplanted from the sunny Phoenix Grove to Fronds — the rainiest, moldiest corner of the Washington Jungle, where the moss grew on the moss. She moved in with her father, Chief Charlie Swoop of the troop police, who communicated mostly in grunts, baskets of figs, and one rust-red fig truck with a growl you could hear from the porch, which he'd fixed up for her the week before. Bella loved that truck more than any monkey she had yet met in Fronds.

About three things Bella became absolutely certain that first term. First, Edward Culiana was a vampire monkey. Second, there was a part of him that thirsted for her banana — the ripest, most distracting banana in the entire canopy, apparently. And third, she was unconditionally and irrevocably in love with him.

It started in biology, over the formaldehyde fig. The pale boy at her lab table gripped the bench, nostrils flared, glaring at her as if she were a fruit bowl at a fasting retreat.

"You're new," he said through his teeth, "and you smell like dessert."

Then he vanished for a week. When he returned, a runaway fig-cart skidded on the wet branches above the ferry dock, straight toward Bella — and Edward was suddenly there, one arm out, stopping three hundred pounds of figs.

"How did you get across the canopy so fast?" Bella demanded.

"Adrenaline," said Edward. "Monkey adrenaline."

"You're impossibly fast. And strong. Your eyes change color depending on how hungry you are. You never eat at the fruit festivals. You skipped the field trip to the zoo on the grounds of, quote, 'political differences.' Say it. Out loud."

"A vampire monkey," Edward said quietly.

He took her to a sunlit gap in the high canopy to prove it, stepping out of the twiglight — that dim green underwater glow where the jungle keeps its secrets — and into a noon beam. He glittered like a thousand tiny bananas crushed into diamonds.

"This is the skin of a killer, Bella," he said.

"You look like a disco fig," she said. "I want to keep you."

"I'm the world's most dangerous predator," he insisted. "You're a harpy eagle's natural prey, and I'm the harpy eagle. Everything about me invites you in — my voice, my face, even my smell."

"And so the harpy eagle fell in love with the sapling," Bella said.

"A very dangerous harpy eagle," said Edward.

"A very foolish sapling," said Bella, and took his cold hand.

They whiled away that whole strange season learning each other in increments. "How old are you?" she asked once. "Seventeen," he said. "How long have you been seventeen?" He watched the canopy instead of her. "A while," he said.

And when she pressed him on the ethics of the arrangement — "So the Culianas are the only vampire monkeys in the jungle on a sap diet, and I'm supposed to bet my neck on a *diet*?" — he took her to see Carlisle at the hospital, the quiet capuchin who had stitched Bella's forehead after the fig-cart afternoon with hands that never trembled. "Edward tells me you're frightened," Carlisle said, warm as noon through leaves. "Good. It means you understand him better than he understands himself." The chart at the foot of the bed listed Bella as Edward's sister, because the admitting nurse could not tell their ages apart at all, which pleased Edward for a week.

She got the rest of the history in installments, the way you get most terrible gifts. In 1918, in a hospital ward in Chicanzoa, a young capuchin lay dying of the jungle influenza while a doctor who had been alone for three hundred years walked the beds deciding, night after night, whether to go on. "I was seventeen and drowning, and Carlisle bit me out of what he'd call compassion and I later called loneliness. We're fruitarians by choice, Bella, not nature. You should hear what his compassion sounds like when Rosalie tells it. She was left for dead on a riverbank, and she's never once forgiven him for interrupting."

His family drank only sap and overripe fruit nectar — "We're fruitarians," explained Dr. Carlisle Culiana — and they welcomed her with varying degrees of fangs. Little Alice read the future in the drift of falling leaves ("You'll be one of us eventually; the leaves are very clear"). Emmett, built like a gorilla twice over, once mistook her for a snack and never fully apologized. Rosalie, a magnificent orangutan with better hair than the entire troop, simply looked at Bella as one looks at a bruised banana: a waste.

There was one field trip worth the permission slip: a beach day at La Pusharo, the scrub coast, where Bella met Jacob Brackle — a younger baboon with an easy grin and a grandfather's supply of legends. He told her, half-laughing, that his troop's old stories warned against the "cold ones" who hunted in the Washington Jungle, and that the cold ones had enemies: monkeys who ran hot enough to chase them off the land. "There's a treaty line," Jacob said, pointing vaguely at the driftwood. "You keep to your side of it, and the cold ones keep to theirs."

Bella looked back up the beach at Edward, cold and glittering in the green shade, and began keeping a list of follow-up questions. "I wouldn't look too hard at that if I were you," Jacob advised, grinning, not knowing he was a sequel.

Then came thunderstorm coconut-ball.

The Culianas played only in storms, because Emmett's swing cracked like a cannon and the thunder covered it. Alice pitched; Edward ran down fly balls through three trees. It was the best afternoon of Bella's life, right up until three strange vampire monkeys walked out of the mist.

Laurent was polite. Victoria was flame-furred and watchful. And James — lean, restless, a tracker by gift and hobby — lifted his nose, found Bella's scent, and smiled.

"You brought a snack," he said.

What followed was part hunt, part herding, all chaos. The Culianas split Bella south to Phoenix Grove, but James was a tracker — he played her mother's recorded hoots over the phone, lured Bella alone to her old dance studio, and had the fight more or less won — one hind leg disabled, a mirror shattered — before he sank his fangs into her wrist.

Edward arrived like weather. James became kindling (the Culianas' cleanup method involves a great deal of fire). And Edward faced the choice that would define him: drink the venom out and keep her mortal, or let the change begin.

"Ripe or not ripe," Bella whispered, loopy from blood loss. "Choose ripe."

He sucked the poison from her wrist with his whole family watching politely elsewhere, an act of restraint that made Carlisle prouder than any of them could safely say, and chose her mortality, either way. Which is how Bella Swing ended the year not in a hospital, but at the Jungle Prom under paper lanterns, dancing on her crutch with a cold, glittering boy while a flame-furred shadow watched from the canopy above, hungry for revenge and sequels.

"Marry me," Edward said, only half joking.

"Ask me again after graduation," said Bella. "I have a bad feeling a new baboon is about to enter my life."
