# Fates and Furries

*A parody of "Fates and Furies"*

**THE FATES**

Lancelot Atterbow — Lotto to everyone, from the moment he was born howling in a Florida grove and didn't stop for a week — was the most charmed howler monkey ever to swing out of a moneyed troop. Fate doted on him the way grandmothers dote: ruinously, and always at someone else's expense.

His father made a fortune in breakfast fruit. His mother, Antoinette, made a religion of disapproval. When Lotto was cast, at eleven, as the lead in a regional production of *The Chimpanzinger*, he understood at once what he was for. "I'm going to be an actor," he announced. His father laughed warmly and paid for lessons; his mother said the theater was a den of swingers, which, in Lotto's case, turned out to be literally true.

At twenty-two, at a snowbound college party in the North, Lotto stood on a staircase and saw below him a bonobo of such still, bright, locked-down beauty that he opened his mouth and proposed marriage then and there.

"Marriage?" said Mathilde. "I've known you eleven minutes."

"And I've loved you for ten," said Lotto.

They were married by the end of the month. Fate clapped its hairy little hands.

What followed, in Lotto's telling of it, was a charmed decade. His father died; the fortune drowned; his mother disowned Mathilde on sight; none of it touched him, because Lotto was a kite who believed in his own wind. There were years of waiting tables and failed auditions. There was his best friend Chollie, a scheming, devoted rhesus agent-of-everything, and Danny, a beautiful actor with a poet's mind and a pickled liver. There were rented attics, and always Mathilde — luminous, ironic, unreadable Mathilde — keeping the accounts and the faith.

Then, at thirty, Lotto caught the fever that should have ended him and instead began him. Delirious for a week, he dreamed a whole play: *Furless*, the story of a troop of monkeys born without hair who build a city on an island and then, one by one, begin to grow coats. He wrote it in nineteen days.

Every theater in New York passed. Chollie submitted it once more, to a tiny house in the Meatpacking canopy, and the artistic director — a mandrill with a face like a lantern — read it twice and wept once.

It opened in November. It transferred in February. It won everything by spring. Critics called Lotto Atterbow the voice of a generation, "a primate of terrible and comic greatness," and Lotto, who believed the newspapers the way other monkeys believe in gravity, agreed.

**THE FURIES**

Here is what the newspapers did not know.

On the night before *Furless* went into rehearsal, Mathilde read her sleeping husband's draft, took out a blue pencil, and began. She was not cruel about it. She was surgical. She moved the second act before the first, cut the angel, gave the monkey with no lines the last speech, and sat back as the play — which had been a kite, all flutter and wind — stood up on the page and stared at her. She did this for every play he ever wrote, for fifteen years, between midnight and four, in the room he called his study and never entered at that hour. He never asked how the plays got so good. Fates never ask.

She had reasons. Furies do.

Mathilde had been born to a mother who unraveled slowly in a hot apartment, and a father who left early, and a twin — Marcus, a gentle loris, slow-eyed and sweet, the only monkey she ever loved without arithmetic. At fifteen, when Marcus fell in with the sap-drinkers and their fermented fog, Mathilde, terrified and fourteen kinds of wise, called the rangers. She meant it as a rescue. The rangers meant it as a capture. Marcus was caged, tagged, shipped, and within a year he was dead of a broken troop. The aunt who took Mathilde in taught her one lesson thoroughly: *if you love something, never, ever let it see you manage it.*

She met Gwan at college — Gwan, the founder of Boogle, the search-furred billionaire, a colobus with stillness like deep water and a habit of acquiring whatever he gazed at. Gwan gazed at Mathilde for four years. On the night of her wedding to Lotto, he cornered her by the ice sculpture.

"You've made a pity match," he said. "He'll be a failure and you'll be the failure's wife. Come to California. I'll build you the treehouse you actually want."

"Gwan," said Mathilde, "you index everything and understand nothing. Go webcrawl yourself."

But years later, when *Furless* was passed everywhere and Lotto lay fevered and their rent was a fiction, it was to Gwan's anonymous foundation that a single envelope went. The grant that saved the theater had one invisible string, and the string was Gwan's, and he held it for a decade like a monkey holding a long vine over a long drop, waiting for the fruit to ripen.

**THE ORCHARD**

Lotto died at forty-six, between one hit and the next, his enormous heart simply stopping in the middle of the night in the brownstone Mathilde had found, mortgaged, and paid off without his ever noticing. The troop of theater monkeys came to the funeral in black fur. Danny was already dead of the pickle. Chollie wept and networked. Antoinette, ancient and terrible, told Mathilde at the graveside that she had drained her son's genius like a vampire bat, which was the only true thing Antoinette ever said, though she said it as a lie.

Gwan came to the brownstone three weeks later with a contract and an orchid.

"Everything he built is depreciating," Gwan said. "The estate is a museum of a voice that's stopped. Sell it to me. I'll make *Furless* a theme park. And you — I've waited, Mathilde. Fates and furies, I have waited. Come to California."

And Gwan, because he could not help it, made his mistake: he handed her proof of the old grant, and told her — carefully, kindly, the way billionaires tell — that Lotto had found her blue-pencil drafts the year before. Every page in the study drawer, every midnight surgery. He had known his wife wrote him, and he had let her keep doing it, and told no one.

"He *knew*?" said Mathilde.

"He wrote it in his will, which you haven't had the nerve to open. I have people."

The fury that came up out of Mathilde then had been in storage for thirty years, and it was in perfect condition. She broke no furniture. She stood very still in her own doorway, and the temperature of the room changed, and Gwan — who commanded satellites — stepped back.

"You held the string," she said, "and the fruit never ripened, and it never will. Here is what you may index, Gwan: no. No park, no purchase, no California, no *ever*. Get out of my house and off my limb."

He went. Billionaires orbit; they do not root.

Then, alone at midnight, she opened the will, and found the drawer she'd feared, and in it no accusation at all — only his last unfinished play, sixty pages, a troop of howlers who have lost their voices and a bonobo who keeps the whole forest's silence in a blue box. On the title page, in her husband's enormous, unlovely, unedited hand:

*For M. — Finish it. It was always yours too.*

She finished it that winter, in the study, at night, with the door open. The mandrill director wept twice. It opened in the spring as *The Blue Box, by Lotto and Mathilde Atterbow* — and the critics, the same critics, wrote that the widow had inherited the voice, not understanding, any more than the Fates ever do, that she had built it.

The play ran a year. On closing night Mathilde took her bow alone, looked up into the dark where the lights blind a monkey to the audience, and spoke to the one seat she could not see.

"Eleven minutes," she said. "You loved me for ten of them. That arithmetic still works."

And in the orchard of the world, the trees went on: one more charmed monkey gone, one furious one, at last, at ease.
