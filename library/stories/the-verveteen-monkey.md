# The Verveteen Monkey

*A parody of "The Velveteen Rabbit"*

There was once a Verveteen Monkey, and in the beginning he was really and truly splendid. He was fat and bunchy, soft grey-blue plush with a cream-white collar, a tail you could wind three times around a wrist, and bright button eyes stitched on with care. He arrived in a stocking one festival morning, wedged between a wooden crocodile and a bag of dried figs, and the Boy grabbed him by the tail and whooped, and the Verveteen Monkey thought this was what living must be.

For a long time he lived in the toy chest, or behind the sleeping mat where the sunlight didn't reach, and nobody thought about him much, except to say his fur was the wrong color for a real vervet and his tail wasn't prehensile enough. Even the wooden crocodile, who was made of jungle wood and therefore considered himself superior, looked down on him.

At the nursery's midwinter party, the mechanicals were insufferable. There was a tin wind-up baboon who walked six steps, clashed his cymbals, and stopped dead, and everyone agreed he was the last word in Real.

"I am Real," announced the tin baboon, ticking. "I have a key in my back. Observe my mechanisms."

"He hasn't any mechanisms," said the wooden crocodile to the rocking lemur, both loudly and slowly, as if the Verveteen Monkey were hard of hearing as well as hard of luck. "He can't walk. He can't clang. He isn't real at all."

The Verveteen Monkey said nothing, because he did not know what to say, and Nana — the old babysitter baboon who ran the nursery with an iron paw — swept the lot of them back into the chest with the remark that a mess was a mess, whatever it thought of itself. But that night, in the dark, the words rattled in him like a nut in an empty gourd.

The only one who was kind to him was the Skin Gorilla, the oldest toy in the nursery, who had been a stalwart gorilla once, before his fur was loved off and his chest went soft and bald. He lived by the radiator and understood most things.

"What is REAL?" asked the Verveteen Monkey one evening, when the Boy was asleep. "Does it mean having things that buzz inside you and a stick-out handle?"

"Real isn't how you are made," said the Skin Gorilla. "It's a thing that happens to you when a child grooms you for a long, long time. Not just to play with, but REALLY loves you, and then you become Real."

"Does it hurt?" asked the Verveteen Monkey.

"Sometimes," said the Skin Gorilla, for he was always truthful. "When you are Real you don't mind being hurt."

"Does it happen all at once, like being swung up a tree? Or bit by bit, like learning to climb?"

"It doesn't happen all at once," said the Skin Gorilla. "You become. It takes a long time. Generally, by the time you are Real, most of your fur has been hugged off, and your eyes drop out, and you get loose in the joints and very shabby. But these things don't matter at all, because once you are Real, you can't be ugly, except to monkeys who don't understand."

The Verveteen Monkey sighed, the way plush sighs, which is not at all, and thought that becoming Real sounded slow, and hoped it would happen to him anyway.

And so time went on, and the Boy groomed him. It began one market day, when the Boy's fancy swinging rope broke and he grabbed the nearest thing with a tail, which was the Verveteen Monkey, and would not be argued with, not even by Nana. After that the Verveteen Monkey went everywhere. Every night before sleep, the Boy sat him in his lap and parted his plush fur strand by strand, hunting pretend fleas, and the Verveteen Monkey's collar went from cream-white to the brown of many hands. The Boy took him up the tallest fig tree in the park, and the Verveteen Monkey got actual burrs in his actual tail, and was proud of every one.

"He isn't a toy," the Boy said, when his aunt sniffed at the shabby monkey. "He's REAL. He's mine."

And the Verveteen Monkey was happy, and shabby, and had never once thought about the tin baboon again.

Then, in the bright part of spring, two strange things happened. The first was that a wild troop of vervets came down into the garden, quick and quarrelsome as spilled figs. The Verveteen Monkey, tucked in the Boy's belt, felt something leap in his stuffing, and when the Boy set him on the wall and ran for his kite, the wild vervets found him.

"I say!" said the biggest one, whiskers first. "It smells faintly of vervet. Are you a vervet?"

"I — yes," said the Verveteen Monkey. "I suppose so. I'm the Boy's."

"Your tail doesn't grip," said the biggest one, poking it. The tail lay there, tail-shaped and useless. "Can you leap the wall? Can you raid the fig-bird's tree? Have you any mechanisms?"

"I haven't any mechanisms," said the Verveteen Monkey. "But I'm Real. The Boy is Real, and he says I am."

The wild vervets sat back on their haunches and laughed until the wall shook, and the Verveteen Monkey, who had never been laughed at by anyone with actual haunches before, stared at his own feet, which were round and plush and would not grip anything at all. Then the kite came down out of the sky, the Boy came running, and the wild troop poured over the wall like water, still laughing.

That night the Verveteen Monkey asked the Skin Gorilla whether loving and being loved counted as mechanisms, and the Skin Gorilla said gently, "It counts for more. But I won't tell you it counts for everything. Being loved is not the same as having legs that work."

Which was true, and the truest things always sit heaviest.

The second strange thing was the fever. The Boy came down with the spotted fever — bright jungle spots like a little leopard's — and the Verveteen Monkey was with him all through it: held, and gripped, and sweated on, and told everything, in the muttering language of fevers. And when the Boy mended, the healer-owl came, and everything the Boy had touched was ordered out of the treehouse to be burned. The Verveteen Monkey went into a sack with the old blankets, because the Boy had held him all through the fever, and set out for the fire pit behind the garden wall.

He lay in the sack all afternoon, under the heavy blankets, with the smell of the coming fire in the air, and he thought about the Boy, and the burrs, and the forty-one ways the Boy said his name. He did not mind for himself, much. But he thought of the wall, and the wild troop, and how his tail had lain there, and something in him went tight all over.

That night, a tear — a real tear, tiny and shining — slid out of his button eye. And out of the tear grew the strangest little flower of light, and out of the light stepped a Mandrill, blue and gold and impossible, with a nose like a sunset.

"I am the Nursery Magic Mandrill," he said. "I take care of all the playthings that children have loved. When a child loves you for a long, long time — grooms you, sleeps with you, tells you everything — then you become Real to him. But once you are loved like that, and once you have wished with all your stuffing to be real to everyone, then it can happen. It doesn't happen to the well-made, or the never-loved, or the merely wound up with a key. But it is happening," said the Mandrill, "to you."

He touched the Verveteen Monkey's head, and the plush stirred, and split, and softened into warm live fur. The button eyes blinked and turned brim-brown. The tail curled — curled once, twice, three times around a wrist, gripped, and let go, surprised at itself. And with a run and a jump, a real vervet monkey went up the garden wall and away into the treetops, where a whole little troop of wild cousins sat waiting, as if they had known he was coming — and this time, when they laughed, he was in on the joke.

In the spring, the Boy — quite well again, and hunting burrs of his own now — visited the wild park. Two vervets came to the rail, and one of them looked at him a long time, sideways, with brim-brown eyes that seemed to remember something. Then he stood on his head, which had always been the Boy's very best trick first.

The Boy laughed until he had to hold the rail. "It's you," he said. "I'd know those eyes anywhere. I KNEW you were real."

And that is how it is, when you are Real. It doesn't happen to monkeys who break easily, or have to be kept carefully, or come with keys. Once you are Real, you can't become unreal again. It lasts for always.
