# Swinging Beauty

*A parody of "Sleeping Beauty"*

Long ago, in a kingdom high in the tallest kapok, King Canopy and Queen Blossom at last had a baby, and the whole troop threw ripe figs at each other for joy.

At the naming feast, three good fairies — bonobo aunts called Flora, Fauna, and Merrybranch — drifted down to give the princess gifts.

Flora lifted her paws. "My gift is beauty. Fur like polished mahogany, and a smile that makes mangoes ripen early."

Fauna lifted hers. "My gift is song. She will outsing the howler chorus, and even the howlers will forgive her."

But before Merrybranch could speak, the lanterns guttered, and through the window swept a mandrill robed in black feathers — Maleficent, painted like a nightmare, smiling like a crocodile.

"Charming," she said. "A christening. And I wasn't invited."

"You weren't wanted," said Flora, who was brave but not wise.

"Pity," said Maleficent. "Because I've brought a gift. The princess will indeed grow in grace and beauty, beloved by all who swing near her. But before the sun sets on her sixteenth birthday, she will prick her finger on the spindle of the Great Vine-Winder — and DIE."

Thunder. Feathers. Gone.

The king burned every Vine-Winder in the kingdom — those great wooden wheels that spun lianas into rope — and the rope-makers, who had done nothing wrong, formed a union that very afternoon and marched. But Merrybranch, quiet all this while, spoke her gift at last. "I cannot break her curse. But I can bend it. Not death — sleep. A sleep that ends when true love finds her."

To hide the princess, the three aunts gave up their magic and lived as ordinary bonobos in a far grove, raising the baby as a woodcutter's niece. They called her Briar Rhesus.

For fifteen years she picked berries and swung through the green, and every creature loved her — the birds let her hold their eggs, the otters held her tail while she swam, and one very old tortoise followed her everywhere at his own speed, arriving always just after she left. It was, all the aunts agreed, a charmed life.

She did not know she was a princess. Nor did she know that in her father's kapok, her betrothal had been arranged to the infant prince of the next tree over, at a banquet where King Canopy and King Hubert toasted each other and argued about the wedding menu.

"She'll marry my boy and that's final," said King Hubert. "A rope of sausages at the feast!"

"Sausages? At MY court? Fig compote and dignity!"

And their children, being babies, were carried home asleep through the whole negotiation, which is how betrothals usually go.

For fifteen years, then, everyone waited for the sun to set on one particular birthday. On the day before it, the aunts planned a surprise — a cake, a gown — and, having sworn off magic, had no idea how to manage either.

"I'll make the cake," said Flora.

"I'll make the dress," said Fauna.

It went as well as you'd expect. The dress came out a lopsided fig-leaf poncho. The cake rose, looked at the aunts, and lay back down. By afternoon they were waving wands in desperation — "Make it pink!" "Make it gold!" "Make it PINK!" — and fired a chimney of rainbow magic into the sky, where a marabou stork who reported to Maleficent saw everything, cackled, and changed his flight plan.

That same afternoon, Briar Rhesus had met someone in the ferns: a cheerful young monkey called Phillip, who swung beside her as if they had done it all their lives.

"I know you," he sang. "I swung with you once upon a dream."

"You shouldn't sing to strangers," she teased.

"We're not strangers if we've met in a dream," said Phillip — who was, though neither of them knew it, the prince from the neighboring kapok, out riding the branches he was supposed to spend being diplomatic.

At sunset the aunts told her everything: "You are the princess. And we love you like our own." And into the dark kapok, drawn by a green light only she could see, Briar Rhesus climbed — up, up, to a hollow where one last Vine-Winder had survived the burning, stashed by a rope-maker with a bad memory and a good hiding place. Its spindle glowed like a ripe fig thorn.

"Touch it," whispered the light.

She touched it. She pricked her finger. She fell.

The aunts found her breathing softly and laid her on a bed of moss, but they could not wake her. So they blessed the whole troop into the same sleep, to keep her company: kings asleep mid-snore, bees mid-buzz, guards asleep with their tails still curled around their spears, and one cook asleep mid-scolding, which meant, for the first time in years, the kitchen was quiet.

Then thorns, thick as a leopard's greed, grew around the kapok.

Maleficent had already chained Phillip in her lair for exactly this purpose. She swept into the dungeon and looked him over like a fruit she intended to keep.

"I'm not going to destroy you, princeling," she said. "I'm going to let you age. A hundred years I shall wait, and then — you may go to her. And she'll still be sixteen, and you'll be a hundred and sixteen, and the whole kingdom will finally admit I have a sense of humor."

"A hundred years to a steadfast heart," said Phillip, "are but a day."

"Write that on a card," said Maleficent, "and give it to me when you're a hundred and sixteen."

But the aunts came that night, freed him with a key they magicked out of a guard's belt buckle, and armed him from the dungeon wall: the Sword of Truth and the Fig-Leaf of Virtue. ("It's the shield that matters," said Merrybranch, pinning it on. "Also the fig. Figs are significant.")

Phillip hacked through the thorn forest, and there, on a black rock wreathed in green fire, Maleficent waited.

"Foolish prince," she said. "You think you love her. Now you shall deal with me — and all the powers of my TROOP!"

She swelled, she blackened, and she came down the rock as a fire-breathing dragon, scales like cooled lava. Phillip's blade rang uselessly off her snout. The flames crawled up the thorn wall and ate his footing, and above him the dragon opened her jaws, wide as a cave, hotter than noon — and Phillip climbed the highest thorn, stepped into the empty sky, and threw the Sword of Truth — swift and sure — into the dark heart of the beast.

The dragon fell. The mandrill fell. The fire went out like a fig-light.

Up through the sleeping kapok climbed Phillip, past guards whose snores curled like ferns, to the bed of moss. And he knelt, and kissed Briar Rhesus. True love had found her.

She woke. Then her parents woke, then the guards, then the bees, then the howlers (last, as usual, and furious to have missed it), and one cook woke mid-scolding, so the kitchen technically never stopped.

The two kingdoms danced until the figs fell. The menu, in the end, featured both fig compote and a rope of sausages, and King Canopy and King Hubert argued about whose toast was louder, and agreed it was a tie.

And Phillip and his Briar Rhesus swung happily ever after — once upon a dream, and every afternoon since.
