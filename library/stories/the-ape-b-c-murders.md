# The Ape-B-C Murders

*A parody of "The ABC Murders"*

The first letter arrived at 221B Banyan Street on a Tuesday, typewritten and smug:

*DEAR MR. HERCULE PONGO — You fancy yourself the one monkey who reads the jungle's mind. Very well. Look out for Andbranch, the 21st of the month. A little crime is coming. Yours in monkey business, A.B.C.*

"Either a crank," said Captain Hastail, Pongo's old friend, who believed the best of everyone and had been wrong about it eleven times, "or a monkey planning mischief."

"Why not both?" said Hercule Pongo, polishing his whiskers. "I have made a career, Hastail, of monkeys who cannot resist signing their work."

On the 21st, in the town of Andbranch, old Mrs. Ape-sher, who kept the banana shop under the crooked fig, was found behind her counter, struck down. Beside her, face-down and deliberate, lay the A.B.C. Banana Growers' Guide — the thick green timetable of every grower and rail-vine in the jungle.

Pongo arrived, sniffed the shop, and made a list. "Why Andbranch, of all the towns that begin with A? Why the 21st, of all days? Why a banana shop, and why the Guide, opened and displayed? A mad monkey, Hastail, does not explain — he merely frightens. I prefer to be puzzled."

The second letter named Bunch-hill. On the appointed morning, Betty Baboonard, a young waitress with a laugh like falling figs, was found strangled on the shingle, an A.B.C. Guide tucked beneath her.

The third letter was addressed to Pongo care of a wrong turning, arrived a day late, and cost a life its head start: Sir Carmichael Chimp-Clarke, a wealthy old gorilla baronet much loved by everyone except his heirs, went for his evening stroll and never came home. The Guide lay beside the body.

"The pattern is the point," said Pongo, pacing. "An old shopkeeper; a merry waitress; a rich baronet. No connection whatever. The alphabet, Hastail — the killer obeys the alphabet, and the alphabet has no motive. That is what he wishes us to conclude: a mad monkey striking at random. But random, mon ami, is the most difficult thing in the world to truly be."

The fourth letter promised Doncaster, and Doncaster duly delivered. In the crush outside the Regal Cinema, a harmless old barber was found struck down, the Guide folded under his arm. And in the crowd, swaying and gray, stood a thin salesman in banana-stocking samples, blood on his cuff, saying to anyone who would listen: "I don't remember. I was at the pictures. I think I was at the pictures."

The police found their mad monkey with astonishing speed. In a Bunch-hill boarding house they arrested Alexander Bonaparte Cust: a thin, pitiable salesman of banana stockings (a product nobody has ever wanted), subject to blackouts since a tree fell on his head, who could not account for the three nights — or now the fourth — who found Guide pages in his bag and train tickets in his pocket, and who, when charged, said only, "Then I did it. I always feared I would do something terrible."

"He pleads guilty," reported the police happily. "Case closed. The letters were typed on his machine. The stockings match. A waiter at the Andbranch café saw him there that morning with the Guide under his arm."

"Mon ami," said Hercule Pongo, "a monkey may be found with the guide, the tickets, and the stockings — and still be innocent. What was that word he used? *Feared.* A guilty monkey boasts or denies. This one *feared.* No — a blackout does not choose the alphabet. Somebody chose it for him."

He visited Cust in the cells and found a monkey hollowed out by a lifetime of failure — headaches, dismissal, pity. "They gave me the round myself," Cust said dully. "My firm. The A.B.C. of it. All laid out, towns and dates, weeks in advance. Every town where somebody died, I was there. It must be me, mustn't it? It must."

"Who engaged you?"

"A gentleman. Chinless. Terribly hearty." Cust's brow creased. "At least — I never actually saw him sign anything—"

That night, Cust tried to hang himself with a stocking and failed; the knot, Pongo noted, was one no salesman would tie. Two nights later he smuggled in a small pistol and pulled the trigger on an empty chamber, and wept with relief when it clicked — which is not, Pongo observed, the tear of a guilty monkey.

Pongo invited the victims' relations to a private room: Mary Drower, Mrs. Ape-sher's niece; Megan Barnard, Betty's sister; Donald Fraser, Betty's steady; Thora Grey, Sir Carmichael's beautiful young companion; and Franklin Chimp-Clarke, the dead baronet's brother — handsome, hearty, loud, and heir to forty thousand banana trees.

He went round the room, searching each face with small courteous questions, and paused, once, on Franklin's. "You were at the Andbranch café that morning, I think, monsieur. The waiter described a monkey 'like poor Mr. Cust.' Short sight has that effect on waiters. It also has that effect on witnesses."

"I have never been to Andbranch in my life," said Franklin heartily.

"I have called you together," Pongo announced, "because the case is solved. By fingerprint."

That night, someone tried to break into the cells and do away with Cust forever. The someone was caught by waiting police, and the someone was Franklin Chimp-Clarke.

"Sit, monsieur," said Pongo, almost gently. "Let us groom the truth together. Your brother would live for years; you had debts, and a young companion you could not marry. You required your brother dead — and required his death *invisible.* What better cloak than a serial killer? A mad A.B.C.! So you found a half-broken salesman with blackouts, invented a firm, and hired him under a false name to sell stockings along your route, posting his itinerary to yourself. He was your alibi that walked. On the chosen nights you did the killing yourself, following him town to town — you even shared his train — planting the guides, typing the letters, even posting one under the nose of the police. And in Doncaster, when a witness remembered 'a monkey like Cust,' the resemblance was yourself, dressed down. Your one mistake was vanity: the letters were too proud, too neat, too pleased with their alphabet. A mad murderer hides. Only a sane one advertises."

Franklin Chimp-Clarke smiled a terrible smile. "Prove it."

"The typewriter of the letters is in your trunk, monsieur — I have had it dusted. The firm that hired Cust has never existed, and the money that funded it traces to your bank. The empty pistol in his cell was arranged, as was the stocking, by a bribed wanderer who has described you to a hair. And tonight you tried to kill the one witness whose innocence would un-hang you. A murderer's signature, in the end, is always his cleverness."

They took him away, the newspapers went bananas, and Alexander Bonaparte Cust was freed, wept on by the relatives of the people he had thought he killed.

"But why," asked Captain Hastail, "did he plead guilty? Why did he *believe* it?"

"Because the world had told him all his life he was a broken monkey, Hastail, until he kindly supplied himself a crime to fit. It took an orangutan" — Pongo smoothed a whisker — "to prove that a monkey's failures are not his fate. And now, my friend, luncheon."

And somewhere in the jungle, the A.B.C. Banana Growers' Guide quietly went back to being the most innocent book on the shelf.
