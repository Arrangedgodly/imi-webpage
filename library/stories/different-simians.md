# Different Simians

*A parody of "Different Seasons"*

Every troop has a storyteller. Ours was an old mandrill with a face like a brilliant sunset, who told four stories every year, one a season. "Different simians," he'd say, tapping the floor, "different seasons. Same old jungle."

**Rope Springs Eternal — Rita Bananaworth and the Shawshank Redemption**

Andy Duffruffed was the smallest lemur ever to walk into Shawshank Zoo-Pen, and the mildest. The jury said he'd squashed his wife and her mango thief; nobody who looked at that face believed it, including half the jury. Red — the only gorilla in the yard who could get things — took his bet anyway.

"I'll take the termite pick," Andy said. "And a poster of Rita Bananaworth."

Red got them both. What none of us figured out for six more years was what a lemur wanted with a rock pick, a poster of a fruit-bat starlet, and a cell wall he asked us never to examine too closely.

The Sisters wanted things from him too — three amorous gibbons with no manners and less charm — but Andy made himself boring, then indispensable, and the Sisters moved on to monkeys who fought fairer. In the spring of his second year he overheard the head keeper grousing about taxes on an inheritance, and by afternoon Andy was tarring the roof with the work detail, sipping cold nectar in the sun, having arranged the matter to the last peel. He sat against the parapet like a free monkey, and for one whole minute, so were we all.

Andy got the library built — wrote one letter a week for six years until the Senate sent money just to shut him up — and taught a dozen cons their letters. He played an aria from *The Mango of Seville* over the compound loudspeaker one morning, and every monkey in Shawshank stood still as carved wood until a guard unplugged it. Young Tommy Williams came in on a two-stretch and told Andy a story from another zoo-pen: a mango thief bragging of a squash that fit Andy's exactly, down to the breed. The warden listened to Andy's request for a new trial with all the warmth of a filing cabinet. Tommy got a transfer so sudden it had jet wash. Andy got two months in the quiet hollow, and came out with his eyes still level.

That same thunderous night, Andy left Shawshank the way he came in — quietly — through a hole five branches deep behind a poster of a mandrill in a leaf bikini (the poster was Rita's successor; the warden never asked why a lemur needed a new one every couple of years), out a river of old fruit mash I decline to describe, down forty miles of creek, into the warden's accounts, and out the other side of them with the numbers in his pocket. The warden, questioned by the Zoo Board, opened his safe and found the ledger, a termite pick, and a note: "You were right, Warden. Mind the moss."

There is a kapok in a hollow near Buxton Branch. Under the black stone at its base, Red — paroled at last, tired of hoping — found a letter and a bus fare. *Remember, Red,* it said. *Hope is a good thing. Get busy living, or get busy dying.*

Red got busy living. I hope the Pacific slope is as green as it was in his dreams. I hope.

**Summer of Corruption — Ape Pupil**

Toddy Bowden was golden-furred, straight-tailed, and honor roll — until the day he knocked on the door of a shabby old orangutan named Mr. Dusseldorf and said: "I know who you are. You ran the Feeding Cages at the old zoo. Tell me how it was, or I tell the troop."

The old ape told him. That was the mistake — telling. Toddy came every Tuesday, like a lesson. "What did it smell like?" he'd ask, leaning in, and Dusseldorf would answer in that dry museum voice, and Toddy's dreams got furrier and his homework got thinner. By July his grades were ash and his dreams smelled of iron. By August the two of them were locked together like ticks, each holding the other's secret: Dusseldorf had told a schoolboy too much, and the schoolboy had, one sleepless night, done a little telling of his own — a note under the right door, and a wino gone missing from the market. Each knew the other's worst thing. Neither could blink.

"What happens," Toddy asked once, "if one of us stops coming?" And the old ape looked at him a long time and said, "Then one of us becomes a story, boy."

When the summer ended, one of them walked away. The other was never exactly seen again, and nobody asked twice. Toddy went back to school in the fall, straight-tailed, honor roll, quiet. He never told stories, and he never, ever knocked twice on any door.

**Fall from Innocence — The Peeling**

Gordy LaChance was twelve and wrote stories on banana leaves. It was Fern Tesse who came pounding up the branch: he'd overheard his brother's gang on the radios — they'd found the body of Ray Browner, a kid our own age, out by the Peel Line, and were keeping it quiet for the reward money.

We went to see it — Gordy, Kris, Fern, and Teddy — four small monkeys and a bag of plantain chips, hiking the vine-line into the dark of the hills. We outran the ten-o'clock banana freight across the trestle, hearts in our throats, rails singing, and Teddy swore at the train, because that was Teddy's way with anything bigger than he was. We swam the pond at midnight to save ten branches, and the pond repaid us with leeches — Gordy came out wearing them like a bad costume, fainted dead away, and never told a soul that Kris carried him the last quarter mile on his back.

We found him at dawn by the river pool. Just a kid. Asleep forever, a plantain chip still in his fist for a trip he never finished. Gordy sat a long time, and while he sat, a doe came down to the water on the far bank, drinking, graceful, not seeing us, or not minding, and after she left, Gordy could breathe again. Then Milo Pressman's gang came crashing through the brush — Ace Merrill and his shoulder fur, with Milo's dog Chopper, of "Sic bananas, Chopper" fame — and Kris stepped up toe-to-toe with Ace, all ninety pounds of him, and said we'd found him first and they could go whistle up the freight. Chopper, as it happened, fetched. Ace didn't.

Kris put a hand on Gordy's shoulder. "Write it down, Gordy," he said.

He did. And Gordy swears — and this storyteller believes him: "I never had any friends later on like the ones I had when I was twelve. Heaven help me — did you?"

**A Winter's Tale — The Grooming Method**

In the old banyan room — no name, no sign, candles in the sconces, and one portrait nobody can look at for long — the elders meet on Thursdays to groom, drink one small sherry, and tell stories. The rule of the room is simple: what is told indoors stays indoors. Dr. McCarron's best is about Sandra, a young tamarin, unmarried and in the family way, and proud as a queen about both things. In that year — the year of the great snow — a doctor would take the cases nobody else would, and Sandra took her teaching from the obstetrician with the calmest hands in the city: the grooming method for her lying-in. Slow strokes. Steady breath. No fear. She practiced it all winter on her own forearms, on her sisters, once, memorably, on a portrait of the troop's founder.

Then came the night of the blizzard, the night she should never have gone out — and she went anyway, walking through snow up to the chest of a bigger monkey, because the method had told her the baby was coming and she was not afraid, and would not be made so. The snow got in everywhere except, Dr. McCarron always says, her eyes.

What he swears happened at the end, when things went as wrong as things can go — that she simply refused to stop, and didn't — nobody in that club has ever laughed at, and nobody tells outdoors. The candles are always turned down for that part, and the one time a junior member tried to chuckle, he found his sherry finished and his coat handed to him before he'd set down the glass.

The old mandrill closed the book on us then, as he always does: "Different simians. Different seasons. Same old jungle."
