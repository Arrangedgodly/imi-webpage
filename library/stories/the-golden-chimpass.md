# The Golden Chimpass

*A parody of "The Golden Compass"*

Lyra Belacqua, a half-wild langur raised by the elders of Jordan Canopy College, was not supposed to be in the Retiring Room. She and her daimon hid inside a hollow fig tree while the Elders met — for in that world, every monkey's soul lived outside them as a small creature, changing shape until its person grew up. Lyra's daimon, Pan, flickered from moth to mite to tiny tree mouse at her ear.

Through the knothole, Lyra watched the Master pour something into Lord Asriel's banana-wine.

"Poison!" she hissed, and when Asriel came in, she burst out and blurted it, and the great monkey swept the cup to the floor with a crash.

For that act of loyalty — or perhaps out of guilt — the Master called Lyra to his study and gave her something wrapped in velvet: a golden instrument like a compass, its face ringed with thirty-six tiny pictures — the banana, the fig, the thunder, the crocodile, the cradle, the bird, the ape, the serpent.

"The alethiometer," said the Master. "The truth-teller. Some call it the Golden Chimpass. It answers any question — if you can read it. Few can. Perhaps, one day, you."

Lyra had no time for riddles, because there soon came to the College a mandrill of dazzling charm: Mrs. Coulter, fur gleaming like a rainstorm, whose daimon was a golden monkey with small, clever, cruel hands. She taught Lyra to bathe, to polish her fur, to hold a teacup; she spoke of airships and the far cold peaks, and Lyra, who had never had a mother or wanted one, followed her like a fig follows summer.

The Chimpass she hid in a torn mattress, because the golden monkey kept searching her room at night, quietly, drawer by drawer.

Then came the evening Lyra's hostess hosted a party, and on the arm of a guest Lyra read the truth in three swings of the needle: the General Oblation Board — the Nibblers, the shadowy crew who carried monkey children off to the far peaks — was headed by Mrs. Coulter herself. Mrs. Coulter WAS the Nibblers.

She ran that very night, Chimpass stuffed down her shirt, alarmed howling at her heels. And this was the truth the instrument had already told, though Lyra had not known how to read it: the gilded mandrill was her mother, and the fearsome Lord Asriel, exiled to the North, was her father.

The Riverfolk macaques took her north on their fig-barges, under the eye of Serafina Pekkala, a langur witch-queen whose daimon was a snow-white hornbill and who owed the Riverfolk a favor the size of a war. And it was Serafina who showed Lyra the boy.

They found him in a smokehouse by the port: a little marmoset, no bigger than a fig, sitting perfectly still on a bench.

"Where's your daimon?" Lyra asked.

The boy pointed to the empty air beside his shoulder. "He went away," he said, "when the machine held me down."

The Separation, the Riverfolk muttered. The machine that tore a monkey child from its soul, so that nothing would ever settle on them again, and Dander would pass them by. Dander — the strange dust that settles on monkeys only when they grow up, the very thing the grown-ups were so desperate to escape. Lyra sat beside the boy and set her own Pan on his empty shoulder, and Pan curled there like a comma, and the boy cried, and so did Lyra. It is hard to say which of them it helped more.

They could not save Tony by staying, so the Riverfolk took him in to heal, and Lyra went north, to war.

In a grim port town she met an armored gorilla — a true guerrilla of the mountains — named Iorek Byrnison, who had lost his sky-iron armor in a card game and was reduced to shoveling fish heads. Gorillas have no daimons, but Iorek had lost something worse: his honor. Lyra told a pawnbroker a lie so layered and so magnificent that the great gorilla got his armor home that same afternoon, and he pressed his forehead to hers, very gently, the way gorillas say thank you and try not to kill you.

"I will carry you," he said, "to the end of the world if you ask."

And she very nearly made him.

They came to Bananavangar, the Nibbler station in the ice — cages, clatter, and the machine. Lyra, disguised in borrowed furs, was caught, numbered, and marched to the Separation room, where the cold clamps came down on her arms. It was then, at the last possible moment, that she understood the needle of the Chimpass sliding to the crocodile and the serpent, and she cried out the only truth she had: "I am Lyra Belacqua, and Mrs. Coulter is my mother, and she will tear this station down to the rivets when she learns what you've done!"

Confusion. Names. Radios. A fire alarm — pulled by a kitchen marmoset named Roger, ROGER! — and through the wall came Iorek like an avalanche with opinions. The cages opened; the children rose; Roger filled his fists with ice; and the horrid place burned down to the ice as they fled, singing a war song with forty verses, most of them about figs.

At the Black Peaks stood the court of King Iofur, a gorilla who wanted, more than anything, to be a monkey — to have a daimon of his very own, a little soul to sit on his shoulder and adore him. It was Lyra who played on that longing. She marched into the throne room and announced that she was Iorek's daimon, escaped and free for the taking.

"A daimon may be won," she declared, "only by combat. Defeat Iorek, and I shall be YOURS, and the whole sky will envy you."

Iofur fought like a gorilla dreaming of being something else. Iorek fought like what he was — a king. He took one terrible blow, and lay still, and just as Iofur turned to claim his prize, Iorek's paw came up and took the challenger's jaw in his fist, and that was the end of the argument and of the reign. The crown went home to the rightful head, and the armored gorilla carried Lyra and Roger away on his back.

Far off, on a peak of ice, Lord Asriel had assembled a laboratory of brass and lightning. He was chasing the secret the grown-ups whispered of — Dander, the dust of growing up — and Lyra, arriving with Roger, felt her heart lift. Family, at last.

But when the tower flashed and the bridge of light began to grow into the sky, up toward a city hanging in the Aurora, Asriel was not looking at Lyra. He was looking at Roger. It takes a child, the instruments said, to make a door.

Lyra reached the summit a heartbeat too late. The light roared; the snow bloomed gold; and Roger's little daimon drifted free of him like a leaf letting go, and lay down in the snow beside him, and nothing Lyra did — and she did everything — could wake him. Asriel stood in the glare, not even watching, already walking toward the city in the sky.

Lyra buried her friend. Then she buried her fury, because fury could wait and the worlds could not.

"Pan," she said, watching the bridge blaze, "everyone we've met wants to climb that thing, or shut it, or sell it. Nobody's asked what's up there. We're going to follow it. All the way. We're going to find out about Dander ourselves, and then we're going to set things right."

Pan curled into a question mark and settled on her shoulder, and off they swung, into the wild blue truth.
