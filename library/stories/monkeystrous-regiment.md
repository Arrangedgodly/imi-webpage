# Monkeystrous Regiment

*A parody of "Monstrous Regiment"*

Polly Peeks stood in the privy behind her father's inn, The Duchess, with a pair of shears in one hand and her long hair in the other, and cut.

When it was gone she buttoned her brother's coat to the chin, stuffed a sock down the front, and became, to any recruiting sergeant who wasn't looking too hard, a young male monkey named Oliver Peeks, off to fight for glorious Borogrovia.

She had worked out the rules of being a lad on the walk to the recruiting table, and wrote them on her cuff to remember: never look at your own feet. Spit from the side of your mouth, not the front. If you can't think what to say, say it louder. Never, ever scream. And check the faces of everyone who checks yours.

The enemy was Zlobenia. The war had been going forever, and nobody could entirely explain it. Borogrovia's god, Nuggan, had declared a list of Abominations — among them the color blue, garlic, cats, bananas stored stem-upward, and *thinking about Nuggan's list being silly* — while the Duchess, the dead(?) figurehead everyone prayed to, smiled from every portrait as the country fell apart.

Polly wasn't in it for glory. Her brother Paul had marched off a year ago, and the letters had stopped. She was going to find him, or find out.

The recruit squad of the Tenth Foot was the strangest regiment in the history of armies. There was Tonker, all fire and fists, and Lofty, small and silent in her shadow — both runaways from the Girls' Working School, which Polly did not ask about. There was Shufti, cheerful, hopeless at soldiering, increasingly unable to hide a certain rounded condition under her coat. There was Igorina, an Igor with a needle, thread, and a medical degree ("It'th amazing what you can do with a thpare part, thir"). There was Wazzer, a nervous little langur who heard the voice of the Duchess all the time, which everyone found embarrassing until it won the war. There was Carbo, an enormous grey gorilla, and Maladict, a reformed vampire of the bat persuasion, on the wagon but with a coffee addiction so ferocious that everyone tiptoed once the beans ran out.

Their lieutenant was Blouse: a nice, clever, entirely useless young officer whose plan to take the Zlobenian fortress involved hiding in the laundry baskets — which worked, in the way things work when Sergeant Jackrum is the one actually doing them. Blouse was, as it happened, passionately in favor of women in the army; he had a pamphlet, with diagrams. He simply had no idea whatsoever that he was standing in one. Jackrum was the squad's true leader: a vast old orangutan with cheeks like storm sails, a razor, a cry of "Onward, my little apricots!" and the whole war on his back.

The laundry assault was Blouse's finest hour, in the sense that it was his and it lasted an hour. The squad went in with the wash. Blouse went in dressed as a washerwoman, having studied the walk from a book.

"Observe," he said, mincing through the gate with a basket, "the counterbalance of the hips. The towel is authentic. I have practiced the giggle."

The squad — every one of them an actual woman, and all of them laundering for real, with real water, on their actual knees — watched him with the frozen faces of monkeys at a funeral.

"You look very convincing, sir," said Tonker, lying with military discipline.

"Mmm," said the Zlobenian washerwoman, to whom the basket was handed. "Nice try, lads."

Thereafter the fortress was taken the traditional Jackrum way: side doors, shadows, a razor, and eleven separate people each convinced they had let the gorilla in by accident. Polly searched every freed prisoner's face for Paul; some faces, she understood slowly, would never be anywhere again. The squad stopped minding about Igorina's needle on the second day of holding the towers, which was the day Igorina took a bullet out of Tonker's shoulder by lamplight, humming, and sewed it back together better than it had been. "Thpare partth," she said modestly, and nobody ever joked about her kit again.

They held the towers against a relieving army — because by then the squad had discovered each other's socks.

It happened the way these things do: one campfire, one confession, then the confessions came out like popped stitches. Tonker: girl. Lofty: girl. Shufti: obviously. Igorina: yes, despite the name. Wazzer: girl. Carbo, the enormous gorilla: "I thould have thaid," sighed Igorina, "a recruiting line wath no place for thubtlety." Every one of them had joined because the world had left them nothing else — not one had run *toward* the army so much as away from what was behind.

That was the night the coffee ran out, and Maladict went rigid by the fire and began fighting an enemy nobody else could see — sighting down an invisible crossbow, quoting regulations in a dead voice, holding a staircase that was not there against a charge that had happened two hundred years ago. Polly knocked him down before he reached the shouting part, and Igorina kept him under, and nobody looked at anybody, because what a vampire is without his substitute is nobody's business but his own.

They also learned, from a clacks message out of Ankh-Morepork, that Nuggan had gone quiet. Gods die when nobody believes in them, and Nuggan had abominated so much that belief had simply moved out. But the Duchess — the Duchess, said Wazzer, white and shining, was not dead at all. She was only asleep, and she was waking up.

Much later, on the last bridge, Polly stood before the captured Zlobenian generals with the squad behind her and told the truth. The generals laughed — until she introduced Sergeant Jackrum.

"Jackrum," she said, "you old scoundrel. How long?"

The vast russet face split into a yellow grin. "Since before you were born, lad. I've served in eleven armies, been a mother twice, and buried every secret that needed burying. Now —" Jackrum pulled out the razor and held it like a grenade — "you can put that in your report and let the little ladies in their parlors faint, or you can win your war."

They won the war. It helped that Wazzer, possessed by the Duchess — a god waiting to wake up — walked through the battle untouched and spoke to both armies in a voice out of the ground: *Enough. Come home. All of you. That is a command, and I am your Duchess.* Armies that had prayed to that voice since childhood found, all at once, that they would rather obey it than anything their officers had ever shouted.

There was a treaty, an amnesty, and medals, and Polly wore hers with her hair grown back, in the new mixed regiment named the Monkeystrous Regiment — "monstrous" had been the Zlobenian newspapers' word, and it is never wise to let an enemy pick your nickname for you.

She found Paul's grave in the end, tended by strangers, his name spelled almost right. She left his favorite thing on the stone: a banana, stem-down, the correct way. Let Nuggan abominate that.

Years later, at The Duchess, where Polly kept the inn and the peace, a young recruit asked, "Sergeant — is it true the whole regiment was—"

"Stand up straight," said Polly, "and I'll tell you the only lesson the army ever taught me: you're not a lad or a lass in a battle. You're whoever's standing in the gap."
