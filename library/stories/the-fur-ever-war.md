# The Fur-ever War

*A parody of "The Forever War"*

The United Troops drafted Willchimp Mandella in the Year of the Ripe Fig, because he was young, healthy, and understood physics, and the troop had decided that combination was dangerous and best gotten rid of.

They trained him on the ice moon of Charon, in powered armor that amplified every twitch two hundred times. "The suit will do exactly what you ask," the sergeant told the recruits, "so be very careful what you ask." A recruit named Jones sneezed at parade rest and arrived two hundred meters away, embedded in a glacier. The sergeant didn't even turn around. "Anyone else want to negotiate with their armor?"

The enemy were the Tamarins. Nobody had ever seen one up close. Patrols had fired on shadows at the edge of the Canopy Gates — the black-hole jumps that folded the jungle of stars — and the shadows had fired back, and a war had grown out of it like mold on a warm fruit.

It was a war of Gate-jumps and light-lag, the troop insisted — fought at ranges where light takes a coffee break — and that ruled out anything clever. No remote drones: the Gates' gravity soup ate radio into porridge, so every machine had to carry its monkey with it. No ambush: the math that let you arrive let the other side see you coming a decade out. What remained was acceleration, armor, and arithmetic, and the troop supplied the arithmetic by drafting its physicists and issuing them rifles.

Marygay Potto was the other surviving smart one, a slow-eyed lemur-cousin with deadlier aim than any chimp in the company. On the first jump out she looked at the drill, the cold, the arithmetic of their survival odds, and said, "I'm a lover, not a fighter." Then she shot the target dummy's head off. "So this is going to be a very confusing millennium."

It was. That was the whole treachery of it. The Gates bent space, and bent time with it: the troop fought a four-week skirmish at the rim of a black hole and came home to find Earth two hundred years older. *That's not physics,* Mandella thought, reading the news of his own great-great-grandtroop. *That's bookkeeping.*

Their first raid found no Tamarins at all — empty groves, empty burrows, then panicked, unarmed Tamarins scattering like birds. Nobody ever established who fired first. It stopped mattering within the hour, and the hour was filmed, and the film was edited.

Promotion in the United Troops worked like this: survive. The survivor of a platoon commanded the platoon; the survivor of a company commanded the company; and somewhere above regimental level, the survivor was promoted entirely out of contact with anything that could kill him, which the survivors below considered the war's only real mercy. Mandella made major twice and captain once, on paper, between freezers, and never commanded anything that couldn't fit in a shuttle.

The second raid, on a world of ammonia snow, found Tamarins with weapons a century ahead of the troop's, because for the Tamarins it *was* a century later. The company went in two hundred strong and came out twenty-six. The medics froze the wounded and hoped the future could fix them; the future usually billed for it. Mandella got a medal for retreat conducted in an orderly fashion, which he kept in the same drawer as his opinion of the war.

"The trouble with fighting a relativistic enemy," he told Marygay in the ship's dark, "is that you're always fighting their newest idea with your oldest one. We're not soldiers, we're fossils with rifles."

"Fossils with good bone structure," Marygay said, which was as close as either of them came to a proposal for several centuries.

Between campaigns they came down to an Earth that kept forgetting them. The money changed. The language changed — English became a dialect of hand-signs, then a hummed drone — and old soldiers had to learn their own home by hypnotape in their sleep.

The first leave was the worst one. The troop had moved everybody who wanted a grove out to the frontier under the Homestead Act, and the cities had gone soft, clean, and strange, and Mandella's mother was living in a Carolina that no longer issued the stamps it still sold. He spent two weeks with her while she faded in and out of recognizing him, and left a day before he had to watch her fail at it entirely. He reported back to the troop early. It was the only time in his life he volunteered for anything.

Mandella tried to resign and be a teacher, and the troop, which could not afford to waste trained fossils, drafted him again before his first class reached midterms. By the sixth tour, the whole species had gone over to clone-castes, and the personnel officer was very kind about Mandella and Marygay's arrangement: "An alternative pair-bond orientation is perfectly acceptable, soldier. Just keep it off the parade ground."

"Two thousand years," Marygay said afterward, "and the only thing that evolved is the paperwork."

The last battle was fought on a cold world inside a stasis bubble where no machine worked. Every rifle, every jet, every wonder of four centuries went dead at the boundary line, and the whole space war came down, at the end of everything, to monkeys with sticks.

The planners had known it would, which was the humiliating part. For two years before the drop, the troop had drilled with sharpened branches on a training field, under instructors who outranked generals, complaining the whole time. Now the Tamarins came across the ice in a wave, and Mandella found his paws remembered everything the sergeant had screamed on Charon, and his body — thirty-one forever, a thousand years of drills deep — did the rest.

"We were suddenly, horribly," Mandella wrote in his journal, "good at it."

Mandella was thirty-one. He was also, chronologically, one thousand one hundred and eighty-six, and the troop he had fought for no longer existed in any form that would recognize him — as, in fairness, he no longer existed in any form it would recognize. The commander of the final assault was a clone colonel younger than his own sidearm, who addressed Mandella as "relic" without malice, the way you'd say "sergeant."

The Tamarins surrendered in the spring. There was a parade, and then the briefing, which the whole surviving species attended. The Tamarins, the envoy explained gently, were a cloned people, and so were the Earth-troops now, and clone could finally speak to clone. The war had ended between two peoples who could at last have been friends from the start.

"Nobody started it," the envoy said. "A patrol fired on shadows a thousand years ago, and the paperwork did the rest."

They offered the old soldiers a place in the collective anyway, with a stipend and a pat on the head.

Instead, the last of the pair-bonders boarded the colony ship *Time Out* with the other relics, bound for a young jungle under a new sun — two monkeys, aged thirty-one, holding four thousand years of used time between them and each other.

"You're late," Marygay said at the berth. "Relatively speaking."

Everything is, when you swing between stars. She took his hand, the ship cleared the Gate, and for the first time in a thousand years, no one anywhere was shooting at monkeys.
