# Poirot's Hairy Cases

*A parody of "Poirot's Early Cases"*

On a wet evening in his retirement, when the rain came down the windows of his London flat like a troop of small determined spider monkeys, Hercule Poirot received a visitor: a young tamarin from the *Gazette*, notebook clenched in both paws, who wanted — for the centenary number — the great detective's memories of his earliest cases.

"One does, you know," said Poirot, straightening his moustaches and waving the young tamarin to a chair, "love to be recognized. But the earliest cases, monsieur, are like the earliest figs of a season: small, green, and remembered mainly by the tree. Still." He moved a lamp. "It is a good night for them. I will tell you four."

---

**The first**, said Poirot, concerned the Fancy Dress Ball at the Canopy Club, where a young howler peer, Lord Conchsong, was found stabbed among the paper lanterns in his harlequin costume — and his little chorus capuchin, Coco, dead the next day of an overdose she was too young to have chosen. Six masks at that ball had worn the same diamond-pattern dress. "Everyone, you comprehend, spent the night being mistaken for everyone else. It was," said Poirot, "the jungle's oldest trick dressed in its newest clothes: *the duplicate costume*. The harlequin seen running from the lanterns at midnight with a bloody paw was not Conchsong at all — and the monkey who wept loudest over him was the only guest who had worn the dress *twice*, once to be seen, once not to be. When I laid the two halves of the pattern side by side, monsieur — the torn point of a diamond, found under the lantern, matching a patch sewn inside a sleeve — the murderer's case fell apart like wet cardboard. The troop had applauded the costume all evening. One does, you know, love to be recognized — but not, perhaps, *quite* so well."

**The second** was the affair of the Italian Nobleape. "A count — Foscapuchini by name, of a Roman family so old it predated fruit — was found dead in his rooms off Regent's Park, his dinner still warm, his valet vanished. The vanished valet, a mangabey called Graves, was the flower of respectability: monogrammed shirt-cuffs, a recommendation from a duke, tears at the funeral. Respectability so complete, monsieur, is like a coat with no seam: one knows at once that it has been *tailored*. The Count had dined that night with his lawyer on the subject of certain missing bonds; and the lawyer, a most correct lemur, had one irregularity — he had called at the rooms *after* dinner, 'not finding himself at the club.' The valet Graves had merely walked out of the front door wearing the bonds in his waistcoat while the back stair was blamed. I found him, in the end, by his shirt-cuffs. A monkey may forge a duke's letter, but he cannot forge *laundry*. The cuffs went home every Thursday to a washerwoman in Soho, monsieur, and a washerwoman, unlike a duke, remembers her own stitching."

**The third** — and here Poirot sat forward, and the lamp made an orange aureole of his fur — "the third you must print exactly as I give it you, for it is the case of which I am the *least* proud. In Brussels, when I was of the police there, a prominent deputy of the government died at his desk after supper, and the suspicion fell upon a cabinet rival. A box of chocolate figs had been sent to the deputy, and upon the box, the fingerprints, as it were, of the political enemy. I proved the case against that enemy with such elegance, such *symmetry* — motive, opportunity, a quarrel in the Chamber only the day before — that I congratulated myself before the court had even sat. And it was the beautiful young *widow* who had done it, monsieur, with the poisoned ones among the chocolate figs, the rival's grudge being only the wrapping paper she tied round the gift. She confessed it at last, cool as a conservatory. 'You reasoned admirably, Monsieur Poirot,' she told me, 'from everything but the box.' From that day to this, I have kept upon my mantelpiece a box of chocolate figs, unopened. It is not a sweet. It is a *vow*: never again to deduce what is probable from what is merely *neat*."

**And the fourth**, said Poirot, "I offer you because it was the least clever and the most important: the matter of the Prime Mate." The tamarin's ears stood straight up. "Yes. During the Great Troop War, when half the governments of the canopy were pretending to be each other's friends, the Prime Mate of the Low Countries — a small gray marmoset statesman with the whole future of the neutrals in his head — was taken from a train at a way station and made to disappear. They gave me thirty-six hours. There was a superbly obvious false trail — a motorcar sighted racing east, a bogus nurse, a report of the little statesman in a hospital on the frontier — all of it correct, all of it *arranged*, and arranged so well that I nearly obeyed it. But the trail was aimed, you see, at my intelligence; it flattered the little grey fur follicles; and the follicles, monsieur, when they are flattered, are already half asleep. The nursing home two streets from the way station, with its ordinary staff and its ordinary smell of camphor, held a 'dying financier' in a darkened room whom no one had ever seen. The Prime Mate was under chloroform in his own household's care, awaiting shipment, and the entire catastrophe had been organized by the secretary nearest him — the secretary of whom everyone said, 'Ah, *him*, one is fortunate in him.' Do not print the name; the Low Countries know it, and have buried it. Print instead, if you must print anything, that I found him with an hour to spare, because the false trail was *tidier than the truth* — and in my experience, which is now very long, the truth is always a little untidy, like the jungle itself."

---

"And the fifth," said Poirot, before his visitor could number them, "you shall have for nothing, since it is short and it concerns a *nest*. A young married couple of my acquaintance — he a minor official of the Treasury, she a thrifty little guenon — came to me in delight: they had found a flat in the most expensive quarter of London at a rent so small it was practically a rumor. I congratulated them. Then I asked to borrow their key, and I spent one evening in that flat with my ears. Messieurs, nobody lets a nest below the market. Above it, always, if the landlord is clever — but below, never. In the wall behind the dining-room paper we found a wire, and behind the wire a listening-post let to the *previous* tenants, a most respectable foreign couple who had sublet their respectability to a government with a keen interest in minor officials of the Treasury. The young couple moved out the same night. They have since had four children and pay the full market rate, and the husband tells everyone I saved his career; *mon vieux*, what I saved was his furniture, for the flat was bugged and the drawing-room chandelier was not, strictly, his." Poirot permitted himself one orange chuckle. "A rent, young monkey, is a fact, and facts do not discount themselves. When something is cheap, it is you who have been priced."

---

The tamarin wrote rapidly for some minutes, then looked up. "Five cases, Monsieur Poirot. But your memoirs list forty."

"My memoirs list what the memoirs list," said Poirot, unbending at last into something almost like a smile. "Forty cases make a bunch, monsieur, and a bunch is carried by its stem. You have heard the stem tonight. The rest is — *detail*." He rose, and straightened a picture frame that had not been crooked. "You are wondering what became of them all. The harlequin's murderer was hanged; the valet was deported; the young couple of the cheap nest named their third child after me, which I count a heavier sentence; the widow of Brussels lived long and beautifully, and once — this is not for your paper — she sent me, every year thereafter, a box of chocolates at Christmas. Untampered, you understand. A duel of courtesy. And the Prime Mate rebuilt his little neutral trees and died in his bed, full of years, saying of me only that I was 'the least comfortable monkey he had ever met.' It is," said Hercule Poirot, restoring the lid to the chocolate-fig box on the mantelpiece with one careful orange finger, "the finest epitaph in the collection."

Outside, the rain went on down the glass like small determined monkeys, and the lamplight held the little box — unopened, exact, and very nearly forgiven.

"Notebook away, young monkey. You will miss your last omnibus, and an editor. Both are more dangerous than anything in my files. Goodnight."
