# The Color Furple

*A parody of "The Color Purple"*

Dear Great Silverback in the Sky,

I am fourteen. My name is Celie. I write with berry juice on fig leaves, because nobody ever asks me how I feel, and You are the only monkey big enough to listen.

Pa gave me away today. He traded me to Mister — an old silverback with a mean streak, a broken-down fig grove, and a yard full of motherless young'uns — because Mister wanted a groomer, a cook, and a branch-sweeper, and Pa wanted a she-goat. I hope the goat is happy.

Mister's boy Harpo asked did I know how to pick ticks. I said yes. I know how to pick ticks, cook banana mash, and not cry. Two of those three is the truth, Great Silverback, but nobody checks.

Dear Great Silverback,

My sister Nettie ran from Pa's branch to mine, fleeing Mister's paw. He chased her to the grove gate, and she turned at the tree line and said, "Nothing but death can keep me from you, Celie. Write." For five years I wrote leaves and dropped them in the river and no answer came. I figured the river ate them, or You did, and I could not work out which of You I was madder at.

Harpo tried to beat his mate, Sofia, because his daddy said a mate should mind. Sofia beat him instead, twice, and told me over the fence: "All my life I had to fight. I had to fight the baboons. I had to fight the howlers. I had to fight my own daddy. But I never thought I'd have to fight in my own troop." I admired Sofia and I was scared of her, because I never fought nothing in my life.

Sofia had six young'uns and a temper apiece. Then came the Christmas the mayor's mate met her on the road — the mayor's mate, Miz Millie, all ribbons and teeth, who said, "How would you like to be my girl?" meaning come sweep my porch and mind my babies for nothing.

Sofia said hell no. Miz Millie slapped her. Sofia knocked her hat so far into the next grove it came back styled by a different monkey.

They caged Sofia for twelve years for that hat. The judge said assaulting a fine lady. The fine lady said she only meant to be friendly, which is what they all say while they take your young'uns to raise and your years to keep. When they let Sofia out on parole — to be Miz Millie's girl after all, which was the joke the law liked best — she came to me a beaten old monkey at thirty, her fight bent double like a sapling under a troop. I nursed her, and she growled at me for it, and I understood the growl. Some kindness arrives so late it looks like another slap.

Meantime Harpo took up with a little chittermonkey named Squeak, sweet as her name and twice as nervous. Squeak went to the jail to plead for Sofia and came back with her insides shaken loose; inside of a month she was singing at Harpo's juke joint, and no monkey called her Squeak anymore. She called herself by her born name. It fit her better.

Dear Great Silverback,

Shug Avery came down from Figphis sick as a wet season — the singer, the famous one, the gibbon with kohl on her eyelids, the one Mister loved and never got. He was too proud to groom her when she was ill, so I did. I picked every burr out of her fur, and she woke up, looked at me, and said, "You sure is ugly."

Six months later, what she said was, "You shore is company."

Then she went poking round Mister's private knothole, and out of it came pellets of folded leaf — fifty of them. Nettie's letters. Every one. Mister had hidden them where he figured no female could reach, the knothole being high.

He had never met Shug Avery.

Great Silverback, Nettie is alive, across the sea at a mission grove, teaching young ones their letters. And the two orphans she is raising — they are mine. My babies. The ones Pa took. They are alive and smart and their fur is curly. And there is worse in that bundle, Shug reading it out slow: Pa is not our pa. Our real daddy was a monkey with a fruit stand so fine the other vendors couldn't stand it, and they killed him for it and locked his grove. Nettie writes the grove is still in the family name. Us own a grove, Great Silverback. Us own the ground our trouble grew in.

That night I groomed Mister with murder in my fingers. He sat there yawning while I worked his shoulders, and I thought: one good yank at the scruff and I could unmake him like a badly woven nest. Shug caught my wrist without even looking. "Not while I'm around," she said. "Your time's coming. But not like that."

My time came at Sunday dinner. I told him what he was — a low-down, tree-blocking, fig-stealing tyrant — and that I was leaving with Shug in the morning.

Mister laughed. "You'll be back. A female without a troop is nothing."

I said, "I'm poor, I'm patched, I may even be funny-lookin', but dear Great Silverback, I'm here. And every monkey in this grove will remember you hid my sister's letters in a hole like a squirrel."

Sofia stood up. Shug stood up. Squeak stood up, and her new name with her. For once in my life, the whole table stood with me.

Dear Great Silverback,

In Figphis I started making grooming trousers — one-size britches with a tail-hole, corduroy, monkey-proof. I called them Folkspants. Us monkeys spend our lives chasing figs and chasing each other, and Folkspants give all that chasing somewhere to go. By the second season every monkey in Figphis wanted a pair, and I had employees, Great Silverback — monkeys waiting on me for a change, asking how I want it done.

Then Pa died, and the grove came home to me and Nettie, just like the letters said, and I moved my business into my own daddy's trees. Sofia and her young'uns came too. Harpo runs the juke joint. Mister sits on his stump getting smaller; last I heard he tried to be sweet to me at the market, and I sold him a pair of Folkspants at full price.

Dear Great Silverback. Dear everybody.

Nettie came home in the spring, in a wagon of the missionary line, with my children beside her — curly-furred, grown, speaking two languages and one of them mine. Mister, old and shrunken on his stump, said it beat him how me and Shug got along. Us sit on the porch at dusk now — me and Nettie, my children, Shug, Sofia — the whole ragged troop of us, grooming each other while the figs come down in the wind. The trees are in full fruit, that color I call furple: dusk settling on a ripe fig.

"I think it miffs the Great Silverback off," Shug said, "if you swing past the color furple in a grove somewhere and don't notice it. Monkeys think pleasing Him is all He wants. Any fool in this tree can see He just wants to be seen."

Matter of fact, I think this is the youngest us monkeys ever felt.

Yours,
Celie
