# Through the Grooming-Glass

*A parody of "Through the Looking-Glass"*

One thing was certain: the bushbaby had had nothing to do with it. Alice the young gibbon was quite sure, because she had been grooming him all afternoon, and he had purred like a fat fig wasp the whole time.

"Now, Quibble," she said, holding him up to the great silver grooming-glass in the grooming hollow, "if you lived inside there, you'd be a backwards bushbaby. You'd un-groom me. My fur would get *messier* the more you picked at it."

In the glass, the mirror-gibbon hung upside down, read books from the end to the beginning, and when the mirror-troop waved, they waved with their tails.

And then, suddenly, the glass turned to silver mist, and Alice found she could climb right through it. She hooked one long arm through, then the other, and dropped softly into the Grooming-Glass jungle, where the best way to look tidy was to try your hardest to look a fright.

Alice checked that her tail still hung down the right way, then set off. In the garden, the orchids were alive and very rude.

"O Orchid!" said Alice. "I wish you could talk."

"We *can* talk," snapped a Tiger Orchid. "We just don't, to creatures with ordinary fur. Run along — the Red Queen wants you, and she is always in a hurry, usually for nothing."

Further on, a huge Red Gorilla in scarlet fur took Alice's hand. "Faster!" she commanded, and they swung hand over hand through the branches, as fast as fast, until Alice was quite puffed — and then they stopped right back where they started.

"In *this* jungle," said the Red Gorilla Queen, "it takes all the swinging you can do, just to stay in the same tree. If you want to get somewhere else, you must swing at least twice as fast as *that*."

"I should like the swing that gets me somewhere," sighed Alice.

"Then hear the rules, for I shan't say them twice — I said them yesterday," said the Red Queen. "You are the White Pawn, and you have just been moved to the Second Square. Swing on to the Eighth, and there you may be crowned a Queen. Speak in turns when you meet royalty, and never remember anything the day before it happens. Off you go!" And away she swung, hard, in the wrong direction.

Next Alice met two round twin macaques under a tree, so alike you could not tell which was which. "Contrariwise," said the first. "If you believe *me*," said the second. Their names were Tweedle-Banana and Tweedle-Banana's Brother, and they were quarrelling over a squashed banana.

"Tweedle-Banana said I squashed his nice ripe banana!" cried one, "so we agree to have a battle!"

They boxed each other's ears politely for four minutes, until an enormous palm cockatoo shrieked overhead, at which the twins screamed, hugged, and hid behind Alice. The battle was postponed until drier weather.

Beyond them, on a narrow wall of branches, sat a vast smug Coconut.

"Don't stand chattering to yourself," said the Coconut. "Tell me your name and your business."

"When *I* use a word," the Coconut said grandly, a little later, "it means just what I choose it to mean — neither more nor less."

"The question is," said Alice, "whether you can make words mean so many different things."

"The question is," said the Coconut, "which is to be master — that's all. When I make a word do a lot of work like that, I always pay it extra. In bananas."

"There's glory for you," the Coconut added, having clearly won. "And what day of the month is it?"

Alice counted on her fingers. "The fourth."

"Two days wrong. It's one of my un-birthdays. There are three hundred and sixty-four of those in a year, you know — three hundred and sixty-four chances of presents. Of course, in this jungle presents are un-given, so I shouldn't wait up."

But the wall was narrow, and the Coconut was round, and round things on narrow walls have only one direction to go. CRACK. All the troop's tails and all the troop's kin could never put Coconut together again — though by springtime, Alice heard, a fine young palm tree had sprouted from the pieces.

A little further on, the trees shook with thumping: the Gorilla and the Lemur were fighting for the crown, because the Gorilla called it his breakfast and the Lemur called it his dinner. A bored old orangutan dealt them banana bread from a drum, at which they stopped fighting at once, each certain he had won.

Alice wandered on to a gentle White Gibbon Queen who lived her life backwards, and screamed a full minute before she pricked her finger. "It's a poor sort of memory," she told Alice kindly, "that only works backwards."

"Do you have banana jam today?" Alice asked.

"Never today," said the White Queen. "The rule is, jam to-morrow and jam yesterday — but never jam to-day."

"It must come to jam *sometimes*," Alice objected, "you know — to jam to-morrow."

"No, it wouldn't be jam to-morrow if it came to-day," said the White Queen. "It's jam every *other* day. To-day isn't any *other* day, you know."

"I don't understand you at all," said Alice.

"That's the effect of living backwards," said the White Queen gently. "One is a little giddy at first."

In a hollow tree Alice found a book she could only read by holding it up to a branch of fireflies, and in it was a poem about the fearsome Jabbermonkey — "'Twas brunchtime, and the slithy sloths / Did spiral and snooze in the boughs" — which seemed to be about twice as many things as it said.

It was on the Eighth-Square road that Alice met the White Gibbon Knight. He was old and gentle, mounted on a slow old tapir with turned-out toes, and he was falling off it.

"Backwards?" he asked hopefully, from the ground.

He looked so pleased that Alice hadn't the heart to say he had fallen forwards.

"You see," he explained, straightening his armour of coconut-shell plates, "the great art of riding is to fall off on the *far* side. Fall off the near side and your head meets the ground; fall off the far side and the ground merely goes by. I nearly always manage the far side. Eventually."

The Knight showed Alice his inventions, several of which were on fire in a small, controlled way; the finest was a box tied upside down over the tapir's rump, to keep the rain off the banana bread.

"But the bread falls out of an upside-down box," said Alice.

"Only when it wants to," said the Knight. "And then it isn't bread any more. It's a discovery."

At the boundary of the Eighth Square he stopped, and Alice felt suddenly sad without knowing why.

"Will you remember me?" the Knight asked. "I shan't forget you, though remembering is confusing here — I shall remember you next Tuesday, which is practically always. Wave your tail at me as you go. A tail is not as good as a handkerchief, but it is far more honest."

Alice swung up into the Eighth Square, and looked back, and waved her tail; and the Knight waved his; and then he fell off the tapir once more — on the far side, naturally — and even at that distance she could tell he was counting it a success.

"QUEEN ALICE," whispered the leaves, and a golden crown slid down the branches and settled, a size too big, over her ears. The Red and White Queens appeared at once, one on each side, to examine her.

"Can you do Addition?" began the White Queen. "What's one and one and one and one and one and one and one and one and one and one?"

"I don't know," said Alice. "I lost count."

"She can't do *Addition*," the White Queen whispered. "Can you do Subtraction?" said the Red Queen. "Take a banana from a troop: what remains?"

Alice considered. "The banana wouldn't remain — and the troop wouldn't remain — but the *grudge* would remain."

"That's not the answer," said the Red Queen, "but it's a truer one."

At last came a great feast in her honour — "banana to-morrow and banana yesterday, but never banana to-day" — where the two Queens bickered over which of them had dreamed Alice up, until the Red Queen flung her scarlet arms around Alice and shrieked, "SHE'S MINE, AND SHE'S PERFECTLY REAL!"

"REAL!" snapped the White Queen. "She's about as real as the jam! And the jam, I need hardly remind you, is NEVER TO-DAY!"

Alice, out of all patience, seized the Red Queen by her scarlet fur and shook her, saying, "I'll shake you into a bushbaby, that I will!" —

And she did. For there, in her lap by the grooming-glass, sat Quibble the bushbaby, purring like a fat fig wasp, with a crumb of banana bread on his nose that he had certainly not eaten to-day.

"Now, Quibble," said Alice, snuggling down to sleep, "who dreamed it all? Was it me — or was it the Red Gorilla King? You were in my dream, of course — but then, you might have been dreaming *me*. And you know, you were a good deal too smeared with jam to have been asleep."

Quibble purred, which is how a bushbaby says *yes*, *no*, and *more jam to-morrow*, all at once.

Which do you think it was?
