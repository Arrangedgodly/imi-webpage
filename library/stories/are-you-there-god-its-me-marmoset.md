# Are You There God? It's Me, Marmoset.

*A parody of "Are You There God? It's Me, Margaret."*

Are you there God? It's me, Marmoset. Margaret Monkeyson's granddaughter — well, everyone just calls me Marmoset, on account of I'm small and my mane is fluffy and my grandfather thought it was funny. Anyway. God, today we moved from the big jungle to the suburbs of Farbranch, and I hate it already, and nobody asked me. Are you there God? It's me, Marmoset.

Marmoset was eleven, small for her age, with a tail she was secretly proud of and a mane she was secretly worried about, because it was still baby-fuzz when everyone knew that grown-up marmosets got thick, glorious ruffs of fur. Her grandmother Sylvia said Marmoset's mother had gotten hers at eleven. Her mother said fur didn't matter. Marmoset had a chart in her hidden notebook that proved otherwise.

The first day at her new school, Farbranch Intermediate, a monkey named Nancy Whisker attached herself to Marmoset at the water spout and appointed her a best friend before lunch.

"We have a club," Nancy whispered. "Very secret. Very exclusive. It's the four of us — me, Gretchen Potto, Janie Bushbaby, and now you. We have rules and a notebook."

"What's the club about?" asked Marmoset.

Nancy looked around, lowered her voice, and said the most exciting words in the sixth-grade jungle: "Growing up."

The four PTs — Pre-Teen Sensations — held their first meeting at Gretchen's treehouse. The notebook had four columns: name, troop, fur status, and DATES (which none of them had, but the column existed, ready). Gretchen, who was heavy and serious, already had wisps of grown-up fur at her collar. Janie, a skinny little bushbaby with enormous eyes, claimed she didn't care about fur at all, then asked six questions about it. Nancy declared that her own fur was "practically in already," which Nancy said about everything.

"We do exercises," Nancy announced. "Everyone knows them. Arm circles. Chanting. It works. My cousin told me."

So the four of them stood in the treehouse, arms out, swinging in circles like the branches of a palm in a storm, and chanted the ancient rhyme:

*"We must, we must, we must increase our FLUFF."*

"Again!" commanded Nancy. *"We must, we must, we must increase our fluff!"*

Marmoset chanted and swung and thought: Are you there God? It's me, Marmoset. Please let me be the first in the club to fluff out. Or at least not the last. I know that's vain. Sorry. Amen.

There was one other subject at Farbranch Intermediate, and her name was Laura Lemur. Laura was tall and rangy and had a full adult tail-ring and a thick grown-up mane and — the unfairness of this was discussed at length — she had skipped a grade, so she was younger than everyone AND fluffier. She hung around with the eighth-grade baboons by the far fence. Nancy hated her.

"She stuffs her mane," Nancy whispered. "With moss. Everybody knows."

Marmoset didn't think Laura stuffed her mane. Marmoset mostly thought about her own mane, which remained baby-fuzz, flat as a peeled banana.

At home there was a whole other kind of growing up going on, and it was worse.

Marmoset's mother had grown up Howler — loud, festive, big family dinners at the troop hall, holidays with drums. Marmoset's father had grown up Tamarin — quiet, studious, Friday nights at the little temple in the fig grove. Neither of them practiced anymore. "Marmoset can choose for herself when she's older," they always said, which sounded open-minded and turned out to mean that everyone got to fight about her instead.

"Are you there God? It's me, Marmoset. I don't know what You are. Do You have fur? Are You a great big silverback? Or more of a presence? Grandmother Sylvia says You're one thing and Grandpa Monkeyson says You're another, and Mom says one thing and Dad says another, and I made a deal with You: if You tell me what You want me to be, I'll do it. Just tell me. A sign. A small one. Amen."

Then the school year turned, and Mr. Benedict, her teacher, announced the assignment that would eat the whole year: a personal project on the question "What I Think About Fur, Faith, and Growing Up." (He'd written only "What I Think About God," but the school board had made him add the other words, which told Marmoset something about adults.)

Marmoset's project went nowhere. How could she write What I Think when she didn't know what she thought? She interviewed her grandmother Sylvia, who took her to the Howler troop hall and fed her until she begged for mercy. "You're my little Marmoset," said Sylvia. "You'll come around. We Howlers have the best songs, that's all there is to it." She interviewed her grandfather, who raised an eyebrow and said, "Do what makes you kind, boychik, and eat something."

Then Grandma Sylvia sent a telegram to the house addressed to MARGARET, WHICH IS HER REAL NAME, YOU KNOW — just Marmoset, and the mail monkey read it aloud: SEE YOU AT THE TROOP HOLIDAY SING, GRANDMA.

At Thanksgiving dinner at Sylvia's, the war came to the table. An uncle from the Tamarin side mentioned the fig-grove temple. A cousin from the Howler side mentioned the troop hall. Somebody said "raised with nothing," and somebody else said "at least we have something," and plates were carried away with great dignity and the dessert was eaten in an unbearable silence, and in the car going home Marmoset's parents didn't speak to each other all the way to Farbranch.

"Are you there God? It's me, Marmoset. Do You see what You're doing to my family? Everybody wants me on their branch. Nobody asks which branch I want. Maybe I want MY OWN BRANCH, God. Did anybody think of that? Anyway. Sorry for yelling. Amen."

The club went on being the club. Nancy got her grown-up fur in first and walked around the halls flipping her mane like a flag, and was insufferable about it, and Marmoset did her arm circles at night with new desperation. *"We must, we must, we must increase our fluff."* Nothing happened. She measured her mane every Sunday with a ruler. Nothing.

Then there was Philip Lemur, Laura's cousin, who had started it — the boy-girl parties, I mean. Laura Lemur had one at her treehouse (which was bigger than the PT clubhouse, the treehouse, everything), and they played a game with a spinning coconut, and whoever the coconut pointed to had to go into the pantry with whoever had spun it. Marmoset spun and it pointed to Norman Fishbein, who was short and had a laugh like a hyena, and in the pantry Norman said "Hi," and Marmoset said "Hi," and they stood there in the dark among the jars of preserved figs until the other monkeys counted to twenty, and Marmoset came out having neither kissed nor been kissed, but having made a friend, which lasted longer.

The year turned again. In spring, when the fig trees leafed out and the sixth graders were practically seventh graders, Marmoset's class took the trip to the city zoo, and it rained, and Marmoset got soaked to the skin like a wet little stray, and Nancy said something snide about her flat baby fuzz in front of Laura Lemur, and Laura — Laura, of all monkeys — said, "Lay off her, Whisker. Fur comes when it comes."

That night, towel-drying in front of the mirror, Marmoset noticed something. Around her collar, where the baby fuzz had been flat as a pancake all year, there was — she leaned close — there was *frizz*. Actual frizz. An unmistakable, preliminary, gorgeous halo of frizz.

"Are you there God?" whispered Marmoset. "It's me. Marmoset. Remember me? I got it! I'm getting it! I don't even care about being first anymore — well, I care a LITTLE — but God, mostly I just wanted to tell You I'm still here, and I still don't know what You are exactly, and my family is still fighting about You, and I still haven't finished Mr. Benedict's project. But I've decided something. I'm Marmoset. Not the Howler kind, not the Tamarin kind. My own kind. If that's wrong, You can send a sign."

No sign came. No thunderclap. No silverback in the clouds. Just the soft sound of rain on the fig leaves outside her window, which, if you were listening for it, was a little like applause.

A few weeks later, at breakfast, her mother froze with the fruit bowl in her hand and stared. "Sylvia," she called to her father. "Come look at your daughter."

Marmoset looked in the hall mirror. Around her face, in a full glorious ruff, stood the beginning of a real grown-up marmoset mane — fluffy as a dandelion, ridiculous and wonderful and entirely, unmistakably hers.

"Are you there God?" said Marmoset to the mirror, to the morning, to the whatever-You-are. "It's me, Marmoset. My fur came in."

She grinned at herself with all her teeth, and went to find her notebook.

*"We must, we must,"* she wrote on the first blank page, *"we must be ourselves."*

And that, God, was the whole assignment. Amen.
