# The Monkey Branch Gang

*A parody of "The Monkey Wrench Gang"*

Dr. Sarvis — Doc to everyone, a silverback gorilla with a gray beard, a surgical practice, and a Lincoln convertible that drank like a camel — drove out into the canyon country of the great dry forest and set a billboard on fire. It was a modest beginning. The billboard said EAT MORE BANANAS: IMPORTED. Doc lit it with the same care he took with a wound, watched the plastic banana curl and blacken, and said to his passenger, "Surgical. Absolutely surgical."

His passenger was Bonnie Bonobo, his assistant, a liberated bonobo of advanced opinions who threw wrenches joyously and laughed like water going over rocks. "Beautiful," she said. "Do another."

They were four, in the end. The newspapers called them the Gorilla Gang, which was exactly one-quarter accurate.

There was Seldom Seen Sifaka, river runner and guide, a dancing lemur of the high plateaus who traveled sideways when he ran and was married to more wives than the law of the jungle allowed, the law allowing none. And there was George Washington Howlerduke, home from the wars with a rucksack of high explosive, a stolen troop truck, and a loathing of machinery so pure it shone. Howlerduke howled. It was not a figure of speech.

They had met, as all great conspiracies meet, over ruined country. The Canopy Conglomerate had come to the canyons with its yellow machines — the great grader-cats, the haulers, the chainsaws that went all day like a nerve being cut — and was stripping the old forest to build the Gorilla Dam, a wall of concrete across the finest red-rock gorge in the world, to drown ninety miles of paradise for the sake of a lake for houseboats.

"I don't believe in monkeying around," Doc said at their first full council, in the back room of a cantina. "I believe in monkey wrenches."

He produced one, sized to an actual monkey.

"Gentlemen and comrade," he said, "the machine has enemies. We are the enemies. The question is whether we are serious ones."

"Serious," said Seldom Seen, sidling sideways even while seated.

"Serious," said Bonnie, bouncing a lug nut off the wall for luck.

Howlerduke replied with a howl, a grin, and a crate of railway fuse. Nobody had ever been less in doubt.

Their first season was small, surgical, joyful. They pulled survey stakes by the hundred, and Seldom Seen relocated them — across the river, into a swamp, once in an unbroken straight line directly off a cliff, which the surveyors obeyed for a week. They put sand in the fuel tanks of the yellow cats and sugar in their oil, and Howlerduke taught them the great catechism of sabotage: a machine taken at night, with wrenches, is worth ten in daylight with speeches.

"It's not monkey business," he said, wire-cutters in his teeth. "It's monkey wrench business. Precision work."

They graduated to the log train. Howlerduke knew the trestles; Seldom Seen knew the water; Doc knew chemistry; Bonnie knew the exact pitch of a small explosion required to inconvenience a corporation without inconveniencing a single ranger overmuch. They dropped a trestle into the gorge at 3 a.m. and watched forty cars of chainsaws and milled timber follow it down, the whole rustling cargo of the dying forest burying itself in the rock.

Then the helicopters came, and the manhunt.

Sheriff Baboon of the county — a heavy, patient, dangerous old baboon who drank black coffee and thought in circles — took it personally. He worked the case like a monkey works a termite mound: patiently, with one long finger, and every intention of reaching the bottom. He interviewed the diner twice and the diner's cat once. He stood on the rim with his hands folded behind his back, looking down at forty cars of chainsaws in the gorge, and the waitress heard him say, quietly, to nobody in particular: "Somebody's having a war up here. And every monkey in it thinks he's the good guy." Then he went back to his coffee and his circles, because a sheriff who can see both sides of a canyon is the most dangerous kind there is.

He wanted them alive, and he nearly had them, twice: once at the cache under the rim, once in the maze of side canyons called, with cause, the Maze. They ran three days on creek gravel and cactus fruit, sleeping in slots, drinking seep-water, while the choppers walked the country like dragonflies with grudges.

In the Maze, Howlerduke went off alone to draw the pursuit. They heard the radio traffic turn jubilant: contact, contact, then a rifleman in a helicopter firing at a fleeing figure on a ledge, and then dust, and silence, and no body.

They grieved in the only way available, which was to keep working. But it was Doc who kept Howlerduke's fork at his place, and Bonnie who wrote his name on the crate of the next operation in grease pencil, and Seldom Seen who danced, one slow sideways step, alone on the rimrock at dusk, facing the canyon.

Meanwhile the dam rose, grey and vast, and the water came up the gorge, drowning the cottonwood groves where Seldom Seen had been young and married and, in his words, seen rarely.

They planned the big one through the winter: a houseboat, the Brave Cowbob, packed from keel to cupola with stolen explosive, to be drifted down the reservoir by night into the face of the Gorilla Dam itself, and touched off with a timed fuse, and blown — they had no illusions — maybe six inches off its stride, maybe none, the point being the six inches, and the statement, and the fun.

It was Doc's policy to operate. It was everybody's policy to remember that four primates and one boat of dynamite were, in the long account of the jungle, not a raid but a tide.

They came together for it in spring, on the reservoir's quiet headwaters — Doc at the wheel of the Cowbob, Bonnie rigging the charge, Seldom Seen reading the water, and Sheriff Baboon somewhere behind them with searchlights and a lifetime supply of patience.

And it was then, on the second night, thin as a whip, scorched, barefoot, bearded to the eyebrows, wearing six weeks of the Maze, that George Washington Howlerduke walked out of the dark and into their firelight and helped himself to the beans.

Nobody screamed. Bonnie poured coffee. Seldom Seen slid over companionably sideways. Doc looked up from his clipboard for a long, surgeon's moment.

"You're late," Doc said.

"Had to shake a chopper," said Howlerduke. "And a rattlesnake, and two Social Climbers from the power company. Fine animal, the rattlesnake. Better manners than the utility." He looked around the fire at the last of the old forest's free gang, and his grin came up like a sunrise with bad intentions. "Well, you ol' gorillas. Did you save my fork?"

"Bonnie saved your fork," said Doc.

"Then let's go sink a dam."

The Gorilla Dam still stands, if you're wondering. Concrete is patient; sheriffs are patient; corporations are patient beyond either. But so is water, and so is green, and so is the stubborn idea that a jungle is not a warehouse. The Monkey Branch Gang rowed away into the dark that night with their heads high and their fuses dry, and somewhere tonight they are out there still — pulling stakes, draining crankcases, dancing sideways along the edges of the flooding world, waiting, in the oldest tradition of the wild, for spring.

You ready?

Ready when you are, you ol' gorilla.
