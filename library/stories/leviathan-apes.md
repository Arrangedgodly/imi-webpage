# Leviathan Apes

*A parody of "Leviathan Wakes"*

Julie Macaque's last log entry, recovered later, half-melted: "I ran away from the family groves to be free, and now I'm locked in a berth on Eros with something growing in me that isn't disease, exactly. It's more like an instruction. If anybody finds this — my name is Julie, and I tried."

Eighteen months before that, the ice-hauler *Canopyterbury* was dragging a mountain of frozen water out to the Branch stations when a distress call crackled in from a derelict scout called the *Scoopolee*. Jim Holden, the ship's chimp second officer, believed the universe was basically fair, which is an expensive belief. He took the shuttle across. The *Scoopolee* was empty, opened up like a fig, with one gray hair of a bio-sample left in a locked freezer — and a stealth ship rose out of the dark and torpedoed the *Cant* out from under him.

The Martians fished Holden's four survivors out of the black and housed them aboard the flagship *Donnager*, which the same stealth ships then sank, in front of witnesses, with weapons only Mars had. That arithmetic was meant to start a war. Instead the shoebox of survivors escaped on a stolen gunship — the *Rousinante*, because a knight needs a horse — and Holden did the honest thing, which was the catastrophic thing: he broadcast everything, to everyone, everywhere, with the guilty weapons signatures attached.

The system lit up like a hot stove. Earthers blamed Mars, Mars blamed Earth, and the Outer Plantain Alliance — the OPA, Branchers who flashed each other the plantain tattoo — got blamed by both, as usual. Riots wore REMEMBER THE CANT like a bandage. Holden's crew ran to the one rock in the system that would hide them: Tycho Station, where Fred Johnson — the Butcher of Anderson Station, ex-Earth, now the OPA's respectably dressed monster — read their broadcast, checked their math, and smiled like a monkey watching fruit ripen. "Congratulations," he said. "You've just made half the system's professional liars work the weekend."

On Ceres, a big grizzled gorilla detective named Miller worked the same fire from the other end. Julie Macaque — the runaway daughter of the family that owned half the shipping in the system — had walked into a station one day and never walked out. Miller traced her through fake names and dead-end berths to a dockside office, where Anderson Dawes — Ceres's OPA chief, a Brancher with a preacher's patience — finally told him the truth like it cost nothing: "She came to us, cop. True believer, ran from daddy's money to save the Belt. We put her on a ship. The ship was the *Anubis*." Miller's captain told him to drop it. His leads kept dying, which told Miller he was close, and his gut kept telling him the case was standing still and the station was moving underneath it. They took his badge. He kept his hat and his gun, which for Miller was only the sentimental half.

The *Rousinante* found the *Anubis* first, drifting with her transponder dead. Holden's crew boarded her in suits and found the crew — or where the crew had been — grown over with the same blue coral as the *Scoopolee's* locker, whole corridors curtained in glowing lattice, and in the drive room a video log from a girl with a Mao nose, apologizing to nobody in particular. Julie had taken the shuttle to Eros. They burned after her.

The trails crossed on Eros.

The trails crossed on Eros.

Eros was one of those stations that never should have worked, a rock honeycombed with tunnels and noodle stands. Miller and Holden's crew docked under false colors to grab a data cube, and found the tunnels glowing faintly blue, and the radiation rising, and something spreading that had been dug out of a moon where it had been sleeping since before monkeys existed: the proto-banana seed. An alien seed, never meant for monkeys, that took whatever biomass was lying around and built with it. They were not the gardeners. They were the soil.

The station emptied its blues at them. Monkeys who had been monkeys a shift ago came apart and came onward, gliding on filaments of light, assembling as they came, curious as furniture is not. One of them had been Miller's snitch. It leaned into the elevator car, half-fused to the wall, and said hello.

"Nobody shoots my informant," Miller said, and shot his informant, and they ran.

The data cube, decrypted aboard the *Rousinante*, was worse than the monsters. Eros had not been unlucky. Eros had been *procured* — a whole station's population locked in with the seed while a corporation's science division watched through the radiation sensors and took notes. Holden read the file twice, put it down, and did the arithmetic on his honesty: the broadcast he'd sent to save the system had conveniently rid certain ledgers of certain witnesses. "Radiation protocol," the file called it. He sent that broadcast too.

They had planted a bomb to sterilize the station, and they meant to see it used. But Miller had stopped listening to the case by then and started listening to the girl. He followed the blue glow down through the docking levels, past the noodle stands, to room 22 at the bottom of everything.

Her room was crystallized in blue. Her body lay fused into the bed, hair spread like vines of light, one arm flung toward the door as if she'd tried, at the end, to go home. Miller, who had fallen in love with a poster, sat down on the edge of the glowing bed and took off his hat.

"I found her," he transmitted, to a crew that had stopped expecting anything gentle. "I finally found Julie Mao."

Then Eros moved.

The bomb they'd planted was dodged — dodged, by a rock — and the whole station changed course and accelerated, carrying its blue light and every dying monkey aboard, straight at Venus. Holden's crew ran for the ship. Miller didn't. The radiation had already written his itinerary.

"Somebody's steering her," he said. "Somebody ought to ride along. Hey, Julie. It's Miller. We should get out of here."

Holden screamed at him over the channel, and Miller — for once in his furry life — didn't argue back. There was nothing left to argue with. He walked into the blue and let it read him, and the station, which had been shouting into the dark for weeks with no one to hear, found one monkey who would listen.

In the inner system, an ancient macaque grandmother named Avasarala, who ran the UN's dirtiest office, watched Eros dodge the combined analysis of every telescope in the system and said, quietly, to a room full of frightened functionaries: "Gentlemen. Whatever that thing is, it just made our navies decorative."

Eros hit the Venusian clouds like a seed into wet ground, and the blue light spread from pole to pole, and every telescope in the system watched a dead world begin, very slowly, to grow something.

Whatever dropped that seed so long ago had finished sprouting. And the monkeys had run out of jungle to hide in.
