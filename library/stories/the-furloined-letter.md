# The Furloined Letter

*A parody of "The Purloined Letter"*

One chilly night, while the wind worried the shutters of the Rue Morgue, I sat in my friend Dupin's library-nest, watching the orangutan fill his calabash pipe and read three books at once, which was his way of relaxing. Dupin was an ape of great leisure and greater nostrils, and since the unpleasantness involving the sailor and his escaped orangutan — a cousin of his; he declined to discuss it — the street had been very quiet.

Then the Prefect of Police came up the stairs like a small landslide.

The Prefect was a gorilla with a chest full of gold braid and no imagination whatever, and for once he was rattled. A letter had been stolen. Not just any letter: a letter from the Queen's own nest, taken by the Minister D—, a macaque of fashion, wit, and pure mischief, while the Queen herself stood by mid-grooming, unable to say one word — because a certain archrival matriarch was watching, and the letter's mere existence, explained aloud, would have brought the whole canopy down.

"And you are certain he took it?" asked Dupin.

"Certain! The Queen was reading it by the nest-fire. The Minister enters — he enters at any hour, it is his fashion — sees the letter, sees the matriarch watching, and understands everything in one flick of his whiskers. He produces a letter of his own, nearly the same size, lays it beside hers as if to compare vines, chats about the weather in the lower branches, bows, and departs — with her letter, leaving his own in its place. She saw the entire theft, Monsieur Dupin. She could not move a paw."

"He took it under her very nose," said I.

"That is the point," said Dupin, filling his pipe. "There is a great deal of nose in this affair."

"The Minister is a schemer, Monsieur Dupin. While he holds that letter, he holds the Queen. He never uses it; he only sits on it, like a hen. We have searched his den twice."

"And found nothing?"

"Nothing! We opened every cushion and turned out the stuffing. We probed every chair leg with a whisker. We checked the knotholes, the hollow tail-posts, the termite galleries, the thatch, the rain spout, and the insides of four hundred books. We X-rayed his bananas. We shook the stuffing from the cushions and the cushions from the stuffing. I assure you, Monsieur Dupin, that no den in Paris-le-Primate has been so thoroughly taken apart, and I confess I am at my wits' end."

"Perhaps," said Dupin, when the Prefect had gone, "the mystery is too simple for a police mind. The Prefect is thorough, and thoroughness is the virtue of monkeys who expect all secrets to be hidden the way they would hide them: down, and in the dark. Consider, my friend: how does a monkey hide one banana from a hungry troop?"

"In a locked box."

"Wrong. Every hungry troop checks the locked boxes first. You stick it in a bunch. It stops being a banana; it becomes one banana among bananas. The Minister is a poet as well as a schemer, and that is precisely why he is dangerous. A mere schemer hides things where the police look. A poet hides them where the police look *past*."

"Then the Minister is a mathematician," said I, "and reasoners go where the mathematics goes."

"He is both, which is why he will be caught. As a mathematician alone he would reason like the Prefect — the Prefect believes any mystery deeper than a knothole must be buried under one. The police can see anything except the obvious. I dare say the letter is not hidden at all."

He said no more for a month. Then the Prefect returned, looking like a gorilla who has been through every cushion twice, and announced that the reward had been doubled: fifty thousand banana coins.

"We took the legs off the pianoforte, Monsieur Dupin. Every leg. The piano plays flat now. We removed the frass from between every floor board with a chamomile spoon."

"And the letter," said Dupin, "is still behind your whiskers?"

"Flat."

"Then make one more search, and consider — precisely — how the Minister would hide so large a thing as a pianoforte."

The Prefect promised, and left; and the next morning he returned, wild-eyed, having torn his own den apart on principle, and Dupin produced from his desk a certain letter. The Prefect seized it, verified it, and wrote out a check for fifty thousand banana coins with the trembling joy of a gorilla who has found a fresh bamboo grove. He did not ask how. The police never do; it saves time.

"The Minister D—," said Dupin, settling into the explanation over the calabash, "is bold only where the police are literal. The following week I called upon him wearing banana-tinted spectacles and complaining of weak eyes, and while complaining of weak eyes I noticed everything. On the mantel, in a tawdry rack stuffed with tail-ribbons and half-molted invitations, hung a letter — much crumpled, much soiled, torn roughly in the middle (the Minister has a habit of maltreating his correspondence) — and it was addressed, in a big bold scrawl, to the Minister himself. Except that the paper was too fine for a tailor's bill, too creased for a friend's, and exactly the size of a certain royal banana. It had been turned inside out, refolded, and readdressed in the Minister's own paw. The vexation this afforded the police, who took it for their own handiwork's leavings, would have been worth the visit by itself."

"And the second visit?"

"Brief. I called again; we discussed poetry, at which he sneered, and sight, at which he boasted. 'In this jungle, Monsieur Dupin,' said he, 'everyone looks; nobody sees.' 'Agreed,' said I, looking. Then, in a display of absentmindedness I had rehearsed twice, I knocked over my own grooming powder, and in the flurry of apologies and general whiteness a facsimile — manufactured overnight and crumpled to the precise historical degree — changed places with the original. As I rose to leave, the Minister D— held the door and smiled the smile of a monkey who has out-clevered everyone in the jungle."

"And left," said I, "holding the door for a monkey who had."

"There is a schoolboy in the Fourth Canopy," said Dupin, "who wins every game of odds-and-evens. Asked his method, he said: 'I look at the other monkey's knuckles. He looks sly, so he has chosen something simple. He looks simple, so I expect a trick. The question is never what *I* would hide. It is what he imagines *I* expect him to hide.' The Minister is that schoolboy grown up; the Prefect never made it past the knuckles."

"The Prefect," I said, "imagined a dragon."

"The Prefect imagined a squirrel with a nut. He hunted through every knothole in the jungle while the letter hung above the fireplace, addressed to the thief, in the handwriting of the thief."

"And the note," I asked — "the one you told me you left inside the facsimile?"

Dupin smiled through the smoke.

"A scheme so foul," he said, "that if it is not worthy of A-treat-us, it is worthy of Thy-eats-us. He will understand it perfectly. I am not vindictive — though the Minister once humiliated me in Vienna, over a lady mandrill. I am merely thorough."
