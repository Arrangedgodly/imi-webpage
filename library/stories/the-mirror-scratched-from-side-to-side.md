# The Mirror Scratched from Side to Side

*A parody of "The Mirror Crack'd from Side to Side"*

The great ape-tress Marina Gregg came to Gibbington Hall like weather. She had been married five times, filmed on four continents, and knelt now in the flowerbeds of her new estate in a broad straw hat, planting azaleas, while her husband — Jason Rudd, a solid gorilla of a film director who adored her to the roots of his own hair — supervised the trellis. Down at the lodge cottage, in a cottage lent her by the family who had once owned the Hall, sat Miss Maple of Nana Mead, in the district for her health, watching the azaleas go in and the preparations begin for the fete in aid of the Jungle Ambulance Association, of which Marina Gregg had consented to be patroness.

The county queued to shake her paw. Among the first came Mrs. Heather Badpelt — a fat, cheerful, radiant macaque, one of those monkeys who arrive everywhere delighted and tell you the whole of their lives while you are still saying how do you do.

"I met you before, you know!" said Heather Badpelt, glowing, seizing the star's paw in both of hers. "In Calcutta — no, Singapore — during the war tour! I was convalescent, monkey-measles, they'd barely let me out of bed, but I *would* come, I slipped out and stood in the rain for four hours, and when it was over you shook my hand! You said 'What a brave girl.' I've never forgotten it as long as I've lived. I think of it whenever I'm down. Well — champagne, did you say? Don't mind if I do."

The photographer's flash caught Marina Gregg's face at that moment, and afterward everybody in the Hall described the photograph differently, because nobody could agree what they had seen. Mrs. Bantry-figures said she looked stricken; the secretary said bored; Jason Rudd said tired. They all knew what they had seen, and they had all seen something different, because none of them, said Miss Maple later, looked at what Marina Gregg was looking *at*. They looked at the mirror, and not at the crack.

Heather Badpelt drank her champagne-cocktail — Marina's own glass, in fact, mixed at Marina's own request, for Mrs. Badpelt had gushed so winningly that the star had waived precedence and insisted they swap — and went home in the best health of anybody in the county, and died that evening of a monstrous dose of sedative, the kind they give elephants.

Inspector Craddock, a patient old baboon, came down from the Yard, and by courtesy called at the cottage first, because that was the custom, and because Miss Maple had trained half the inspectors in England without ever holding a rank.

"Suspicion," he offered, "falls on the secretary, Ella Zealinsky — she admitted she'd quarrelled with the dead woman over some trifling thing. And there's Ardwyck Fenn, the gossip columnist, who knew Mrs. Badpelt in Singapore. And Lola Brewster, the rival ape-tress, who was standing nearest with the glass in her paw."

"Ask yourself instead," said Miss Maple, snapping a deadhead off the sweet peas, "why a famous she-monkey, being told a long rapturous story by a total stranger, should look like that. Not frightened. Not bored. *Possessed.* As if she had looked into a glass," said Miss Maple, "and seen something walking in it that could not possibly be there. I have seen that face once before, Inspector, in Nana Mead, in quite another connection — a monkey looking at somebody who is telling her about a fine healthy grandchild, while her own child, that died young — is behind her eyes all the time. She was not looking *at* Heather Badpelt at all. She was looking *through* her. And what she saw came out of the past and hit her like a branch."

They were the right peas, and the right branch. For Ella Zealinsky, the chic, tired vervet secretary, died within the fortnight — prussic acid, at her desk in the west wing, in a room nobody could prove another monkey had entered.

It was Miss Maple who put the last piece in, and she put it, characteristically, by remembering an injury. Marina Gregg, years before, expecting her first infant — the one from the marriage everybody had forgotten, before Jason, before the last two husbands — had gone on working to the seventh month, because one does, and one day among the convalescent well-wishers on a war tour had come a devoted young macaque with monkey-measles, escaped from her sickbed, radiant, shaking her hand for four rain-soaked hours. Marina's infant was born stricken, and lived his small life in the shadow of the trees, and Marina Gregg had never wholly come out from under those trees again. Every garden she planted since was azaleas; every face that beamed up at her out of a queue, she searched — for the sickness walking in it.

"Ask Jason Rudd," said Miss Maple, "about Calcutta and Singapore. And then arrest nobody in the west wing. Heather Badpelt reminded her aloud of the day she had ruined her life, without ever knowing it — cheerfully, Inspector, *cheerfully*, in the voice monkeys use for the proudest day of their lives. To Mrs. Badpelt it was the memory of a handshake in the rain. To Marina Gregg it was the face of the instrument. She looked through the smiling stranger, saw the measles, saw the cot, saw her whole private graveyard — and she took the sedative from her own supply, put it in her own glass, and made sure it was the glass that eager, trusting, delighted paw closed round. Two mothers' days, one afternoon. And Ella Zealinsky had counted the tablets — secretaries count, it is their whole nature — and mentioned her arithmetic to the wrong monkey."

Marina Gregg never came to trial. She was herself found dead in her bed within the month — an overdose, said the doctors, tolerant of the famous; and it was Miss Maple, walking in the Gibbington shrubbery with Inspector Craddock, who said, without any tolerance at all, "You may write it down as you please, Inspector. I shall write it down myself, privately, in my own way. Jason Rudd loved her past all sense, and knew at last what she had done — and Marina, who could face a camera and a queue, could not face *him* knowing. One of them put the dose in the bedtime cocoa and I think it was the one who wept. He is welcome to whatever peace the law allows him. It will not be much. He will direct nothing again but his own memory."

They stood a while at the gate. The azaleas, all four hundred of them, had taken; the estate's gardeners said the soil had never suited anything better.

"It is a strange thing," said Craddock, "what a face can hold. All those witnesses at the Hall, and every one of them sure, and every one of them wrong."

"They were sure of what they saw *in her face*," said Miss Maple, "but the face was a mirror, Inspector, and none of them asked what it reflected. There is a poem young monkeys used to be made to learn:

*Out flew the web and floated wide;*
*the mirror scratched from side to side;*
*'The curse is come upon me,' cried*
*the Lady of Marmoset.*

Shalott, I believe, in the original. Poor lady. She looked at something lovely through a glass, and it killed her; and Marina Gregg looked at something terrible through a smiling stranger, and it killed — well. Three monkeys, at least. And no amount of polishing, you know," said Miss Maple, taking out her knitting and settling toward the gate, "will ever quite take a scratch out of a mirror, once it has gone from side to side."

The azaleas bloomed, the following spring, like a fire.

The Jungle Ambulance Association realized rather more than usual from the fete. Nobody ever uses the west door now, if they can help it, at Gibbington Hall — and old Jason Rudd, they say, keeps a small glass on his desk that was once hers, and has never once looked into it, and never once put it away.
