# Chimpocchio

*A parody of "Pinocchio"*

Once upon a time, in a workshop at the edge of the jungle, there lived an old gibbon carver named Geppetto, who made wooden spoons, nesting hooks, and — on one remarkable day — a chimp.

He had traded his best coat for a talking balsa log ("Mind the splinters! I bruise easily!"), and from it he carved a puppet with long arms, a painted grin, and a curly tail like a question mark. He named the puppet Chimpocchio.

The moment Geppetto carved the last curl of that tail, Chimpocchio sprang off the workbench, snatched the old gibbon's moss wig, and bounded straight out the window.

"Stop! Come back!" cried Geppetto — but a passing constable gorilla arrested him for the disturbance instead, and the poor old carver spent the night in the stocks, which is how the jungle first learned that parenting can begin in prison.

At midnight, the Blue-Faced Mandrill Fairy floated in on a beam of moonlight.

"Little wooden Chimpocchio," she said, "you may become a real chimp — brave, truthful, and unselfish. Until then, take this conscience." And she appointed a tiny tamarin, no bigger than a fig, to the post.

"Tamarin Tiny, Official Conscience, at your service," squeaked the tamarin, tipping a leaf for a hat. "The pay is nothing and the hours are everything."

In the morning Geppetto came home, forgave everything — that is what fathers' hearts are for; they are the softest wood in the forest — and sold his own breakfast bananas to buy Chimpocchio a school satchel. "Off to Jungle School, my son. Mind your letters — and mind strangers with twitchy whiskers."

But on the way to school, Chimpocchio heard drums and trumpet-birds: THE GREAT BABOONINI'S MARVELOUS PUPPET FOLLIES, ONE DAY ONLY. He sold his school bananas for a ticket before you could say "education."

He was a sensation. The crowd threw figs. Baboonini, the puppet-master, was thrilled to have him — right up until the night the show ran short of firewood, and a wooden puppet, as Baboonini observed, burns beautifully. Chimpocchio wailed so hard and so honestly at the thought of becoming kindling that Baboonini, who had a soft heart under a hard rump, gave him five gold banana coins instead. "Go straight home to your father!"

He did not go straight home. At the first fig stall he met Kitty the Kinkajou and Sneak the Coati, two con artists with very shiny manners.

"Gold banana coins? Plant them at the Grove of Wonders! Bury them tonight, and by sunrise you'll have a whole tree of golden bananas. Wonders take patience, of course. And moonlight. And a friend to hold your coins while you climb."

They led him up a tree, hung him by his suspenders "so the moonlight could ripen the coins," and ran off with all five. When the Blue-Faced Fairy found him — still hanging, moderately humbler — she asked where the coins had gone, and Chimpocchio told the truth, more or less, and every "less" made his tail grow another hand-span.

"A macaw took them!" — sprout. "Two macaws!" — sprout, sprig. "Two macaws and a crocodile!" — leaves, a blossom, a small branch. Tamarin Tiny kept up a squeaky running commentary: "LIE! LIE! ALSO A LIE!" The Fairy called her woodpeckers to trim the tail back to size, and Chimpocchio promised — more or less — to try.

He was doing rather well, for about a week. He even went to school — once. He made it as far as the school fig, where a gang of boys was headed the other way, down to the shore, to see the terrible Sea-Monster the fishermen swore they had seen offshore.

"School will keep," said the biggest boy. "Monsters won't."

Tamarin Tiny, from Chimpocchio's shoulder, cleared his throat with the sound of a very small bell. "This," he squeaked, "is the part where we go to class."

They saw the monster instead. It was a log with a hat on it. Chimpocchio came home wet to the whiskers, late for school, and full of the first lesson he ever taught himself: the trouble with missing class for a monster is that the monster is never worth it, and the class never comes back.

Then he met Banana-wick, a baboon with bad intentions and worse friends.

"School?" said Banana-wick. "Why, you poor thing. Come to Pleasure Isle. No books. No baths. Nothing but swings and sugar-cane soda, all day, every day, forever."

A coach drawn by six midnight lemurs carried them across the water. Nobody checked tickets, because Pleasure Isle had no rules, which was, the lemurs agreed, the whole trick of it.

It was every bit as wonderful as it sounds, for about four months. Then one gray morning Chimpocchio woke up with long grey donkey ears and a tufted tail, and Banana-wick was gone, and the ticket office was a crate office. Donkey-eared little chimps, he discovered, were being boxed up and shipped off to circuses, to pull little carts and wear little hats forever.

Chimpocchio dove off the dock into the sea and swam until the sea went dark — GULP — swallowed whole by Monstro, the monstrous catfish of the deep.

What Chimpocchio did not know, having been a donkey at the time, was that Geppetto had gone to sea weeks before — sailing a washtub with a candle for a mast — asking every fish for news of his son, and every fish pointing deeper, until the deepest fish of all was Monstro, and the asking stopped.

And there, on a little raft of fish-crates, sitting by a candle made of a sardine, sat a shaggy, salt-crusted old gibbon who had sailed every river in the world, searching for his son.

"Chimpocchio? Is it really you?" cried Geppetto, and the two hugged so hard that Monstro hiccupped.

They built a raft inside the beast, of crates and candle-ends and hope. "When he yawns," said Geppetto, "we ride the wind out."

"And if he doesn't yawn?" asked Chimpocchio.

"Then we make him," said Geppetto, "an offer."

When Monstro's great jaws parted, Chimpocchio tickled the roof of the enormous throat with the last of his tail fur. The sneeze that followed blew raft, gibbon, and puppet out of Monstro's mouth and halfway to the horizon — with the monster in grumpy, geysering pursuit, sneezing seawater and complaints. "Row, Papa!" shouted Chimpocchio, and rowed so hard and so bravely that Geppetto reached the shore safe and sound, though the little puppet's wooden body was worn clean to splinters.

"Mind the splinters," the log had said, long ago. It had not lied.

At dawn the Blue-Faced Mandrill Fairy stood over the small, wrecked frame, and found what she had come looking for. "Brave," she said. "Truthful — eventually. Unselfish — at last, when it cost you everything." And she touched the splinters with one blue paw.

Where the puppet had been, there lay a real, live chimp — breathing, warm, and grinning his very own grin, which looked exactly like the painted one, except that now he had thought of it himself.

The Fairy's parting words were brief. "Brave, truthful, unselfish — and now, one more thing: school." And Chimpocchio went — walked there himself, satchel and all, and stopped to look at only ONE poster for Baboonini's Follies on the way, which Tamarin Tiny agreed, grudgingly, counted as progress.

Geppetto danced till the branches shook. Tamarin Tiny received a golden coat and the title Official Conscience, First Class — and never once let Chimpocchio forget about the tail, not at his wedding, not at Geppetto's birthday, not ever, which is what consciences are for.
