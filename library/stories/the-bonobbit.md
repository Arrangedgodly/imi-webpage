# The Bonobbit

*A parody of "The Hobbit"*

In a snug, round nest in the crown of an old fig tree there lived a bonobbit. Not a messy, drafty nest of wet leaves and beetle-husks; it was a bonobbit-nest, and that means comfort. It belonged to Bongo Bonobbit of the Brier, who never did anything unexpected, thank you very much, breakfast being the most exciting event of any day.

Until the morning an old silverback with a crooked walking-stick stopped beneath his tree: "Good morning!"

"Do you wish me a good morning," said Bongo, "or do you mean that it is a good morning whether I want it or not?"

"All of them at once," said Gandalf the Grayback, "and also that I am looking for someone to share in an adventure."

"Terribly sorry — no adventures here. Nasty, disturbing, uncomfortable things. Make you late for dinner." And Bongo dove into his nest and pulled the leaf-blanket over his head.

The next evening, thirteen gruff stump-tailed macaques let themselves into his kitchen bough, ate his pantry, sang rowdy working-songs — far over the mangy mountains old, to dungeons deep and fig-groves old — and their leader, Thorin Oakenbough, grandson of the King under the Fig-Mountain, laid out the business: generations of gold in an ancestral hall, all stolen by Smaug — a fire-breathing monitor lizard with a jewelled hide and opinions.

"A dragon," said Bongo. "In the jungle."

"Where else would he be comfortable?" said Gandalf. "We need a burglar: someone small, quiet, and light on the vines."

"I have never burgled in my life," said Bongo — and by breakfast he was running down the high road with no pocket-handkerchief, a lesson to us all, as he told everyone for a week.

There followed adventures, of an instructive variety.

Three slow lorises caught the company and argued the recipe — "shish-ke-baboon? No, braised, in banana leaf" — while Gandalf threw his voice into the dark: "It was you took the last fig, Loris." "It was NOT." It very much was, and they were still litigating it at dawn, whereupon sunrise caught them mid-snarl and turned all three to stone, for everyone knows a loris caught arguing at daybreak is just firewood with opinions. The company helped itself to their purse of dried beetles and moved on.

In the Last Homely Grove, Lord Elrondado unrolled Thorin's map in moonlight and found moon-letters, legible only under the moon that wrote them: "Stand by the grey stone when the fruit-thrush knocks, and the last light of the setting sun will shine upon the keyhole."

"A door that opens one evening a year," said Thorin. "We have a thrush, a season, and a burglar."

"Luck," said Gandalf, "is a tool of the trade. So is breakfast. Eat."

They took the high pass, where stone giants pitched boulders at each other in a thunderstorm — half a game, half a feud — and sheltered in a cave with a trapdoor. This is how adventures work: a door says EXIT until you are inside, and then it says GOTCHA. Gob-rillas dragged the whole company, minus Gandalf and Bongo, under the mountain roots, down to a hall where the Goblin-King judged newcomers by how edible they looked.

Then the lights went out, there was a flash, a howl, and one dead Goblin-King — the Grayback worked fast in the dark — and up the rafters went Bongo, smallness being, for once, a professional qualification. Lost in the black tunnels he pocketed a small ring of woven golden vine, pockets being a bonobbit's answer to uncertainty.

At the bottom of the dark lay a cold lake, and on an island in it lived Golly, a pale, hairless tarsier who spoke to himself in two voices and treasured what he called "my precioussss." He proposed riddles, with eating as the prize structure.

"Thirty white apes on a red hill: first they champ, then they stamp, then they stand still."

"Teeth," said Golly at last, trying to make it sound easy.

Then Bongo asked the one that mattered: "What have I got in my cheek-pouch?" — for he had just slipped the ring off the floor of the boat and into it, and Golly's cave-trained ears worked that out a beat too late.

"What has it got in its nassty little cheek-pouchesss?" said Golly to Golly, and went for the answer with his teeth, and found the answer gone. Bongo, ring on, followed the hissing to the exit, leapt clean over Golly in the dark, and did not strike him — partly pity, mostly a hasty pair of legs.

Reunion, and then hornbills, great grey ones — a favor done to a wizard early in a story gets cashed in at altitude. They bore the company to the Carroc, where they offended Beorn, a local who was a bear most days and a gorilla the rest, never in a convenient order; he fed them anyway, on the principle that a guest you have fed is a guest you can interrogate.

Then Mirk-mood: the forest black as the inside of a fig, where the enchantment makes you sleep in the wrong trees and dream the wrong dreams. They left the path — never leave the path — and the spiders came: great grey rope-spinners, hanging the company in silk hammocks and discussing them as a menu.

Bongo, invisible, cut cords and threw stones and insulted the spiders in verse — "Old fattattercop! Attercop! You eat too much and your silk is derivative!" — which drew them all away in a fury, earning eternal grudge from the spiders and provisional respect from Thorin.

Then the wood-elves, forest-courtly and allergic to explanations, imprisoned the lot for trespassing in a meal-threatening manner. Only Bongo stayed free, invisible, patrolling the Elvenking's halls until he found the way out was also the wine cellar: empty barrels, floated down the Forest River to Lagoon-town. He packed thirteen macaques into thirteen barrels — the phrase "a barrel of monkeys" meaning, ever after, "a fun mess" — and rode the fourteenth. Exactly as fun from the outside, Bongo admitted later; from the inside, mostly bruisings.

Lagoon-town, a village of fisher-apes on stilts, opened the barrels and chose legend over jailer: they threw a festival — as leadership goes, not the worst. And Bongo, to his own lasting astonishment, did his job: he waited by the grey stone, the thrush knocked, the last light found the keyhole, and he walked down the secret tunnel alone, stole a golden cup, and woke the owner.

"Thief," said the dark, in a voice like a landslide with a lisp. "I know the smell of macaque. Tell me your name — I do so enjoy a named meal."

"I am he that hides in the canopy and walks unseen," said Bongo, knees knocking. "Burrower of tunnels! Winner of riddles!"

"Flattery," said Smaug, unbending one golden eye. "Take my advice: don't have macaques for friends. They arrive with relatives. Also, your barrel is showing."

And Bongo — a burglar now in fact as well as contract — looked long at the great jewelled breast and saw the bare patch where one scale had knocked loose. Then he ran, and Smaug came out that night in a fury, burned the mountainside, and turned for Lagoon-town.

But the fruit-thrush, who had overheard Bongo marvel at the bare patch, did what thrushes do: carried the news to Bard the boatman-ape, who stood on a rooftop with the last black dart as the sky burned. "Black dart," said Bard, "go now to the hollow under the left arm." It did not miss. Smaug fell into the lagoon and boiled it, which the fisher-apes considered very rude, and Bard considered a job.

Then came the quarrel no epic skips. Thorin sealed the mountain. The lagoon-folk and the woodland elves came for shares of the hoard — a dead dragon's debts being everyone's favorite inheritance — and Bongo, watching gold do to Thorin exactly what gold does, went down by night and gave the Arkenstone, the Heart of the Mountain, to Bard and the Elvenking: the one treasure that could buy peace, and the one thing Thorin could not forgive losing.

"You took my ARKENSTONE!" Thorin thundered, when the parley revealed it in Bard's paw.

"I gave away my share," said Bongo, "and my good opinion, which you are spending badly."

Armies were drawing up when the Gob-rillas arrived in their thousands, and everyone remembered whose war this actually was. The Battle of Five Armies: hornbills to the rescue, Beorn in a truly terrible mood, and Bongo unconscious under a rock by ten o'clock, having, as Gandalf observed, the best of it.

Thorin Oakenbough fell in the front line, and dying, took Bongo's paw: "There is more good in you than in a whole mountain of fruit, burglar. If more of us valued grooming and good cheer above hoarded gold, it would be a merrier jungle — and a poorer one, so let us not go too far with it."

Bongo went home with two chests of silver and a small sword named Thorn, to find his nest being auctioned ("Lot 47: one doily, presumably load-bearing"); and Gandalf came by the following spring, as if passing.

"You are a very fine little ape, Mr. Bonobbit," he said, "and I am quite fond of you. But you are only quite a little fellow in a very tall forest after all."

"Thank goodness," said Bongo, and put the kettle on. There, and back again — and he never did find his pocket-handkerchiefs.
