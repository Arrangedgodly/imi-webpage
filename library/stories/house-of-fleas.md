# House of Fleas

*A parody of "House of Leaves"*

I still get the itch when the wind comes off the water.

I am Johnny Pruant, and this is how it found me: a dead orangutan and a trunk of scratch-marks. The orangutan was Zampango, famous once for essays nobody read; they found him cold in his nest, with claw-marks "of no flea known to science." His executor gave me his papers: thousands of lines scratched on napkins and banana crates, all analyzing a film called *The Primate Record*, by a documentary monkey named Will Banavidson.

I copied the manuscript out by hand. I do not recommend this. Every time the word "flea" appeared, Zampango had underlined it three times, and my shoulder itched every single time. On the fourth day of copying, I scratched my shoulder in my sleep and was woken by my own claws. I took the manuscript to a park bench to finish it, and a pigeon watched me the whole afternoon without blinking, which I did not appreciate.

Here is what the Record showed.

The Banavidson family — Will, his mate Karen, and the twins — moved into a fine old fig on Banana Tree Lane. It was, the listing said, charming and deceptively spacious. The listing agent later declined to say which of those two words she was most sorry for.

Then, one quiet Tuesday, a door appeared in the trunk that had not been there Monday. It opened on a hallway.

The hallway was cold. The hallway was dark. The hallway took five and a half minutes to walk down, which was wrong, because the outside of the fig measured forty feet around, and there is only so much hallway you can keep in forty feet of tree. Will measured it twice. Then he hired surveyor monkeys — paid, thanked, and never hired again — who measured the ground floor at forty feet and a quarter inch, and reported the quarter inch as "within tolerance," though none of them would say of what. Will stopped measuring and started filming.

Karen would not go in. Karen was a monkey who could not climb. Heights undid her; she took the ground path everywhere. "I am a perfectly good monkey," she said, "who happens to be a ground monkey. My family were burrow chimps for nine generations, and we are proud of it." And now her own home had grown an inside with no outside, an inside that went down and down, and the deeper it went the less it was a hallway and the more it was an itch.

Because that was the thing about the fig. Everyone who went in started to itch.

Will brought help: a famous explorer named Hollybough, with two guides. On the first day the hallway had grown a staircase, going down; on the third, a further staircase, the flashlights dimmed, and the itch began — first the shoulder, then everywhere, the kind of itch that lives under the fur and, you begin to suspect, under the skin, and possibly under the world. On the fifth day, Will's tape records Hollybough whispering "there is no flea, there is no flea, there is no flea," in the voice of a monkey who has stopped believing in nouns.

Some of them came back. Hollybough did not. They found his comb, forty feet down, beside a patch of nothing the tape records as a big slow animal turning over in its sleep. Zampango's footnote here is one word, underlined three times: *[flea]*.

There is another reel, Exploration Z: Will went in alone with a lantern, on camera, counting branches aloud. The tape runs ninety minutes. The scratch-marks in Zampango's manuscript for those pages number one thousand and eleven, and three of them are exclamation points.

The itch got worse. The twins drew pictures of the hallway at nursery school, all four walls of them, and the teacher called. Will slept in the yard. Karen stood at the little door with her paw on the frame, not going in, and said, for the cameras, in the flattest voice I have ever read: "I would like our house back, please."

Then came the last descent, and the last reel. Will went in with a lantern, alone, while Karen waited at the little door with the twins; and he did not come back by dark, and the hallway had never once given anybody back late. And so Karen Banavidson, burrow chimp of nine proud generations, put on the climbing harness she had never once used, tied the rope to the strongest root, kissed the twins on their heads, and said to the camera: "I am a ground monkey, and I am going up."

She climbed down into it. The tape hisses with scratching on all sides, vast and unhurried, like a house being combed; and under the scratching you can hear her, counting branches aloud the way Will used to, in his words, in his cadence, as if repetition were a rope of its own. Ninety-one minutes down, she found him at the edge of the dark — dazed, mid-itch, comb still in hand — and above the final doorway, scratched by nothing at all, were the words:

THIS TREE IS NOT FUR YOU.

And the tree, at last, began to answer the measurement. The hallway narrowed. The walls came together like a fig deciding its own size, and the last minutes are just the two of them climbing — hand over hand, branch over branch, her voice steady, his remembering how — the dark coming up like water, the scratching all around them combing shut — and then daylight, and the little door, and the twins, screaming, which in that family meant joy.

They nailed the door shut that evening. The Banavidsons moved out that week, back to a ground-floor burrow, which Karen found a great relief and Will found, eventually, funny. The fig still stands. Real estate listings describe it as "charming, deceptively spacious" — the italics are the listing agent's; someday I mean to visit that agent, comb in hand.

I finished typing it on a Tuesday. On Wednesday I noticed the first itch.

I changed my fur-wash. I moved burrows. I crossed a river, then a mountain, then a whole season. The itch came with me, courteous, patient, like a passenger with a ticket to wherever I'm going. And I understand at last that the fig was never about space. Every monkey carries a fig inside him — a hallway with no end, a door where no door was on Monday — and the deeper you go into yourself, the bigger it gets, and the more it itches.

Zampango's last note, on the last napkin in the trunk, isn't analysis at all. It's a little verse, and I'll end with it, because he clearly meant somebody to:

Little solace comes to those who grieve,
when thoughts keep drifting
and the fleas keep shifting
and the hall goes down beyond belief —

so hang your hammock, cousin, high,
where the moon can find your branch;
whatever's scratching down below,
don't answer. Never scratch.

I wrote all that out fair and square, and my shoulder is itching, and since you have followed me this far into the trunk, into the tree, there is only one thing left to tell you — the same thing the hallway told Will, the same thing it told Karen at the top of the dark, the same thing the itch is telling me now, softly, under the fur, in the night:

This is not fur you.
