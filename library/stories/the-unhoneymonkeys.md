# The Unhoneymonkeys

*A parody of "The Unhoneymooners"*

Olive Torres was born unlucky, and she had the résumé to prove it: bitten by a monkey at the zoo (unprovoked, she always insisted, though the monkey's account differed), struck by lightning through a car window, and fired from a smoothie stand for a fruit-related incident that was legally not her fault. Her twin sister, Ami, was born lucky. Ami won raffles, parking spots, and door prizes the way other primates shed fur: constantly, and without effort.

So it surprised nobody in the Torres troop that when Ami married Dane at the Marigold Banquet Hall, every single thing — the dress, the flowers, the cake, the ten-day honeymoon to Maui — had been won in a contest. And it surprised nobody either that when two hundred guests ate the buffet, the buffet fought back.

The crab salad did it. The crab salad, sitting out in the June sun in a vat the size of a rain barrel, plotting. By nine o'clock the reception had become a documentary about a troop going collectively bananas: two hundred monkeys, in formalwear, in a line for the men's room, while the DJ — the only vendor Ami had actually paid — played "Shout."

Two guests remained upright: Olive, who had a shellfish allergy and had given the buffet a wide, historical berth, and Ethan, Dane's older brother and the best man, a tall, fastidious macaque with the warmth of a laminated card, who had refused the buffet on principle. ("Mayonnaise plus sunshine equals a bioweapon," he'd said, at the reception, to no one, taking notes.) He did infection modeling for a living. He had been described by three separate aunts as "a very serious young man."

Olive and Ethan had history, and none of it good. They'd met at a Troop Thanksgiving, argued about whether monkeys should pay taxes, and disliked each other instantly and completely, with the loyalty of true hate.

Now they stood over a battlefield of groaning relatives, the last two primates standing.

"Great," said Olive. "The two of us. The girl who can't eat shellfish and the guy who won't."

"Statistically," said Ethan, "one of us should be vomiting. I'd have bet on you."

"I'm allergic, not cursed."

"Cursed is exactly the word I'd use," said Ethan, and was nearly proven right by an airborne aunt.

---

The next morning, everyone recovered, and Ami, pale but still lucky, did the math out loud. The honeymoon was non-transferable, booked in the winner's name, departing in six days. Ten days in Maui, oceanfront, all-inclusive — and she and Dane couldn't go, because Ami had won the trip before the wedding and now couldn't leave the pharmacy counter where she worked, having used every favor she owed to cover the disaster.

"You take it," Ami told Olive.

"Alone? On your honeymoon?"

"The booking's under my name. You're my twin. You go as 'Ami Torres.'" She paused, guiltily. "There's a catch. It's a couples package. That's how I won it. You'd need... a husband."

And that is how Olive Torres came to be standing in the Kahului airport holding the hand of Ethan, the most annoying macaque in the continental tropics, both of them wearing leis and matching expressions of a hostage situation.

"Rules," said Ethan. "You take the bed. I take whatever a hotel considers a couch. We tell no one we're not married, because if the resort figures out the prize was fraud, they bill my brother full rate, and Dane cried at the deposit."

"Fine. And we don't touch, talk, or otherwise interact beyond the minimum required to sustain the charade."

"An agreement I can respect," said Ethan. "Also, don't eat the resort crab."

"I'm *allergic*, you insufferable — "

"Statistically, I worry."

---

For three days it worked. They were two civilized primates, cohabiting in paradise with the emotional texture of a timeshare presentation.

Then, on day four, at the breakfast buffet, a voice said, "Olive Torres? From the Bannah Genome Project?"

Olive turned slowly, a pineapple spear halfway to her mouth, and found Dr. Melvin Silverback — her boss. Her enormous, silver-backed, decision-making boss, from the lab where Olive answered phones and dreamed of the coordinator job that was being posted in September. He was in a bucket hat. His wife was beside him. There was no escape that evolution had provided.

"Olive!" said Dr. Silverback joyfully. "And this must be the husband! Ami mentioned — no wait, she said you weren't — well! Married! Mazel! To the happy couple!"

The resort manager, arriving at that exact moment to greet "Mr. and Mrs. Torres, our contest winners," sealed it. Olive had approximately one second to choose between her job and the fraud, and, in the grand Torres tradition of unlucky choices, chose both.

"That's me," she said. "Mrs. Torres. Married. To this husband, Ethan. My husband. Legally."

"How long?" asked Mrs. Silverback, misty.

"Years," said Olive.

"Two," said Ethan, at the same moment.

"Two years of years," said Olive.

The Silverbacks loved them. That was the horror of it. Over the next week, the Silverbacks loved them at breakfast, loved them at the luau, loved them on the sunset cruise, and requested their company with the enthusiasm of baboons adopting. And Olive discovered, in the middle of performing a marriage with her worst enemy, the following inconvenient facts: that Ethan was funny in a deadpan, academic way, like a documentary with jokes; that he'd spent his third year of grad school in a lemur colony and apologized to every lemur since; that he'd taken the couch without complaint and left the good towels on her side; and that when he laughed — really laughed, once, when she fell off a paddleboard directly onto a floating birthday cake — his whole serious face un-laminated, and something in her chest went bananas.

Ethan, meanwhile, was observed by the reader (though not by himself; he was an empirical man and this was not empirical) to have started leaving the crab-free dishes beside her plate "in case of statistically likely mishap," and to have recalculated his whole model of the world around a clumsy capuchin who was, by every metric he trusted, an outlier.

On the last night, at the luau, Mrs. Silverback said, "You know how I knew you two were real? The way he watches you when you talk."

That night, on the lanai, the minimum viable marriage stopped being minimum.

"So," said Olive, watching the water. "This was the best worst vacation of my life."

"Olive," said Ethan. "About the charade — there's something I've been pretending — "

And at that exact moment Mrs. Silverback came around the corner with two mai tais and a folder, saying, "Melvin has the job posting right here, he wants you to apply the moment you're home, he says a married woman is so *stable* — what's wrong, dear?"

What was wrong was that Olive was holding a folder labeled COORDINATOR SEARCH — JANUARY and had just heard the man she'd gone ape for begin a sentence with "about the charade."

"I need to not hear the end of that sentence," she said, and went inside, and lay awake recalculating.

---

The flight home was two silent seats and a war. At O'Hare they split a cab only as far as the sign.

"You could have finished the sentence," said Ethan.

"You were going to say you'd been *pretending*," said Olive. "I heard the first half. I'm unlucky, not deaf."

"The end of the sentence," said Ethan, with enormous effort, "was going to be *that I've been pretending not to be in love with you since the buffet*."

There was a pause the length of a lightning strike through a car window.

"Oh," said Olive. "That's — a different second half."

But she was already mad, because mad was easier than terrified, and she was Olive, and the universe had taught her that good things were a trap. "You know what the worst part is? I have to tell Silverback the truth. The job will evaporate. I'm going to be the girl who faked a marriage in a fraud honeymoon, because I am *cursed*."

"You're not cursed," said Ethan. "You're the only primate at that wedding who avoided a bioweapon. Bad luck dodges you by hitting everything near you. From a data standpoint, Olive, you might be the luckiest monkey I know. She just has lousy aim."

He got out of the cab. Then he leaned back in, deadpan as ever.

"One more statistic. The resort manager comped us a future stay. Contest fraud aside, we're still booked. Next June. Honeymoon suite. It would be a shame to waste it twice."

---

The truth, when Olive told it, went the way truth goes with bosses who like you: Dr. Silverback laughed for a full minute, said his own honeymoon had been worse and real, and posted the job anyway. ("Married, single, fraud — the genome doesn't care, Torres.") Ami, on hearing the whole story, threw banana bread at her sister and then hugged her for an hour.

Olive took the job, the desk by the window, the aim of a lucky monkey. And in June, at the Kahului airport, she stood holding the hand of her husband — actual, documented, laminated, hers — wearing a lei, and dared the universe to do its worst.

"Ready?" said Ethan.

"Statistically," said Olive, "something terrible is going to happen."

"Yes," said her prime mate, and un-laminated his whole face. "You married me. Nothing worse is possible."
