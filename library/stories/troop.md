# Troop

*A parody of "Scoop"*

While still a young colobus, William Boot had been brought to the capital by his sister and established as the nature columnist of the Daily Branch, and there he had remained for thirty years, producing, every week, six hundred words of the countryside under the heading LUSH CANPIES.

"Feather-fingered through the ferny fen passes the questing loris," William wrote, in the strip-light over his rented desk, a hundred miles from any fen. "The home-fur whispers. The vole and the vixen keep their ancient tryst. Somewhere, in the wet green dark, the badger sharpens his argument..." He wrote of his "cottage" — in fact a modest flat above a tobacconist — of his "geraniums," of "nut-time on the village green." He was, in his small weekly way, a national monument, like the Zoo or the rain.

It was, therefore, with no surprise that the Branch's foreign editor, Mr. Salter, received the order: *GET BOOT.*

The order came, in the way things came at the Branch, from Lord Coppersimian, the proprietor, a press-lord so rich that disagreement had been abolished around him as a courtesy. When a subordinate said "Definitely, Lord Coppersimian," that meant yes; when he said "Up to a point, Lord Coppersimian," that meant no; and nothing else was ever required to be meant.

The trouble was that Lord Coppersimian had meant one Boot and his instructions concerned another. At a dinner party given by Mrs. Stitch — a tamarin socialite the width of a saucer and the influence of a monsoon — the fashionable young novelist John Boot had sighed that what he really needed was a change of air. *Somewhere foreign.*

"Darling Lord Coppersimian," Mrs. Stitch had pronounced, patting his great silver knee, "you must send Boot abroad. I insist."

"Definitely," said Lord Coppersimian, and the machinery began to turn. Mr. Salter was sent to fetch Boot, and Mr. Salter — who had been foreign editor for nine years without once leaving the building — went to the address on file and found, instead of the sharp young novelist, a large, shy, elderly colobus in a moth-eaten cardigan, eating a boiled nut.

"Young Mr. Boot? You wrote that lovely book about the young monkeys in the trees?"

"Good gracious, no," said William. "I'm William. I do Lush Canpies. The countryside. I haven't been out of the city since the General Strike of the zookeepers."

Mr. Salter's train back to the office took forty minutes, in which he considered his choices, his mortgage, and Lord Coppersimian; and by the time he arrived, William Boot, nature columnist, had been appointed Special War Correspondent of the Daily Branch, salary ten bananas a week, and could not be unappointed by anything quieter than an earthquake.

"But I don't know anything about war," William protested, at the airport, being loaded like cargo. "I know about voles."

"War," said Mr. Salter, with the weariness of nine imaginary campaigns, "is about waiting for the flight. You'll do splendidly."

William was bound for Ishmaelia — a hot, damp republic of slopes and slogans on the far side of the world, where the howler factions had been on the edge of a revolution for a fortnight, and the world's press had gathered in the capital, Howlerville, to wait for it the way voles wait for rain. The rival papers were there: Pigge of the Beast, Shumble of the Wire, the great Wenlock Jakes himself — Jakes, of whom it was said that he had once wired home, from a quiet capital, a complete fictional account of a revolution, with names, factions, and casualty figures, and that the revolution, reading his cable in the international papers, had obligingly broken out along his lines.

"Fact," said Corker, a small, bald, cheerful chimp from the Wire, who adopted William at the hotel bar. "Jakes is news the way weather is weather. Don't fight it, old monkey. Now look — here's the thing about Ishmaelia. Nobody knows a thing. The Government's called the Patriots, the opposition's called the Reds, they're all named Jackson, they all hate each other in the family, and the only news that's ever filed is invented. Your paper expects it. Every couple of days you cable something with REVOLUTION in it, they're happy, you're happy, no one's bananas are wasted."

"But what if there isn't one?"

"Then it will have to happen without us, won't it? Have another nut. It's the only thing that's free."

Corker taught William the trade as they waited: how to cable in the Branch's private grammar (UPSTRICKEN MINISTERIAL PREMIER IRON BANANA), how to spot an approaching coup (the dignitaries stop paying hotel bills), and how to fend off Kätchen.

Kätchen was the pretty young wife of an Icelandic scholar nobody had ever met — a bright-eyed chimp girl who took William's arm, called him "Mr. Bootle" in a way that made his cardigan prickle, and borrowed, over the course of one golden week, his fountain pen, his torch, his spare cash card, and — on the night the embassy Black Banana Ball reached its height — his cable-filing privileges, so that for two days the Daily Branch printed, under Boot's name, dispatches of genius that William never wrote, describing troop movements that did not exist, and described them so well that the other correspondents, reading them, packed and ran for the frontier to cover the fighting.

"Kätchen," said William, "you've had my pen, my torch, and my forms."

"But you are so kind," said Kätchen, "and I am so frightened of the Reds. Or the Patriots. One of them."

It was on the morning the frontier correspondents had fled that the actual revolution arrived, unreported, through William's own doorstep. A Jackson — the wrong Jackson, or perhaps the right one; even the Jacksons, voting, could not afterwards agree — seized the radio hut, the banana reserve, and the Ministry of Feathers, and since William was the only correspondent left in Howlerville, he strolled out, bought a nut from a street cart, and watched it happen from beginning to end.

His cable ran: REVOLUTION HOWLERVILLE MODERATE SUCCESS GOVERNMENT CHANGED SAME FAMILY ALL QUIET NUTS REASONABLE.

At the Daily Branch this was received like a thunderclap of genius. "Boot!" cried Lord Coppersimian, slapping the tape. "The only man in the world who didn't miss it! KNEW he was the monkey. Salter! Give Boot a rise. Twenty bananas a week."

"Definitely, Lord Coppersimian," said Salter.

"Was he in the Oxfordshire Boot family?"

"Up to a point, Lord Coppersimian."

Mrs. Stitch, discovering that her John Boot had not, after all, been sent anywhere, arranged for him to go out and replace William — "The darlings swap," she said, "and no one need ever speak of it" — and William came home, decorated in his own mind with permanent shellshock, and found his desk at Lush Canpies exactly as he had left it, under the strip-light, with a fortnight of unread tributes from schoolchildren and a telegram from his sister.

The only lasting damage was editorial. Shaken, the Branch appointed a committee to guarantee that such a mistake could never happen again, and the committee's first act was to issue William a formal contract: he was to be, in perpetuity, the Branch's roving ambassador of nature, un-fireable, un-transferable, and contractually spared from all foreign news forever.

"That's settled," said Mr. Salter, who privately knew exactly which Boot had gone where, and had a mortgage.

So William Boot sat down again, took up his fountain pen (a replacement; Kätchen had kept the other), and wrote, as autumn came to the capital a hundred miles from any fen:

"Feather-fingered through the ferny fen passes the questing loris. The nut-brown autumn treads the lanes. Home is the hunter, home from the hill, and the war correspondent — home from the war he never quite met — is the colobus, gratefully, at his geraniums..."

Outside, somewhere, the world went on going briefly and violently bananas, in the competent hands of other, sharper monkeys. William's kettle began to sing.

"Up to a point," said William Boot, to nobody, comfortably, and put another nut on to boil.
