# The Dharma Baboons

*A parody of "The Dharma Bums"*

Hopping a freight north out of the fig groves that October, I thought about the old Chinese poet Han Siamang, who quit the jungle entirely and lived in a hollow tree, laughing at monkeys who wrote poems about climbing instead of climbing. Han Siamang, I decided, was the king of the dharma baboons, and I was merely a busboy in his kitchen.

I was a snub-nosed layabout named Ray with a rucksack, a sleeping sack, and a religion picked up thirdhand: Buddhism as taught by anybody who'd ever given me a dried fig. Down in the boxcar an old howler bum was already asleep under a newspaper. He'd asked me for a bite of my lunch and I'd given him the whole banana and felt incredibly holy about it for maybe forty miles.

It was in Berkeley, in a tumbledown cottage that smelled of wine and woodsmoke, that I met Japhy Rhesus.

He came through the door at midnight with a pack bigger than he was, boots muddy, eyes like two lit windows. Japhy Rhesus was a poet, a translator of Han Siamang, a student of the old texts, and a logger who could fall a tree and then sit under its stump and meditate on the pity of it. He'd been a forest lookout, a sailor, a berry picker. "Berries," he told me once, "are the only economy where nobody's cheated but the bear."

That night at the cottage there was a party of the whole Berkeley jungle — Alvah Goldbanana, whose cottage it was and whose religion was a kind of joyous sloppiness; little bongo players; red-cheeked poetesses; a bristle of beards. Japhy drank tea and stayed stone sober while the rest of us went beautifully out of our trees.

"Ray," he said, "you call yourself a Buddhist but you're melancholy. That's like calling yourself a swimmer and sinking."

"What do I do about it?"

"Practice. Charity, tenderness, sympathy, and that's it — that's the whole curriculum. The rest is commentary." He leaned close, grinning. "Also, first thought, best thought. Say the thing. Swing for the far branch. The branch knows you're coming."

Somebody asked him about his plans for the mountains and Japhy laid it all out: a climb in the spring up the Matternest, the tallest rock in the Sierra Nana-da, and afterward a camp by a lake with a girl everybody called Princess, on the strict dharma understanding that everything is empty and pleasant and everybody ought to share the blankets. "It's not monkey business," he insisted. "It's just kindness, arranged outdoors."

In June we went up the Matternest, Japhy and me and a stout philosophical mangabey named Morley who complained the whole way in Latin. We climbed out of the pines into the light. Japhy bounded up the talus in canvas sneakers, whistling Han Siamang's songs. I suffered magnificently. At ten thousand feet my legs turned to rope and my lungs went on strike, and I sat on a rock considering the doctrine of impermanence, chiefly as it applied to me.

"Take your time," Japhy called back, not even breathing hard. "The mountain's been here a million years. You're the new arrival. Be polite."

At the top of the peak we stood in a wind so clean it felt newly invented. Below us the valleys had gone hazy gold, and range after range ran north like waves of a slow sea that had decided to become stone. Japhy passed around dried figs and salt plums. "This is what the old Ch'an baboons meant," he said. "This is the body of the truth. No words up here need apply."

Coming down I froze on a ledge, hugging the rock, suddenly certain I would blow off the world entirely. Japhy stood below me on a sloping shelf with his arms out, perfectly relaxed, and hollered the strangest, sanest thing anybody ever said to me:

"You can't fall off a mountain!"

"What?"

"You CAN'T FALL off a MOUNTAIN! Worst you can do is fall off a CLIFF, and there aren't that many of them! Now quit clinging and come down!"

And I looked, and it was true — the slope ran down forever, broad as a barn roof. There was nowhere to fall to. I laughed out loud at the million anxieties I'd carried up with me, left them sitting on that ledge, and came down the scree in great leaping strides, half skiing, half dancing, roaring like a lunatic baboon with the joy of it, while Morley picked his way down behind us translating his own complaints into Greek for variety.

That fall I lived in Berkeley and worked when I had to and mostly didn't. Japhy and I drank tea in his shack while he translated poems about cold mountains, and we talked all night about emptiness, which, as Japhy observed, is the only subject you can't run out of. "Emptiness is form," he'd say, pouring. "Therefore be kind to form. Especially to whoever's pouring."

He taught me to make tea properly — warm the pot, one spoon for each baboon, one for the pot — and he called it a tea ceremony without any ceremony, and it was the most religious thing I did all year.

We scattered for the winter. I went down the coast and picked beans with the migrants and thought dharma thoughts in the rows. On my way back I stopped at my mother's tenth-floor nest, where a well-scrubbed baboon named Goodbranch was trying to save her soul through real estate pamphlets. He asked me what I did. "I'm a rucksack wanderer," I said, and ate everything in her icebox as an act of non-attachment. Goodbranch said he'd pray for me. "Pray fast," I said, "I'm hungry."

But everything that year bent toward one thing: Japhy had gotten a small inheritance and was leaving for Japan, to sit zazen in a monastery and study the old poems at the source, and before he went he told me my marching orders.

"Ray, you think too much about being sad. Go be a fire lookout. I did it — sixty days alone on a peak, and it'll settle your hash. You'll see the clouds come in like herds and go out like regrets, and you'll come down knowing something."

"Knowing what?"

"You'll know it when you know it. That's the fun part."

We had a last supper of tea and rice at Morley's, and Japhy gave me his old canvas knapsack, patched with his own hands, and I gave him a poem I'd written, and neither gift was worth anything and both were worth everything. In April I watched his ship clear the Golden Gate with him somewhere on the deck, a small straight figure with a pack, bound for eighty-one days of ocean and then the mountains of Japan, and I stood on the pier grinning and grieving in exactly equal parts, which I believe is the correct mixture.

That summer I took the train north and climbed to my lookout on Desolation Peak, alone above the clouds, exactly as promised. My job was to watch for fire; my actual job, I understood within a week, was to watch. Period.

I boiled tea on a little stove and drank it looking at the mountain they call Hozobean, a double-topped rock of black and terrible beauty, and I said out loud, as Japhy had taught me, "Hozobean, Hozobean, you old shaved head of the universe, I'll always love you."

Sixty days alone. No troop, no talk, no telephone. Just me and the wind and the mice in the woodshed and the enormous silence, morning and evening, the silence of ten thousand empty branches, and the strange discovery, which I had been told a hundred times and never believed until then, that the emptiness is not lonely. The emptiness is full. It's the chattering that's lonely.

One night late in the season, when the first cold came down, I made tea in the old way — warm the pot, one spoon for the lookout, one for the pot — and I thought of Japhy across the ocean sitting facing a wall, and Han Siamang in his hollow tree a thousand years gone, and every bum in every boxcar, and I understood at last what the tea was for.

You can't fall off a mountain, I thought. The whole world is the mountain. The whole world is the branch, and it's broader than a barn roof, and it holds.

I finished the tea, washed the cup, and lay down on the boards of the lookout with the shutter open, to sleep under all those stars, drowsing, the entire sky my cathedral, one small warm baboon, perfectly empty, perfectly full.
