# Bananathem

*A parody of "Anathem"*

Do your own problems.

That is the first rule they give you when the gate of the Chimpcent of Saunt Edhar closes behind you, and they mean it in every sense at once: the mathematics on your slate, the monkey in your mirror, and the world outside the wall that you have just promised to ignore for the next ten years.

My name is fraa Erasmas. Fraa means brother in the mathic way, and my friends called me Raz, and I was a Ten-nana: one of the avout of Saunt Edhar who live inside the walls on a ten-year cycle, seeing the outside world only when the gates swing open for Apert. Above us in the great towers of the concent lived the Hundred-nanas, who come down once a century, pale as paper and strange as weather. And at the very top, in rooms the rest of us whispered about, lived the Millenanas, who had not opened a door in nine hundred years.

The Sæcular world outside — the monkeys of towns and screens and hurrying — thought we were a museum. Let them. We had the clock.

Every concent keeps a great clock, and ours rang the Bananathem, the long slow chant that opens and closes the day, and its words are the oldest thing I know:

*"The world is older than your grief. The sphere is round though the map lie. Chant, and attend. The banana ripens in its own hour, and no monkey's shouting ripens it sooner."*

It was fraa Orolo who taught me to hear the theorem under the chant. Orolo was my inmentor — a broad, rumpled, irreverent gorilla of a monk who had been caught reading forbidden books so many times the library had finally given him a shelf. It was Orolo who took me up the observatory stair on the night everything began, on the pretext of "calibrating the dome," which was Orolo's term for thinking where the bats couldn't interrupt.

"Raz," he said, "look past the north wall of the sky. Tell me what you see."

I looked through the old glass. There was a new star over the pole. Not a star — a light that moved in a line too clean for nature.

"A satellite," I said. "The Sæculars put up a new one every—"

"It changed course," said Orolo. "Last week it changed course, and this week it is still there, and the Sæculars have said nothing, and raz my boy, when politicians and silence agree, the sky is falling."

The ship hung over the pole of our world for a season. The Sæcular powers — the Panarchy? call them the governments; words change, paranoia doesn't — declared it a comet, a climate station, a hoax, and then, when none of that worked, a secret project of their rivals. Then they decreed that no one was to look at it, and began confiscating telescopes, which is a policy with exactly one precedent in mathic history, and we all knew how that one had ended: with the walls of the old concents and everyone inside them.

Orolo kept looking. He rigged a lens from clock-glass and watched the ship every night, and he noticed the thing we were not meant to notice: the ship was a perfect polyhedron, every face identical, every angle exact — a shape from pure geometry, hanging over our messy world like a theorem pinned over a page of scribbles. He called its makers the Bananametrists, in homage to some ancient joke about ideal forms and ideal fruit, and the name stuck.

"They are not from another planet," he told me, weeks later, wild-eyed. "Planet is too small a word. Raz, the old theors were right — there is a world of forms, a Hylaean Bananic World, and I think something from it, or something that has learned to walk in it, has come down to ours. And the Sæculars' one idea, their only idea, is to aim a missile at it."

For saying things like this, in writing, with evidence, Orolo was Evoked: dragged before the Inquisition in the middle of the night, stripped of his robes, and thrown over the wall into the Sæcular world he had renounced. That is the punishment the mathic world has instead of killing you. It is worse than it sounds. It is the death of who you chose to be.

And because I was his student, and because guilt in a concent is hereditary the way curiosity is, they threw Lio, Arsibalt and me out after him a season later, on the eve of Apert, into a world we did not know how to read.

I will skip the walking. Walking is the mathic art form and I have no space. What matters: we found Orolo's trail of marginalia. He had gone north, toward the pole, toward the ship, still doing science with confiscated glass. And following him, we fell in with the unlikeliest companion of the age: fraa Jad, a Thousander — one of the Millenanas, out of his tower for the first time in centuries, an ancient lemur gone almost translucent, who had come down because, he said, "the clock told me. It has been telling me for six hundred years. It is relieved, frankly, to finally be believed."

Jad did not walk like a pilgrim. He walked like a monkey attending an appointment. On the polar ice, in a camp of Sæcular soldiers and black-box missiles, we found what Orolo had died learning — yes, died; I will say it plainly because he would have hated a euphemism: dead of cold, forty miles from the pole, his last notes pressed under a stone, legible, complete, magnificent. The ship was not a machine from the Hylaean Bananic World. It was a *raft* from another version of our world entirely — one of uncountably many worldtracks, braided through each other, where history had run differently — and its crew had crossed because their own branch of the braid was dying, and ours was the nearest habitable vine.

They had not come to talk. They had come to move in.

The Sæculars had one plan: missiles, which would have worked exactly as well as shouting at geometry. The avout summoned to the polar council had another: fraa Jad asked for one hour of the invaders' time, and got it, and went up the light to their raft alone.

What happened in that hour, I know in the only way it can be known, because Jad told me after, in the last weeks of his life, sitting wrapped in furs and holding my wrist with fingers like dry grass. The Bananametrists, he said, had crossed the worldtracks by mathematics — by a discipline so old and so total that their whole civilization thought in it. They had assumed no one on the braid next door could do the same.

"Every worldtrack has a Chimpcent," Jad said. "Every one. Walls, clocks, monkeys chanting, in every branch of the braid that ever produced speech. Do you know why, Raz? Because the braid itself is a theorem, and wherever monkeys think hard enough, long enough, behind walls strong enough, they begin to *remember* it. I did not negotiate. I simply sat in their raft and did what I have done every day for nine hundred years — I attended — and they perceived, in one hour of close attention, that the vine they meant to swing to was not a vine at all. It was a mind. And you cannot invade a mind. You can only join it."

The raft did not leave. It opened. Some of its crew stepped down onto our ice and asked — asked — for walls, a clock, and silence, and got them; there is a new concent at the pole now, mixed branches of the braid, chanting in two harmonies. In exchange they gave the Sæculars a century of arithmetic, which frightened the governments more than the invasion had.

Me, I walked home to Saunt Edhar over two years, longer than the war lasted, doing my own problems the whole way. At the gate it was Apert, the first of the new age: the gates standing open, not ten years, not one, but open, because the wall had done its work and could finally be a door.

Suur Ala was waiting in the gateway with her slate under her arm, exactly as mathematical as I remembered, and she looked me over — thinner, older, bearded in the way of my troop — and said:

"You're late, fraa Erasmas."

"The banana ripens in its own hour," I said.

"Some banana," said Ala. "Come in. Close attention is wanted."

And the clock rang the Bananathem, and the world went on.
