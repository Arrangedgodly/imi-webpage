# The Epic of Gibbonmesh

*A parody of "The Epic of Gilgamesh"*

He who saw the canopy, who knotted the vines of the land — come, hear of Gibbonmesh, king of Uruk-of-the-Fig-Walls.

Gibbonmesh was two-thirds god and one-third gibbon: strong beyond measure, beautiful beyond patience, and impossible to live with. He raced the young and beat them, wrestled the strong and threw them, and never let anyone else sleep. The people of Uruk groaned to the gods: "Send us a rival! Anything to slow this monkey down!"

So the gods made Enkidu — a wild monkey of the far hills, fur full of burrs, who grazed with the gazelles and freed every animal from every trap, purely to annoy the trappers. The exasperated elders sent Shamhat the temple groomer, for no beast could resist her grooming.

Shamhat went out to the waterhole and sat, and held up her comb. Six days and seven nights she combed him, and afterward the beasts ran from him, for he was smooth, smelled of fig-oil, and something had changed in his eyes. "Come, Enkidu," said Shamhat. "I will take you to Uruk, where the king runs wild. It is time somebody wilder ran back."

They met at the gate of the fig-walls and, without a word, simply gripped. They wrestled like two storms caught in one tree; the walls shook; and when Gibbonmesh felt his knee touch the ground — the first time ever — he let go, laughed, and embraced Enkidu.

"You are my equal — my brother! Together we will do the thing the whole troop remembers."

The thing was the Great Cedar Canopy, guarded by Humbaba — his face a coil of living vines, his mouth fire, his roar a flood. "Fifty monkeys daren't approach him," said the elders.

"Then two will," said Gibbonmesh.

On the road, Gibbonmesh dreamed terrible dreams: a mountain fell on him; a rain of rocks poured down. Each dawn he woke in terror, and each dawn Enkidu interpreted. "The mountain is Humbaba. You will fall on him. Excellent dream. Dream more like that one." Every dream was a good dream. Enkidu was that kind of interpreter, and the brothers marched to the forest grinning.

Humbaba came out roaring, and the gods sent the Thirteen Winds — north, south, east, west, and nine spares — which pinned the forest-guardian flat. "Spare me!" wept Humbaba. "I will cut the cedars myself! I will serve you and your children and your children's figs!" The brothers looked at each other — and Enkidu, remembering what the elders had said about sparing monsters, said "Finish it." The friends cut the tallest cedars, and the troop sang of it.

Back in Uruk, the goddess Ishtar watched Gibbonmesh bathing and called down: "Come, Gibbonmesh, be my bridegroom! I will give you a chariot of gold and lapis, kings and princes beneath your feet!"

"What happened to your former lovers?" said Gibbonmesh. "Let me count. You loved the tamarin and dressed him in bells, and when he bored you, you struck him into a sloth. You loved the lion — seven and seven pits you dug for him. You loved the stallion doomed to whip and barley. You loved the shepherd and turned him into a wolf; his own dogs chase him now. And the gardener — you turned him into a frog, and there he sits in the pond, saying it was worth it, which is somehow the saddest part. Shall I eat rotten figs and husks, and be handed a palace of reeds? No, goddess. You are a fig-pit fire that goes out in the cold."

Ishtar threatened to smash the underworld's doors and raise the dead to eat the living unless she got the Bull of Heaven. The Bull ate every fig in the land, drank two rivers at a draught, and opened pits with each snort that swallowed a hundred monkeys — until the two friends slew him, threw the haunch in Ishtar's face, and the troop danced on the Bull.

For the Bull and for Humbaba, the gods decreed: one of the two must die. It was Enkidu.

He sickened, and dreamed the dream of the underworld: the house of dust, where the dwellers eat clay and wear feathers like birds and see no light. "He who died in battle lies on a bed, and his father and mother support his head, and his wife weeps beside him," he told Gibbonmesh. "But he whose corpse was left on the plain — his spirit does not rest. I would rather have died in battle." He cursed the door of the gods and the groomer who tamed him — then, thinking again, blessed her, and lay back. Gibbonmesh sat beside him. "Won't you wake? You have become as dark as the earth on me. Enkidu — six days and seven nights I have sat. The worm does not come out of your nose. I roar like a lion: he who stood by me is gone."

Then he left Uruk with torn fur and a torn heart, roaring: "If my brother must die, I will find the secret! I will find Utnapishtim the Faraway, who survived the Great Flood, and ask him how to live forever!"

He ran to the ends of the earth, ate raw figs on the run, and killed the lions of the pass out of pure grief. At Mount Mashu, where the sun goes under the world each night, the Scorpion-people guarded the gate — their glance is death, their faces glare. But they saw he was two-thirds god, and one-third desperate, and let him run the sun's own tunnel: twelve leagues of dark, no light ahead or behind, until he came out into a garden whose trees bore fruit of carnelian and lapis — a jewel orchard, where even the shade smelled expensive.

He talked his way past Siduri the barkeeper, who poured him a drink and the standard advice ("Be merry, eat your figs, wash your fur — the gods kept eternal life for themselves"), wore out the stone-gorillas that guard the way, and made the ferryman Urshanabi row him across the Waters of Death.

There sat Utnapishtim — an old, mild monkey who had once loaded a wooden crate-ark with every beast two by two (the bees were the hardest, he said, and the woodpeckers nearly sank them) and ridden out the Great Flood on a mountaintop, grounding on Nisir and sending out first a dove, then a swallow, then a raven that did not come back, which is how you know it is safe.

"You want eternal life? Then stay awake seven nights." Gibbonmesh sat. Gibbonmesh slept — seven days, like a log — while Utnapishtim's wife baked one loaf a day and lined them up, fresh to stale, by the time he woke. "How will you conquer death, monkey, when you cannot conquer a nap? Look: yesterday's loaf, the day before's, the week's whole bakery — that was your eternal vigil."

But Utnapishtim took pity. "There is a plant at the bottom of the Great Water — the Banana of Youth. Eat of it, and some of your young strength returns."

Gibbonmesh tied stones to his feet, dove, and rose glowing, clutching the pale fruit. "I have it! I will feed it to the old monkeys of Uruk!"

That night he bathed in a pool — and a serpent smelled the Banana of Youth, took it, ate it, shed its old skin, and slid away young forever.

He wept on the bank. "For whom have my hands toiled? For a snake's breakfast?"

He looked down the long green valley, and there, far off, the fig-walls of Uruk shone upon their hill. "Why," he said slowly, "look at the walls. I built that."

He went home, rebuilt the walls, groomed the young, told the story, and ruled well. He died like all monkeys die, but famous: the monkey who saw the canopy, and learned that the walls of Uruk are the only forever a monkey gets.

And it is enough.
