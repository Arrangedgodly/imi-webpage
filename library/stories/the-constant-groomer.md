# The Constant Groomer

*A parody of "The Constant Gardener"*

Justin Quayle was a gibbon of modest gifts and immaculate manners, second secretary at the High Commission in Nairobberry, and famous in three embassies for the gentleness of his hands. Other diplomats networked; Justin groomed. He picked burrs from the shoulders of ambassadors, thorn-seeds from the tails of ministers, and listened — politely, endlessly — while the whole canopy of power relaxed under his fingers and told him everything, because nobody is suspicious of a monkey who is only being kind. He once eased a tick off the Deputy Under-Secretary at a garden party, mid-sentence, without interrupting either the tick or the Under-Secretary, and had the fig-trade budget explained to him by dessert. It was the closest the High Commission came to intelligence work, and Justin never noticed, which was the beauty of it.

His mate Tessa was his opposite: a young bonobo with fire in her fur and no brake on her tongue. She went where she was not invited and asked what was not asked. "You groom the powerful one flea at a time, Justin," she told him. "Somebody has to bite."

At the High Commission she was officially tolerated and privately a scandal. She had once asked the Permanent Secretary, at dinner, whether his conscience came with the post or was billed separately, and the Permanent Secretary had laughed for an hour and never forgiven her. Her garden on the compound was her one concession to diplomacy: she grew only things she could not argue with — beans, marigolds, one stubborn fig. It was Justin who weeded it. He was weeding it the morning the wire came.

She bit ThreeFleas Pharmaceutical. The great company had been testing its wonder-vaccine — DyNanaXa, the golden bullet against jungle fever — on poor troop-villages up north, in return for free bananas and a clinic wall. The records said miraculous. The graves said otherwise. Tessa collected the graves, village by village, in a school exercise book with a flower on the cover. She had stood in a clinic doorway up north while a ThreeFleas ape in a pale suit counted a line of infants against a clipboard, and asked him what happened when the count said no, and been invited to leave the country through the better of its two doors.

"You're embarrassing the Banyan House," Sandy Twigrow, the Head of Chancery, told her at a reception, oiled and smiling. "There are contracts, my dear. Jobs. Relations. Be a sensible girl."

"Be a decent ape, Sandy," said Tessa, and went north with her friend Dr. Arnold Bluhm, a vast gentle gorilla physician who had counted the little graves himself.

The news came back by wire, the way the jungle delivers its worst: Tessa and Bluhm found dead at the salt lake crossing, "killed by bandits," said the communiqué, and the communiqué was signed by decent monkeys, approved by decent monkeys, filed by decent monkeys. The messenger stood with his cap in his paws while Justin read it twice, because Justin read everything twice; the second reading changed nothing at all.

"There are larger considerations," Sir Peregrine of the Banyan House told Justin, with a paw on his shoulder, which is what important monkeys say when they mean smaller ones. The Commission, meanwhile, sent him memos about his leave running out, each worded more warmly than the last, warmth being the house style of threat.

Justin did not rage. Gibbons do not rage; they sing, very quietly, at a pitch other monkeys cannot hear. He asked for leave. He asked for her notes. And he began to groom her trail.

The city came to the funeral, and the city talked. About Tessa and Bluhm, and what a young wife and a gorilla doctor had been doing at a salt lake. The High Commissioner praised her compassion in the past tense, carefully, the way one praises a fire once it is out. Justin stood through all of it with his hands folded, grooming nothing, hearing everything. Afterwards a young galago clerk from the Commission called Ghita — who had idolized Tessa and taken dictation from her tongue — pressed a bundle into his paws: Tessa's spare notes, carbon copies, kept "against accidents." There had been an accident. There were copies.

It was, he found, beautifully combed — by professionals. The friendly driver who had seen "bandits" had been paid in medicine for his sick infant. The police report was typed on the Commission's own machine. And the woman physician from ThreeFleas who had signed the drug's safety report — Dr. Lorbeeroo — turned out never to have visited the north at all, having been too busy that season being invented. Justin wrote to ThreeFleas, politely, twice, and was answered first by lawyers and then by two serious gorillas in matching windbreakers who called themselves auditors and stood too close. It confirmed everything a softer monkey would have missed.

Where Tessa had gone, village by village, Justin went; where she had been handed a cup of sour milk, he was handed the same cup, and the same warning, and — because his hands were known to be kind — a little more truth each time, flea by flea by flea. In the third village a mother brought out a small tin with a ribbon on it. "She had the injection," the mother said, "and then she slept for two days, and then—" After that Justin stopped writing in the notebook, the notebook not being large enough. And always, at the edge of every memory, the same detail: a white helicopter that had come for the trial files, and a clinic wall repainted overnight.

The truth, assembled, was this: DyNanaXa killed some of the children who took it. ThreeFleas had known, and had spent a season and a fortune not knowing it. Tessa had carried proof toward the newspapers. The bandits were employees. The contracts — the ones signed in Nairobberry's own Commission, by Sandy Twigrow, with a nod from Sir Peregrine of the Banyan House — were worth two billion bananas a year.

Justin did something thoroughly Justin. He wrote letters. Fourteen of them, in Tessa's memory, each with copies of the proof, addressed to the widows of the trial villages, the jungle's newspapers, the medical registry, the Crown, and one, with love, to Sandy Twigrow. Then he posted them, unhurried, from fourteen different branches, the way a constant groomer works: unable to do everything, refusing to do nothing.

Then he went to the salt lake where she died, and sat by the water with his hands folded, and watched the flamingos standing on their reflections. They came for him at dusk, of course — the tidy kind of monkeys who tidy twice. There was no struggle that anyone could have heard above the waterbirds.

The letters arrived the following Tuesday, all fourteen, and would not be tidied. ThreeFleas' golden bullet was recalled by every government in the canopy, Twigrow and Sir Peregrine retired into permanent shame, and in the trial villages the mothers planted a fig tree for Tessa and one, at a little distance, for the quiet gibbon who had finished her combing.

What the lake took, the lake kept. But the keeper of the lake will tell you, if you bring him something sweet, that a young fig tree by the south shore grows faster than any tree in the Rift — as though something down there in the cool mud is patient, and persistent, and constantly gardening.
