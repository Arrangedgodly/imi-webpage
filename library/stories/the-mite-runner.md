# The Mite Runner

*A parody of "The Kite Runner"*

Winter was kite season in the great banyans of Canopybul. Every monkey in the troop climbed to the crown at dawn to watch the leaf-kites rise — fig leaves lacquered with sap, strung on twisted vine — until the whole gray sky buzzed with them and even the oldest gibbons went quiet.

I was Amir, son of Baba, the tallest silverback in the troop, a monkey so wide that branches bowed when he sat on them. Baba wrestled bears before breakfast, mocked book-learning, and could not understand a son who preferred poetry to wrestling. "A monkey who cannot defend his banana," he told me, "will never defend a troop." With me, always, came Hassan: a mite of a monkey, flat-faced, with a cleft lip and a tail like a question mark. He was the son of Ali, our groomer, one of the low-canopy Hazelnut folk, and he would have fetched me the moon if I'd pointed at it.

We grew up in the same branches, and in the summers we sat in the hollow of our hillside fig tree, where I had carved our names with a thorn: AMIR AND HASSAN, SULTANS OF THE BANYAN. I read him stories there, because nobody would teach him letters, and he listened the way you drink. Once, in a tale about a monkey who wept onion tears to fake his grief, Hassan frowned.

"But Amir agha," he said, "why didn't he just cut an onion? Then nobody has to die at the end."

I stared at him. The greatest poets of the jungle had missed that hole for nine hundred years, and Hassan, who could not read his own name, had found it in one hearing. I teased him with words he didn't know — *imbecile* means smart, I told him, and he thanked me — and he never held it against me, which was the unbearable part. It is a terrible thing, being forgiven daily by someone braver than you.

Assef found us once, before the tournament — a young baboon with walnut teeth and a coconut half-shell over his fist. He called Hassan a flat-faced low-canopy and me worse, a friend of low-canopies, and Hassan flicked up his sling, loaded a tamarind seed, and held it between Assef's eyes with a hand that did not shake. Assef promised us both a reckoning and backed away laughing, the way cowards keep accounts.

Then came the winter tournament. "Do you want me to run that kite for you?" Hassan asked me, the morning of. "If you want," I said, the way young masters say it. His cleft lip bent into his crooked smile. "For you, a thousand figs over," he said, and dropped through the branches like a falling fig.

I won. The last kite to fall was the blue one, and Hassan ran it — a mite runner, the best in the city, leaping from branch to branch while the whole troop roared, chasing glory for another monkey's son. Baba's arm was around my shoulders. For one whole afternoon I was the son he had ordered.

I went looking for Hassan. I found him in the dead-end gully behind the fig warehouses, cornered by Assef and his two goons.

"The blue kite," Assef said. "Hand it over, low-canopy."

"Amir agha won it fairly," Hassan said. "It is his."

I hid behind a curtain of leaves. I watched the goons pin Hassan's arms. I watched the coconut come down once, twice, while Hassan only hugged the kite to his chest and made no sound at all. I could have shouted. Instead I ran — ran from the gully, ran from a smile I could not stand to meet — and that was the winter I learned that a monkey can be whole and hollow at the same time.

Afterward, Hassan still groomed my shoulders every morning, and I stopped being able to breathe under his hands. So I did a coward's arithmetic: I took my birthday figs and my father's brass grooming comb and pushed them under Hassan's sleeping mat, then told Baba the mite boy had thieved.

"Did you steal them?" Baba asked, heartbroken.

Hassan looked at me for one long moment — a look that said *I know, and I will take it anyway, because for you, a thousand figs over.* "Yes," he said.

Baba forgave him on the spot, because Baba's own father had taught him that theft was the one unforgivable sin. Ali and Hassan left the troop that night anyway, quietly, two shapes going down the trunk in the rain. Baba stood at the window-branch until morning. I never asked him why that was.

Then came the years of falling trees — men with saws, then baboons with fire — and Baba and I fled, half a jungle between us and the far zoo by the Golden Gate, where the fog came in every afternoon like a herd of ghosts and nobody knew a silverback's son from a beggar. Baba sold figs at the flea market on Sundays and haggled like a monkey eating a bear. I finished school, sold my first story, and married Soraya, who loved poems and had a past of her own that everyone gossiped about and nobody needed to forgive. Baba coughed through my wedding and kept working the market until the cough won. He died in his sleep, enormous to the end, one arm flung out like a branch.

Twenty years later, old Rahim Khan sent word: *There is a way to be good again, Amir.*

There is no worse sentence in any language for a monkey with my ledger. I went back.

Rahim Khan was small now, and coughing, and honest. Hassan, he said, had come back to guard our empty banyan with his wife, and had been shot at the gate by an invading horde for refusing to hand over the fig stores — Hassan, standing in front of the last tree of our childhood. His son, Sohrab, had been taken as a trophy by the horde's enforcer: Assef, grown now, wearing brass knuckles of hammered coconut shell.

"There is one more thing," Rahim Khan said, and looked at his paws. "Ali could not have children. Your father and Ali's wife — Amir, Hassan was your brother. Half by blood, whole by every law of the banyan. Baba paid for his lip surgery; do you remember? Baba wept when they left; do you remember? Now you know the size of what he was hiding."

I sat very still. A monkey who wrestles bears, feeds the whole street, cries at his servant's departure, and never once tells his son the truth — that is the largest kind of coward, and I had spent my life being the small kind.

What happened in that burrow was short and ugly. Assef beat me while his goons held the boy, and I laughed — laughed so hard my lip split open into a cleft just like Hassan's — until a small shape erupted from the rafters: Sohrab, whirling a sling of vine, firing tamarind seeds like a fury of wasps, his hand as steady as his father's ever was. We ran. Of course we ran. Some debts you pay by laughing; some by running.

The orphanage keeper wept at the gate as we left. "I sell a child a month for rice," he said, "and I will answer for it to whatever Silverback keeps the ledgers. Take him."

Getting Sohrab out took the rest of my credit with the world: papers, favors, promises, and an adoption process so slow that a small monkey who had been caged, sold, and beaten heard the word *wait* and understood it as *returned.* He stopped eating. He stopped speaking. Monkeys who have been caged learn that silence is the one thing no one can steal. We nearly lost him that spring — a vet with kind paws and no questions pulled him back, and I sat by his cot for forty nights, and neither of us said a word, and both of us knew the word was *stay.*

Then one Sunday, in the zoo's monkey park, Sohrab stopped at the fence. Zoo children were flying kites — paper ones, honest ones. His eyes flicked up.

"Do you want me to run one for you?" I asked.

Nothing. A tiny shake of his head — and then, from somewhere deep inside the cage of his silence, a nod. For one second he looked exactly like a mite of a monkey with a crooked smile.

"For you, a thousand figs over," I said.

I flew the kite into the wind, cut a green one loose, and pointed. Then I was over the fence and running — an old monkey, arms pumping, chasing a falling kite across the grass, chasing the boy I had failed, chasing a fig I did not deserve.

A grown male running after a paper kite. It was undignified. It was tradition.

I ran.
