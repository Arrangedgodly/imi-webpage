# Rakkety Tamarin

*A parody of "Rakkety Tam"*

The Marching Song of Rakkety Tamarin, sung going into battle, had forty-one verses, thirty-nine of them about porridge. Tam MacBurl — Rakkety Tam to every beast who'd ever fought beside him — was a tamarin of the borders, small as a teacake and twice as fierce, with his brow-curl slicked into a warrior's peak. At his right hand always: Wild Doogy Plumm, an emperor tamarin with a magnificent grey moustache and no talent whatsoever for backing down.

They had sworn an oath to a foolish king. That's how it always starts.

King Araltum of the Borders was a red-handed tamarin with a throne, a fan, and an overdeveloped sense of ceremony; his court had gold braid on everything, including, on state occasions, the bananas. Tam and Doogy had bound themselves by the old debt — the king's father had saved their troop — and now they stood at court ceremonies wearing little hats while monsters went un-fought, which to highland tamarins is a form of dying.

"Ye cannae make a warrior intae a hat-stand," Doogy muttered, at the Feast of the Ninety-Third Toast.

"Haud yer wheesht," said Tam. "We swore."

But when a bloodied courier staggered in from the north with word that Gulo the Savage was coming — Gulo, the mad wolverine of the ice forests, who had killed his own father for the throne and gnawed the bones of his enemies, just to make conversation — King Araltum's contribution was to suggest the fortifications be draped in more braid. And when the king refused to let them go fight, Tam and Doogy broke their oath the highland way: loudly, in front of everyone, with a song. The song had one verse, and it was not about porridge. The king confiscated their hats, which everyone agreed was the revenge of a very small monkey.

Gulo came down from the northlands like winter given teeth, with his horde of white vermin behind him, and he came for one thing only: the Walking Branch. His brother Askor had stolen it and fled south, and without it Gulo could never be King of all the Northlands — a splinter in his gum, violently, forever.

"FIND IT!" he howled, in a voice that emptied rookeries. "I am Gulo the Savage! I eat my enemies! I eat the enemies of my enemies! I have eaten things that were only *thinking* about being my enemies!"

He was very unpleasant. When a rat named Sinneral brought him the wrong intelligence, Gulo ate the intelligence, and sent the rat back as a warning. His own horde marched behind him in perfect order, which is what terror is for.

Askor, as it happened, was already dead — he'd made it to the borders before his wounds caught him, and he'd hidden the Walking Branch where no northland beast would think to look: with two scruffy tamarin mercenaries who'd buried him decently and taken, as payment, a small stone-hard object wrapped in his cloak. Tam and Doogy had assumed it was a fossilized plantain. They'd been using it as a camping stool.

All of this came out at Redwall-by-the-Water — an abbey of peaceful woodlanders sitting on the great south path like a plum on a stump, with its orchards, its Dibbuns, its gentle Abbot, and its total lack of any concept of Gulo. Tam and Doogy arrived ahead of the horde — Tam with a vermin javelin in his shoulder, carried the last half-league on Doogy's back — and the Abbey took them in the way Abbeys do: skeptically at first, and then forever.

"You'll not turn us out," Doogy told the infirmary, as Sister Armel drew the javelin and Tam refused the cordial on the grounds that he was merely resting. "Yon beast Gulo would dig us oot of heaven itself, and complain about the digging."

The Walking Branch spent the siege in the orchard, still employed as a camping stool. Nobody connected a fossilized plantain with the crown jewel of the frozen north until Gulo's herald came to the gates and demanded it by name, and every head in the orchard turned slowly toward two very still tamarins on a very small stool.

The siege was a thing of horrors and improvisation. The Abbot, being an Abbot, offered Gulo sanctuary, food, and the chance of a quieter life. Gulo ate the offer — the actual bark-paper it was written on — and demanded instead the Branch, the Abbey, and dessert. After that, Gulo's vermin went over the walls in the night; the Dibbuns found a way to weaponize dessert, and hot plum-duff proved devastating at short range. Gulo parleyed once, in the traditional manner: he ate the parley. Gulo himself came to the gates to demand the Branch, and Tam MacBurl stood on the walltop, very small against very large, and said the words that would follow him into every song:

"Ye'll get the Branch, Gulo the Savage — the same way my father got his porridge. Cold, and in yer face."

Midwinter nearly settled the matter before the swords could. Food ran short in both camps, but vermin eat anything and Abbots refuse to, on principle. It was the Dibbuns who turned the famine into tactics: they baked a decoy Branch — a fossil-shaped banana of flour, paste, and prayer, browned to five thousand years of convincing — and let it be "stolen" by a captured vermin thief, who was released with great ceremony and a full stomach so he'd remember Redwall as the place with the food. Gulo's horde fell on the decoy at their own war-council, fought each other for it, and discovered the fraud together, which did more damage to morale than any siege engine in history. Gulo ate the decoy anyway, on the grounds that authority must never admit a mistake, and declared it delicious, and his horde believed him, which is the whole trick of terror.

They fought on the earthworks, tamarins and squirrels and otters and one enormous gentle bankvole with a soup ladle, against white vermin who had never been beaten. The otters lured a raiding party onto the frozen pond and introduced them to the water under it. Doogy lost half his moustache to a javelin and called it a haircut. Sister Armel worked the walltop with bandages and a soup tureen, telling the wounded they were being very brave and the vermin they were being very rude. And when the horde finally broke and ran, Gulo the Savage did the thing Tam had prayed he would: he fought his way inward, to the small tamarin with the curls, and bared his teeth.

Single combat, on frozen ground, the whole battle stopped to watch.

Gulo was bigger, madder, and made of muscle. Tam was faster, colder, and had learned from every one of his forty Highland defeats. The fight lasted long enough for two verses. It ended with Rakkety Tam MacBurl standing over the Savage of the Northlands — who had eaten kings and brothers and messages, and was beaten in the end by a teacake with a sword — and the Northlands' next king, a distant, less edible cousin, kneeling to receive the Walking Branch.

Which was, everyone agreed afterward, without any doubt whatsoever, a fossilized banana. Five thousand years old. Sacred beyond price.

"It's a banana, Doogy," said Tam.

"Aye," said Doogy Plumm, cradling it like a bairn. "But it's *their* banana."

They gave it to the north, turned south, and were never hat-stands again. King Araltum's court sent a formal letter requesting their return to ceremonial duty; the Abbey returned it unanswered, but with a recipe for plum-duff, which historians agree was more devastating. Tam stayed at Redwall-by-the-Water, and Sister Armel maintained forever that she'd only kept dressing his old shoulder wound out of professional pride, and the Marching Song gained a forty-second verse.

It was about porridge. Some things are sacred.
