# A Swiftly Tilting Primate

*A parody of "A Swiftly Tilting Planet"*

The news came to the troop on the night of the great feast, when every bowl was full and every branch was warm: far to the south, in the groves of Vespugia, the warlord Mad Gorilla Branzillo had built the Fire-Flower, and threatened to burn every tree from the canopy to the coast. One hour, he said. One hour and the whole jungle goes.

The toucan that brought the news was so tired it rang instead of speaking, and had to be given water and a fig before anyone could understand anything at all. And Grandmother O'Cocoa, the ancient owl-monkey who had seen everything twice, turned her enormous eyes away from the fire, stood up at the council branch, closed them, and recited:

*"At the great fig tree, in this fable, I summon my senses:*
*sight for seeing the ripe from the rotten,*
*grip for holding and heart for not letting go.*
*In this hour of wind and shadow I summon my heart's fire*
*to melt the fear that freezes."*

"Now," she said, opening her eyes, "somebody must go where the winds go. And it will have to be somebody small."

Then she pressed her tiny old hand against little Charles Wallace's cheek and whispered, "For you, small one. For Chuck."

Charles Wallace was the youngest and smallest chimp in the troop, and the only one who could hear what trees were thinking. That night he climbed to the top of the highest kapok to be alone with his worry — and the wind began to sing.

Out of the starlight came a gibbon made of silver, with eyes like struck matches, moving between the branches the way melody moves between notes.

"I am Gaudior," said the silver gibbon, which means "the more joyful." "I ride the winds of time. You are expected."

"Expected? I'm the smallest —"

"The winds only carry the small," said Gaudior. "Now. Long ago, in the far south, there were two baby brothers of one old line: Maddock the gentle and Gedder the greedy, and the Fire-Flower grew from Gedder's branch of the family like a fungus with a fuse. The Branzillo who threatens the jungle tonight descends from one of them. Which one — that is not yet decided."

"How can it not be decided? It was centuries ago!"

"Because history is not a branch," said Gaudior. "History is a river. You cannot move a river. But a might-have-been is a stone in it, and stones can be leaned on. I will carry you within the moments of your ancestors, at the forks where a heart chose hardening or softening, and you will lean. Do not try to change what happens. Lean on what might. And hold on to my fur and do not let go."

They rode. The jungle tilted beneath them — not through space but through time, seasons unspooling like vines — until Gaudior stopped above a rocking ship's deck, centuries back, where a homesick young sailor macaque clutched a letter. Charles Wallace slid down the moonlight and went *within* — feeling the macaque's loneliness as his own, cold and salty and long, and deep inside, at the one forked moment where the sailor chose to harden his heart against a stranger's flag, Charles leaned. The sailor folded the letter, and wept, and stayed kind.

Again they flew — into a healer-macaque bending over a sick child she was too exhausted to save. Charles felt her weariness like a stone, and at the moment she nearly turned the child away, he leaned. She put her hands back on the small hot forehead.

Again — into a mother of twin babies, one who reached for the world and one who reached for her, one grown fat and greedy at the nipple of the troop's stores. Charles leaned, gently, the way you tip a leaf so the rain slides off. The mother laughed, and kissed both babies, and loved them the same.

And each time, out of the cold between the branches, the Echthroi came — the shadow-things, the un-makers, gray winds that wanted everything hollow. They buffeted Gaudior with the smell of ash and the sound of falling trees.

"Say the rune!" sang Gaudior, faltering. "Charles Wallace, SAY THE RUNE!"

*"'At the great fig tree, in this fable, I summon my senses,"* Charles shouted into the black wind, *"sight for seeing, grip for holding, and my heart's FIRE — to MELT the FEAR that FREEZES!"*

The words went through the shadows like sun through fog. The Echthroi shrank back to the spaces between branches, hissing, cheated.

But not every heart can be leaned on. Once — and Charles Wallace never forgot it — they came to a young soldier macaque on a burned plantation, standing over the last standing tree with an axe in his fists, and everything in him balanced at the fork. Charles leaned with everything he had. He poured warmth down through the young macaque's shoulders like a noon. And the macaque lifted the axe anyway, and split the tree, and laughed at the sound, and Charles felt the Echthroi pour through the fork like cold through a broken window, howling with triumph.

He fell out of the wind. He could not find his hands. And Gaudior dropped — actually dropped — out of the sky with a silver tear in his shoulder where a shadow had raked him, and they crashed together through the branches of a strangler fig and hung there, breathing.

"I broke it," wept Charles Wallace. "I made it worse. I leaned too hard."

"You leaned at all," said Gaudior, gravely, his matchstick eyes dim. "The Echthroi do not howl over what does not matter. Hear me: the wound you could not close is a wound they fear you will. That is why they struck at me and not you. Now — I am going to say something I should have said at the start. You are not doing this work alone."

And out of the dark, faint as breath on fur, came his sister Meg's mind, touching his from the next tree and the next century, the way it always did: *little brother. Little brother, are you all right?*

*I don't know,* Charles answered, honestly.

*Then I will know for you,* said Meg. *At the great fig tree, in this fable — say it with me, Chuck — I summon my senses...*

And together, brother and sister, a jungle apart, said the rune all the way through to *the fear that freezes*, and Gaudior's shoulder closed like a ripple closing over a thrown stone, and the wind came back singing.

They rode one last time — into the cradle of two babies, long ago, one who reached for the world and one who reached for her. Maddock the gentle. Gedder the greedy. And in the hush between their two small hearts, at the true first fork of everything, Charles Wallace did not lean. He simply held them, both, equally, the way the night holds two stars — and the mother gathered both babies up, and loved them the same, in a way that echoed down every branch of the family tree at once.

They rode until dawn, and in the dawn Charles Wallace sat under his own fig tree, a small chimp with silver fur on his shoulders that wasn't his, listening to his sister's mind touch his: *little brother, little brother, are you all right?*

*All right,* he said. *Leaned on.*

Far to the south, Mad Gorilla Branzillo woke from a dream of a rocking deck and a healed child and two babies and a burned tree that had never fallen — blinked — and dismantled the Fire-Flower himself, bolt by bolt, and planted a fig tree in the crater it left, and sat by it in the mornings looking vaguely surprised at his own happiness. The groves of Vespugia called him Blue-Eyes after that.

"Some things have to be believed to be seen," said Grandmother O'Cocoa when he told her, opening her enormous eyes at the sunrise. "The winds only carry the small, and the small only need to lean."

And far overhead, if you listened — you could hear the wind, being joyful.
