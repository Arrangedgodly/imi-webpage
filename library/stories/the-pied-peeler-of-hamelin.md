# The Pied Peeler of Hamelin

*A parody of "The Pied Piper of Hamelin"*

In the jungle town of Hamelin, in the hollow of the Great Banyan by the brown river, the monkeys had never known such trouble. Rats! Rats with a taste for bananas — rats in the pantries, rats in the granary hollows, rats in the nut cellars; rats who out-fought the cats, out-swam the otters, rifled the banana stores, licked every spoon, and nested in the mayor's own nightcap, twice, the second time with curtains.

The town council held an emergency session, which began with a roll call and ended in a food fight, and settled the following: the cats were tried, and the cats resigned. The ferrets were consulted, and the ferrets moved to the next town and left a note. The otters formed a committee, which still meets.

A delegation of rats was invited to negotiate terms, and ate the invitation. A sign was posted on the granary: NO RATS. The rats replied, in the mayor's best ink, on the mayor's best stationery: THANK YOU — WE KNOW.

The mayor, a magnificent mandrill, sat at her wits' end, wringing her nightcap. "We'll be eaten out of tree and branch! We'll go broke! We'll go bananas! A thousand golden bananas to anyone who clears the beasts away!"

The very next morning, up the river road came a stranger — the strangest monkey ever seen in Hamelin: a long coat stitched all over in banana peels, half yellow and half red (which is what *pied* means, if you were wondering), a peacock feather at a rakish angle, and a pipe of seven green bamboos slung across his back.

"Fine town, Hamelin," said he. "Shame about the rats."

"Shame about the—" spluttered the mayor. "They're in my nightcap! Can you be rid of them?"

"Madam Mayor, I am a Peeler. A Pied Peeler. I can peel a banana at forty paces, charm the fleas off a grumpy badger, and pipe anything with fur or whiskers over hill and hollow. I have emptied the warrens of Whangdoodle and the burrows of Banyanboo. For a thousand golden bananas, as posted, your rats shall trouble you no more."

"And your terms?" asked the treasurer, faintly.

"Terms are for monkeys who fail. I keep a rule instead, and the rule is simple: pay what you post." He said it pleasantly, the way you mention the weather. Nobody in Hamelin wrote it down, either.

"Done!" cried the mayor. And a bargain was struck before the treasurer had finished whispering, "Perhaps we should get that in writing."

The Peeler stepped into the street, put the pipe to his lips, and played a tune so sweet, so strange, so bouncy that even the bananas on the trees leaned in to listen. And out of every hollow and cellar and nightcap in Hamelin came tumbling the rats — big rats, small rats, lean rats, brawny rats, grave old rats and cheerful young rats, brown rats, black rats, shy rats, rats with their own tiny ladders — dancing after him down the river road. He piped and they hopped; he stepped and they skipped; and at the bank of the brown river — plop, plop, plop — every last rat plunged in and was swept downstream and away, never to be seen or smelt in Hamelin again.

Even the mayor's nightcap rats went, pausing only to fold the nightcap.

The town went wild. The mayor stood on a barrel and cheered. There was dancing in the Great Banyan, and banana bread for everyone, and the Peeler stood politely through eleven verses of the town anthem. But when he held out his paw — "A thousand golden bananas, as agreed" — the mayor's grin turned sly.

"A thousand! Ha! You played a little tune; the rats would have drowned themselves eventually. They were leaning that way already. Here's fifty, and be glad of it. No monkey business with the town treasury."

"Perhaps the rain did it," said the town clerk, who had come to watch and stayed to gloat. "Rain is free."

"A tune did it," said the Peeler.

"Then the wind carried your tune," said the clerk, "and the wind, sir, is public property."

The treasurer stared at his sandals. The Peeler's coat seemed to flash yellow and red at once, like a flame deciding.

"Fifty?" said he, very quietly. "Very well. Pied Peeler's rule is: half pay, half payback."

He put the pipe to his lips and played a different tune — softer, sweeter, wilder, a skippety-hoppity shimmer of a tune, the kind of melody that gets into the feet and unpacks there. And out of the doorways of Hamelin came the children: chimp children, gibbon children, macaque children swinging by one hand, toddlers riding pet coatis, one solemn little orangutan rolling a hoop he had just been awarded for excellence — all laughing and leaping, following the Peeler out the town gate and up the winding path toward the hollow mountain of Kopia.

The grown-ups waved, assuming it was a parade. Grown-ups assume everything is a parade until it is too late.

Almost all. One little chimp named Coco, who had a twisted leg, hobbled after them as fast as he could, which was not fast, and never caught up. From the foot of the mountain he saw — and never forgot — a door open in the rock, and the children dance in, singing, and the Peeler tip his peacock feather, and the door swing shut as sweetly as a fig closes.

Coco hammered on the rock with both fists until his palms were green. Then he sat down in the path. Then he got up, and limped the whole way home in the dark, alone, which was the longest journey any chimp ever made, and he was nine.

And the Peeler and the children were never seen in Hamelin again.

Coco told the parents everything: the tune, the dancing, the door. And the mayor offered him fifty golden bananas to stop describing it, which tells you most of what you need to know about how much Hamelin learned. Hamelin wept, as towns do, and counted the years of its sorrow from that very day, and — because some lessons must be written where the palm cannot reach — braided the whole tale in colored peels across the town gate, so it would never be forgotten.

Hamelin still dates everything from that day. Baskets are stamped BEFORE THE PIPER and AFTER. The town rolls list every child born since as "owed one," and the ledger where the thousand golden bananas were never paid is kept open to the same page, in case.

The treasurer counted it out at last, long after: a thousand golden bananas, less fifty. He kept the fifty in a little box marked WIND. On windy nights, the box rattles, though nothing is in it but the fifty.

And whether the children came home again by another road, no story tells — though travelers swear that in a green valley beyond Kopia there is a town of young monkeys where the bread is always fresh, nobody skips a payment, and the rats tell, humbly, of a piper who once showed them a river.

But the moral is right there in the braid, plain as a ripe banana:

Pay the piper. Pay the Peeler. A bargain's a bargain — or you'll pay double. And that's not monkey business; that's just good sense.
