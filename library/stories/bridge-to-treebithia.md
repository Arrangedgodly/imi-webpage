# Bridge to Treebithia

*A parody of "Bridge to Terabithia"*

Jesse Aarons had spent the whole dry season training. Every morning before the troop stirred, he was out on the practice lianas, hand over hand, until his palms went raw and his tail ached. He was going to be the fastest climber in the fifth grade if it killed him.

He would have made it, too, if it hadn't been for the new monkey.

She swung in on the first day of school like the wind had handed her there: a gibbon named Leslie Burke, with long pale arms and a banana-skin satchel. When the fifth graders lined up for the climbing races, she beat every single boy, looping over the top of the course as if she'd been born upside down.

"Girls can't climb faster than boys," said Gary Fulcher, who was a bully and partly a baboon.

"This girl just did," said Leslie.

Nobody knew what to do with her after that, so mostly they left her alone. Jesse would have too, except that evening, when he ambled down to the dry gully behind the old fig grove to fling sticks at crocodile logs, he heard a voice above him.

"You're the fastest guy in class. Almost."

He looked up. Leslie was hanging by one arm from a branch, upside down, blinking at him.

They got to talking the way you only can with someone brand new, who doesn't know anything about you. The gully below them was deep and fern-choked; in the wet season a brown river ran through it, and the crocodiles came up from the lowlands. On the far side stood a kapok so huge it made every other tree look like a stick.

"We need a place," Leslie said suddenly, "just for us. A whole secret kingdom. That tree could be the castle."

"How do we get there?" Jesse asked. "The vine's on this side."

"Then we need a better vine."

She found a thick liana hanging from the tallest fig, and by the end of the week they had both learned to run off the bank, seize the vine, and swing screaming across the gully — the only way in, the only way out.

"It's not just a tree," Leslie announced, landing in the kapok. "It's a stronghold. Enemies cannot cross without the vine. Gorilla-warlords cannot enter. But we can. Only we."

She named it Treebithia, and she made Jesse king, and she was queen. He built their fortress out of fallen branches; she wrote prayers and curses on mangrove seeds. They fought off tree-pirates and invaders and one truly evil baboon named Janice Avery — though in real life they got even with Janice using a fake love letter from the biggest brute in seventh grade, which was almost as good as a battle.

At Easter, May Belle, his little sister, came home from the grove church worried sick. "The teacher says if you don't believe, you don't go to heaven," she whispered. "Leslie doesn't believe. Is she going to hell?"

"God doesn't send people to hell for picking the wrong troop," Jesse said, which was more theology than he'd ever done in his life, and May Belle believed him because he was her big brother.

They told each other everything, even things they had never said out loud. When Jesse admitted he wanted to draw pictures instead of hauling bananas forever, Leslie didn't laugh. At midwinter she gave him a set of berry paints and real pressed-leaf paper. He gave her a grooming comb he had carved himself, and a tree frog he'd rescued from the wasp nest, which she crowned on the spot: Prince Terrien, Lord of the Realm, may he reign croakily.

Then came one perfect morning. The music teacher, Miss Edmunds — a cool capuchin who snapped her fingers instead of scolding — invited Jesse to the Fruitsonian Museum in the city. Jesse floated the whole day. It was the first time anyone had ever taken him anywhere. He stood in front of a painting of a lion for twenty minutes and forgot to be embarrassed about anything.

He came back at sunset to find the troop gathered at the gully, his little sister May Belle crying, and his father's face gray as river stone.

The vine had snapped. Leslie had tried to swing to Treebithia alone, and the old, worn, trusted liana had come apart in her hands.

Jesse spent the worst hours of his life on that bank while the searchers went out with torches. He thought about the river, and the crocodiles, and how he had been off looking at paintings while his best friend fell.

They found her at the bend below the kapok, where the water ran slow and quiet. She had struck her head on the rocks, the adults kept saying, as if that explained anything. Mr. Burke carried her home himself, and Jesse ran until he couldn't, and it didn't matter where to.

He had never known his father could be gentle. But that night Dad picked him up like he was small again and just held on, and Jesse sobbed into his shoulder about museums, of all things, about how he'd been gone, and Dad said, "Son, she had a choice about the vine. Nobody gave you a choice about the river."

The worst part came after, when everyone at school said "how terrible" in soft voices and then went back to their races, and the world just kept going, which felt like a betrayal by everybody in it. He almost hated Leslie for leaving. He almost hated himself for the paints, for the comb, for every single day he had gotten to keep and she didn't.

At the funeral, May Belle tugged his fur. "Is she damned?"

"She's not," Jesse said. "She wasn't even scared of crocodiles. Hell would've sent her back with a note."

And slowly — because it was that or stop — Jesse understood something Leslie had been trying to teach him all along: she had handed him a kingdom, and keeping it was now his job.

So he did the only thing a builder could do. He took poles and lashings and every vine he could trust, and he worked through his grief hand over hand, and he wove a real bridge — solid, tested, wide enough for two-way traffic — and hung it where the old swing had been. Then he went and found May Belle, who had been begging for weeks and who was, when it came down to it, the only other person in the world who believed the kapok was a castle.

"Come on," he said, and he carried her across the bridge into the magic grove.

He set her down on the kapok's great root, stood back, and made the announcement he'd waited his whole life to make.

"Arise," he said, "Queen of Treebithia."

May Belle looked nine years old and royal at the same time, which is a rare trick. Behind them the river ran brown and ordinary, keeping whatever it kept.

And if some evenings, when the wind moved the kapok's highest leaves, Jesse thought he heard a queen laughing on the far bank — well. Kingdoms keep their own secrets.
