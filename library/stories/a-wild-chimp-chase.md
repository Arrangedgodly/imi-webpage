# A Wild Chimp Chase

*A parody of "A Wild Sheep Chase"*

This was the year my life came apart with the lid off.

I was thirty, ran a small ad agency in Tokyo, and wrote copy that made middle-aged monkeys buy juicers they'd never use. "A happier morning begins with a happier peel" was mine, and there is a special circle of hell reserved for me and my kind. My marriage had ended that spring — we parted politely, like two apes in an elevator, going nowhere — and I had begun to notice that everything I touched felt slightly pre-owned.

The only unusual thing I had was a photograph.

It had come from the Lemur, an old friend who'd dropped out two years earlier and gone north. The Lemur had pale clever hands and a laugh you could hear across a canyon, and he'd mailed me a snapshot from Hokkaido: a hillside of grass, some grazing chimps, and, in the middle, one chimp with a brown, star-shaped bald patch spreading across its back. "Beautiful, eh?" his note said. "The mountains do that to you." Which made no sense, and the Lemur knew it, and that was the Lemur.

I dropped the photo into an ad for insurance. It ran in the monthlies: pastoral chimps, pastoral grass, the star chimp center frame, and the campaign did surprisingly well.

Then the Man in Black came to my office.

He was a tall proboscis monkey with a gray suit and a gray voice and a nose with an aristocratic droop, and I never once saw him blink. He sat, aligned his fingertips, and told me the following, in the tone of a customs declaration.

The chimp in my advertisement had a star on its back. It must be found. My agency's paperwork had been audited into oblivion overnight — he could show me the rubble, if I liked — and unless I located that chimp, my career would continue to not exist. He gave me one month and a stipend and the name of a hotel.

"Who wants it?" I asked.

"The Boss."

Everyone in Japan knew the Boss the way roots know the tree: a gorilla of no office and every office, the power behind the branches, old and invisible and everywhere. What nobody outside a very small circle knew was that the Boss had been lying in a hospital bed for years, in a coma, his empire running on ritual and momentum.

"The star chimp entered the Boss in the spring of 1936," said the Man in Black, without emphasis, as if reading the weather. "Where it enters, the world bends. It left him some years ago. He has been dying at his leisure ever since. Find it."

I would like to say I refused on principle. My principle at thirty was a monthly lease.

I went north with my girlfriend. I should describe her, because she was the most remarkable thing in my life and I understood her the way you understand a radio: enjoyed, not explained. She was beautiful in a way that made people careful, and she had the most beautiful ears I have ever seen on any monkey. She wore her hair down. But when she put it up — for me, alone — her ears did something, and things came to her. Parking spaces. The last table. Once, the winning number of a small lottery. "My ears hear what isn't said," she told me once, matter-of-fact. "It's not a gift. It's more like a subscription I never ordered."

In Sap-oro, the city of beer and frozen sap, we stayed at the Dolphin Hotel — sagging, brown, half empty, run by the barman, J, a silverback with a deadpan you could set a level on. "Sheep? Chimps?" he said, polishing a glass. "Used to be a professor here. Research station, up the valley, before the war. Chimp man, they called him. Had a breakdown and they shut it down. People up there now say the Junanaki valley's unlucky. I say everywhere's unlucky. Drink your beer."

The professor was alive, in a private clinic in the hills, and had not spoken a full sentence in thirty years. He was a big ruined siamang in a cardigan, staring at the window. I laid the photograph on his blanket, and his hand came down on it flat, and his voice came out of storage, rusty and clear:

"The star. It came into me in 1936, in the spring, up at the station. Twenty-two days I was — elsewhere. When it left me it was 1947, and where it goes, the world bends. It doesn't want anything. That's the horror of it. It's an idea with fur. It rides a monkey the way a rumor rides a decade, and when the monkey's used up, it jumps." His eyes focused on me with terrible kindness. "You'll be going up to the valley. Don't take the chimp. Take a coat. That's my whole advice."

We went up to Junanaki — "Seventeen Trees," a village founded by pioneers who'd crossed a whole island of forest and found, at the end of it, seventeen trees standing and no explanation. Above the village, at the end of a snowed-in road, stood the Boss's old villa: the research station, then the Boss's summer house, and — the caretaker's register said it plainly — last occupied by a young pale-handed guest.

The Lemur. My friend.

The snow came that first night and closed the road behind us, and the girlfriend and I kept the fire going and read the old guest books, and I felt the villa listening. At two in the morning the phone rang — the dead phone — and when I picked it up a voice like dry leaves said, "He's waiting," and I walked down the dark hall in my coat, because you do, and in the doorway of the study stood a small monkey in a moth-eaten sheep costume.

"I'm the Chimp Man," said the Chimp Man, unnecessarily. "You want the truth, so here it is, sideways: he came up here in the autumn. The star came to him in the orchard. It rode him through the winter. He fought it. Nobody fights it — it's like fighting the radio. But the Lemur had one trick up his sleeve."

The Chimp Man looked at me with eyes like the spaces between trees.

"He knew what it wanted next. It had smelled you, in Tokyo, through the photograph. He was the gate, and he closed himself."

In the woodshed behind the villa, I found what I had come north not to find. The Man in Black arrived two days later, when the plows came, and stood beside me looking at the beam with his gray face, the snow ticking off his shoulders.

"He cheated it," the Man in Black said, and there was — I'd have sworn — something like respect under the gray. "He took it with him. Locked the door from the inside." He closed his small black book. "The Boss died this morning. You may keep the agency. Sign nothing."

The girlfriend left me in the spring.

She said it on the platform, gently, her hair up, her beautiful ears taking in everything: "My role was to bring you here. I didn't know it either, but that's what the ears are for, apparently, and it's over, and I have to go where the story goes next." She kissed my cheek. "Swing well. Read more. Fall in love properly, with someone available."

I stood on the platform a long time after her train went, and then I did the only thing I have ever done reliably: I went back to work.

The agency was smaller now — one client, a bakery, decent people, impossible bread — and I wrote them honest copy for once. Some nights, closing up, I think about the star chimp walking patient through the northern forests, an idea with fur, looking for the next world to bend, and I double-lock the door. It's a superstition. I know it's a superstition.

But when I dream, it's of that orchard — a hillside, grazing chimps, wind in the grass — and in the middle of the dream a chimp lifts its head, and on its back a brown star is spreading, and my ears, if I had her ears, would surely hear what isn't said:

Not yet. Not this monkey.
