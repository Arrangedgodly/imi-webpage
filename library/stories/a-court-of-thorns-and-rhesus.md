# A Court of Thorns and Rhesus

*A parody of "A Court of Thorns and Roses"*

Feyre hunted in the winter forest because her family didn't. Her father's back had been ruined by creditors, her sister Nesta snarled like she was owed a better troop, and Elain — sweet Elain — once wept over a shriveled banana. So Feyre, a lean young rhesus with a bow of blackthorn, tracked a huge grey jaguarundi through the snow, put an ash arrow through its eye, and only then heard it speak.

"You... rhesus," it rasped. "You have no idea... what you have begun."

"We had to eat," said Feyre, and skinned it anyway. Being sorry doesn't fill a cooking pot.

That night something enormous burst through the trees — a great beast with a lion's mane and a mask of living bark grown over its face. Beside it loped a red howler with a copper eye and a smirk.

"You killed one of mine," the masked one said. "A life for a life. Come."

"Or?" said Feyre, nocking an ash arrow with shaking hands.

"Or I let Lucien finish his joke about macaques," said the beast. "There is no third option."

They carried her over the Great Bramble Wall into Primate-ian, where the seven Courts of the Fae had once glittered like a fruit bowl of stars. Now the whole Spring Canopy was blighted — blossoms rotting on the branch. The masked beast, she learned, was Tamlin, High Lord of the Spring Court, and his curse was worn plainly on his face: a mask grown of bark that no blade could cut, as punishment from a tyrant queen.

For answers, Feyre went hunting something stranger than dinner. In the deep brush she snared a Suriel — all legs and ribs and long fingers, with the sigh of a monkey doing taxes.

"Release me and I shall answer three questions truthfully."

"Whose curse is on Tamlin? What is the blight? And how do I get home?"

"Amarantha's," it said. "Amarantha's. And the wall is that way, little rhesus — but you already knew that, or you wouldn't have asked."

"The curse," Feyre pressed, and the Suriel's remaining patience went with it.

"Amarantha gave him a riddle: take a mortal who hates our kind, and make her love him without magic and without telling her why. He solved it badly. She killed his friend for the answer and masked him for it. Until a mortal loves him truly — and says so — the masks stay on. That is all. That is quite enough."

Feyre stayed — officially a prisoner, practically a guest. She painted the walls of her room with jungles. She was schooled in Fae etiquette by Lucien ("don't touch their food, don't say thank you, and *never* ask a red howler if the ear is a war wound"). She swam in hot springs under the blossoms and discovered that High Lords groom better than anyone, and that Tamlin was surprisingly gentle company beneath the mane and the growling.

At Fire Night, when the Spring Court danced around the bonfires, a stranger with midnight fur and eyes like scattered stars cut in: Rhysand, High Lord of the Night Court, feared by everyone. He bowed over her hand.

"Whatever game you're playing," he murmured, "I do hope you win it. I *love* an underdog with paint under her claws."

The Attor came that same night — a vulture beast with abominable manners — and Tamlin's bark-mask cracked with rage as he drove it off. Lucien arrived with a bandage where his smirk should have been, and Tamlin roared at Feyre for wandering, which is how she learned that fae males say "I was frightened" in roar. After that he went grim and quiet, and one morning Feyre woke to find her trunk packed and the border paid for. "Go home," he said, flat as slate. "You'll be safer hating me."

So she went, and was safe, and was miserable, in that order — until Alis, the bristly aye-aye housekeeper, appeared over the wall in the snow with the rest of it: the court dying, the deadline near, the beast lying flat with his crown in the dirt. Feyre looked at her family, who could finally eat. Then she looked at the wall.

She went back over it in a snowstorm. The manor stood shut like a jar. The Attor was waiting.

It carried her Under the Volcano, to a throne room of black rock, where Amarantha, the crimson-furred self-styled High Queen of Primate-ian, sat wearing Tamlin's leash on her little finger.

"A mortal," Amarantha purred. "How quaint. Tell you what, rhesus. Three trials. Complete them, and your beast and his court go free. Fail—" she smiled "—and everyone you've started loving dies."

"She is nothing," said Tamlin, level and cold, and Feyre's heart did not understand him, so the rest of her made a plan.

The first trial was a maze whose doors opened only to riddles carved in stone — *I have a bed but never sleep; I run but never walk* — and a Great Grub-Wyrm that hunted by sound. Feyre solved the doors, deliberately dragged her mud through the maze, blinded the Wyrm with it, and drowned it, reflecting that riddles are just hunting for patient monkeys. The second was a pit with two Fae, one who always lied, one who always spoke truth. Feyre asked one question, concluded that truth was overrated in tyrants' pits, and shot them both with ash arrows, because her mother's old rhyme existed for a reason.

Between trials Amarantha's court broke her arm for entertainment, and Rhysand, playing both courts against the middle, painted her bruises with stardust and kept her standing. The night before each task he pressed his thumb to her palm and left a mark like a small eye there. "Luck," he said, "of the borrowed sort." She scrubbed; it stayed; she resented it; it worked. "Say nothing," he told her, "least of all thank you."

The third was a riddle, delivered while her mended arm burned:

*There are some who find comfort in sorrow, and some who must bleed to feel whole. Kingdoms have burned for my sake, and the coldest heart carries me still. What am I?*

Feyre, bleeding into the black sand, thought of a ruined back and two hungry sisters, of a masked lord grooming her hair by firelight, of an entire blighted court, and she laughed.

"Love," she rasped. "It's love, you rotten peach."

"Wrong," said Amarantha — and broke her neck.

Which was when everything went wrong *for Amarantha*. The seven High Lords — even one with starry eyes — poured their light into the little rhesus who had answered true. Feyre woke immortal, painted, and furious. Tamlin's mask cracked like a nutshell, and his claws ended Amarantha's reign mid-scream.

Later — after the bargain with Rhysand for one week each month, which is a story for a darker court — Feyre stood in the Spring blossoms with her beast.

"I got you killed," said Tamlin.

"You got me *remade*," said Feyre. "Now hush, High Lord. You're interrupting the grooming."

And in a manor on the mortal side, her family ate every day from baskets that arrived without a bill — because High Lords, it turns out, also keep promises.
