# The Swing in the Willows

*A parody of "The Wind in the Willows"*

The Marmoset had been working very hard all the morning, spring-cleaning his little burrow under the roots of the old fig. First with brooms, then with dusters; then on ladders and chairs, with a brush and a pail of whitewash; till he had dust in his throat and eyes, and an aching back and weary arms. Spring was moving in the air above and in the earth below, penetrating even his dark and lowly little home with its spirit of divine discontent and longing. It was small wonder, then, that he suddenly flung down his brush, said "Bother!" and "O blow!" and also "Hang spring-cleaning!" and bolted out of the burrow without even waiting to groom his tail.

He pushed up through the soil, popped his head into the sunshine, and found the whole world ablaze with leaves.

"Hold up!" said an elderly monkey at the riverbank — a comfortable, glossy proboscis with a splendid pot-belly, perched in a little blue boat. "I say, Marmoset, would you like to come along? This is a day of days."

"May I?" breathed the Marmoset.

"Do," said the other, "and mind you tread on the seats."

The Marmoset let his paws trail in the cool current. "I say," he murmured, "what is your name?"

"Ratty," said the proboscis, who swam like an otter and kept a lunch basket packed at all hours. "And this is my river."

"Is it your very own?"

"Believe me, my young friend," said Ratty, lazily reaching for a banana, "there is nothing — absolutely nothing — half so much worth doing as simply messing about in vines. Simply messing: swinging in or out of them, it doesn't matter; that's the charm of it. Whether you get away or whether you don't; whether you arrive at your branch or whether you never get anywhere at all, you're always busy, and you never do anything in particular; and when you've done it there's always something else to do, and you can do it if you like, but you'd better not."

The Marmoset rowed and swung and ate until the stars came out, and on the way home Ratty taught him the two rules of the riverbank: never mention the Ferret, the Stoat, and the Weasel; and never, whatever happens, go into the Wild Wood.

Naturally, the Marmoset went into the Wild Wood. It was autumn by then, the light going pewter, and he had decided to call on the great Badger himself, of whom everyone spoke in whispers and nobody had an address. The trees closed behind him like a door. Whistling faces appeared in the knotholes — pointed, whittled faces with cold eyes — and then came the pattering, all around, of many small feet, and the Marmoset ran, blundered, hid in the hollow of a dead tree, and shook there until Ratty — brave, splendid Ratty, who had found a stick and his temper — came crashing in after him. They could not find the way out. What they found, in the snow, by the lucky chance of a door-scraper, was a badger's door, and behind it the great gorilla himself, in a dressing-gown, in an underground hall hung with portraits of monkeys who had left the trees for the solid earth long ago.

"Come in," rumbled Badger, as if he had been expecting them for a year. "Badger's the name, and this is my hall, and in this house nobody is ever turned away in the snow. Supper's in ten minutes." (The Marmoset learned that night that underground monkeys keep very good Supper.)

It was Ratty who took him, one golden afternoon the following summer, to call on Toad of Banana Hall — the richest monkey on the river — who was in the grip of a brand-new craze. The current craze was the third that year: first the houseboat, then the gipsy cart ("Travel! Change! The open road!" — which had lasted until the cart went into a ditch), and now—

"Motorvines!" cried Toad, boiling out of his front door in goggles and a duster. "The poetry of motion! The only way to travel! Here today, in next week tomorrow! Villas at a clip! Glorious, glorious! Poop-poop!"

They had a bad summer of it after that. Toad crashed seven motorvines in the first month, hospitalizing three of them (the motorvines, that is, and one countess), paid the fines by the sackful, and finally, having taken a fancy to a gleaming red motorvine standing outside an inn, taken it — a detail his lawyers somehow failed to explain away — and was hauled before the magistrate baboons and sentenced to twenty years in the deepest dungeon of the Wild Wood jail for aggravated monkey-larceny, rude driving, and gross impertinence to the constabulary.

"It is the end of the great Toad," said Toad, weeping into his prison blanket — until the jailer's kind young daughter took pity on the silliest monkey in the empire, dressed him in her aunt's washer-monkey shawl and bonnet, and passed him out through the gate with a lecture on ironing which, Toad admitted later, was the finest thing in the whole adventure.

"Toad has escaped!" cried the newspapers, and Toad — still washer-monkeyed, and by now rather enjoying the character — flagged down a railway engine and told the driver, in poshest laundress-voice, a tale of escaped Criminal Toads so thrilling that the driver wept and gave chase to the pursuing constabulary for nothing but the joy of it. They threw him off at the junction with his washing. Then a barge-lady took him aboard, worked him like a washer-monkey for two days, discovered he could not wash (he told her so, honestly, from a great height), and pitched him into the canal; and the gipsy who bought the horse he had been given as compensation was so charmed by Toad's misery that he paid in silver. Toad walked the last miles to the Wild Wood in the dusk of his fortunes, still hungery, still washer-monkeyed, and arrived at last, hollow-eyed, at the door of the great gorilla Badger, deep in the Wild Wood, where he fell upon the supper bananas and told his tale in installments, each one more glorious than the last.

"Never mind all that," rumbled Badger. "The Wild Wood is in arms. The weasels and the stoats have taken Banana Hall. They hold your ancestral vines, and Toad is a monkey without a home."

"MY Hall?" said Toad, rising, for once in his life, quiet. "Then I shall have it back."

So at dawn, through a secret branch-tunnel dug long ago by Toad's great-great-grandmother — a subterranean burrowing monkey of genius — the four friends rose up through a trapdoor into the dining room of Banana Hall, where the weasels were at breakfast, celebrating the birthday of their chief with songs about the Toad they had conquered.

There was a brief and terrible moment: the crash of crockery, the jangle of cutlery, the squealing of weasels; a gorilla like a thunderstorm in the middle of the room; a proboscis monkey with a stick; a small marmoset swinging, shrieking with battle-joy, from the great chandelier; and under the table, forty weasels, begging for mercy and promising to be very good monkeys from now on.

The victory banquet was the talk of the river for months — the merriest barrel of monkeys ever assembled under one thatch. Toad sang his own composition ("THE TOAD! CAME! BACK!") four times, was gently removed from the table twice, and made a speech about his own heroism which the assembled company let stand only because the food was so good.

And Toad — Toad was a changed monkey. He wore no goggles; he claimed no speed records; he sat quietly in his chair, and when a motorvine went by, poop-poop, he only lifted his chin, groomed his tail, and said nothing at all. (The riverbank agreed it was the finest acting anyone had ever seen, and let it be acting, because a reformed Toad who was also quiet was the best Toad there could be.)

And the Marmoset and Ratty went on messing about in vines, quite as before, and found it enough.
