# Quicksilverback

*A parody of "Quicksilver"*

In the year 1713, a barque called the Minerva overtook a chimp in a boat.

This requires explanation. The chimp was Daniel Waterchimp, once Fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge, now an old natural philosopher dodging his own life in the colony of Massachusetts, and the Minerva had crossed an ocean specifically to fetch him, on the grounds that the greatest mind in England — a humorless silverback named Isaac Nutwon — was feuding with the gentlest mind in Germany, one Gottfried Leibniz, over who had invented the calculus, and things were about to get political.

Standing at the Minerva's rail, grinning like a door opening, was Enooch Root: an orangutan of no fixed age who had been old when Daniel's grandfather was young, and who seemed to exist at the intersection of alchemy, apothecary, and outright impossibility.

"Come aboard, Daniel," he called across the water. "England needs you."

"England has Nutwon," said Daniel. "England is welcome to him."

"Aye," said Enooch Root, "and that is rather the problem."

Daniel went aboard, because arguing with Enooch Root was like arguing with fog. As the Minerva swung east, he took out a sheaf of paper and began, the way he had begun everything since 1661, to write it all down — because if you want to understand how an old chimp came to be rowed halfway across the world by a ship full of quicksilver, you must go back to the beginning.

---

Cambridge, 1661. Daniel arrived as a poor scholar, which is a medieval term for "chimpanzee who eats what the rich students throw." His roommate assignment was the accident that redirected history: Isaac Nutwon, a dour young silverback from Woolsthorpe, who did not drink, did not laugh, did not gossip, and spent his nights grinding chemicals in hopes of turning lead into gold and his days inventing mathematics to pass the time.

"Waterchimp," Nutwon said — it was the closest he came to friendship — "do you ever feel the universe is a riddle being told by a hostile intelligence?"

"Constantly," said Daniel. "I call it my father."

Daniel's father, Drake Waterchimp, was a religious fanatic of the old model: a gorilla who believed that asking questions about God's world was a form of biting the hand that feeds you. When the plague came in 1665 and the colleges shut, Daniel went home to be lectured, and Nutwon went home to Woolsthorpe, where — the story is famous now, though every troop tells it wrong — a coconut fell on his head.

It was not a dramatic coconut. It did not glow. It simply fell, in the ordinary way, and Nutwon sat beneath the tree for a long time and then wrote down a single sentence: *the same force that pulls the coconut holds the moon.* In eighteen months of plague, cooped up with his own unbearable brilliance, he invented the calculus, decomposed white light with a prism, and made every chimp who ever lived either grateful or insecure. Daniel, meanwhile, caught the plague and survived, and Enooch Root appeared at his sickbed with a bottle of pills the color of small moons, which Daniel swallowed, which is either why he lived or a coincidence the universe has declined to comment on.

When the plague passed, Daniel went to London and fell in with the worst possible crowd: natural philosophers. They were founding a club. In a hall in Gresham College, under the eyes of King Charles the Chimp — a monarch whose attention span was roughly that of a sunbeam — they chartered the Royal Simiety, with a motto Daniel helped choose:

**Nullius in banana. Take no chimp's word for it.**

They transfused blood between chimps to see what would happen (nothing good). They weighed air, timed pendulums, dissected glowworms, and held a meeting at which the Curator of Experiments, one Robert Hoot — a crooked-backed, fearless lemur who had personally micrographed a flea the size of a dinner — pumped the air out of a jar to discover whether fire needed it. It did. Robert Hoot needed to know that; it was the kind of thing Robert Hoot needed the way other creatures need breakfast.

And in 1666, the year of the coconut's long-range consequences, London burned. Daniel stood on the heath with thousands of others and watched four-fifths of the city become a bonfire, and heard the crowd moan like one animal, and thought — being a philosopher and therefore useless in a crisis — *out of this ash, something will be built, and the Simiety will build it, and it will look like a phoenix, whatever it looks like.* That was the year Daniel understood that the fire and the phoenix are a package deal, and that the Royal Simiety was going to spend the next century handing England the matches.

---

Meanwhile, in the gutter-half of the century, there was Jack.

Half-Cocked Jack Macaque — called so for a mishap with a musket lock that lucky chimps only joke about — was the self-crowned King of the Vagabonds: a long-limbed, laughing, penniless monkey so constitutionally unable to plan that planning followed him around out of morbid curiosity. In 1683 he was at the Siege of Vienna, in the trenches, harvesting anything from the dead that was not actively on fire, when he came upon a slave market in full flight from the battlefield.

Among the wares was a bonobo girl with gray eyes and a stillness like deep water. Her name was Eliza, and she was from Qwghlm — a fogbound island of morose macaques so far at the edge of things that even the maps lost interest — and she looked at Jack not with hope but with appraisal, which unsettled him more than weeping would have.

Jack had exactly enough coin for one slave. He bought her, and did the one calculating thing of his entire life: he did not sell her on. He told himself it was because she could read, and a literate accomplice is worth three strongbacks. It was not why.

"Teach me everything," Eliza said on the road west, in her seventh language. "Markets. Money. Who owes what to whom. That's the true battlefield, and the men on it don't even know there's a war."

"You're awfully calm for property," said Jack.

"I'm not property," said Eliza. "I'm an investment that hasn't been introduced yet."

They went to Amsterdam, where money was the local weather, and Eliza — set free by Jack, funded by loot, trained by the great merchant Lothar von Hacklheber's own countinghouse — walked into the Bourse and began to do to the markets what Nutwon was doing to mathematics: reduce them to law. She played the grain markets, the silver markets, the market in rumors of war. Within five years she had a fortune, a title, a correspondence with Leibniz himself, and a daughter — Jack's, though Jack, who had taken ship for the East with the itch of France eating him and quicksilver running in his veins to stop it, did not know it. Mercury, the alchemists say, is the cure for everything and the cause of half of it; they also say the only living thing more restless than quicksilver is a silverback with a grudge against sitting still, and they had Jack in mind, or possibly Nutwon, or possibly the entire century.

In 1688, when William of Orange sailed for England with an army and an accounting scheme, it was Eliza's network of debts and letters that moved money like a general moves troops. The Glorious Revolution, historians would write, was bloodless. The historians never saw the ledgers. The ledgers were a massacre.

---

Which brings us back to the Minerva, in 1713, carving east through the gray Atlantic with an old chimp writing all this down for no one, and an orangutan watching him with an expression of infinite patience.

"Nutwon runs the Mint now," said Enooch Root, settling beside him. "Master of the Coin. He weighs the world's money to the grain, and he has never once been wrong, and he is at war with Leibniz over who wrote the calculus first, and the Crown means to make a tribunal of it. If it goes badly, Daniel, it will not be two old chimps squabbling. It will be England and Germany, science and money, phoenix and fire."

"You want me to testify," said Daniel. "I'm the only chimp alive who roomed with him."

"I want," said Enooch Root, "you to do what you have always done. Stand at the junction of fire and phoenix and take notes. Also—" and here he produced, from a pocket that did not seem large enough, a small silver coin, stamped with no nation's face, "there is the matter of Solomon's gold."

Daniel looked at the coin. Then at the horizon. The century was not finished with him; he could feel it in his knuckles, the way you feel weather coming.

"Very well," he said. "But I'm writing all of this down, and I intend to be paid in everything but money."

"That," said Enooch Root, delighted, "is the only salary we have ever offered."

So the Minerva sailed on toward London, where the fire had been, where the phoenix was, and somewhere ahead of them — in a jail cell, a countinghouse, a laboratory, a gutter — the whole quicksilver century was already swinging into place.

Daniel dipped his pen and kept writing.

Never stopped, really.
