# Antony and Chimpopatra

*A parody of "Antony and Cleopatra"*

In Alexandria, the great gorilla general Antony — triumvir of Rome, terror of battlefields, breaker of sieges — was behaving like a lovestruck lemur.

"Nay, this dotage of our general's o'erflows the measure," grumbled Philo, watching his commander drape himself across a cushion. "Those goodly eyes, that o'er the files and musters of the war have glowed like plated Mars — now bend, now turn the office and devotion of their view upon a tawny chimp!"

Chimpopatra, Queen of Egypt, lay in a hammock of woven grass, being fed figs by servants. When Antony approached, she turned her head with the air of one doing the Nile a favor.

"If it be love indeed, tell me how much."

"There's beggary in the love that can be reckoned."

"I'll set a bourn how far to be beloved."

"Then thou must needs find out new heaven, new earth."

"That's the most banana-flavored thing anyone has ever said to me," said Chimpopatra, pleased. "Say it again."

Enobarbus, Antony's dry lieutenant, muttered to Philo: "He's her fool. When she first met him, she sailed up the river Cydnus in a gilded barge — the barge she sat in, like a burnished throne, burned on the water. Purple sails, silver oars, flute-players keeping time to the waves. And Chimpopatra herself, making defect perfection — no longer a mere queen; she outdid nature. I'll say it now, drunk or sober, to all the world: age cannot wither her, nor custom stale her infinite variety. Other monkeys cloy the appetites they feed, but she makes hungry where most she satisfies."

News from Rome kept arriving like stones through a window: his wife dead, his brother in rebellion, Pompey's fleet gathering, Octavius Caesar furious. "Let Rome in Tiber melt," said Antony, "and the wide arch of the ranged empire fall! Here is my space. Kingdoms are clay. But here, in Egypt, we feast on figs."

Duty — and Caesar's cold, furious letters — dragged him back to Rome, where the fix was political: he married Octavia, Caesar's sister. Asked whether the marriage would hold, Enobarbus snorted. "Antony will to his Egyptian dish again. He will to her he loves best — and that's Chimpopatra, who has power over him still, though he abandon grace, virtue, and the whole troop of Rome itself."

The triumvirs sealed the peace aboard Pompey's galley, and fig-wine flowed until the decks swam. Pompey's captain Menas leaned in mid-party and murmured: "Sire — I have you all aboard one ship. Say the word, and I cut the cable, and you are lord of the whole canopy."

"O," sighed Pompey, gesturing at the three drunken rulers of the world, "this thou shouldst have done, and not have spoke on't! Reproach me not with what I cannot help. If the doing were lawful, and the doing seen — but for it to be done and not discussed: that is a deed I must praise and wish undone, forever."

"Then," said Menas, refilling his cup, "I shall wish it done for both of us."

There was one other thing in Rome: a soothsayer, an old grey loris who told fortunes by the dregs of fig-wine. "O Antony," he said, "leave Rome. Caesar's guardian spirit is fed by yours. If you part from him, your fame grows; if you stay, you are his ape — his shadow, his second fiddle, his fig-peeler. I make not the laws; I only read them."

"Then I will make of fortune a fair paw," said Antony — and went east again, sending Octavia ahead to negotiate peace, which is not what a husband's departure looks like in any history ever written.

War came, and chose its ground at sea — Actium — because Chimpopatra insisted her fleet be there. The night before the battle, the troops sat on watch with Antony and heard, under the earth, a strain of flute-music, swelling and fading.

"Hark! The music i' the air — no, under the ground."

"'Tis the god Hercules, whom Antony loved, now leaves him," said a soldier quietly.

"Now he leaves him," said the others, and drew their cloaks closer, because a god's goodbye is not something any ape argues with.

They were right. Mid-battle, the wind shifted, and Chimpopatra's sixty barges turned tail and fled.

"Your emperor continues to be a fool," cried Canidius — for Antony, seeing her flee, abandoned his own fleet, his own honor, his own almost-victory, and sailed after her. "Never was a general so publicly, so catastrophically in love."

"I have fled myself," Antony admitted to her. "I am a runner from my own good name. O, whither hast thou led me, Egypt?"

She only had to weep and offer her lips, and he forgave her. "Fall not a tear, I say; one of them rates above all my victories." Which was, of course, the whole problem.

The end came in pieces. Enobarbus, one betrayal too many, deserted to Caesar's camp — then learned that Antony, knowing of it, had sent his treasure after him with gentle words. "O Antony! Nobler than my revolt is infamous. I have done ill." He died of shame under a broken fig-tree, crying out for "a moon and stars" of his own, Antony's name on his lips.

Envoys came and went like flies. Caesar's man Thyreus was soundly whipped for kissing her hand too long; Chimpopatra's answer to Rome was a list of her possessions that ran to "one fig-grove, several rivers, and whatever the crocodiles are keeping safe for me." Then her own treasurer Seleucus betrayed her in front of Caesar's clerk, groaning that the list was short a fortune. "O slave, of no more trust than love that's hired! What, gone without a word?" — and Caesar had to smooth it over, oily as balm, promising everything: "You shall find yourself honored, madam. Truly."

"His words are a physician's," she told her women when he'd gone, "gentle, and full of comfort. Which is why I trust them not at all." Dolabella, the kinder Roman, told her plainly that Caesar meant to lead her in his triumph through Rome. "Madam, as sure as the Nile rises — he will."

"Shall they hoist me up," she said, "and show me to the shouting monkeys of Rome? Nay, I shall be played at leap-frog with the crocodiles first."

Beaten at last, and deceived by a false report of Chimpopatra's death, Antony fell upon his sword — botched even that — and was carried, dripping and dignified, up to her monument, where he died in her arms among the boughs.

"I am dying, Egypt, dying. Give me some wine, and let me speak a little."

"Had I great Juno's power, the strong-pawed Antony should live," wept Chimpopatra. Then, to her women: "You laugh, girls? You had a leader, and I a queen — and now our stage is done. No more, but e'en a woman."

Caesar came, smooth and sympathetic, promising honor. Chimpopatra bowed her head and agreed to everything — then went home and did exactly as she pleased. First she prepared, carefully: her crown robes, her asps in a basket of figs, her ladies sworn. When a rustic monkey arrived with the basket — "here is a fig of a very fine worm" — she knew the delivery was right.

"Remember, girls: the stroke of death is as a lover's pinch, which hurts and is desired. Let's do it after the high Roman fashion, and make death proud to take us."

Dressed in her crown robes, with Charmian and Iras beside her, she took the pretty little serpent of the fig-tree — "that kills and pains not" — and let it bite her arm. Caesar arrived too late, and stood in silence before the smallness of what was left.

"Now boast thee, death, in thy possession lies a lass unparalleled. She shall be buried by her Antony. No grave upon the earth shall clip in it a pair so famous."

And such, said the Egyptian monkeys ever after, is the price of being the most glamorous queen in the canopy: even your enemies end up writing your eulogy.
