# Hard Climbs

*A parody of "Hard Times"*

"Now, what I want is Facts. Teach these boys and girls nothing but Facts. Facts alone are wanted in life. Plant nothing else, and root out everything else. Nothing else will ever be of any service to them. Stick to Facts, sir!"

Thus spake Mr. Gradgrind — a big, square, cannon of a baboon — to the pupils of the jungle school in red-brick Coketown, where the serpents of smoke trailed themselves for ever and ever, and the pistons of the fruit-packing works worked monotonously up and down.

"Girl number twenty," said Mr. Gradgrind, pointing.

Sissy Jupe, a circus chimp with flowers woven in her fur, stood up. "Sissy, sir. It's my father's name."

"Then we have no more to do with her father. Give me your definition of a horse."

Sissy, who had loved horses all her short circus life, was thrown into the greatest alarm. "Quadruped. Graminivorous. Forty teeth..." She could not do it.

"Girl number twenty unable to define a horse! Bitzer, your definition."

Bitzer — a pale, cold, light-eyed young chimp raised entirely on Facts — recited it perfectly: quadruped, graminivorous, forty teeth — grinders, eye-teeth, incisives — and so on through sheds and hoofs, until even Mr. Gradgrind's cannon-mouth was satisfied.

"Now, girl number twenty," said Mr. Gradgrind, "you know what a horse is."

Mr. Gradgrind's closest political friend was Mr. Josiah Bounderby, a bullying red-faced gorilla who owned the fruit-packing works.

"I was born in a ditch!" Bounderby roared. "Abandoned by my mother! Left to climb my own tree! And now look at me!"

He said this so often that the ditch itself might have sued for defamation. In his house lived Mrs. Sparsit, an elderly ape of great family, with a Coriolanian nose and eyebrows like two black caterpillars on a corporate march, who kept his keys.

When Sissy's father ran off and left her — a circus performer losing his grip, in every sense — Mr. Gradgrind took her into his own house as an act of pure statistics.

Mr. Gradgrind had five cubs of his own, who had never seen a banana that wasn't measured, or a story that wasn't a lesson. His daughter Louisa was a solemn young ape with a hungry curiosity that had never once been fed. His son Tom — "the whelp" — was a sullen young baboon. They were caught one night peeping at Sleary's Circus through the fence — the very circus where Sissy's father had ridden — and dragged home.

"Louisa," said her father, "never wonder." That was the whole of his philosophy.

At the circus gate, old Mr. Sleary — a ringmaster chimpanzee with one fixed eye — delivered the counter-philosophy as they left. "People mutht be amuthed, thir. They can't be alwayth a-learning, nor yet they can't be alwayth a-working. They mutht be amuthed. Thee how it ith." Mr. Gradgrind walked away at the word "amuthed," but the word stayed behind, and waited.

Louisa, grown up, was married at twenty to Bounderby's fifty — with her father's blessing, since Bounderby's fortune was a Fact in her favor.

"Does it take a year to choose a husband, father?" said Louisa. "I have chosen."

Hollow-eyed, she said yes as though reading it off a chalkboard. Tom urged the match on (the whelp's keep at the Bounderby trough being his price). The wedding breakfast was gruel.

The working chimps were called "the Hands," as though they were nothing but the paws that did the work. One of them, a stubborn, brooding ape named Stephen Blackpool, loved the gentle Rachael but was chained to a wretched, drunken mate. He asked Bounderby how a poor ape might be free of her, and was told it was impossible — and that asking questions was seditious monkey business.

"'Tis a muddle," said Stephen, shaking his head. "All a muddle. The whole jungle's a muddle."

Then came the strike. Stephen, refusing to join the union and refusing to betray it to Bounderby, was cast out by every ape in Coketown — by the Hands for saying a fair word for the master, and by the master for saying a fair word for the Hands.

"There is a good time comin', loovely dears, when the 'ollowed-out monkeys and the filled-up monkeys will be as one troop in the sight o' heaven. I ha' fell into th' pit — but look yonder. Look up!"

Before he left town, a strange thing happened: young Tom Gradgrind whistled Stephen into a dark corner and asked him to loiter an hour or two outside Bounderby's bank on his way — just to confuse the timing, harmless, nothing to it.

Into Coketown came James Harthouse — a languid young ape with a lazy smile, who had tried everything in life and found it all equally dull, and who therefore took up the hard-Fact school as a new toy. He saw Louisa at her marble height and made it his amusement to reach her.

"You have trained yourself out of all your natural instincts," he told her. "It is the only thing about you I find interesting."

"Then you are easily interested, sir."

Mrs. Sparsit, turned out of Bounderby's house when he married, took up her post at the bank, where she passed her days imagining a great marble staircase in the sky, down which Louisa Bounderby would one day come, rushing to ruin — Mrs. Sparsit counting every step. "Come down, ma'am!" she murmured privately. "Come down!"

One night, in thunder and wind, Louisa came near to Harthouse — past caring, believing ruin was simply her next scheduled station. And then, instead, she fled — through the rain to her father's study, where she fell down before him.

"I never had a child's belief or a child's fear. You have been so careful of me, father, that I never had a child's heart. All within me was left unfinished, and I am what you made me."

And Mr. Gradgrind bowed his head, knowing at last that there is a Wisdom of the Head, and a Wisdom of the Heart, and he had given his cubs only the first.

Harthouse, waiting smugly in town for his ruin-romance, was met instead by Sissy Jupe, who took his arm in the dark and simply told him it was all over, that he had never cared and never would, and that he must go away. And he went — packed that same night, musing forever afterward that he had been done, utterly done, by a circus chimp with flowers in her fur.

Then came the bank robbery. Tom, deep in gambling debts, had robbed Bounderby's bank and let suspicion fall on Stephen, knowing Stephen had been seen loitering — at Tom's own whistled request. Old Stephen, walking back across the moors by night to clear his name, fell into the Old Shaft and lay dying in the dark until they raised him into the light.

"I ha' no wrath for no man," said Stephen, holding Rachael's paw. "My trouble is that the poor will always be with us, unless some monkey of a better kind teaches the rich to wonder."

And Bitzer? Cold Bitzer, pure Fact Bitzer, tracked down the fleeing Tom — hidden at Sleary's circus, painted for the ring — and arrested him.

"Bitzer," said Gradgrind, white to the muzzle, "have you a heart?"

"The circulation, sir," returned Bitzer, smiling, "is carried on by the pump. I was trained at your school. I'll have Tom's place at the bank."

And Mr. Gradgrind looked upon the perfect fruit of his system and saw that it was a monkey with no heart at all.

Sleary, however, had one last trick: while Bitzer argued, a circus pony walked slowly and deliberately across the yard until Bitzer found himself danced out of all influence — and Tom was bundled off to the New World by way of the ring. He sent home, years later, one letter saying he was sorry, signed with no name.

Then came the last accounting. Bounderby was confronted at the bank by a plain old she-chimp in a country cap: Mrs. Pegler, his mother — alive, tender, proud of him, kept away all those years on a small pension and a promise so that he could preach his ditch-parable.

"Josiah, my dear boy! I have kept away, as you bade me — it was your doing, not mine! You were born in a neat cottage, and raised on love and plum jam! I never deserted you, my jewel!"

Bounderby's myth exploded in the very marketplace where it had so often been fired. Mrs. Sparsit's eyebrows rose in a private salute. He died later of a fit in the Coketown street, and his wealth went mostly to the lawyers, who at least never asked where he was born.

Mr. Gradgrind spent his remaining years making Facts the servants of Faith, Hope, and Charity — and no ape was slower to learn them, or prouder when he did. He made his large facts small, and passed them round like a plate of little cakes. Sissy Jupe grew into the merriest ape in Coketown, with a troop of children who were never once called "cub number twenty." Louisa lived in her gentler shadow, taught to wonder.

Dear reader! Never wonder — the one commandment Coketown got wrong. Unless you would rather be an ape with a heart — in which case, wonder away, and let the Facts take care of the peeling.
