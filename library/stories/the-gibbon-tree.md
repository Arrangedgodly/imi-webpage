# The Gibbon Tree

*A parody of "The Giving Tree"*

Once there was a tree, and she was called the Gibbon Tree, because her branches grew in long, looping silver arcs that dipped and rose like the paths of happy songs. Her figs were sweet, her shade was deep, and she loved a little gibbon.

And every day the little gibbon would come. He would gather her leaves and twine them into crowns and play King of the Canopy, decreeing that all falling figs were his loyal subjects. He would swing out along her longest branch, drop with a wild "ooh-ooh!", climb back up, and swing again, and again, until his hands were sticky and his laugh was gone hoarse. He would play hide-and-seek with the sun among her leaves, and lose on purpose, because hiding from the sun in a friend's leaves is the safest hiding there is. And when he was tired, he would sleep in her forked elbow, and the tree would hold her branches very still, so as not to wake him.

"Tell me a story," the gibbon would say, already half asleep.

"I only know one story," the tree would say. "It is about a little gibbon, and everything is all right in the end."

"That's a short one," the gibbon would murmur.

"Most of the good ones are," said the tree.

And the tree was happy.

But time went by, as it does in jungles, quietly and then all at once. The little gibbon grew long and restless, and he came less, and then hardly at all, and the tree spent her days counting the raindrops and eavesdropping on the parrots, who were not good company but were better than nobody.

One day the gibbon came and sat on her roots with a sulky face, and the tree rustled all over, the way a heart beats faster, and said, "Come, gibbon, come and swing and be happy."

"I am too big to swing on you," said the gibbon, not looking up. "I want to buy things at the bazaar. I want baubles and bright feathers and a mirror-polished gourd like the one Kiko has. Can you give me bananas?"

"I have no bananas," said the tree. "Bananas are for banana trees, and I have never been sorry about it until this very minute. But I have figs. Take my figs, gibbon, and sell them in the market. Then you will have baubles, and you will be happy."

And so the gibbon climbed her highest branches and stripped every last fig and carried them away in baskets, and the market monkeys said the figs were the sweetest in the jungle, and the gibbon came home with a mirror-polished gourd and a feather and no figs at all, and the tree stood all that season with her arms empty.

And the tree was happy. But not really.

And after a long time the gibbon came back, and the tree shook with joy, dropping two dry leaves and one whole green one in her excitement. "Come, gibbon, come and swing and be happy."

"I am too busy to swing," said the gibbon. "I want a nest of my own. I want a prime mate and little gibbons swinging all around me, the way it says in the songs. Can you help me?"

"I have no baubles," said the tree, "and no gourds and no feathers. But you may cut off my branches and build your nest. Then you will be happy."

And the gibbon looked at her branches for a long moment — at the long, looping silver arcs that dipped and rose like the paths of happy songs — and then he went and got his knife. And he lopped off her branches, great looping armfuls of them, and carried them off to build his nest in a taller tree, on a higher hill, where — he said — the mornings came earlier.

And the tree was happy. But not really.

And after a long time the gibbon came back again, and the tree was so glad she could hardly speak. "Come, gibbon," she whispered, "come and swing and be happy. I am not much for swinging anymore, but you could sit, and I could lean."

"I am too old and sad to swing," said the gibbon, who was gray now, and his hands were cross with him. "I want a boat that will take me far away. I want to branch out — past the big river and the blue mountains. The jungle is too quiet, and the quiet is full of things I don't want to think about. Can you give me a boat?"

"Cut down my trunk and make a canoe," said the tree. "Then you can paddle far away and be happy."

And so the gibbon cut down her trunk, and hollowed it out, and the wood was fragrant, and he did not say anything while he worked, and neither did she, because there was nothing either of them could say that the knife wasn't already saying. And he paddled away down the river, past the big river and the blue mountains, until the canoe was a speck, and then a rumor, and then not anything at all.

And the tree was happy. But not really.

After a long, long time — so long that vines grew over the stump and parrots nested in the ferns around it, and quarreled there, and raised their parrot children there, and those children raised children there — the gibbon came back at last.

"I am sorry, gibbon," said the tree, and her voice was older, and gentler, and still hers. "But I have nothing left to give you. My figs are gone. My branches are gone. My trunk is gone. You cannot swing on me, for I am only a stump. I am sorry..."

"I don't need much now," said the gibbon, who had gray fur and slow hands and a heart that had swung around the whole world and back again, and had found that the world, on inspection, was mostly other trees. "Just a quiet place to sit and rest. I am very tired."

"Well then," said the tree, straightening up as much as a stump can, which is not very much, but is something, "an old stump is good for sitting and resting. Come, gibbon, sit down. Sit and rest."

And the old gibbon sat down on the stump, and it held him, and held him well, and the tree was happy.

The whole jungle, which had watched her give everything away — the parrots, the ferns, the fig-borrowing market monkeys, the wind, the rain that had known her longest of all — leaned in close with its leaves and its long silver light. And the gibbon patted her bark gently with one slow hand, the way you pat a friend who never once said no, even the times she should have.

"You know," he said at last, "I told stories all the way around the world. Storms and rivers and blue mountains. And do you know what I told them about, mostly?"

"No," said the tree.

"About a tree," said the gibbon. "And everything was all right in the end. It's a short one. Most of the good ones are."

And the tree was quiet for a while, in the way of trees that are being happy without admitting it.

"You know," he said at last, "of all the trees in all the world, you were the top banana."

And the tree was happy.
