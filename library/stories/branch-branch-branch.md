# Branch Branch Branch

*A parody of "Dance Dance Dance"*

The Howler Hotel stood on the same corner where the old Dolphin Hotel used to rot, except that it was now twenty-six floors of glass and marble, with a lobby fountain, a jazz bar, and staff who greeted you in two languages before you'd finished revolving through the door.

I had come back to Sapporo to find a promise. Six years ago, in the old crumbling Dolphin, something had happened to me in a room on a floor that was no longer numbered, and I had been walking in circles ever since, writing restaurant reviews and ad copy — "Rich, full-bodied, the taste of a better jungle" was mine, and I'm not proud, or not very — and calling it a life. I do the dishes. I feed the cat. I iron my shirts, mostly the collars. But the surfaces only stretch so far.

"There must be some mistake," I told the front desk. "I'd like a room."

"Of course, sir. The Dolphin Suite is available."

"The what?"

"It's our smallest room. Oddly, many guests request it. It's on the mezzanine." The clerk smiled the smile of a hotel that knows exactly what it is and is paid not to say.

Sometime after midnight, unable to sleep, I rode the elevator without pressing any button, and it stopped between the fifteenth and sixteenth floors, in a corridor that did not appear on the fire map, smelling of old wood and warm fur. At the end of it, under a bare bulb, sat a small monkey in a moth-eaten sheep costume.

"You came back," the Chimp Man said. His voice was the sound of dry leaves agreeing with each other. "Good. You gotta keep connected. That's the whole thing. Connected."

"I've been busy."

"Busy's what they give you instead of a life. Listen: it's simple and it isn't. You keep swinging. As long as the music plays, you swing. Branch, branch, branch. Don't stop to grade yourself. The tree doesn't grade you. The tree just holds."

"And if the music stops?"

"Then you stop. But it hasn't stopped." He leaned forward, wool unravelling at the elbow. "You got questions. The woman. The beach. You seen her."

I had. Kiki. Three weeks earlier, at 2 a.m., a movie on TV, an old B-film I'd seen twice before — and there, in a hotel-lobby scene that had never contained her, stood Kiki, the beautiful woman with ears like seashells who had once led me down the corridor of the old Dolphin and then walked out of the world. In the film she turned from the desk, looked straight into the camera — straight down the years, straight into my untidy apartment — and moved her lips. No sound. Then the scene cut to a man buying a newspaper, and she was gone.

"You find things," I told the Chimp Man. "That's your reputation."

"Findings easy. Understanding's extra." He pointed down the corridor with one woolly arm. "Door at the end. Go on. Visit."

The door opened on a hotel room: dim, warm, a kettle just gone cold. Six chairs in a ring, and in them six skeletons, seated politely, hands folded, as if waiting for a conference to begin. The tea in front of each had been cold for years. On the wall, a print of a shore. And standing in the window, the light of a different latitude on her face, Kiki.

"You're connected," she said, in the voice you hear at the bottom of a shell. "That's why you can come here, and why you can't stay. Somebody's got to carry it, and you're the one wired in. It costs. It's also the only thing there is." She looked at the six patient skeletons with something close to tenderness. "Everybody ends up in somebody's memory. The lucky ones get kept warm."

"Whose are they?"

But the kettle ticked as it cooled, and the room folded up like a letter, and I was standing in a linen closet on sixteen, holding a folded towel.

—

In the mornings I worked. I was in Sapporo to write a magazine piece, and my editor, a chimpanzee of appetites, had also arranged a car: the actor Gotanda's Maserati, of all things — Gotanda being an old classmate who was now famous, whose face sold sake on billboards from here to Sendai, and who had a deal with the dealership. We drove the coast road together, doing ninety in his silver toy, two not-young monkeys with nice collars.

"I saw a film last month," I said, casually. "There was a woman in it. A call girl, maybe. She vanished from a scene she was never shot in."

Gotanda watched the road. Gotanda had divorced his wife that spring, and had been performing himself so successfully for so long that he had lately begun taking phone calls in the third person.

"I've done things," he said, to the windshield. "Or I dreamt them, and the dreams are indistinguishable from the doing, which, for an actor, is a professional hazard with no insurance. If I told you one, you'd have to keep it. That's not a threat. It's arithmetic."

"Keep dancing," I said. "The music's still playing. That's the best I've got."

"It's garbage advice," said Gotanda, and then, quietly, "and it's also the only advice," and he opened the car up along the cliffs until even his sadness had to hold on.

—

There was a girl, too, that winter: Yuki, thirteen, a rare white colobus with a deadpan delivery lethal at forty feet, whom I'd met in Hokkaido through her disaster of a family — Ame, her father, a photographer who specialized in photographing things just after they left, and Rikka, her mother, a novelist famous for books about not being able to write. Yuki's mother's beach house sat above a wintering sea, and I stood on the terrace with Yuki while the waves came in like the numbered days of some ancient calendar.

"Your faces," Yuki said, sipping a hot grapefruit drink. "Adults. You look like everybody's haunting you."

"Everybody's what?"

"Nothing. Forget it. Buy me a decent hamburger and I'll say you have potential."

At the station, leaving, she said — eyes elsewhere, as thirteen requires — "That woman you're looking for. I think she was in that film because somebody had to be in it. Somebody's always in it. That's how memory works. Now wave goodbye like a normal person."

—

The Chimp Man was gone my last night. I rode the elevator to the unlisted floor and found only corridor, bulb, and a faint smell of wool. On the wallpaper where he'd sat, if you squinted, a patch slightly newer than the rest, the shape of a small seated monkey.

I stood there a long minute with my hand on the wall.

"Connected," I said out loud, feeling ridiculous, and not.

In the morning I flew back to Tokyo, and the world resumed its usually scheduled programming. I wrote my piece. I called Gotanda once a month; the calls got easier, then shorter, then real. I fed the cat, ironed a collar, learned one good recipe. Some months the music is thin, monkey business of the cheapest kind. You swing anyway. Branch, branch, branch.

Then, one evening in February, the phone rang in my apartment, and the voice on the line was thirteen and white-furred and unimpressed by distance.

"Hey."

"Hey yourself," I said. "You're supposed to be in Sapporo."

"I am in Sapporo, idiot. It's cold. The vending machines freeze and everything stops. I have to buy gloves. I'm calling because you sounded like a hamburger emergency last time."

"Yuki," I said, "you have no idea how glad I am to hear from you."

"Obviously." But she stayed on the line, and the line hummed, the way lines do, connected. Outside the window, Tokyo went on glittering, millions of monkeys in millions of rooms, each holding somebody warm.

"So," she said. "Are you dancing or not?"

I looked around my tidy apartment at the ironed shirts and the fed cat and the blinking machine. "Dancing," I said.

"Good," said Yuki, and hung up, the way Yuki does, and I stood there a long time holding the receiver, listening to nothing — connected, connected, connected.
