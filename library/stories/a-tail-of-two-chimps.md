# A Tail of Two Chimps

*A parody of "A Tale of Two Cities"*

It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the season of bananas, it was the season of empty peels — in short, the period was so far like the present period that some of its noisiest authorities insisted on its being received, for good or for evil, in the superlative degree of comparison only. Also, there were two chimps, and this is their tail.

In the year 1775, in the fog above Dover, a message traveled by vine post: RECALLED TO LIFE. Jerry Cruncher, a russet-furred odd-job chimp and secret midnight "resurrection man" (he dug up freshly buried apes and sold them to science, and called the practice fishing), delivered it to Tellson's Bank — for Doctor Manette, a broken old chimp unjustly imprisoned eighteen years in the Bastille-fortress of the Paris Troop, had been freed, and his daughter Lucie, a golden-hearted young monkey, was sent to receive him.

They found him in a garret above a wine-shop, hunched at a cobbler's bench, making shoes with trembling hands.

"Father! You have a daughter!" Lucie cried, gathering his gray head to her golden fur. "You are not alone!"

And the old chimp wept into her hair, recalled to life. (The hair, which she wound about her neck like a golden thread, became the thing he clung to in every storm after.)

In London, a young French exile named Charles Darnay stood trial for treason — and was saved, absurdly, when his defense called attention to a dissolute, heavy-lidded lawyer slouching in the courtroom: Sydney Carton, so exactly Darnay's double that no witness could swear which chimp was which. Darnay walked free; Carton walked to the tavern, where he lived.

For Carton was the other chimp of our tail — brilliant, dissolute, self-loathing, drinking spiked nectar by the gourd, in love with Lucie from the moment he saw her, and calling himself a disappointed drifter who was never going to climb anywhere.

"I am a chimp who has wandered and struggled and failed," he told her once, "careless and despairing and swift to follow. But for you, and for any dear to you, I would do anything. Think now and then that there is a chimp who would give his life, to keep a life you love beside you."

Lucie married Darnay, who had renounced his cruel inheritance — for Darnay was truly an Evrémonde, nephew of the Marquis St. Evrémonde, a monkey who ran over peasant children with his fruit-cart and tossed a banana out the window as compensation. ("It is extraordinary to me," said the Marquis, "that you people cannot take care of yourselves and your children. One or the other of you is always in the under-canopy.") That night the Marquis went to sleep in his château and woke up dead — a knife through his heart, a note pinned to his chest: DRIVE HIM FAST TO HIS GRAVE. THIS, FROM JACQUES.

In Paris, at the wine-shop of the Defarges, the revolution ripened like storm fruit. Madame Defarge — a cold-eyed female with a toothpick of doom — sat knitting, always knitting, her needles clicking the names of the doomed into her patterns.

"Knitted in her own stitches," murmured her husband, "the register of all the traitors to the People. Vengeance, Vengeance, Vengeance."

"There is no time to lose," said the third Jacques, a woolly macaque. "The Bastille falls today."

It fell. And when Defarge plunged into Cell One Hundred and Five, North Tower, he found not a prisoner but a paper — Doctor Manette's own hidden account of why he had been imprisoned: how, long ago, two Evrémonde brothers had destroyed a young female and her brother, and how the doctor had been buried alive for knowing it. The female had a sister. The sister had escaped. The sister knits, madame.

Madame Defarge was that sister. This was not politics anymore. This was family.

In 1792 Darnay received a pitiful letter from a faithful old servant of the Evrémondes, condemned for no crime but his master's name. "He shall not perish if I can help it," said noble, foolish Darnay — and sailed at once for Paris, where the revolution seized him instantly and lodged him in La Force. Recalled to life? Buried to death, more like.

Doctor Manette, heroic in his freedom, marched into Paris with Lucie — and his eighteen years in the Bastille made him, of all chimps, a saint of the revolution. His word freed Darnay at the first trial. They carried the doctor home on shoulders, weeping with joy.

That same night, four soldiers came back. Darnay was re-arrested — denounced by three voices: the Defarges, and one other. At the second trial, the third voice came from the dock itself: Doctor Manette — no, not by his will, but by his own buried paper, read aloud to a silent court, shrieking its conclusion: THE EVRÉMONDES, THEIR RACE, TO THE LAST OF IT. Condemned. Twenty-four hours.

Lucie fainted into the street; her father's mind broke again, and he sat in a corner making shoes that fit no one.

But Sydney Carton had wandered into the wine-shop that day, and overheard Madame Defarge's final plan: that Lucie, and her child, and her father — the whole line, the whole household — must also be denominated, to the last hair. Carton walked the wet stones all night with a candle of memory burning in him, and he made two purchases with the last of his money: a little vial from a chemist, and a promise from John Barsad, a spy whose double-dealing Carton happened to hold proof of.

He went to the condemned cell.

"I am a chimp who has wandered and struggled and failed," he said, gripping Darnay's hand as the potion took hold. "But I have one life, and I am about to spend it. You never knew how much you were loved, dear Darnay — go, and tell her, some far-off day, how deep and true the pity of my heart was for you both. Go. Your family waits. And remember: it is a far, far better tail that I swing than I have ever swung; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

They carried Darnay, senseless, out in Carton's place; and Carton, with his hair already snipped to match, sat down to wait for dawn.

Madame Defarge came for Lucie that same hour — to denounce the whole line — but found, instead, Miss Pross: Lucie's fierce, devoted, deaf-as-a-post English governess, blocking the doorway with her whole small body.

"I am a Bri-ton," said Miss Pross, to a monkey who could not hear a word of it. "A true-born Bri-ton, and we never give ground, not for any furry foreigner that ever was born!"

They struggled — knitting needles against fingernails, vengeance against a governess — and Madame Defarge, tangled in her own yarn, went for the pistol in her breast, and it went off on the instant of struggle, and struck no one but herself. The revolution had eaten its own children with the appetite of a starving gorilla. Miss Pross, deaf forever, held the victory and never once heard it.

And in the tumbril, rolling toward the Great Peeler — the terrible machine that stripped the fur and pride from aristochimps with one awful squeeze — through a jeering crowd, sat Sydney Carton, serene as a monkey who has finally found his branch. A young seamstress-lemur clung to his hand.

"I am not afraid, if I may hold your hand. Are you dying for him?"

"And his wife and child. Hush! Yes."

"If I may ride with you, will you let me hold yours? I am a poor little seamstress, and you are so brave."

"Brave at last," said Carton, and smiled.

The Peeler fell. The crowd roared. And in the hush after, it seemed the whole troop of Paris heard him:

"I see a beautiful city and a brilliant troop rising from this abyss. I see the lives for which I lay down my life, peaceful, useful, prosperous and happy. I see her, an old female, weeping for me on the anniversary of this day. I see that I hold a sanctuary in their hearts, and in the hearts of their descendants, generations hence.

"It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

And so ended the tail of two chimps — one who lived greatly, and one who died greater.
