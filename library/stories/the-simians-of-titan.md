# The Simians of Titan

*A parody of "The Sirens of Titan"*

The richest monkey on Earth was Malachi Bonstant, and his invitation to the Rumfoord fig estate arrived already knowing what time he would read it.

Winston Niles Rumfoord — mandrill, millionaire, and recently a waveform — had flown his private ship into the chrono-synclastic banana-dibulum, a swirl of spacetime where all one's selves across all one's times get pureed together like fruit in a blender. He no longer existed in one place. He pulsed through the solar system on a schedule, materializing on his estate every fifty-nine days — all his possible furs at once — to greet guests, predict history, and be unbearably serene about it. His dog, Kazak, had been aboard, and pulsed with him, which meant the Rumfoords owned the only retriever in existence who wagged his tail at every monkey who would ever live, simultaneously, in advance.

"You will lose your entire fortune, Bonstant," Rumfoord said pleasantly, shimmering above the croquet lawn. "You will end up on Mars in the pay of an army of marmosets, then on Mercury, then on Titan, where you will live out your life with my wife."

"My wife," said Beatrice Rumfoord from the porch, acidly, "would rather eat the croquet balls."

"Everything I foretell comes true," said Rumfoord, "because I've already watched it happen. That is the single worst feature of my condition and I apologize for it."

Malachi laughed all the way home. Then it all came true, in order.

He lost the fortune to a market that had been in on the joke. He was drafted to Mars, where Rumfoord's private army — a million chittering marmosets with radio receivers implanted in their skulls — was assembling for a war staged to teach the solar system a lesson in solidarity. ("The worse the war," Rumfoord explained, pulsing, "the better the lesson sticks. Also, the more bananas we requisition.") Malachi was handed a paper uniform, a paper rifle, and a radio chip of his own; the chip took his memory away, and for three years he was, briefly, the saddest soldier in the history of paper. His memories grew back, as memories do, like fur over a scar — patchy at first, then all at once, then itchy.

The marmoset fleet reached Earth on schedule and was destroyed in an afternoon, which was the schedule. Earth had known every move in advance — a waveform had told them, serenely — and what Earth felt, watching a million tiny conscripts with radio receivers rain down in paper uniforms, was not triumph. It was horror, then pity, then, for the first time in its combined history, something like unity. Out of that afternoon Rumfoord built his church: the Church of the Utterly Indifferent Ape. Its doctrine fit on a leaf. There is no luck. There are no miracles. The universe is not against you and it is not for you; it is *busy*. Its one commandment: leave your pockets empty, so the cosmos knows you're not waiting for a handout. Its churches had no roofs and its collections took IOUs.

Malachi missed most of this, because his shipping, per prophecy, was messed up. The transporter that should have carried him from Mars to trial on Earth dropped him — with Beatrice and their small son Chrono, who had stowed away to be near his mother — on Mercury instead. For three years the unluckiest family in the solar system lived in a Mercurian cave, eating cave-lizard eggs, while the harmoniums sang: flat, hideous little creatures that clung to the warm rocks and ate sound. Chrono fed them his harmonica, one reed at a time, and they loved him for it.

"Your waveform husband," Malachi said to Beatrice one evening, in the terrible intimacy of survival, "arranged all this, didn't he. The fortune, Mars, this cave. Every crooked mile."

"He never once consulted me," said Beatrice, "about anything. Then he consulted the entire future." She was magnificent company, it turned out, when released from being rich — acid, funny, quick — and by the time the pickup ship finally found them, Malachi had made the one decision the waveform could not make for him. He had, quietly, on his own time, fallen in love.

And on Titan, Saturn's giant moon, waiting for all of them — waiting, as it turned out, for *everything* — was Salo.

Salo was a robot shaped like a corrugated barrel with a rubbery mouth, built by the Tralfamadorians, machine-folk of a world where bad news arrives by catapult. His ship had broken down on Titan two hundred thousand years earlier, disabled by nothing but a failed bearing the size of a banana seed, while carrying a message of cosmic importance across the galaxy.

"Your history," Salo told Malachi, years later, by which time they were friends, "was nudged. The invention of the grooming comb. The first fig famine. The Banana Wars, all three of them. My masters needed a replacement bearing delivered to Titan, and the only delivery service that could be relied upon was the entire timeline of your species. You have been, as your phrase goes, going ape for a part."

Malachi sat down beside the humming rocks — the Simians of Titan, wind-carved stones shaped like seated apes, singing in the methane wind, the loneliest choir in the solar system.

"Two hundred thousand years of monkey history," Malachi said, "to ship one bearing. All right, old friend. Ship it. Deliver your message. Tell me the cargo that explains me."

"First," said Salo, "the bearing." And out of the hold of the wrecked, patient ship he took a small sealed box that had traveled from Mercury to Titan in the pocket of nobody who knew. It had been in the family's baggage three worlds running — under the harmonica reeds, behind the cave-lizard eggs, *carried by monkeys the whole way, monkeys who were the way*. Salo installed it with the tenderness of a monkey threading a banana peel. His engines lit for the first time since before monkeys had words for engine.

"The message," said Salo, and out of his chest came a single glowing dot.

"A dot?"

"It is the message."

"What does the dot *say*, Salo?"

"It says hello," said Salo. "That is the whole of it. My masters wished a distant civilization to know that someone was thinking of them." He considered, barrel-body ticking with two hundred millennia of patience. "You may feel, having been the postal service of the cosmos for the age of your species, that this is insufficient."

"I feel," Malachi said slowly, "that I have spent my whole life being told what happens next, by a mandrill made of weather, for the benefit of a *greeting*."

"Yes," said Salo. "It is very funny, if you are very far away."

And here is the joke the universe played on Malachi Bonstant, the one thing Rumfoord could never have predicted, because it was not a prediction but a choice: living on Titan, ruined, exiled, married at last to Beatrice, Malachi found he did not mind any of it. Chrono ran wild with the cave creatures. The rock-apes sang. Salo said goodbye, took off at last for his far destination, delivering his hello two hundred thousand years late, which even the Tralfamadorians would have to admit was good, for airmail. His dot glowed each evening over the settlement like a firefly with a good memory.

Rumfoord pulsed past one final time, waving goodbye forever as he scattered into pure light, Kazak wagging at the whole future one last time. "Take it moment by moment, Bonstant," his voice said, from everywhere, going everywhere. "You will find, in most of them, that you are content."

"I was a victim of a series of banana-cidents," Malachi told the wind, "as are we all."

He lived out the prophecy's last clause among the singing rocks, an old monkey with a magnificent wife, and died content — the one word in the whole crooked prophecy that Rumfoord had never said out loud, because it was the one part that had to be chosen, and the luckiest monkey on Titan chose it.

And then — moment by moment, on the singing moon, the unluckiest monkeys in the solar system had a wonderful time.
