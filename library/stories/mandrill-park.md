# Mandrill Park

*A parody of "Mansfield Park"*

Thirty years ago, Mrs. Norris said to her sister, Lady Bertram of Mandrill Park: "Let us take one of your sister Price's kits. It will be charity, dear sister — and charity, done from a great tree, costs us nothing but the food."

So Fanny Price came: a small, shivering vervet among the great blue-and-gold mandrills, too frightened to climb.

Lady Bertram, a loris of infinite rest, opened one eye on the best branch. "Let her not make a noise," she said, and slept again.

Fanny might have been utterly lost, had not Edmund, the second son, found her crying on the stair-branch and said: "You will climb with us. Here — take my shoulder. It has never once dropped anybody."

Mrs. Norris's rule was laid down early and never relaxed: "Fanny must never have the second-best banana." By her vigilant management, Fanny rarely had any banana at all.

Ten years passed, and Fanny grew gentle, useful, and nearly invisible. Her one extravagance was her brother William, a young sailor-monkey of the Patrol Groves, who sent her from foreign boughs a simple amber cross; and Fanny wore it on a string against her fur, because it was the only thing in the great house that had cost her nothing but love.

Then, while Sir Thomas was away inspecting the fig plantations of the far islands, Henry and Mary Crawford arrived from the London groves — spider monkeys with easy laughs and quicksilver swings — and even the heavy old boughs of Mandrill Park felt them.

"Let us act!" cried Tom, the heir. "A little theatre among the fig-branches! *Swingers' Vows* — a play of the loosest vines!"

The casting was a comedy of vanities. Maria wanted the part that flirted; Julia wanted the part Maria wanted; Mary wanted the part that would scandalize; and Mr. Yates, a visitor whose whole soul was propped scenery, wanted everything else. Edmund was torn: it was improper, and yet the part of the steady clergyman was to be played opposite Mary Crawford. Fanny alone refused a part, and was scolded for her virtue, which is the usual fee; and she was set to making copies of the script, where she read, blushing to her ears, all the loosest vines in the jungle.

Then Sir Thomas returned, unannounced, and stood in his own fig-hall looking at a curtain.

"You will take down that theatre before my tail has touched the ground," he said. "My own house — a stage. And the fig-hall — of all boughs."

The lumber came down that afternoon; the family came down with it. Julia sulked into the shrubbery; Mr. Yates followed her, talking scenery, and did not for one moment stop.

To welcome the peace, Sir Thomas gave a ball — the first at Mandrill Park in twenty years — in Fanny's honor, and Fanny danced her first two dances with Edmund, which everybody saw and nobody but Fanny understood. Mary, in high feather, lent her a bright tail-chain of London gold for William's amber cross; Edmund brought, privately, a plain vine-chain he had bought her, blushing like a fig in October; and Fanny wore Edmund's, because it was Edmund's. Mary examined it, cried that she knew the weave — it was Edmund's own gift to her, three years old! — laughed beautifully, and was the only creature at the ball who found nothing in the coincidence at all.

Maria, his eldest, married Mr. Rushworth of the Rushworth groves — a wealthy orangutan who was dull even by orangutan standards — partly to escape the house, and mostly to out-swing Julia, who had wanted Mr. Rushworth herself. Julia, to recover, beamed at every male in the county and got none of them.

Henry Crawford, unoccupied, decided to pass the season making a small hole in Fanny Price's heart. "Only a pin's hole," he promised himself. "In that little marmoset? A pin's hole and no more."

He found instead that the hole was in his own. He made William a lieutenant; he quoted figs at her; he actually meant it, or as nearly as a spider monkey can; he proposed; Sir Thomas was delighted; Fanny, to the ruin of all her standing, refused him.

"Refused him!" thundered Sir Thomas. "A Crawford! Four thousand bananas a year! Refuse! What can you mean?"

"He makes females love him," said Fanny, quietly, "for practice."

"You shall go to Portsmouth, madam, and learn what a small grove means."

In the noise and dirt of the Portsmouth low-branches, Fanny learned it very well — the shouting, the smallness, the scramble for every fig — and chiefly through her sister Susan, who ran the Price boughs on gumption and shrieking, and to whom Fanny, sitting quietly amid the scramble, became an astonishment: a sister with patience. "You are the only thing in this grove," Susan told her, "that ever stops moving." It was the finest compliment Fanny had ever received, Mrs. Norris not being generous with compliments that did not reflect on Mrs. Norris — until Henry came down to charm her in it, and was charming, and was almost believed. She might in time have believed him entirely. But London cut the season short, for the scandal came, written on a leaf that ran through the jungle like fire: *Mr. Crawford has run away with Mrs. Rushworth.*

The jungle, which has never once lowered its voice, whispered it in every tree in England. And a second leaf followed the first before the ink was dry: *Julia Bertram has eloped with Mr. Yates — by the scenery-ladder, at midnight, to be married.*

They had married, at least, in the ordinary way; and Mr. Yates's one triumph, all his life, was having entered the family over Sir Thomas's dead veto and under a load of lumber.

Edmund, heartsore, went to Mary Crawford expecting a shoulder, and got a judgment instead.

"The fault," said Mary lightly, "was not the running off. Lovers will swing. The fault was being *discovered*. A little management, and it could all have gone on as before. And then your brother-in-law Rushworth must roar for a divorce — as if any grove ever healed by being publicly shaken."

"And Maria?"

"Maria must be taken in somewhere and sat down gently. Distance, time, new curtains. It is not a tragedy, Edmund. It is a season."

Edmund turned from the window very slowly.

"Her heart is all swing and no root," he said. "I have loved that heart half my life, and it was never there."

He walked out to the old shrubbery, and there, of course, was Fanny — who had been sent for at last, and had come home on the first mail-vine, and had heard everything, and had wept for him, and mended the fire, and put his favorite figs where his paw would find them without his asking.

"I have been a blind baboon," said Edmund.

"You were only looking at spider monkeys," said Fanny. "They are very fast."

"They are," said Edmund, taking her paw. "And I am a mandrill, and mandrills climb slowly — and arrive."

So Fanny came home to Mandrill Park at last, as its mistress, and found herself branching out in a house that had always been hers. Even the ball given for their wedding went off without a scandal, which the county noted with disappointment. Mrs. Norris removed herself to the Rushworth grove, to be a tyrant somewhere useful, and the house breathed. Lady Bertram opened both eyes for the occasion, and was heard to say that it was very agreeable, and slept again, at peace.

And at the wedding, everyone agreed, the bananas were the finest in the county — the second-best ones, Fanny insisted, being perfectly good enough for anyone who loved her.
