# James and the Giant Peel

*A parody of "James and the Giant Peach"*

Until he was four years old, James Henry Monkey had a happy life — until one day a rhinoceros escaped from the jungle zoo and gobbled up his parents, which is a nasty way for any story to begin. James was sent to live with his two aunts on a bare hillside: Aunt Sponge, a fat baboon with a greasy rump and a simper, and Aunt Spiker, a lean baboon with a spiky crest and a screech.

"You miserable little mange-tail," they said every morning. "Chop the wood! Haul the water! And never, never go past the fence."

"Why don't we eat it ourselves?" Aunt Sponge asked Aunt Spiker, nodding at James. "Plump little thing."

"Patience," said Aunt Spiker. "First he fetches the water."

James did not go past the fence. James did not go anywhere. Until, one boiling summer day, an odd little creature sidled out of the bracken — a grizzled aye-aye with one long witchy finger and a paper bag.

"Crocodile tongues," the aye-aye whispered, showing the bag. "Ten thousand of them, stirred backwards into a brew and boiled in the skull of a howler. Each one is a second of raw magic. Add them, drop by drop, to whatever you please, and it will grow, grow, grow — and marvels will come of it. Take them, child, and take care."

James ran, thrilled, for home — and tripped. The bag flew, and the tongues — glowing green, wiggling with importance — scattered and burrowed into the earth beneath a scraggly old banana tree at the hilltop.

The tree shuddered. The hill groaned. And by dawn a banana hung from its single stalk — a banana the size of a troop hall, golden and gleaming, blotting out the sun.

"Spectacular!" cried Aunt Spiker, who could smell money the way other baboons smell ripe fruit. And the money came — monkeys by the thousand, paying to gape, while James swept up the peels of the crowds and slept under the ticket table.

That night, locked out as punishment for standing too near the Wonder, James heard something impossible: the banana creaked, and a warm voice said, "Come in."

There was a split near the base — a doorway. Inside ran a tunnel into the creamy gold heart, and in a hall of fruit-flesh sat an astonishing crew: a Ladybug with a shell polished like a conch; an enormous Old Green Grasshopper; a gloomy Earthworm; a Centipede in at least a hundred boots; Miss Spider with a carpetbag of silk; a fat Silkworm; and a sleepy Glow-worm lighting the ceiling like a chandelier.

"Ah — fresh fruit," said the Grasshopper. "Welcome aboard, my boy."

"Aboard?" said James.

"The magic that made the banana made us," rumbled the Ladybug. "It got into us when the tongues did, and now we are large, which is inconvenient, and ripe, which is worse. Do you know what happens to large ripe fruit when the crowds go home? Pie. We are leaving tonight."

"Night, that is," muttered the Earthworm, "when the sharks can eat us in the dark, which is politer."

"Nobody is eating anybody," said Miss Spider. "We roll down the hill, we float out to sea, and we are free. Centipede, if you please."

"About my boots," said the Centipede. "A hundred boots, forty-six laces, and one hour to tie them. Which is why," he added grandly, "I have delegated." And he scuttled to the wall and bit through the great stem.

WHUMP! The giant banana tore free, rolled down the hill, bounced over the fence — flattening both aunts flat as two dried fig leaves, which served them entirely right — and plunged into the sea.

For a whole day it was heaven. The banana rode the waves like a golden island. The Grasshopper made music with his legs; the Ladybug collected dew in her shell for tea; James, for the first time since his parents, was called by his name and asked his opinion. Then the Earthworm, who was dangling a toe in the water for coolness, went pale.

"Sharks. Thousands of them. Chewing the peel."

The banana shuddered as tooth after tooth tested it. "We are a fruit boat," said the Centipede, "and they are eating our hull."

"Then we fly," said Miss Spider calmly. She and the Silkworm strung their silk and began. "The trick is parrots. Parrots are strong, parrots are vain, and parrots cannot resist a worm."

"I KNEW it," said the Earthworm.

"You're the bait, dear," said Miss Spider. "You wiggle; they swoop; we lasso. James, keep their tails out of your ears."

And so it was done — five hundred and two silken loops, five hundred and two wild parrots caught mid-swoop, the Earthworm wiggling heroically at the end of a stick, and, with a creak and a lurch and a great spray of seawater, the giant banana rose out of shark-water and sailed up into the sky.

"Note," panted the Earthworm, being hauled in, "that I did not complain even once. Audibly."

It was a good crew, aloft. The Grasshopper fiddled with his legs. The Glow-worm dimmed herself politely at bedtime. The Centipede stood at the prow shouting navigation ("Cloud dead ahead! ...Other cloud! Also dead!") until the cloud answered.

The Cloud Monkeys were grey, hooting shapes who lived inside thunderheads and hurled hailstones for fun. A hailstone the size of a mango knocked the Centipede clean overboard, and James reeled him back in by his bootlaces — all forty-six — soaked, sneezing, and immediately claiming he had gone overboard on purpose to inspect the weather.

"Sure you did," said James.

"For research," said the Centipede. "The weather is very wet. Publish that."

James steered with a peeled rudder and learned every one of his friends by heart: the Ladybug's nine children (all points of the compass, plus one direction she kept to herself), the Silkworm's thousand dreams of flight, the Earthworm's firm belief that everything would end badly, disproven hourly.

And then, below them, rising out of the morning mist — the tallest tree in all the world: the Great Kapok Tower in the middle of Jungle City.

"It's a bomb!" screamed the macaw brigade.

"It's a bird!"

"It's a dirigible!"

"It's a BANANA!" — and the giant fruit settled gently onto the very topmost crown, and the city went absolutely bananas. Fire-tenders rushed to the rescue with ladders far too short. The Mayor arrived by hot-air basket, took one look at the fruit, one look at the insects, and one look at James, and said the only sensible thing: "Medals. Everyone. And breakfast."

The friends made lives in Jungle City: the Grasshopper joined the orchestra (having the only violin legs in town), Miss Spider wove ribbons for every marmoset's hair, the Ladybug married the Chief of the Fire-Tenders, the Earthworm became the face of sunscreen ("No Burn for a Worm"), the Glow-worm lit the library, and the Centipede was hired at once by the Bootmakers' Guild, which counted his boots, recounted them, and wept with joy.

The great banana itself fed the whole city — monkeys nibbling in a line ten miles long — and the one hard seed at its heart, James carved into a snug round house, where he lived with his friends, happy as a monkey in a mango tree, never once lonely, and never, ever again, anywhere near a rhinoceros.
