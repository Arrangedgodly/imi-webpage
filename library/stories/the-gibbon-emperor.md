# The Gibbon Emperor

*A parody of "The Goblin Emperor"*

Maia found out he was emperor from a knock on the door of his exile, which is one knock more warning than most monkeys get.

He was the half-blood son of the Langur Emperor of the Ethuveraz — and everyone said it like it tasted bad. His father had married a gibbon princess of the barrier groves for politics, deposited Maia in a remote fig orchard with a drunken cousin for a guardian, and forgotten him for seventeen years. Maia had his mother's long arms, her dusky fur, her way of moving through the canopy like poured honey. At court this was a scandal. In the orchard it was simply who he was.

The guardian, Setheris, had spent seventeen years assuring the boy he was worthless, mostly as a hobby. When the courtiers came, Setheris dressed in his best ruined coat and began planning the empire he'd rule through his stepson's shadow. Maia looked at him across the last breakfast of his old life and said the bravest sentence he had ever said.

"No," said Maia. "You may come to court. You may not come near it."

Then the airship. The *Windlaid*, the emperor's great balloon of bamboo and spider-silk, had fallen out of the sky with the emperor and all three of his full-blooded heirs aboard — not weather, the Inquisitor suspected, but wickedness with a toolkit. The whole Canopy of the Empire went silent, and the courtiers came for Maia.

"We are very sorry, Serenity," said the courier, Civet, a neat little mongoose of a monkey with immaculate manners. "You are emperor now." At court he would be announced, Civet explained later, as Serenity Thronogirdil, Radiant Ape of the Ethuveraz, Lord of All Between the Rivers — and other titles besides, ninety minutes of them at formal occasions, during which the emperor was expected to stand very still and regret nothing.

"I am a gibbon," Maia said, which he felt covered everything.

"Yes, Serenity. The court has noticed."

The court had noticed, and the court was not kind. They called him the Gibbon Emperor behind his back, and sometimes in front of it. The great of the realm — the whiled and begemmed aristos of the upper branches — bowed to him with their faces arranged like they were smelling something dead. They groomed each other's backs in the corners of receptions and left his un groomed. A monkey can survive many insults in court. An ungroomed back is not among them.

He was clumsy at the etiquette. He said "thee" to servants, which no emperor had done in living memory, and the servants nearly fainted. He asked Civet what things meant instead of pretending he knew, which frightened the court far more than cruelty would have, because a ruler who asks questions is a ruler who might one day ask them about *you*.

"You are brachiating into greatness, Serenity," Civet offered, one endless evening of petitions deep. "Thee need not do it alone."

"Thee is kind," said the emperor, "and I am very tired."

There were two great matters before him, and both were ropes with frayed ends.

The first was the airship. The *Windlaid* did not fall by accident. His Inquisitor — an ancient, irritable colobus with half a tail and all of the empire's suspicions — came back from the wreck site with a findsheet full of sabotaged rigging. Someone had wanted the emperor dead, and all his heirs, so that the crown would slide sideways onto a more convenient branch of the family tree. The trail of bribes led, eventually and inexorably, to a clutch of great lords who had found Maia's father too stubborn and Maia himself, they assumed, too controllable — install the orchard boy, wind him up, rule through the insult. His uncle stood too conveniently near, and said too smoothly, *I grieve with thee, Serenity* — and the old monkey had never grieved for anything in his life.

The second was marriage. The council wanted him wed swiftly, to bind the great families, and presented a parade of eligible archduchesses groomed to gleaming. Maia chose Lady Csethiro — a daughter of a lesser house whom the council had listed somewhere near the bottom, because she fenced too well, read too much, and had opinions with the safety off. He chose her for exactly those reasons, which the council found alarming and Csethiro found hilarious.

"He chose me," she told her sisters, delighted, "because I beat him at sparring. What kind of emperor *is* this?"

"The kind," said her sister, "that you had better keep."

The court, having failed to control him with brides, tried a coup instead. His aunt Sheveän — Setheris's wife, and the only monkey in the empire with more grievance than grooming — smuggled armed guards into the palace to seize Maia and rule through her own young son, Idra, who was six and would have made a splendid rubber stamp.

The plot reached Maia's study at midnight. The guards expected a frightened orchard boy. They got an emperor in a dressing gown who set down his tea, looked at the traitors down the full length of his long gibbon arms, and said: "Thee has frightened the servants. I will require thee to kneel, and I will require an accounting of who paid thee, and I will have thee know that I have been locked in rooms before, by professionals, when I was nine. Thee will have to do better than my cousin did."

They did not do better. The household guard, whom Maia had greeted by name every morning for a year, arrived at a dead run and did considerably better themselves.

"Execute no one," Maia ordered, before bed. "Idra is a child, and children keep whatever we teach them. Teach him thees."

At the trial of the saboteurs, his uncle expected to be excused — for the good of the realm, for the quiet of the realm, for the vanity of the realm. Maia, who had spent seventeen years being nobody, looked at the monkey who had sneered at his mother's memory, and did not shout, and did not weep, and did not take the easy branch.

"You committed treason against children," the Gibbon Emperor said quietly. "You killed my brothers, who never once spoke to me kindly, and you made me their heir in blood as well as grief. I will not have a monkey say the throne was worth that. Not even you. Not even me."

He did not take the heads. He exiled instead, and half the court called him weak, and slowly — the way a jungle grows back over a lightning scar — they began to call it something else. The empire settled into the strange new weather of a court where questions were safe and nobody's back went ungroomed; and Maia, who had spent his whole life on the wrong side of a distance, ordered his masons to begin the empire's first bridge over the Istandaärtha gorge, because he was a gibbon, and gibbons know better than anyone that the whole point of long arms is the far side.

On the night of his wedding, Maia and Csethiro sat on the palace roof with their legs dangling over ten thousand lanterns, and groomed each other's backs, which among gibbons is the whole of matrimony and most of the government.

"I am still a gibbon," Maia said.

"Yes," said his empress. "But now it is a title."
