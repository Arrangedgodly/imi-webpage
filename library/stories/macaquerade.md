# Macaquerade

*A parody of "Maskerade"*

The note was pinned to the dressing-room door with a knife, which is traditional in opera houses, where people are passionate about stationery.

*You must stop the show tonight,* it read, in elegant looping script. *Disaster will follow. Bad luck. Woe. Also the second act is too long. — The Ghost*

"Who's the Ghost?" asked Agnes Nitt, a big young howler with a face like a friendly cliff and a voice measured by frightened professors.

"Nobody knows," breathed Christine, the slim gorgeous chimp Agnes secretly sang for. Christine's own singing voice was thin enough to slip under a door. "He leaves flowers. He leaves notes. He leaves *career advice*. Oooh, I hope he leaves ME a career."

He had, in a way. Christine starred at the Ooking House of Ankh-Morepork and Agnes was the sound coming out of her: Agnes howled like an angel in the wings, Christine stood in the light and moved her lips. In the profession this is called a macaquerade: everybody is pretending to be somebody else, and at least one of them is a monkey.

Inside Agnes's head, a second, sassier voice named Perdita kept a running commentary on all of it. *You could be up there,* said Perdita. *You could be up there in a dress that doesn't look like a tent with ambitions.* But Agnes had agreed, because the money was real and because it was easier to be the power behind the throne than the monkey in front of the firing squad. Perdita called this cowardice. Agnes called it a lease.

Two hundred miles away in Lancre, Granny Weatherwax had discovered her cottage had been sold while she was away, to an opera-mad couple who wanted the view. Nanny Ogg, who had personally brokered the sale and personally hoped nobody would do arithmetic, tried to look innocent, which on Nanny Ogg resembles a sloe gin with a secret.

"We're goin' to Ankh-Morepork," Granny told her.

"Is it about the cottage?" said Nanny.

"It's about the principle," said Granny, who had also read in a newspaper that the opera had a Ghost, and witches took a dim view of unlicensed hauntings. Doing it to *opera* was beyond the pale. Also, a young witch of Lancre was in that city pretending to be somebody else's voice, and Granny Weatherwax took a dim view of that too, mostly because she suspected it was the wrong half of the arrangement.

The buyers of the cottage, meanwhile, discovered that the cottage came with a sitting tenant: a cold glare that arrived before the knock, and stayed in the best chair. They developed, within a fortnight, a powerful urge to see the mountains instead, and the realtor's ledger shows the sale reversing itself in handwriting no one could later produce.

The Ghost was busy. The Ooking House had a tradition — red velvet, huge emotions, a chandelier of a thousand glass bananas hanging over the stalls like doom in formal dress — and somebody was murdering it. A stagehand fell from the flies. The Ghost's notes grew shriller: *I said stop the show. Woe. WOE. — The Ghost*

"He's getting worse," said Walter Plunge, the shambling, sweet-natured odd-job monkey who worshipped opera, adjusted the glass bananas nightly with a feather duster, and was teased by everybody because teasing Walter was the house tradition beneath the tradition. "It's not supposed to have woe in it. It's supposed to have *singing*."

In the third row sat a seven-foot skeletal ape in a black hooded cloak, watching rehearsals with empty eye-sockets and applauding politely at the arias. The management had given it a free seat. You do not argue with a skeletal ape who turns up nightly. You sell it a programme.

"MUSIC," said APE, thoughtfully, on the third night. "IT IS NOT OVER YET."

"How can you tell?" hissed Signor Nut-zella, the musical director, a sleek baboon with lace cuffs and a temper like a squashed wasp. "It is NEVER over. Four more acts, two ballets, and an intermission long enough to grow old in."

"THEN IT IS DEFINITELY NOT OVER," rumbled APE, "UNTIL THE LARGE LADY HOWLS."

Granny and Nanny arrived on the night of the Grand Masque, by which time Granny had been in the city four hours and learned its whole caste system, currency, gossip, and which alderman was blackmailing which archbishop, by the traditional method of listening while people assumed she was furniture. They got themselves into the opera by the traditional witch method too: walking in as though they owned it, which works everywhere except banks.

Granny looked at the glittering house for about four seconds, listened to one note from the wings — one note, from a howler with a friendly cliff of a face — and worked out the whole thing, because opera is only witchcraft with an orchestra.

"There's two ghosts," she announced from the stage, in the middle of the soprano's big death scene, to general outrage. The soprano died harder; it was the best thing that had happened to her all season. "One's a sad old monkey who loves this place and only wants the shows to stop killin' people. The other is *you*, Mr. Nut-zella. You've been stealin' the house funds, coverin' it up with murders, and blamin' it on a ghost you invented. I could tell because your grief had lace on its cuffs, your books have got two sets of numbers, your woe is spelled with a flourish — and that's stage blood on your sleeve, and I can smell it ain't from the stage."

"Seize her!" screamed Nut-zella, which is what guilty people say instead of "explain further."

There followed ten minutes no Ooking House audience ever forgot: Nut-zella fled to the flies with the takings; the rope he slashed to drop the scenery instead dropped the great chandelier of glass bananas, which fell like the wrath of a fruit bowl; and Walter Plunge — gentle, stammering Walter, who knew every rope in the building because he had polished every rope in the building — swung out over the stalls and caught Christine, who had fainted in precisely the wrong place, as trained. Perdita, in the back of Agnes's mind, noted that Walter did it in time with the music, because of course he did.

The chandelier missed Christine. It did not miss Nut-zella. The season closed with a sound like a truck of crystal hitting a sack of wet cement, followed by applause that went on for an hour, some of it bony.

"IT IS OVER," agreed APE, standing to leave. "THE LARGE LADY HAS HOWLED."

Agnes howled. She stood alone in her own spotlight at last — Christine having retired shrieking into marriage, the house needing a voice, and Granny having stood in the wings staring at the management with an expression that communicated *she sings, or I return* — and held the note so long and so purely that the chandelier's ghost trembled in the rafters.

The new management of the Ooking House, after due deliberation and one look at Granny, appointed Walter Plunge to run the place. He conducted his first opera with a feather duster, wept openly through the overture, and banned the tradition of teasing, and the company obeyed him absolutely, because there is nothing the theatre respects more than a monkey who was there the night the chandelier fell and came out of it kind.

Backstage, Granny took a curtain call with the expression of a monkey who has stepped in something at a garden party.

"Can't be havin' with it," she muttered, accepting a bouquet. "But I'll take the flowers."

"Known you long enough," said Nanny, "to know that's a toast."

The cottage was waiting when they got home. The grass had even been cut. Granny entered, hung up her hat, and said nothing about it at all.
