# Twenty Thousand Leaves Under the Trees

*A parody of "Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas"*

The year 1866 was made strange by a monster.

Troops on three continents swore to it: an enormous tawny mass that glided beneath the canopy at the speed of a diving hawk, upright as a giant seed pod, wreathed in its own glow. It shredded vine-bridges. It bounced a merchant's hammock half a mile. Something had to be done, and something, as usual, meant a professor.

I am Pierre Aronnax, gibbon, naturalist, author of "The Mysteries of the Great Canopy Layers," and I was aboard the frigate Abraham Lianacoln with two companions: Conseil, my lemur valet, who classified every creature we met and misnamed half of them, and Ned Land, a Canadian baboon, the prince of harpooners, who could hook a flying fig at forty branches and brooded for days when he could not.

For three months we saw nothing. Then, one midnight in the Pacific jungle, the sea of leaves lit green, the frigate shuddered, and Ned, Conseil, and I were flung onto the back of the monster itself.

Ned hammered it with his fist. "Professor," he said, "this beast is riveted."

A hatch opened. Eight masked figures in sea-fur drew us below, and we met the master of the impossible craft: a tall capuchin with dark fur and eyes that had looked into the deep places of the world and decided against most of them.

"I am Nemo," he said. "It means nobody. You have stumbled onto my vessel, the Nautilus — a gourd of iron driven by the juice of ten thousand thunder-figs. You may stay. You may never leave. I ask only this: that you knock before entering my grove."

"Captain," I said, "what have you against us?"

"Nothing that walks. Everything that cages."

"Then we are prisoners," said Ned, bristling.

"Passengers," said Nemo, "with unlimited passage."

Ned did not accept the distinction. Ned marked days on his cabin wall with a claw — "Day forty, and I've counted every banana" — and pressed me at breakfast: "When, Professor? When do we run?" I had no answer, because I did not want one. The Nautilus, for all her steel, was the freest place I have ever slept.

Nemo had built a salon with one wall of glass, and through it we watched the canopy's hidden nation pass in review: rivers of ants on their silver roads, orchid reefs, herds of sloths grazing the high leaves like brown clouds. In the diving suit I walked the floor of the underworld and stood among the sunken columns of the First Grove, where the first apes planted the first orchard before the Great Flood of Forgetting. Nemo laid his paw on a stone root and said nothing at all for an hour.

Once, in the Torres Straits, the Nautilus ran aground on a reef-branch and sat there, ticking, while the tide of leaves went out from under her. Ned took it for an invitation and stocked the dinghy with fruit and fresh water, ready to run at first light. But the reef was not empty. A hundred war canoes of the island troops came out at dawn, painted and drumming, and laid siege to the stranded gourd. Conseil, peering through the glass, classified them as a migration of oversized water beetles and went below to lie down. Nemo ordered no guns. He ordered the rail wired. When the first boarding lines touched, the railing bit back with the leopard-shock of a thunder-fig, and the boarders left the water in flight, drumming their own retreat on the sand.

"Barbarians?" said Nemo, reading my face through the glass. "They fight for their branch. I fight for mine."

Ned put the fruit back, grumbling, and marked day fifty-one on his wall.

He showed me his treasury, too, though he never called it that: a grotto where the Empire's sunken harvests lay heaped in the black water — gold fig-coin, stamped with the faces of kings who had sold his family at a wharf. "Yours?" I asked. "Theirs," said Nemo. "I merely deliver it." That night a basket of it went ashore at a port in Crete, on a coast where a troop was rising against its masters, and I understood that nobody aboard the Nautilus was free of cargo.

One gray morning we followed the crew in sea-fur to a glen of pale coral, where they bore a shipmate who had been wounded past healing. They laid him in the orchid-white roots with tool on chest, and Nemo knelt and cut a cross of living fronds, and spoke four words I have never since been able to forget: "Here, past every cage." The crew answered as one voice. Conseil, who classified everything, could not classify that, and held my arm the whole way back.

We voyaged twenty thousand leaves and more. We were trapped in a bower of frost in the southern winter — and this was no melodrama but arithmetic: the air grew stale, day by day, and the ice above us did not care. Nemo, who feared nothing that breathed, feared a lid. He put the crew in shifts with the last air-fronds, chipping upward, singing to keep time. When my shift ended I found a frond pressed into my paws — from Conseil, who had given up his, and from Ned, who had given up Conseil's. "The professor will classify our air for us," Ned said, gruff, "so it's an investment." We broke through at dawn. I have loved those two misers ever since.

Farther south still, we came to the last tree in the world, a wind-bent fig at the foot of the pole itself, and Nemo nailed a sign to it: NO CAGES. He claimed that frozen grove in the name of no one, which is the only name he answered to.

A harpy eagle with talons like boat-hooks attacked the glass; old Darjeeling, a sailor, gave his life so that Ned might live, and Nemo wept openly, and I understood that the man had not frozen entirely — only chilled.

And twice I saw the other side of him. Off Ceylon he emptied a comb of wild honey into a poor gatherer's paws. And then, one black night, an iron ship of the Empire of Cages came grinding through the branches — a zoo transport, her holds all bars — and Nemo rammed her, and stood at the glass in silence while she sank.

"For every cousin they took," he said at last, "one cage at the bottom of the leaves. I am the law of the free canopy. Do not look at me like that, Professor. You have never had your troop sold for pennies at a wharf."

I had not. I stopped looking.

In the end it was the ocean itself that released us. Near the northern capes we were seized by the Whirlvine — a spiral typhoon of vines and spray that marched along the canopy like a waltzing giant — and the Nautilus, gripped fast, was wound into it. Ned, Conseil, and I cut ourselves loose in the confusion. When I came to, I lay on an island of ferns with my two companions staring down at me.

Was the Nautilus lost? Is Nemo alive still, roaming the green dark with his thunder-figs and his grief? I cannot say. I only set this down as honestly as I can: thus ended our journey of twenty thousand leaves under the trees. May vengeance spare the mighty branches, and may the boldest swinger of them all come to rest at last.
