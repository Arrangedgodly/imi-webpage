# The Very Hungry Capuchin

*A parody of "The Very Hungry Caterpillar"*

In the light of the moon, a little egg lay on a leafy nest at the tip-top of a kapok tree.

One Sunday morning, warm and bright, the sun came up and — pop! — out of the egg came a tiny, tiny, very hungry baby capuchin.

He was no bigger than a banana flower, with a sad little bare tail and a stomach that rumbled like distant thunder.

"Mama," he squeaked. "I'm hungry."

"I know," said his mother, sighing the sigh of every capuchin mother since the beginning of time. "You were born hungry. You'll be hungry forever. That's the whole deal."

He started to look for some food.

On Monday he ate through one banana. But he was still hungry.

"ONE 'nana?" said his uncle, aghast. "Nobody eats just one 'nana. Not at breakfast. Not at a funeral."

On Tuesday he ate through two mangoes. But he was still hungry. He ate the second one slowly, to make it last two whole minutes.

On Wednesday he ate through three plums. But he was still hungry. ("Those were MY plums," grumbled his cousin, but nobody fights a hungry capuchin. It's like fighting weather.)

On Thursday he ate through four guavas, seeds and all. But he was still hungry. "One for each hand," he explained to nobody, "and one for each foot."

On Friday he ate through five figs. But he was still hungry. By Friday evening, the trees on the kapok's left side were noticeably barer, and the birds were holding a meeting about it, which concluded that the trees on the kapok's right side should perhaps be warned.

The troop began to take an interest, the way troops do — which is to say, they gathered around at a safe distance and offered unsolicited commentary.

"That's a growing boy," said Great-Aunt Marigold.

"That's a bottomless pit with ears," said his cousin.

"He's going to eat the calendar next," said the uncle, darkly. "First the Sundays, then the whole week."

On Saturday, he ate through one piece of banana bread, one coconut custard, one slice of fermented durian, one wedge of stinky jackfruit, one honey-dipped fig, one lollipop made of pressed tree sap, one grove-fresh cherry, one slice of watermelon, one lump of raw cacao, and one ripe, perfect, speckled 'nana.

That night he had a stomachache.

A BIG stomachache. A swollen, round, eyes-watering, regret-everything stomachache.

"OOOOH," he groaned, lying flat on his back with all four feet in the air while the troop nodded wisely and took turns telling him they had predicted it.

"Told him," said his cousin.

"Nobody ever listens," said Great-Aunt Marigold, patting his forehead with a cool leaf.

"It was the durian," moaned the capuchin. "The durian was a mistake. The durian is always a mistake."

"Write that down," his cousin told the parrots, who did.

The next day was Sunday again. The very hungry capuchin felt slow, and sad, and sorry for himself, and his stomach had gone as taut and round as a drum. Then he crawled to the freshest, greenest branch on the tree, and ate through one nice green leaf — well, one nice green FIG leaf, capuchins being firm on this point — and after that he felt MUCH better.

But he was still small. And still, somewhere deep down, hungry.

"Now comes the strange part," said his mother, and she smiled, because she had done this herself long ago, and so had her mother, and hers, all the way back to the very first capuchin, who was also hungry.

The little capuchin stopped eating. He stopped swinging. He stopped even complaining about being hungry, which frankly alarmed everyone far more than the eating had.

"Is he ill?" asked the uncle.

"He's entering his cocoon-nest," said his mother, misty-eyed. "I remember mine. It was a Tuesday."

The little capuchin gathered the biggest banana leaves in the canopy — one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten — and he wrapped himself up tight, cozy as a dumpling, and built himself a little hanging house called a cocoon-nest. Then he slept inside it for two whole weeks.

He snored. The troop took shifts watching him. The parrots reported hourly. His cousin started a betting pool about what, exactly, would emerge. ("Moth," said one uncle. "Lawyer," said another. "A moth lawyer," said the cousin, hedging.)

Rain came and went. A troupe of tourists macaques came and photographed the cocoon-nest, hanging in the kapok like the world's most boring fruit. His mother sat under it every evening and told it a story, in case he could hear.

Midway through the second week, a storm came up the valley and shook the kapok like a rattle. The cocoon-nest swung wide, back and forth, over the drop, and his mother stood beneath it all night in the hammering rain with her arms up, saying what capuchin mothers say at such times: "Not MY baby, not TONIGHT," which is the oldest prayer in the jungle. The branch held. The storm went off to bother some other tree, and in the morning the whole troop pretended their eyes were red from the wind.

On the last day, the hole appeared in the leaves at dawn — and then stopped, half-nibbled, because he had gone back for one more minute of sleep, the way you would, after two weeks of practice.

"Any day now," said the cousin, checking the pool.

"Today," said his mother, who knew.

After two weeks, he nibbled a hole in the banana leaves, pushed his way out, and emerged into the golden Sunday light, and — he was a BEAUTIFUL capuchin!

He had a glossy golden-brown coat, a creamy white chest like a fresh-pressed shirt, clever black hands, and a magnificent strong prehensile tail, fully furred at last, curled behind him like a proud question mark. He was big. He was splendid. He was, at long last, full.

The troop gasped. Great-Aunt Marigold sniffled into her leaf. Even his cousin conceded the loss of the betting pool gracefully ("moth lawyer — nobody guessed moth lawyer"), and the uncles shook his new sleek hand one at a time.

And then, with a beat of his heart and a whoop of joy, the beautiful capuchin launched himself off the branch into the bright blue sky — not flying, capuchins don't fly, but swinging, which is the next best thing and considerably more dignified — swooping from branch to branch to branch, over the figs and the guavas and the plums and the plum tree and the whole wide jungle, all the way home.

"Look at him go," said his mother, shading her eyes. "Hungry his whole life, and look what it made him."

"Made him strong," said the uncle.

"Made him quick," said the cousin, closing the betting book forever.

"Made him ours," said Great-Aunt Marigold, sniffled once more, and went in to fix him dinner, which, out of respect, was nine courses.

And the very hungry — no, the very FULL, very fine capuchin swung on into the sunset, where, if you listen closely on a warm night, you can still hear his stomach rumbling.

Just a little.

Old habits.
