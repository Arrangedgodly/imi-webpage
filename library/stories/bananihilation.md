# Bananihilation

*A parody of "Annihilation"*

The tower, which was not supposed to be there, plunged up out of the earth like a banana pushed through the peel from the wrong side.

There were four of us on the twelfth expedition: the surveyor, the anthropologist, the psyschimpologist, and me, the bananaologist. We had no names — the agency said names were a tether, and tethers, they said, were what Area X used to reel you back in wrong. So: four titles, four packs, four doses of mission training, and one border to cross, which we did at dawn, stepping through a shimmer in the air like the skin on old pudding, and out the other side into a wilderness that had swallowed eleven expeditions before ours.

The psyschimpologist led us, a broad gray female with a voice she kept in a drawer. On the first night, hypnotized in our tents, we received our conditioning, along with certain words to be used only in emergencies — the deepening word, the countershock word. I remember thinking, in the amber light of trance, that we had crossed a border to study the uncorrupted wild, and had brought our own leash with us, coiled.

The tower stood a mile from base camp, where the maps said nothing stood. Stone steps spiraled up and around its outside, up into nothing, and the entrance at its base opened not onto rooms but down: a spiral staircase descending into the earth, lit by no lamp that we had brought. The surveyor, an orang with a soldier's shoulders, took one look and named it a tunnel.

The psyschimpologist named it a tower, and the rest of us felt the truth of that in our spines, and did not argue.

It was inside, on the second descent, that we found the words. They grew on the tower's inner wall, in a band perhaps a hand wide, trailing down beside the steps like a flowering vine made of letters — letters of golden green, lush, faintly fungal, breathing. They had not been painted. They had been *written*, the way a plant writes its leaves, and they descended in a spiral ahead of us into the dark, fresh where we had not yet walked. I knelt and put my face close to them, close enough to see the letters bloom and fatten, because I am a bananaologist and I have spent my life on the fine edges of fruit, and I understood, with a calm that should have terrified me, that I was not reading graffiti.

I was reading growth.

The words said, in part:

*Where grows the strangling bunch that drops from the bunch-stem into the bowl, into the palm, into the dark of the mouth? Where the seed that will not plant, the fruit that feeds and hollows, the yellow coat that splits to show the coat beneath, that splits to show the coat beneath, that splits — O monks of the second orchard, does your orchard not also rot from the topmost branch?*

"Stop reading," said the psyschimpologist, from the steps above. "Bananihilation."

And my mind, obedient, slid down a level into the dim, and I climbed out of the tower holding a thought I did not know I was holding.

Things went wrong in the correct order after that. The anthropologist went back to the tower with the psyschimpologist to photograph the words and came back alone, fevered, torn where something with a flat, patient mouth had held her — she died at dawn, whispering the letters she had copied, and her copy had continued past the point where the writing had stopped. The surveyor heard the moaning thing in the reeds that night — the long, rising, almost-ape moan that had followed every expedition since the first — and began to count her ammunition like beads.

I will tell you about my husband, because everything I did after the tower, I did inside his absence. He was on the eleventh expedition. He came back a year before I crossed the border — walked out of the marsh with his pack and his crooked smile, and for six months we lived as monkey and wife while he died of the tumors that Area X had seeded in him, growing in him like a message in the wrong medium. My marriage, my grief, my grant applications: I had a heart full of questions, and the agency had a border full of answers, and the psyschimpologist had chosen me for the expedition because grief makes a good instrument. She admitted it, in the end. They all admitted everything, in the end. It is the last thing most of us do.

The surveyor decided I had been compromised. She was right, which is the joke of it. She shot at me in the ruins of the old camp — two rounds, one of which I still carry somewhere in me, migrating like a seed — and I did not return fire, and that unsettled her more than fire would have. When I found her again, she had stopped being decided about anything. Area X had taken her vote.

The lighthouse came next. It stood on the coast where the maps promised, broken, blooming with strange interior gardens; and in the keeper's room, amid a decade of journals from every expedition — journals we had been told did not exist, journals stacked like the tower itself, one spiral of testimony after another — I found the psyschimpologist at the top of the stair, broken on the rocks below the light, having chosen the door over the debrief. Her last note was in my pack. It said: *I was the director. The lighthouse keeper began as a man and became the words. Do not let them call it contamination. There is nothing to contaminate. It was always here. We were the brief visitors.*

I went down the tower one last time, past the spiral letters, down and down past the depth where breathing becomes swallowing, and at the bottom, in a light that came from the walls themselves, I found the crawler: the lighthouse keeper, or what the lighthouse keeper had become, fused with the tower, the letters rising from him like breath in cold air, his mouth a century open, his eyes two gardens. He was writing the words. He had been writing them since before the border, since before the agency, since before there were expeditions to write about. He was not dying, and he was not living. He was *transcribing* — the strangling bunch, the second orchard, the coat beneath the coat beneath — and the spores of his letters settled on my fur like the gentlest ash, and I breathed them, and I understood the words, and the words were not an instruction and not a warning.

They were an invitation.

That is the thing I can make no one at the agency understand, in my reports they will never read: I did not fall. I was not contaminated, compromised, bananihilated. I *ripened*.

My husband, at the end, had opened his eyes and looked past me and said, "It's not an expedition. It's an orchard." I thought it was the tumors talking. I have since stopped underestimating tumors and orchards both.

So: the last journal entry, then, whoever finds it. The surveyor is at rest. The anthropologist is a footnote in the tower's margin. The psyschimpologist chose the rocks over the report. The border, I believe, is coming down on its own — not invading, *spreading*, the way an orchard spreads, which is to say without any opinion of us at all.

I am not going back through the shimmer. I have taken the lighthouse's little boat, and I am going out past the shallows to the island no expedition ever mapped, where the moaning comes from, where the oldest journals say the whole thing began — the first tree, the first split peel, the first word written on a wall that had no business holding it.

I have my field kit. I have my 'nanas. I have my husband's crooked smile somewhere in my face now, because that is how this place loves you: by borrowing.

The sea is very calm.

The tower is very full.

I am going down to read the rest.
