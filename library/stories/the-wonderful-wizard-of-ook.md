# The Wonderful Wizard of Ook

*A parody of "The Wonderful Wizard of Oz"*

Dorothy was a little golden monkey who lived in the middle of the great grey jungle of Kanzoo, where no banana had ever grown. She lived in a tree-house with Aunt Em and Uncle Henry, and her only friend was Toto, a tiny spider monkey who got into no end of monkeyshines — untying the washing and retying it to a toucan, borrowing the eggs and forgetting where.

One afternoon a terrible monkey-nado howled through the grey trees, picked up the whole tree-house and spun it away, Dorothy and Toto tucked inside, going bananas with fright.

When the house thumped down, it landed in the brightest jungle Dorothy had ever seen — right on top of the Wicked Witch of the East, squashing her flat. A crowd of tiny Marmosetkins cheered: "Thank you, sweet giant! You have freed us from the Wicked Witch!"

"The Wicked Witch of where?" asked Dorothy.

The Marmosetkins pointed politely at the little red banana-peel slippers under the tree-house, which had belonged to the Witch. A Good Gibbon in a bubble of pink sap floated down.

"I am Glinda," she said. "Wear the slippers, my dear, and follow the Banana-Brick Road to the Emerald Canopy. Only the Wonderful Wizard of Ook can send you home to Kanzoo." She blew a kiss, and the bubble carried her off like a soap bubble with opinions.

So Dorothy and Toto set off, and along the way they met three companions.

In a guava grove hung a Scarecrow stuffed with straw, whose job was frightening parrots, though the parrots mostly perched on him and critiqued his posture.

"Good morning," said the Scarecrow, very stiffly.

"Did you speak?" asked Dorothy.

"I did, and nobody answered, which is what comes of being stuffed with straw. The farmer built me and painted the finest ears in the county onto me, and in a whole year they have never heard a thing." He sighed. "If I only had a brain, I could be the smartest *scare*-monkey in the jungle — I'd know every parrot by name, and they'd be *frightened*."

Dorothy cut him down and he joined them, wobbling like a monkey learning to walk for the second time.

In a guava rain they heard a groan: "Oil me... oil me..." Under the vines stood the Tin Chimp, rusted solid. They oiled his jaw first, so that he could complain properly, then his elbows, until he could move.

"A cursed witch enchanted my axe," he said, "and every swing chopped away a bit of me, until a tinner rebuilt me out of tin plate — and the tinner forgot the heart. So I have no heart, and nobody will groom me either; tin fur snags combs. If I only had a heart."

So he came too, carrying his oil-can like a treasure.

Last they met an enormous Gorilla trembling behind a bush, terrified of a bumblebee. "I'm supposed to be fierce," he blubbered. "The other gorillas laugh at me. I weep at sunsets and duck at butterflies. If I only had courage."

"Cheer up," said Dorothy. "You're the biggest thing in this jungle."

"That," sniffed the Gorilla, "is exactly what worries me." He joined them, flinching whenever a leaf landed anywhere.

The road led through a ravine, where the Katzees attacked — beasts with the bodies of bears and the striped heads of cats, all whiskers and appetite. The travelers ran for a bridge of woven branches, and as the Katzees thundered across behind them, the Tin Chimp chopped the far vines with his hatchet. The bridge tumbled; the Katzees tumbled; and the Tin Chimp felt something thump inside his tin chest, which annoyed him pleasantly.

At the river, their vine raft spun to the middle and stuck fast, pitching the Scarecrow onto a rock midstream, pole and all. "If I only had a brain," he called, upside down, "I'd have leaned left." A Stork Queen flew by. "I can carry anything," she said, "that is not too self-important." The Scarecrow promised on his stuffing to stay modest, and she bore him to the bank. Then the Gorilla, eyes tight shut and teeth gritted, leapt the rest of the river with the others on his back — and discovered, on landing, that his legs had been brave the whole time and had simply not been consulted.

Then came the field of Sleeping Orchids, whose perfume drifted them all to sleep — all but the Gorilla, too heavy to be snored over by mere flowers, who carried out Dorothy and Toto, then the Scarecrow, and fell asleep mid-step on the way back for the Tin Chimp. A brigade of Field Marmosots, whom Dorothy had once freed from a snare of snapping figs, arrived in their thousands with harnesses of woven grass and hauled the great Gorilla free like a tug-of-war with a mountain.

"One banana," said the Marmosot Queen, when Dorothy thanked her. "Payable never."

At last they reached the Emerald Canopy, where the Guardian of the Gates clipped banana-tinted glasses on everyone ("so the emeralds don't blind you," he said; and nobody noticed that the whole city was merely painted), and led them to the Throne Room. There, in smoke and fire, appeared a giant silverback head, roaring: "I AM OOK, THE GREAT AND HAIRY! WHO DARES DISTURB ME?"

"We do!" said Dorothy. "I want to go home, the Scarecrow wants a brain, the Tin Chimp a heart, and the Gorilla courage."

"SILENCE!" boomed the head. "First, bring me the broom of the Wicked Witch of the West. Then we shall see."

In the West, the Wicked Witch saw them coming and wished her one wish into the Golden Cap: "Fly, my baboons!" A shrieking squadron of flying baboons — guerrilla gorillas with macaw wings — swooped out of the clouds. They dented the Tin Chimp against the rocks, unstuffed the Scarecrow and hung him on a peg, caged the Gorilla, and snatched Dorothy and Toto away to the stone tower.

"Now your little red slippers are MINE!" cackled the Witch, and grabbed for them.

"They're mine!" cried Dorothy — and, pulling away, she flung the nearest bucket over the Witch. It was the wash-water, for Toto's bath.

"MELTING!" shrieked the Witch, dissolving into a greasy puddle. "Who knew! A witch defeated by *soap*!" All that was left of her was a hat, a broom, and a smell like wet carpet.

The flying baboons, delighted to be free ("she never once let us preen," their captain sobbed), straightened the Tin Chimp's dents, restuffed the Scarecrow, and bent open the Gorilla's cage as easily as a banana.

But at the Emerald Canopy the Wizard hemmed and hawed — "Come back in three days; better, make it a week" — until Toto tugged a curtain aside, revealing a marmoset no bigger than a fig, bellowing into a coconut megaphone.

"PAY NO ATTENTION," he squeaked, "to that monkey behind the curtain!"

"You're no wizard!" gasped Dorothy. "You're a little show-off marmoset!"

"A humbug," he admitted cheerfully. "I blew here from Omaha-by-the-Sea in a balloon, and the monkeys crowned me out of politeness. But I'm a clever one. Now — gifts!"

He mixed the Scarecrow a head-stuffing of banana mash and pinned on him a diploma: DOCTOR OF CHIMP-ION THINKING. "Brains, my friend, are what you already have; everyone knows it but you." He sewed the Tin Chimp a red velvet banana-shaped pincushion heart. "A heart is judged by how it's carried, not how it beats." And he poured the Gorilla a cup of fermented sap. "Courage is being frightened and swinging out anyway — and you've done it every day since I met you."

For Dorothy, the humbug patched a great banana-silk balloon, with OZ, THE GREAT AND TERRIBLE painted on the side. But just as it rose, Toto spotted a scarlet macaw in the crowd and gave chase; Dorothy scrambled down after him; the ropes slipped — and the little marmoset sailed away over the canopy, shouting, "REMEMBER ME KINDLY! AND KEEP THE GLASSES!" The city wept for a week, until his farewell note was read from the ramparts: "The Scarecrow rules in my stead. He thinks best."

"But *I* still can't get home!" cried Dorothy.

Then Glinda descended in her pink bubble. "You've had the power all along, my dear. Those slippers can carry you anywhere — but you had to learn it for yourself, and you never would, while you believed a humbug was doing your thinking."

Dorothy hugged her friends — the Scarecrow, the Tin Chimp, the Gorilla, and Toto last and longest — clicked her little red heels, and whispered: "There's no place like the troop. There's no place like the troop. There's no place like the troop."

Whump. Grey leaves. Grey sky. Grey old Kanzoo — and Aunt Em's arms.

"Wherever have you been?" cried Aunt Em.

"In the Land of Ook," said Dorothy, hugging Toto tight. "And oh, Aunt Em — I'm home. Even a jungle with no bananas is home."
