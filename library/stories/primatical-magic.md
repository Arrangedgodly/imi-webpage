# Primatical Magic

*A parody of "Practical Magic"*

For more than two hundred years, the Ohanana women have been blamed for everything that went wrong in the town of Mangoachusetts. If a soufflé fell, if a vine dried up, if a troop of mandrills packed up and moved to Salem overnight, the Ohananas got the stink-eye. Also the actual stink — the neighbors had a long tradition of throwing things.

So the Ohanana women kept to themselves, in the great grey house at the end of Lantern Lane with its deep porch and its garden of suspiciously healthy lilacs, where they practiced primatical magic — the practical kind, the kind with rules.

Always throw spilt salt over your left shoulder. Keep rosemary by your garden gate. Crack your bananas from the stem end, never the tip. Fall in love whenever you can — carefully.

Carefully, because of the curse. Every monkey an Ohanana woman loved was doomed to die. It had been true since the first Ohanana, Maria, survived her own hanging and walked away into the orchard with a smile, and it was still true two centuries later when the aunts — Frances and Jet, two ancient, elegant gorillas who wore black lace and drank gin with lunch — took in their orphaned nieces.

Sally was the practical one, serious as a seed catalog. Gillian was the wild one, golden and restless, forever one branch past the safe ones. The town children taunted them — "Witch! Witch! Your mama's a witch and so are you!" — and the aunts simply served them chocolate cake, because chocolate cake is how the Ohananas weaponize hospitality.

Each month, at midnight, the townswomen came creeping up the porch steps. They wanted love spells, mostly. To be adored. To be avenged. To have a certain gorilla's new wife develop an embarrassing skin condition. The aunts obliged, and the sisters watched from the stairs, and learned everything a girl shouldn't.

At thirteen, furious and frightened of the curse, Sally went into the garden and performed the most careful magic of her life: a spell to summon her one true love — a monkey so specific he could not possibly exist. Let him have one blue eye and one gold. Let him juggle six figs while riding a unicycle backwards. Let him have a birthmark shaped like a banana on his left shoulder. Only then, said the spell, would she know him. Only then could her heart be at risk. Since no such monkey existed, Sally had effectively locked love out of the house and hidden the key under her own sensible fur.

"Smart," said Aunt Frances, watching from the window.

"Sad," said Aunt Jet.

"Same thing, most days," said Frances.

Sally grew up and escaped to Long Island, married a kind builder named Michael, and had two daughters, Antonia and Kylie — and for a few years the curse seemed to nap. Then one Tuesday a branch fell on Michael in the orchard. A branch. The one thing in all the world a monkey trusts. Sally stopped believing in anything, moved the girls to a grey neighborhood of identical burrows, and became the most aggressively normal capuchin in the state of New York.

Gillian ran off at seventeen with the first monkey whose fur smelled like trouble, and spent eighteen years leaving a trail of broken hearts across four time zones, growing more beautiful and less careful in equal measure.

Which brings us, as all Ohanana stories eventually do, to the trunk of a stolen red convertible doing ninety through the night with a dead howler monkey in it.

His name was Jimmy, and Gillian had loved him the way you love a forest fire, briefly and then with the fire department involved. When Jimmy got mean, Gillian had dosed his tequila with the aunts' old calming tonic — a splash of nightshade, a whisper of lavender, nothing serious. Except she'd poured it like maple syrup, and Jimmy had gone to sleep and then simply stayed there.

"Gilly," said Sally, in her sister's headlights at two in the morning, eighteen years of letters and silence between them, "why is there a howler monkey in your trunk?"

"He's not a howler monkey anymore," said Gillian, who had been crying for three states. "He's a shouldn't-have-listened-to-him monkey. Sal. Sally. I need you."

And here is the whole of it: your blood is my blood, and when your sister arrives with a body, you put out the good gin and you get a shovel.

They buried Jimmy under the lilacs at midnight — my blood, your blood — and Sally planted rosemary over him for luck, which, she would later reflect, showed a certain optimism. Then she scoured the kettle, put the aunts' house back in order, and tried to return to being normal.

But dead boyfriends planted in cursed gardens do not stay polite. The lilacs bloomed out of season and enormous. Toads converged on the property from three counties. And Jimmy — or the warm gold light that had been Jimmy — started turning up in mirrors, fondling the tequila bottle, whispering into Gillian's ear like a loris with a grudge: dig me up, sugar.

That was the situation, more or less, when the doorbell rang and revealed an orangutan in a linen suit with a badge and a notebook.

"Gary Hallet," he said. "I'm looking for a howler monkey named Jimmy. Last seen in the company of a woman matching your sister's description." He looked at Sally. He kept looking at Sally. Investigator Hallet had spent his whole career being certain of things, and the moment the porch light hit Sally Ohanana he lost certainty the way other monkeys lose their keys.

"He's not here," said Sally.

"Right," said Gary, and wrote nothing down, and felt the strangest sensation, like fate juggling six figs on a unicycle.

While Sally and Gary circled each other — he helping fix the porch gutter he was professionally obligated to notice, she pretending not to watch his shoulders — Jimmy's gold light got impatient. It slid into Gillian the moment she napped, and Gillian woke up wrong: eyes flat, voice a register too low, hands reaching for the tequila and the shovel and, eventually, for Kylie.

Antonia screamed. Kylie threw the good gin. Sally took one look at her sister's flat gold eyes and did the most Ohanana thing imaginable: she sent her daughters running through town with the news that the Ohanana house needed help, and waited to see who actually came.

The townswomen came. The very ones who'd crept up those porch steps at midnight for love spells and vengeance, the ones who'd thrown things and named their dogs after the sisters — they came, with salt and brooms and their grandmothers' remedies, because two centuries of blame is a kind of kinship too. They held Gillian down on the kitchen table while Jimmy howled out of her, and Sally — sleeves rolled, voice iron — read him out of this world and into whatever was next.

"You were loved," she told the gold light, at the end, almost gently. "You just weren't good for anybody. Get out of my sister."

The light went. The lilacs, mercifully, closed over the spot like water.

Jimmy's ring was found in the garden and turned in as evidence; Gary Hallet filed a report describing a howler monkey who had died elsewhere, wandered off, and been formally evicted by the household, a sentence which no superior officer ever questioned, because some paperwork exudes an aura of do-not. He stayed for the porch, the gutter, and the woman. And it was on the second Sunday of October, helping him paint the railing, that Sally saw it: a birthmark on his left shoulder, unmistakably banana-shaped. She checked his eyes and found one blue and one gold, and sat down hard on the porch steps, and laughed until she cried.

"I asked for you," she said, "when I was thirteen, so that you couldn't be real."

"Took the scenic route," said Gary Hallet.

Gillian stayed too — for once in her life not packing — and grew, if not sensible, then steady, like a tree that finally quit swinging. The aunts returned from their travels, observed the painted porch, the orangutan, and the astonishing absence of a curse-shaped hole in anybody's life, and served chocolate cake until the whole town came down with contentment.

And on midsummer's night, the house at the end of Lantern Lane threw open its gates, and the town that had stink-eyed the Ohananas for two hundred years came dancing under the lilacs at midnight, glad of the magic at last.

Fall in love whenever you can, the aunts always said. Carefully, the Ohananas added.

But do fall. That part was never optional.
