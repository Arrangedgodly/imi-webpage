# Vines and Fruit

*A parody of "Wind and Truth"*

On the last night of the world, the tower of Oothiburu hung full of banners, and every banner was a troop that had sworn to die together, which is an odd thing for monkeys to do, and exactly the thing monkeys do best.

The contest had been agreed: one champion for the apes, one for Banodium, god of rage. Winner takes the world. It had been Banodium's idea, because Banodium was clever — cleverer now than a god should ever be, for his new body had belonged to Taravangian, the smartest old ape in any tree, who had stolen a god's skin by out-thinking it while it was wearing him.

There were ten days until the contest, and the tower spent all ten of them holding its breath.

On the tenth day out, Queen Navane finished her instruments — a wall of measured storm, gauges and graphs and one dial labeled DREAD, for if you cannot fight a god you can at least take its temperature. "The Everstorm gains a mile a night," she announced. "I have written it down. Writing it down is how we say no."

On the eighth day, Jas-nut the scholar-queen annexed three libraries for the war effort and drafted a battle plan with footnotes. "Rage has numbers too," she said. "It simply refuses to show its work."

On the seventh day came Szeth, the Assassin in White Fur, sworn now to Dalinar, who carried everyone's luggage and his own sins and stood guard facing outward. "Truth-Stone," he said, showing the black fruit of his oath. "I have done evil believing it was law. Now I do law believing it must first be kind."

On the sixth day, the troops held a feast of last things — because monkeys facing the end of the world cook everything in the larder, and there is no better argument with doom than a troop of full apes.

On the fifth day, Mangoash came at Kaladin on a rooftop with a knife and an apology in his head that belonged to the god. Kaladin disarmed him in four moves and offered him a grooming appointment. Mangoash fled across the tiles, chewing on his own bitterness, which had by then become his entire diet.

"Some knots comb out," said Syl from Kaladin's shoulder.

"And some," said Kaladin, "you leave for the rain."

Kaladin stood on the highest bough and looked down at the armies of the Refused, winds rolling backward, and did not hate them, which had taken years.

"You can't comb out rage by yanking," he told the young Windswingers, who wanted very much to yank. "You hold the fur. You wait. Rage is just a tangle somebody never helped with."

That was Kaladin's heresy, these days: he taught enemies. He'd have groomed Banodium himself, given a quiet evening and permission. The god knew it, too, and feared the invitation more than any spear.

Below, Shallan wore no mask at all, for once, which frightened her more than the coming battle. Adolin checked his dueling braid twice. Renarin and his quiet fren Glys sorted bandages, having calculated precisely where the wounds would be. And old Dalinar, silver-backed, one-eyed, walked out alone into the ring of cleared branches between the two armies, unarmed, to be the champion of everything.

"Bold," admitted Banodium, from his golden throne of heat. "And pointless. Shall I choose a champion, then?"

"I already see him," said Dalinar.

(The contract for the contest had taken a week to draft and remained the only battle of the age fought entirely by lawyers: *best of one fall; no substitutions; no grooming below the belt; in the event of apocalypse, monsoon permitting.* Navane had annotated every line. Banodium had initialed them in heat.)

The god's champion stepped out of the storm wearing Kaladin's shape, and Shallan's, and every ape's own worst self, grown vast and beautiful: it was every grudge you'd ever nursed, given shoulders. It grew whenever anyone glared at it, and every troop had a grudge or two in reserve; the champion drank them like sun.

"Vines and fruit," Dalinar said mildly, to his own side. "That's what Taravangian never understood. Rage is a vine — it grows in the dark, it climbs, it strangles. But truth is the fruit. It only grows out in the open, and it's heavy, and when it drops, everyone hears it. So." He raised his voice. "I'll go first. I burned the city of Ratha-leaf. My wife Ivy died of my own cleverness. I loved her and I did not say so in time. THERE. Pick that up, monster."

The fruit dropped. The champion flinched, and shrank an inch.

Then Adolin stood, laughing, and shouted his own confession across the branches — a blamed little brother, an old shame, a murder no one had asked him about, told plainly to ten thousand apes. The champion shrank a handspan. Renarin admitted he had pretended to be weaker than he was so everyone would leave him alone with his books, and half the armies admitted the same. Szeth confessed his whole life in flat sentences, like stones laid in a row, and the champion lost a yard of height. Shallan stood up and said, to ten thousand apes, "All my masks were me. Every single one. Even the annoying one," and sat down, shaking, free. Navane read out the true account of one lost battle, and signed it, which is the bravest kind of confession: the notarized one.

The troops took to it like a new grooming game. Confession rolled down the boughs — small ones, large ones, one silverback admitting he had never once liked climbing, which cost him considerably. Every truth told was fruit fallen; every vine of the champion's strength withered by the width of one honest sentence. It never got the chance to feed: apes, given a stage and permission, will confess forever.

Banodium's champion dwindled and dwindled until it was the size of a marmoset's doubt, and then it was nothing but a knot of old vine lying in the ring, and Dalinar picked it up and combed it out in his fingers, unhurried, the way you comb any tangle: from the ends.

"Impossible," said Banodium, rising in gold. "The contract—"

"You want to re-peel the deal," said Dalinar. "That's the trouble with clever apes and clever gods alike: you think truth is a negotiable fruit. It isn't. It's the only crop that grows without lying about the weather."

The golden throne guttered. And Kaladin, watching, walked out past the champion's empty ring, sat down at the foot of the god's dais, spear laid flat on the grass — a Windswinger's whole vocabulary in one gesture — and said, gently, the thing Banodium had no answer to:

"You don't have to be angry to be strong. Somebody should have told you that when you were small. I'm telling you now."

The Everstorm unspun. The backward winds turned forward. On the walls, Navane's DREAD dial swung quietly around to HOPE, a reading she had never calibrated, it having never previously occurred. Jas-nut's battle plan survived first contact with the enemy, which none of her footnotes had predicted, and she spent the day revising in triumph. And in the strange quiet after, the vines of the great tower — every troop's banners tangled in them — did not need to be cut. They knitted, and the fruit that ripened along them that morning was sweet, and honest, and shared, and there was, at last, enough.
