# The Lemur's Last Sigh

*A parody of "The Moor's Last Sigh"*

I, Moraes Zogoiby — called Moor by my mother, Lemur by my enemies, and worse by my creditors — write this from the top of a stone fig-tower in the far jungle, where a mad howler keeps me prisoner for a painting I have never seen. Below, at the tower's foot, a young artist named Aoi Uê takes down every word I say and sketches it as fast as I can sigh it. That is the arrangement: I supply the sighing, she supplies the seeing.

We Zogoibys are a family of fast-forward creatures. We age at double speed: at thirty we are sixty, our hearts hammer twice the pace, we live half the lives and grieve at twice the rate. By the time you finish this page, I shall be older than my father ever got. That is not a complaint. It is a scheduling note.

My mother was Aurora da Gama, the great painter of the canopy. She worked in berry-pigment on fig-bark, and her pictures of the fading forest made loggers weep into their sawdust. On one side of my cradle stood Grandmother Epifania, matriarch of the da Gamas, who composed verse epics in which every rhyme was a curse on somebody. On the other stood Grandmother Carmen, matriarch of the Zogoibys, who answered every epic with a sadder one. Between them lay my mother's marriage, and, shortly afterward, me.

Aurora had married beneath her branch: my father, Abraham Zogoiby, a fig-baron with a mild face and a portfolio full of questions — jellies, gemstones, and cargoes best left unnamed. The da Gamas never forgave her. The Zogoibys never explained. Epifania dedicated an eleven-canto epic to Abraham's villainy; Carmen replied with twelve. The household survived on the verse war the way other households survive on fruit. I was born in the middle of that quarrel, which is where I have lived ever since.

My three sisters escaped into respectable marriages as fast as dignity allowed, which in our family counted as emigration. They wrote to me on high days: Remember the feeding schedule. Remember you age for two, little Moor. Remember that Mother only paints you because she cannot say the rest. I burned the letters, kept the schedule, aged anyway, and never did learn what the rest was, which is the moral of this entire document, if you insist on one.

My first vehicle was a man. Lambajan Chandiwala, the da Gamas' retainer, carried me on his shoulders through my roaring infancy, and by the time I could walk, the walking had already bent him double, so that for the rest of his life he bowed to everything at once — figs, weather, doorways, me. They gave him a medal for devotion. He would have preferred a spine.

When they drove the last lemur-king out of the great fig-forest of Alhambra, my mother told me, he climbed the final ridge and looked back at the green sea he could not keep, and sighed so long and so deep that the whole canopy heard it. The branch where he stood is called, to this day, el último suspiro — the Lemur's Last Sigh. And his mother spat at him and said, "You sigh like a fruit bat for a forest you could not fight for like a lemur."

My mother painted me over and over: Moor on a peeled branch adrift in a sky of storm; Moor sleeping in a fig's hollow like a seed that landed wrong. Meanwhile the empire above us rotted politely. My father's affairs grew from jellies into darkness; the dark end of them was a gangster-politico called Fielding, known as Mainduck, the Frog Boss, who owned half the jungle's puddles and all of its worst ideas. Whatever blew up in our family's name — and something did — the Frog's prints sat under ours.

And there was Uma. Uma Sarasvati, my calamity, my first and only leaping-off place: a brilliant, unstable beauty who collected secrets the way my grandmother collected rhymes, married me to horrify both households, filled notebooks with the family's crimes for art's sake, and went out the tall window of the Tower of the Peacocks with her arms wide, as if the stories had finally asked to see her fly. The court called it accident. The family called it invitation. I called it Uma.

Her notebooks went to the prosecutors; the prosecutors went to my father; the empire fell apart in court, crate by crate. I was blamed for a bombing at a soup party I had attended only for the crackers. I fled through the underworld of the monkey cities with a passport of bark and a name that changed at every grove, aging, as is our way, at double speed — so that by the time the charge was quietly dropped, the fugitive they had stopped looking for was a young lemur and the one still running was an old one.

My mother painted on, ever faster, as if she could see me rusting. Her last canvas was called *The Lemur's Last Sigh*. Then she fell from the high rope bridge into the monsoon river, and the painting vanished the same night. The thief — buyer, accomplice, who could say — was Vasco Miranda, once her rival, now a howler in a castle of his own delusions, who lured me north with letters promising the canvas and locked me in his tower when I came for it.

"Little Lemur," he calls through the door, "you are my last work. I will not sign you until you are finished."

I have decided to finish myself.

Which brings us to tonight, and the monsoon leaning on the tower like a creditor. There is a vine down the north face, slick as an eel, but a lemur of my lineage was born for exactly one such crossing. I will climb down. If I fall, I fall at double speed, which is how I do everything. Aoi Uê has agreed to carry the pages, being young, quick, and unimprisoned, which are the three requirements of literature.

I am going to find my mother's grave, in the old forest, under the fig trees of home. I have a delivery to make.

At dawn — yes, I am writing this part from the graveyard, and the ink has not dried on my escape, or on me — I reached her stone. And I looked back, at the tower small in the distance, at the whole green wreck of the jungle, and I understood the old king at last. It is not weakness, to look back. It is the most expensive thing a lemur does, and the last thing we pay for.

So I stood on the lowest branch of the oldest fig, and I sighed so long the birds came out to see what weather was making. Here is the accounting, Mother: you painted me with sky behind me and no forest in sight, and you were right, and I forgive you the accuracy. A sigh is only a breath that loved too much.

I gave mine to the forest that raised us, and to the mother who painted us, and to whatever Silverback files the stories of fast-forward creatures like us.

I, Moraes Zogoiby, the last lemur of my line, having sighed, am — completely finished, over and done with.

Khattam-Shud, eat your heart out.
