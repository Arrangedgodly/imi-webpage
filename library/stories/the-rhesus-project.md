# The Rhesus Project

*A parody of "The Rosie Project"*

Don Tillman, associate professor of rhesus genetics at Columbia University of the Canopy, was a macaque of routine, and the routine was excellent: 6:00 a.m., tai chi; 7:12 a.m., coffee (exactly 180 degrees); 8:00 a.m., teach *Genetics 101* to students who arrived late and wept about it; 5:30 p.m., karate; Thursday, groceries; Friday, one beer at the Wallingford Bar with Claudia and Gene, his only friends, who were married to each other and philosophically opposed to monogamy, respectively.

Don was, by his own measurement, a highly functional primate. He simply had no mate. He was also — though no diagnostician had ever managed to complete an interview with him — not neurotypical. Meetings distressed him; eye contact was a solved problem he saw no need to revisit; his schedule was less a habit than a skeleton, without which he would collapse into a pile of brilliant, quivering monkey.

"If you want a wife," said Gene, a cheerful, silver-tongued bonobo who hit on everything that moved and some plants, "quit waiting for Random Chance. Random Chance is a terrible matchmaker. Do what we do in the lab."

"Standardize the assay," said Don.

"Standardize the assay," said Gene.

And so, at 7:15 p.m. on a Tuesday, Don opened a spreadsheet and began The Wife Project: a sixteen-page, double-sided questionnaire covering medical history, alcohol tolerance, punctuality, kayak ownership, and fruit-ripeness preferences, distributed to females aged 25–38 with a completion deadline.

It did not go well. One candidate arrived nineteen minutes late and was disqualified before her coat was off. Another gave an excellent answer on statistics (Question 31) but believed in astrology (Question 9: automatic disqualification; Question 9b, "which monkey isn't," also disqualifying). A third spent the whole dinner scoring Don himself against a private questionnaire, which Don found inefficient and, later, alone, at 2 a.m., fair.

Then Rosie Jarman walked into his office.

She was a doctoral student in psychology — a wild-haired, red-furred, gloriously improper young chimp in motorcycle boots — and she was twenty minutes late, a smoker, a vegetarian by politics and a carnivore by appetite, and she had come, she announced, because his questionnaire said he was a genetics expert and she needed one.

"Your questionnaire's in the faculty lounge," she said. "It's a car crash. I came to watch. Also — my mother's dead and my father's a doctor I'm not allowed to name, and I want to know who he is. Can you do that? With science?"

"Illegitimate-paternity testing is a solved problem," said Don. "It requires the father's DNA."

"He's one of the medical-school class of '88. My mother's year. There are forty-two candidates and no names, only nicknames from her diary."

Don should have said no. Rosie failed the Wife Project at Question 1, subheading a, and Questions 2 through 16. Instead he heard himself say, "I have access to a sequencer and no moral position on subterfuge. The Father Project begins Monday."

---

The Father Project, Don soon discovered, was not a solved problem; it was a caper. Over the following weeks, Don and Rosie: infiltrated a class-of-'88 reunion with Don wearing a borrowed bow tie and an expression he had practiced in the mirror labeled CONFIDENCE (there was an incident with the champagne pyramid); harvested DNA from forty-two commemorative wine glasses in eleven minutes flat, Don's personal land-speed record; escaped through a kitchen, a loading dock, and, in Rosie's case, a hedge; and sequenced forty-two samples while Don's teaching schedule quietly collapsed around him like a scaffold.

"You know what I like about you, Don?" said Rosie, on the run, alive, teeth bared in the greatest grin in the five boroughs.

"I arrived on time. I brought a second pen."

"You don't pretend. Everyone else pretends. My whole family's a float in a parade, and you're just — here. Actually here."

Don experienced a sensation in the thorax. He considered Googling it. He did not Google it, which for Don was the emotional equivalent of a sonnet.

The results, when they came, were definitive and devastating: none of the forty-two matched. Rosie's father was not her father's-adjacent anyones; the match, when Don widened the search to the diary's second circle, was the one monkey no test was needed for — "Uncle" Phil, her mother's oldest friend, the gentle dentist who had paid Rosie's tuition and cried at her graduation. He came to the city, and Rosie asked him, shaking, and Phil said, "Thank God. Thirty years of wanting to tell you. Yes." It was the happiest result Don had ever generated, and he had once bred a strain of banana-resistant fruit flies.

But by then there was a problem Don could not standardize.

"I'm leaving," Rosie said, at the airport gate. "Melbourne. Postdoc. Don — you should know I've gone a bit strange about you. Which is inconvenient, because you're on a schedule and I'm a mess, and messes break schedules."

"Irrelevant," said Don, his heart slamming. "I've amended the schedule. I've been amending it since April. I jog at your pace. I eat at your hours. My Wednesday is now unscheduled. Rosie, I have no evidence I am capable of love, but I have a strong hypothesis, and the preliminary data are — significant — "

"You can't say things like that at a gate," said Rosie, crying, and got on the plane anyway, because that is what Jarman females do, and Don went home to an apartment that had a Rosie-shaped hole in it at every hour of the day.

---

He did not mope. Moping was inefficient. Instead, in secret, over five brutal weeks of a bicycle-powered, mirror-traumatizing curriculum, Don Tillman — who could not dance, had never danced, and held that dancing was unstandardized public chaos — learned to dance: specifically, the routine from Rosie's favorite film, to be deployed at the Genetics Faculty Ball, where the band, and half the department, and a very skeptical DJ, watched Professor Tillman take the floor alone in a borrowed tuxedo and execute, with the terrible gorgeous precision of a robot falling in love, the lift, the dip, and the spin.

Someone in the crowd was filming. Someone in the crowd was filming because someone in the crowd had flown back overnight, stood at the edge of the ballroom in a green dress with her mouth open, and said, very clearly, over the band: "You absolute *idiot*. You wonderful idiot."

The spin ended with Rosie in his arms, exactly as choreographed by nobody.

"Your questionnaire," she said, up close, "disqualifies me on fourteen counts."

"Fifteen," said Don. "Smoking. I have shredded the questionnaire. It was a flawed instrument. It measured Wife Material and failed to measure Rosie."

"Hypothesis," said Rosie. "Go on."

"That I love you, that the data are overwhelming, and that marriage, while irrational, is the only experiment I want to run for the rest of my life."

"Results pending," said Rosie, kissing him in front of the entire department of genetics, several of whom fainted, "but I like your odds, Professor."

---

The wedding was scheduled for the first Saturday in June, at 2:00 p.m. precisely, in the rose garden at the medical school, under a canopy, because Rosie had insisted on a canopy, and Don had agreed, because the schedule now belonged to two.

On the morning itself, at 6:00 a.m., Don did tai chi, drank 180-degree coffee, and ironed his own shirt badly on purpose, because Rosie would find the crease funny. Then he opened his laptop, titled a fresh spreadsheet, and began designing the assay for their next great collaboration.

He called it The Baby Project.

Rosie, reading over his shoulder an hour later in her dress, deleted row 1 ("ovulation window — already pregnant, Professor") and laughed until the garden shook, and Don Tillman, neuro-atypical, madly in love, and on schedule for the first time in his life, kissed his prime mate as the band played and forty-two geneticists wept.
