# The Monkey's Faux Paw

*A parody of "The Monkey's Paw"*

Night. A cold wet night in the Laburnum Grove, and in the small parlor of the Whites' nest, Mr. White and his son Herbert were playing chess under the lamplight, while Mrs. White sat by the fire knitting and pretending, as mothers do, not to watch.

"Listen to the wind," said Mr. White, and moved his bishop into a trap so obvious that Herbert laughed out loud.

"I should hardly think he'll come tonight," said Mrs. White, meaning the guest they were expecting.

"Mind the branch," said Herbert, as something scraped the gutter overhead.

The knocker rapped, and Mrs. White returned with the visitor: Sergeant-Major Morris, an old mandrill, gray about the muzzle, his famous face red and blue and wise and sad, a face like a church window that had seen some things. He sat, accepted the whiskey and the chair nearest the fire, and answered their questions about the world with the politeness of a monkey who would rather discuss anything else.

"And the faux paw," said Herbert, at last, boldly. "The thing you told Father about. The monkey's faux paw."

"Nothing," said the Sergeant-Major, quickly. "Leastways, nothing worth hearing."

"Monkey business," said Herbert, wickedly. "Surely a paw — a faux paw, I stand corrected — isn't beyond a good story."

The old mandrill took the thing from his pocket and set it on the table between them, and they all leaned in. It was a small dried paw, curled and mummified, so like a monkey's paw and so unlike one that the eye kept sliding off it — a counterfeit, a paw that had been made, not lost.

"Faux," said Morris, "because it is false, and because no monkey makes a mistake with it that is not final. It was dressed with magic by an old fakir langur, to show that fate ruled monkeys' lives and that those who interfered did so to their sorrow. Three separate owners could each have three wishes from it. That is how he put it."

"And has anybody wished?" said Mrs. White, in a hush. "I mean, used it?"

"The first owner's wishes were his own," said Morris. "I don't know what the first two were, but the third was for death. The paw passed to me for a moment when I'd have liked it longer. I have had my three wishes. I won't say what they were."

"Then it's done its work," said Mr. White. "It's finished, surely."

"It has caused enough mischief," agreed Morris, "and it can cause more. I keep it only to remind me. If you must wish," he added, looking at Mr. White with sudden severity, "don't wish for anything sensible."

He tossed the paw suddenly onto the fire. Mr. White, moving with an agility that surprised everyone including himself, snatched it out with a poker and stood coughing over it, the fur of his wrist singed.

"Better let it burn," said the Sergeant-Major, quietly.

"If you don't want it, Morris," said Mr. White, "let me have it. I only ask."

Morris sighed the sigh of a mandrill who has given this speech before. "Take it. But take the consequences. It will bring you sorrow, cleverly, the way a vine finds a window. I warn you, White: don't blame me if you go wishing. Monkey see, monkey wish — that is the whole of the curse in four words. And whatever you do, wish like a frightened monkey, not a clever one. Clever is what it eats."

He took his glass, said good night, and went out into the wet, and they watched his lantern dwindle down the grove.

"I dare say I'll never see him again," said Mrs. White.

"He's gone to his tree, likely," said Herbert. "Well, Father — you've got your talisman. What are you going to wish for?"

Mr. White turned the paw over in his fingers. "It looks like an ordinary paw," he said slowly. "I hardly know what to wish for. It seems to me I've got all I want."

"If you cleared the grove mortgage off with the paw," said Herbert, wicked again, "you'd be a happy old chimp. Wish for two hundred pounds of bananas. Then, my boy, you could pay off the house and sit in a hammock the rest of your days, being peeled and fed by servants."

Mr. White smiled, held up the paw, and — half laughing, wholly careless, in the way the paw likes — said: "I wish for two hundred pounds of bananas."

The paw moved. All three of them heard it — a little wet flex, like a knuckle cracking in the next room. Then it lay still and dead in his hand.

"I declare," said Mr. White, shaken, "it moved. Or I imagined it."

"Trick of the firelight," said Herbert, less certainly.

"First wish down," said Mrs. White, briskly, gathering the cups. "And nothing to show for it but a fright. Bed, the pair of you."

Herbert lingered at the door, grinning. "Well, I expect I'll be a holiday hamster by morning — four hundred pounds, half for the mortgage, half for me. If the bananas come at midnight, I want first pick." And he went up whistling, and they heard his branch creak as he settled, and slept.

In the morning a brightness over the world; a wet clean grove; a joke at breakfast. "I suppose," said Herbert, "you're still two hundred pounds of bananas shy of a wish come true." He kissed his mother, ruffled his father's fur, and loped off to the depot of the firm of Paw & Meggins, engineers and dealers in hard fruit.

He did not come home to lunch. He did not come home to supper.

At half past eight, when the lamp was lit and Mrs. White had begun to say his name in the tone she reserved for worry, a stranger came knocking: a visitor in black waterproofs and a bowler hat, with a face like a proboscis monkey doing its very best to look kind — a solicitor's man, very sorry to disturb them. There had been an accident at the works.

"Is he hurt?" Mrs. White asked.

The visitor's silence answered for him.

"He was caught in the machinery," he said at last. "The sorting press. It was quick — the firm wishes me to say quick, and I believe it was. The firm wishes me to convey their sincere sympathy. Paw & Meggins are not, in any sense, to blame. No payment could be considered, of course."

"But there is compensation," said Mr. White, hoarse, not knowing why he said it.

The visitor looked miserable, and prim, and cornered. "The firm," he said, "wishes me to state that, in consideration of your son's services, they will present you with a sum."

Mr. White put out a hand to the table. "How much?"

"Two hundred pounds," said the visitor. "Of bananas. The first consignment arrives in the morning."

Mr. White dropped the paw on the floor with a small dry sound, and put his hands over his face, and his wife, swaying, took him in her arms, and the visitor hurried out into the rain as if all the trees of the grove were after him.

That night the old couple sat up with their grief, and at some black hour Mrs. White sat bolt upright.

"The paw," she said. "The second wish. We can have him back."

"My God — you don't mean—"

"He was quick, they said. It was quick. The other one — was quick." Her eyes were terrible and bright. "The paw. Where is the paw?"

Downstairs, by the dying fire, she found it, and her husband begged her, and she would not hear. She held it up. "I wish my son alive again."

The paw moved. Somewhere down the grove, in the wet, a knuckle cracked.

The wind died. The clock ticked. Mr. White sat in the dark chair with his heart going like a hammer in a sack, thinking of the sorting press and what it leaves, of the creak of a branch overhead, and of the lantern thirty yards away that could not, could not, be knocked at by that.

The knock came. Soft. Patient.

At the door. At the door of the house.

Mrs. White rose. "It's my boy! It's Herbert!" she cried. "I'm coming, Herbert, I'm coming!"

"The bolt," her husband croaked, catching her arm — and the thing outside knocked again, and he flung her back, and she fought him with sudden mad strength, all fur and grief, crying her son's name into the rain.

"The candle," she gasped. "Strike a match — the candle! I can't reach the bolt—"

And Mr. White, alone with the paw on the floor, did the only sensible thing a frightened old monkey has ever done with it. He found it by feel. He thought: it is my son at the door — and it is not my son at the door — and it will be, it will be, whichever I choose, forever.

He wished.

The knocking stopped.

When Mrs. White, weeping, wrenched the door wide, the road stood empty. The rain had thinned to a mist. The lamp at the corner of the grove threw its light across wet gravel, all the way to the trees, and there was nothing there — nothing but the night, and the wind in the branches, and, far off, so faint it might have been a memory, the sound of a small dry paw, curling.
