# Scruff

*A parody of "Snuff"*

It was a moonlit night. That old song. As though murder had the decency to check the weather first.

Commander Sir Samuel Vimes, Duke of Ankh, had been banished to the countryside. His wife, Lady Sybil — a gorilla of enormous heart, fortune, and lung capacity — had decreed a holiday at Ramkin Hall, the family estate, on the grounds that even the most stubborn policeman in the world deserved fresh air, green hills, and six weeks away from criminals. Vimes regarded this as exile among a different sort of ape: the kind with land.

The countryside, in Vimes's professional opinion, was a crime scene waiting for a map. Too many hedges. Too many places to hide a body. And the locals kept smiling at him in a way that in Ankh-Morpork generally preceded an invoice.

"Try to relax, dear," said Sybil. "Breathe. Gaze at the view."

"The view," said Vimes, "has line of sight on three unlocked barns."

Still, he tried. He played cricket with the squire's monkeys, badly and honestly. He attended dinners where he was introduced as "Sam" to people who'd spent forty years calling monkeys like him "the scruff." And each morning his son Young Sam, six years old and nature-mad, presented him proudly with the day's findings.

"Dad! Dad! Badger poo! Fresh!" cried Young Sam, holding it in both paws like a relic.

"Lovely," said Vimes, who had once fought a war and would rather have done it again than deal with the countryside's attitude to poo.

The butler, Willikins — a marmoset of immaculate tailoring who had carried Vimes out of a burning building and, in a prior career, removed a rival's kneecaps with a sharpened spoon — oversaw the estate with the serenity of a blade in a drawer. His ice knife lived in the ice bucket, where guests assumed it was for the wine.

It was Young Sam, naturally, who found the body first — or rather, the place where one had been. Down by the old well on the boundary of Ramkin land, in the tangle where the wild bananas grew, the earth had been disturbed, and in the hedge hung a tiny locket made of folded tin and beetle-wing, the kind of thing a tarsier makes out of the world's garbage and calls beautiful.

Tarsiers. Huge-eyed, gentle, night-folk, despised everywhere as vermin. In the eyes of the law — and Vimes felt the law's gaze slide away as he thought it — they were not people at all. Not murdered. Merely pest control.

"That," said Vimes quietly, to the hills, "is where you're wrong."

His wife sighed the sigh of a gorilla who has married a weathervane and knows which way the wind is blowing. "You're going to investigate, aren't you."

"There's been a death, Sybil. I'm a policeman. Being on holiday is like being dead. You either are or you aren't."

The local constable turned out to be Feeney Upshot, a colobus of about nineteen who had inherited the job, the helmet, and his grandmother's bicycle. Feeney deputized Vimes on the spot, which Vimes found touching, in the way that a drowning monkey finds a barnacle touching.

The trail unpeeled like a bad banana. The dead tarsier girl had been part of a shipment — and there had been many shipments. Somebody was raiding the tarsier dens, taking the little folk by the scruff of their necks, cage by cage, and selling them south. Sold to whom? To the great banana plantations of Howondaland, where labor that costs nothing is worth stealing, and where nobody asks a tarsier to sign anything because nobody has ever agreed a tarsier can.

Behind it all stood respectability. It usually did. The local magistrate, the plantation agents, a shipping family of excellent teeth: all of them could look a duke in the eye at dinner and speak feelingly about tradition, and law, and how the little folk were honestly better off.

"Better off," repeated Vimes, in a voice like a cellar door.

The beast stirred in him then — the Guarding Dark, the black dog he kept chained behind his ribs, which howled at cruelty the way other hounds howl at the moon. It whispered to him that he was a long way from his city, alone, angry, with a knife-wielding butler and a borrowed bicycle, and that the men who had done this had done it a hundred times.

Good, said the Guarding Dark. Then we are owed a hundred.

"No," said Vimes out loud, alone on the dark lawn, to the thing inside himself, as he had said every night for thirty years. "We do it by the book. The book is the whole point."

The book led to the river.

The smugglers were loading the last barge that night — forty cages of tarsiers bound downriver for the coast, and in the biggest cage, blinking in the lantern light, a tarsier girl named Tears of the Mushroom, who had been taken for the same reason a magpie takes a diamond: because her people had made her a harp out of wire and wishbones, and her music was the one treasure worth stealing.

What the smugglers had not factored into their timetable was (a) a duke with insomnia, (b) a butler, and (c) the river.

There is a chase they still tell of in the Crundee hills. The barge went tearing downriver in the dark, poles and lanterns flying, and behind it came a commandeered ferry, its boiler overwrought, its wheel held by a colobus who had never driven anything faster than gossip. At the prow stood Vimes, badge in one paw and rage in the other, and at his shoulder stood Willikins, who had asked, very politely, if he might deal with the gentlemen with the poles.

"Ladies and gentlemen," said Willikins, producing from his sleeve a blade thin as a truth and twice as sharp, "I was not always a butler. Please do not make me nostalgic."

The river did the rest, as rivers will when asked politely by an angry man. The barge struck the weir. The cages came open. And forty tarsiers stood up in the shallows in the moonlight and looked at the monkeys of the shire — the farmers and the hunters and the gentry dragged out of bed by the noise — and one of them began, very softly, to play.

Tears of the Mushroom played the harp her grandmother had strung from the wreckage of the world, and she played it in front of monkeys who had spent generations calling her kind vermin, and she played what the river says, and what a dead girl's locket says, and what the morning says when you have survived the night. Hard men wept into the water. Feeney's helmet fell off. Lady Sybil, arriving at the bank in her dressing gown, cried like a geyser and began, immediately, to organize.

They tried to hold the trial in the old way — no persons, no murder, no case. It did not go well for the old way. The Duke of Ankh stood up in court with forty witnesses on the benches behind him, a tin locket in his fist, and a small constable in an oversized helmet taking notes that would become a famous examination answer, and he asked the assembled shire the only question that mattered:

"When you buried that girl in my hedge and called it nothing, which of you checked? Which of you even looked?" And then, quietly, "The law is a mirror, gentlemen. It shows you your face. Have a look."

The gavel came down the way weather does. Personhood, said the court. Retroactively, and with apologies, which the law is not supposed to make but did.

The plantation men went down for a very long time. The magistrate's excellent teeth were last seen in a Howondaland quarry, smiling at a rock pile. And on a green hill above the river, on land Lady Sybil had granted with the stroke of a pen and the full terrifying weight of gorilla hospitality, the tarsiers built a town of marvelous rubbish: lockets and lantern-towers, harps and bridges, a post box, a school, and, within the month, by popular demand, a tiny police house.

Young Sam was given the grand tour and came back ecstatic with a jar of authenticated tarsier poo. Vimes took a walk at sunset with Sybil, who observed that he had gone away on holiday and somehow come back with a whole new people.

"That's the countryside for you," said Vimes. "Full of surprises."

"You hate the countryside."

"Yeah," said Vimes, looking at the little lights coming on in the rubbish town, and the harp starting up somewhere, and Young Sam dancing badly. "But I'm growing fond of the neighbors."

And in Ramkin Hall, in the ice bucket, Mister Whistler the knife waited politely for a better world, and was — for the present — merely excellent with the wine.
