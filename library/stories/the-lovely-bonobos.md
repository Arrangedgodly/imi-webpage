# The Lovely Bonobos

*A parody of "The Lovely Bones"*

My name was Bonobo, Susie Bonobo, "like the ape that makes love, not war," which is what my father always said, and which I did not entirely understand on December 6, 1973, when I was fourteen.

That was the day I took the shortcut home through the bamboo.

My neighbor, Mr. Harvey, had built a little hideout in the stand of bamboo behind the corn — bamboo he'd cut and lashed himself, which should have told somebody something. He had a way of building things: dollhouses out of seed pods, tents, underground dens. "I've made a little hiding place," he said to me, standing in the dusk with his hands folded like a polite old chimp, which he was not, entirely. "Come and see."

"Don't make me tickle you," he said, when I backed away.

I will not tell you what happened in the bamboo, because I don't have to, and because the last thing he took from me was the telling. There was a charm bracelet on my wrist — a little banana, a tiny monkey with jointed arms — and there was an elbow, mine, that he forgot in the dark, and then I was in my heaven.

My heaven was the canopy I never got to climb. There were fig trees always in fruit, a grooming line that went on forever, and a troop that never once found anything to criticize in my fur. Franny, the intake counselor, an old bonobo with reading glasses she didn't need, told me the rules on my first day: "You get the heaven you build, sweetheart, and the door out opens when you stop wanting the door out." My roommate Holly planted bamboo around our fig trees, to be ironic, and I loved her for it. Everything I wanted stayed one branch out of reach — which, I learned, is what makes a heaven a heaven.

I watched. That is the main thing the dead do, if we are honest. I watched Detective Len Fenerman come to the house with his little notebook and his kind, tired shoulders, and put my charm bracelet's missing banana in a bag, and never find enough. I watched my father in the garage, taking his sap-bottle ships — perfect little pods with sails of woven grass — and smashing them, one by one, as he tried to make sense of a neighbor with a polite face. He was the one who worked it out, on his nightly run, running until the answer came up alongside him: the man built dens. The man had built the den. He went after Mr. Harvey with Buckley's lacrosse stick in the field of rustling corn, and his heart, which had been keeping his grief on schedule all that year, gave out in the furrow — and the neighbors came, and the ambulance came, and he lived, and the whole troop finally had something to do.

I watched my sister Lindsey grow a hard shell around her heart and then crack it open herself, bravely, sneaking into Mr. Harvey's den while he was away, with her boyfriend Samuel holding the lantern, and tearing a page from his sketchbook: the bamboo hideout, the staging pen, the flags in the yard like a troop of one declaring war. She ran with that page in her fist like it was my hand. And once — I have never told her this — she took the penalty kick in the county final with the whole troop watching, and for the length of that run I was inside her leg, and the goal was mine as much as hers, and the dead do not get many celebrations, so I have made that one last. Years later Samuel swerved his motorcycle to miss a deer with Lindsey on the back, and I watched them skid into the ditch holding on to each other, and I understood that my sister had decided to grow up, which is the bravest thing a surviving sibling can do, and the rudest, and the best.

I watched my little brother Buckley grow from a small ape who fed the geese into a boy who, one spring, stood in the garden with a young fig tree and asked to plant it for me, and my father said no, not that one, and Buckley said, "She's dead, Dad. You're the only one who still talks to her," and my father, who did not know grief could reach him from two directions, threw a clod of the garden at his living son, and then held him in the dirt, and let us both be planted. Some of the dead watch for vengeance. I was watching for moments like that.

I watched Ray Singe, the langur from my school, the boy who had pressed a note into my textbook the day before, which I never answered. He liked my fur, he'd written, "which is the color of good soil," and he hoped I did not mind him saying so. I minded very much that I never answered it. And I watched Ruth Connors, the strange pale girl no troop claimed, who had been walking the edge of the corn that evening and collected my passing like a burr on her sleeve — who filled notebook after notebook with poems about the dead and carried my weight in her chest for eight years, while Ray, who had loved me one branch out of his reach, carried hers.

And I watched my mother go. She went to the far forest, to the orchard jobs, because grief in a small troop is a spotlight and she could not live in it. My grandmother Lynn moved in, drank gin out of a jelly jar, taught Lindsey to plant and Buckley to lie beautifully, and played the ponies at the county track with a beret on. My mother came back, in the end, the night my father's heart broke in the corn; she came down the rows at a run, and something she had left on the west coast never came back with her, which is another way of saying she was home.

They never found me. That is the part the living hate. He had locked me in a safe and dropped it in the sinkhole out past Flanigan's quarry. They drained the sinkhole once and found an old tire. And then, the year the county decided to fill the sinkhole in — truck after truck of development dirt, the developers with their clipboards ticking the past off a list — I stood at the front of my heaven and screamed at the earth to open, at the truck to tip, at anyone. The safe went under the fill and the grass grew over it like a held breath. Somewhere under there I am still fourteen. The earth does not give things back because you are right.

But here is the thing about the dead: on the last day, I got my turn. Ruth stood at the sinkhole with Ray Singe at her shoulder, and for one moment I slid down through her like rain through leaves. I took her hands and put them in Ray's.

I kissed him. I kissed him the way I had been saving up in heaven, which is a long time to save anything. Then I let go of Ruth, went up, and the horizon of my heaven opened like a fig in season, and I stopped watching the world so hard, because I had finally been in it.

Mr. Harvey got his, by the way. Not from the police. From a coconut. He was stalking a young troop in a parking lot far from home, under a palm tree, when a coconut — big, green, hard as a fist and dumb as luck — came loose in the wind, caught him on the shoulder, and knocked him into the ravine, where nobody bothered to look, because he was nobody's neighbor anymore.

My name is Susie Bonobo, "like the ape that makes love, not war." I wish you all a long and happy life.
