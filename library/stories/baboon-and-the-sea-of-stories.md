# Baboon and the Sea of Stories

*A parody of "Haroun and the Sea of Stories"*

In a jungle so thick that even the sunlight had to climb, there lived a baboon named Rashid Khalifa, called the Shah of Blah — the finest storyteller of the age, a monkey so full of tales that fruit sellers paid him in figs just to hear the endings. His son was named Baboon, which was, everyone agreed, the most honest name ever given.

Baboon's mother, Soraya, ran off one moonless night with Mr. Sengupta, a neighbor with a narrow face and a ledger for a heart. "What's the use of stories?" Sengupta had asked, sniffing. "Are they sweet? Do they fill an empty belly? Can you trade them for figs?"

And Baboon, miserable and rude, had said nearly the same thing to his father — *what's the use of stories that aren't even true?* — and the words landed on Rashid's gift like frost on a flower. At the next rally, before the fruit sellers and the vote-counters, the Shah of Blah opened his mouth and produced, from the great silence inside him, nothing at all. The politician on the platform beside him smiled the smile of a monkey whose ledger was winning.

That night a small blue figure appeared on their window-branch with a toolbox: Iff, a fig genie, come to disconnect Rashid's invisible Tap of Story Juice, per regulations.

"Disconnect it and I'll disconnect you," said Baboon.

"Everything," Iff said stiffly, "is P2C2E. Processes Too Complicated for Even a Primate to Explain."

But Iff, who was softer than his toolbox, agreed to take Baboon to the Earth's second moon, Kahani, to file a complaint. They flew on Butt the Hoopoe, a yolk-crested bird whose name meant "rear end" and whose flying meant it too.

"What is the matter with you?" Baboon asked over the first cloud.

"The matter with me," said Butt the Hoopoe, "is that I am equipped with a brain and required to use it, which in my experience ruins birds."

On Kahani's sunlit face lay Gup City, the City of Chatterers, where everyone talked at once, arguments were the national sport, and Baboon had never felt so at home in his life. Ten monkeys spoke to him simultaneously and each considered himself the only one talking. The Library Council sat in permanent session; General Kitab swore by the whiskers of every book he loved, which took a while. Attending Baboon at the Library was his page, a small sharp monkey called Blabbermouth, who carried messages, juggled figs, and never, ever blabbered.

Baboon filed his complaint first thing. The Complaints Office of Gup City accepted the form, stamped it in four colors, lost it, found it, read it aloud, objected to its meter, and referred it to a subcommittee of ten, each of whom wished to speak at length about his own complaint instead. By the time the complaint was officially understood, the war it complained about had already started, which the Guppees considered excellent timing.

Below the city stretched the Sea of Stories: the Ocean of the Streams of Story, ten thousand thousand tales, every current a different story, sweet to the tongue if you knew which stream to sip. Plentimaw groupers swam there, inhaling many stories at once and blowing them out mixed, like stew.

But the sea was sick. In the twilight zones the currents ran gray, and Plentimaws surfaced coughing. Out on the poisoned water floated the Dark Ship Bezban — which in old monkey speech means Tongueless Town — a vessel woven entirely of shadow-thread, stitched by the Cultmaster's tailors. On it stood Khattam-Shud, the Prince of Silence, the Anti-Story — a huge cold monkey, or the shadow of one. In old monkey speech, his name means Completely Finished, Over and Done With.

"Every story corrupts," said Khattam-Shud. "Every tale told makes a monkey want another. I shall plug the Wellsprings, poison the sea, and after the last story dies there will be silence — perfect, orderly, silent order — forever."

"You," said Baboon, "have clearly never been to Gup City."

His guards were Chupwalas with their lips stitched shut for uniformity, which cut down on workplace chatter and everything else. His mines ate story-water and spat gray. And his finest general was Mudra, the shadow-dancer, who spoke only in gesture — Abhinaya, the language of the body, which any monkey can read: a flick of the wrist for *war*, a bowed head for *tired*, a slow clenched fist for *this job, this endless job, in the dark, for a boss made of nothing*.

The Guppees declared war, then debated the declaration for two days, which everyone agreed was the most Guppee thing ever done. Prince Bolo, a pea-brain with a title, wept for his fiancée, Princess Batchee, held captive in the Twilight Strip. And in Chup City, under the ice, no monkey spoke above a breath — which was, Baboon reflected, the scariest politics imaginable: a whole city on mute.

Baboon sailed the poisoned sea with a small crew: Iff, Butt, and Mali the Floating Gardener, a walking topiary of good intentions who weeded the story-streams and wept gently at gray water. Blabbermouth stowed aboard, and when a wave took Baboon's grip, it was the small sharp page who hauled him back by the tail with a face that said *we will not discuss this.*

"I'm a girl," Blabbermouth said at last, daring him to make something of it. "Pages aren't supposed to be girls. So I decided to be the best boy there ever was, which was easy, because I'm excellent."

"Filed under P2C2E," said Iff, moved.

They slipped into the Twilight Strip, past the stitched-lip sentries, and opened Batchee's cage, and Batchee boxed Bolo's ears for taking so long, which in Chup is a marriage proposal. On the way back the battle began, and it turned at dawn: shadows are strong at night and nothing at noon. Mudra turned on his master, because the shadows had served silence for years and silence had never once served them; he signed *enough* with his whole body, and his shadow legions signed it after him. When the sun cleared the ice, Khattam-Shud unraveled. A shadow without a body at noon is only a rumor, and the Chupwalas bit their stitches out laughing to hear it.

Then Baboon dove into the Wellsprings, past the gray, past the cold, to the Source at the bottom of everything, where the plugs sat like corks in the world's imagination, and he pulled them, and the sea came back bright, ten thousand currents running sweet. Mali weeded out the last of the poison. The Plentimaws blew rainbows.

He used his wish to turn the moon so Gup always had day — and Blabbermouth was made First Page of the Library, and required to talk. And the sea itself granted the second wish, the one for a happy ending, because a Sea of Stories loves nothing more than a good plot.

Home again, Rashid told the whole adventure at the assembly without missing a beat, and the story came out of him with the mother and the genie and the war in it, and the crowd went sincerely bananas, and the politician had to admit that stories had their uses after all, having been outvoted by one. Soraya came home too — Sengupta had run off with a lady baboon who shared his interest in compound interest — and she sang in the evenings again.

"Father," Baboon asked one night, "are your stories true?"

"True enough to save a sea of them, little one," said the Shah of Blah.

And somewhere below, a blue genie stamped the file closed: P2C2E.
