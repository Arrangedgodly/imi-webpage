# Five Chimps and a Nit

*A parody of "Five Children and It"*

The five chimp siblings were sent to the riverbank to dig for grubs, the worst chore in the troop, until their shovels (sticks, technically) hit something soft and grumpy.

"Stop that," said the something.

It was round as a coconut and gray as old fur, with long whiskers, eyes on stalks like a snail's, and the expression of a great-aunt interrupted mid-nap for sixty years.

"Are you a nit?" asked Anthea politely, because she was the polite one.

"I am THE Nit," it said. "Once there were nits in every troop's fur in England. We granted wishes to anybody who dug us up politely. There were nits in castles. Nits in palaces. Queens kept us in velvet combs. Now look at me. Buried under a riverbank. Combed from history." It eyed them. "You may have one wish a day, all together, and mind: whatever I grant turns back to nothing at sunset. And do NOT try to groom me. I bite."

The five — Cyril, Anthea, Robert, Jane, and baby Nanner — held a meeting on the spot, which consisted of everybody talking at once.

"I wish," announced Robert, who had thought hardest, "that we were beautiful."

The Nit puffed itself up, and suddenly the five of them gleamed like sunrise — fur of gold and silver, coats silky as spider silk, tails like candles. They paraded home to the troop's great fig tree.

"STRANGE MONKEYS!" screamed Aunt Mangle, and drove them off with a stick. "GOLD VAGRANTS! SHOO!"

"But it's US!" cried Jane.

"Never seen you in my life," said Aunt Mangle. "My nieces are ordinary — and no dinner for strange golden monkeys."

They sat hungry in the branches, gorgeous and unrecognized, until the sun touched the treetops — and the gold melted off like butter, and five ordinary chimps climbed down to leftovers and very superior looks from Aunt Mangle.

The next morning Nanner, who was two and did not understand the etiquette of wishing, toddled up to the Nit alone.

"I wiss EVERYBODY WANTED ME," said Nanner.

By noon the whole troop wanted Nanner — to groom her, squeeze her, feed her, carry her, bounce her, and fight over her — and by late afternoon Nanner was the hemisphere's most loved and most exhausted baby, screaming "NO MORE WISS!" as a wall of aunts advanced with grooming fingers.

That was the afternoon the traveling baboon troupe came through, with their drums and their painted faces and their little caravan of vines. And of course they saw Nanner too — a baby so charming that total strangers fought over her — and their leader, a grand baboon with a silver ruff, made them an offer.

"Friends," he said to the troop, "we have traveled from the mountains to the sea, and never have we seen a baby so beloved. We shall take her. Not steal — TAKE, which is different when done artistically. She will be our mascot. She will ride on the drum."

"She is not for taking," said the wall of aunts — but they could not quite close ranks, because each of them was privately relieved to have a turn coming, eventually, once the baboons were gone.

"They're taking her to the mountains!" shrieked Jane, and the four of them pelted after the caravan, which was making surprisingly good time for a conveyance made of drama.

They caught it at the ford. Anthea, being quickest with words, planted herself in front of the silver-ruffed leader.

"You don't understand," she said. "She's only this wonderful because of a wish. When the sun sets, she turns back into an ordinary baby. A plain one. A baby that screams for grubs and bites."

The baboon looked doubtful. "She would still be a baby," he said. "Mascotry is mostly screaming."

"She bites HARD," said Robert.

"She has already bitten me," admitted a young drummer, holding up a finger.

"And," said Cyril, who had thought hardest again, "when the sun sets, so does our wish, and the wishing wears off the taker too — you'll be stuck on a mountain with a drum you don't want."

The sun was, in fact, just then sliding toward the treetops like an orange being peeled in slow motion. The whole troupe stopped to watch it. Nanner, on the drum, seized her moment and bit the drum, which deflated with a heartbreaking note.

"TAKE HER," said the leader, with dignity. "The mountains have plenty of babies."

At sunset the wanting evaporated, Nanner was merely Nanner again, and she hid in Jane's lap for an hour, emerging only to say, "Never again," which everyone agreed was a fine plan that would last until morning.

"For riches this time," said Cyril firmly, the day after. "Wish for riches. I wish we were rich — gold, treasure, everything."

The Nit inflated, and around them rose a hill of golden bananas, glowing like little suns. They marched to the market with their arms full.

"Golden bananas!" cried the shop-gorilla. "Painted, more like. FAKE 'NANAS! Where'd you steal 'em, you little criminals?" And the troop guard confiscated the lot, plus the hill, and at sunset every gold banana winked out of existence — including the ones the guard had already eaten, which he never forgave.

They also wished — the following day, and nobody now remembers whose fault it was — to be GROWN-UP. It seemed foolproof. Grown-ups ate when they wanted and nobody told them to wash their paws. The Nit puffed, and four tall, serious, heavy-browed chimps stood on the riverbank feeling extremely dignified for nearly an hour.

Then the troop council spotted them.

"Excellent," said the head of council. "New adults. You can carry the fig-buckets. And you — settle the boundary dispute at the third branch. And you — you're on tick-watch tonight. And one of you needs to listen to Aunt Mangle's opinion of the young these days, it's been weeks, it isn't fair to her."

By teatime they had been assigned, blamed, taxed, invited to three meetings, and asked to hold four babies, one of which was Nanner, who bit them. Being grown-up, they discovered, was mostly being tired with better posture. They sat under a bush waiting for sunset like four prisoners waiting for a pardon, and when the wish finally drained out of them and they were small again, they ran home to be fed, which is the whole point of being children.

"Time was," grumbled the Nit, "monkeys wished for PROPER things. A dry nest. A tall tree. A moderate number of fleas. Now it's beauty, riches, wantedness, adulthood. Feh."

"Wings," said Jane dreamily. "I wish we had wings."

That one was glorious. For one whole afternoon the five chimps soared over the canopy like bright birds, racing swifts, tickling clouds, looking down on the river like a brown ribbon and the sea like the sky's twin. It was the best afternoon of their lives, which is why they entirely forgot the sunset.

The sun sank while they were a hundred feet above the river.

The wings fell out of their feathers like autumn.

Five splashes later — counting Nanner, who had been carrying Cyril instead of the other way round — a bedraggled, river-weedish family of chimps dragged themselves up the bank, where the Nit sat waiting, dry and smug.

"You knew," spluttered Robert, wringing his tail.

"I always know," said the Nit. "It's the best part of the job."

"Isn't there anything you can't do?" asked Anthea.

"Not get combed from history," said the Nit, and looked so briefly, terribly old that none of them laughed.

On the last day, they held a final meeting. "We could wish for anything," said Anthea slowly. "A treehouse with a fig elevator. Never to be itchy again. To be big as gorillas."

"But everything we wish goes wrong," said Jane.

"Wrong at sunset," agreed Cyril.

So instead, when they dug the Nit up politely one last time, Anthea cleared her throat and said, "We wish you a good sleep, and a comfortable bank, and — well — thank you."

The Nit's stalk-eyes blinked. In sixty years of granting, nobody had ever wished anything FOR it.

"Hmph," it said, in a rather wobbly voice, and puffed itself up one final time — and somewhere that evening, in a burrow no chimp ever found again, a very old nit fell asleep smiling.

The five walked home without a single wish, which felt, for once, exactly enough.
