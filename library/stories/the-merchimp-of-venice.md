# The Merchimp of Venice

*A parody of "The Merchant of Venice"*

In the swamp city of Venis, where the mango canals ran between the roots and every gondola was a grapevine punt, there lived a melancholy macaque named Antonio, the Merchimp of Venis. He owned a fleet of banana barges, and he was sad, though he could not say why. Perhaps, his friends said, he was simply tired of bananas.

"Horn of plenty, Antonio, why so down in the tail?" asked his dearest friend, Bassanape, a handsome young ape with expensive tastes and empty fruit-stores.

"Bassanape, I do not know. My spirit goes ape with worry for my barges. But tell me — why do you look worse than a plucked tamarin?"

"Because I am in love with Portia of Belmont Bough, the fairest capuchin in the canopy, and to win her I need three thousand bananas for feathers and grooming. Her father left a puzzle: any suitor must choose between three gourds — gold, silver, and lead — and only one holds her portrait. Choose wrong, and you are banished from her branch forever, sworn never to woo again."

"Three gourds?" crowed Gratianape, swinging in. "That's just banana roulette."

"My barges are all at harvest," said Antonio. "But my credit swings wide. We shall borrow from Shylocko."

Now at Belmont Bough, the suitors were already queuing. The Prince of Morocco, a magnificent mandrill in a rainbow of his own face, chose the golden gourd — "the portrait of so rare a monkey deserves nothing less than gold" — and found inside a coconut husk and a scroll: *All that yellows is not ripe banana.* He left, still explaining himself, and nobody listened.

The Prince of Arragon, a languid silverleaf, chose the silver gourd, "for I deserve no less" — and found the portrait of a jester's cap, grinning up at him. *Some monkeys deserve exactly what they think they do,* said the scroll. He left with his dignity, or most of it.

Bassanape, when his turn came, walked past the gold and the silver and chose the dull lead gourd, whose scroll read: *Who chooses me must give and hazard all.* Inside lay Portia's portrait, and Portia herself, who said, "I am not half so fine as you are about to tell me I am, and I would keep you here for half my bananas if you would stay for all of me."

"I'll stay for all of you," said Bassanape, "and it is I who am honored."

"Gladly," said Portia. "But no half measures."

Meanwhile, in Venis, Bassanape's old servant Launcelot Baboonio was having an argument with himself in an alleyway — one half in a fancy collar, one half in rags, representing his conscience and his appetite.

"Run to Bassanape," said the conscience. "Stay with Shylocko," said the appetite. "Flee this fiend of hunger," said the conscience. "He does feed you," said the appetite. Launcelot solved it the traditional way, which was to go where the food was, and to describe himself ever after as a philosopher.

Shylocko, meanwhile, was a mandrill moneylender with a muzzle like a thunderstorm and a memory like an elephant's. "You called me a banana-hoarding baboon. You spat fig seeds upon my striped coat. You lent bananas free of interest and undercut my business. And now — now! — you come to me, hat in hand, tail between legs."

"Shylocko," said Antonio, "I would lend to you my own enemy the same way. I spit not on you, but on my own bad luck."

"Let this be the bond, then," said Shylocko, smiling a smile of many yellow teeth. "Three thousand bananas, and if you forfeit, I take one pound of your prime pelt — cut from the spot nearest your heart. A monkey's word, merchant. Shall we say, a merry sport?"

"A merry sport indeed," said Antonio, who had never read a contract in his life.

While Bassanape sailed to Belmont Bough and won Portia, Shylocko's own daughter, Jessecaca, eloped with a young ape named Lorenzoo, taking a chest of ducat-bananas and her father's favorite turquoise ring. Shylocko's friend Tubal the tamarin brought him the news, piece by terrible piece.

"My daughter! My bananas!"

"Jessecaca spent eighty bananas in one week," said Tubal.

"Thousand?!"

"Eighty. Also, she traded your turquoise ring for a macaw."

"THE RING! I had it of my Leah when I was a bachelor! I would not have given it for a *wilderness of monkeys*!" — and here Tubal gently noted that Shylocko was, in fact, demanding a wilderness of monkeys' worth of pelt from Antonio, and Shylocko told him to mind his own ledgers.

Then came the news: Antonio's barges had wrecked on the Spider Shoals. Every banana lost. The bond fell due.

The court of the Duke assembled in the great fig tree, where Shylocko whetted a mango knife on his heel.

"Shylocko," said the Duke, "every monkey in Venis has begged you to show mercy. Take thrice the bananas and let the merchant keep his pelt."

"I have my oath," said the mandrill. "Hath not a monkey eyes? Hath not a monkey hands, organs, dimensions, senses? If you groom us, do we not coo? If you tickle us, do we not laugh? If you wrong us, shall we not sulk for a generation? The pelt is mine. I crave the law!"

"Which of us," muttered Gratianape, "could sulk like that if we tried?"

Then entered a young lawyer, Balthasara, robed and solemn, with a suspiciously glossy coat (and a suspiciously familiar lady-clerk named Nerissa, who was taking excellent notes). "The quality of mercy is not strained," said Balthasara. "It droppeth as the gentle banana from the tree. It blesseth both the giver and the groomee. But very well — the law is the law. Shylocko, take your knife, and your pound of pelt."

The mandrill bounded forward, knife gleaming.

"One thing only," said Balthasara, raising one clever finger. "The bond names a pound of *pelt*. Not a pound of pelt *with hair attached*. Shed not one follicle, spill not one drop of blood, and take exactly one pound — not a gram more nor less — or, by the law of Venis, all your goods are forfeit."

Shylocko's knife drooped. One cannot skin a monkey and spill no blood, nor pluck him bald and leave him at exactly a pound. "Then give me thrice the bananas!"

"Too late. You have conspired against a citizen's life. Half your wealth to Antonio, half to Jessecaca and Lorenzoo — and, by the Duke's mercy, you shall join the Sunday grooming circle until you learn to be merry."

"A grooming circle?" whispered Shylocko. "I shall be picked over by strangers?" And something in his shoulders, which had been knotted for thirty years, quietly let go.

Balthasara, as payment, demanded the ring from Bassanape's finger — the very ring Portia had given him, with a promise never to part with it. Bassanape gave it up with a moving speech about friendship, and Nerissa, at exactly the same moment, demanded Gratianape's ring on the same grounds. Both gentlemen rode home to Belmont Bough braced for trouble, and found it: Portia and Nerissa had never heard of any rings, and made them grovel most satisfactorily — until Portia, laughing like a barrel of monkeys, produced Bassanape's ring from her own finger and revealed herself as Balthasara, and Nerissa as Nerissa, who had seen everything twice.

That very hour, word came that three of Antonio's barges had come safely to port after all, loaded to the gunwales with bananas. He settled his half of Shylocko's fortune on Jessecaca and Lorenzoo's firstborn, and sent a basket of the first bananas to the Sunday grooming circle, c/o the mandrill who was learning, slowly, to be combed.

"A merry sport," said Antonio, and for the first time in the whole story, the Merchimp of Venis smiled.
