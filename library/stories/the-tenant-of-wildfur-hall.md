# The Tenant of Wildfur Hall

*A parody of "The Tenant of Wildfell Hall"*

You will hear my side of it, and then hers, and I confess hers is the better story — as hers was the better conduct.

My name is Gilbert Markham, and when the events of this tale began I was a young farmer of two-and-twenty, with a mother who managed me, a brother Fergus who hunted, a sister Rose worth the lot of us, and a comfortable, middle-sized opinion of myself. Our farm lay in a fold of the northern moors, where the wind came off the crags like a slap from an aunt, and the whole neighborhood's business was everybody's affair.

Into this peace, one autumn, came the Tenant.

Wildfur Hall was an old moorland hold above us — a grim, gaunt place of black stone and hanging woods gone to furze and fox, long empty and popularly believed to be haunted, or worse, damp. It was now, abruptly, inhabited by a single small family: a lady, a child, and one ancient servant.

The lady called herself Mrs. Graham. She was a dark, slim langur, neither young nor old, with a pale, proud, worn face and eyes that met every stare in the lane and returned it with change. She dressed in a plain black cloak, kept her boy — a little fellow of five, with her own dark fur — on a lead of such severity that the village held committees about it, paid her bills, wanted nothing, thanked nobody, and walked the moors alone with a painter's box under her arm.

It was presently discovered — by the boys who climbed to peer in at her windows, and by the mothers who deplored the boys and quoted them — that Mrs. Graham painted pictures and sold them. In the market town. For money. The general bench concluded, with great relief, that she was "no better than she should be," the canopy's universal verdict for any monkey who is better than her neighbors expect.

I am ashamed to say I helped them to it. I am prouder that I stopped.

I met her first on the crag path, in October, quarrelling with an easel in the wind. I steadied the easel; she said thank you as if filing it under a future debt; and I walked home three miles out of my way in a state of considerable irritation. By Christmas I was at her fence every day it did not rain, and most days it did. She was cold, provoking, and contradicted every third thing I said — and she read better than any monkey I had ever met, and under all that black fur and blacker manner I could feel something crouched, waiting, wounded, that made every protective hair I owned stand up and volunteer.

"You are very contentious, Mrs. Graham," I told her once.

"You are very young, Mr. Markham," she said. "Do you know what it costs a female to be gentle? A great deal, when gentleness has been spent on those who trampled it. Be friends with me if you like — but slowly. Slowly."

The neighborhood took a nobler interest. A young gentleman of the county, Mr. Frederick Lawrence, was observed to ride up to Wildfur Hall rather often — more often, in fact, than it happened — and I, being two-and-twenty and an ass, observed it most of all. I hated him with a completeness that left no room for checking anything.

The end of it came at a party. She was there, pale among the ribbons; Lawrence was attentive; some fool said a word too many; and Mrs. Graham and I had a set-to in a doorway that cleared the cream dishes for six feet around. A few nights later, riding home late, I met Lawrence alone on the moor road.

I will tell you plainly what I did, because she made me tell everybody plainly ever after. I pulled his monkey off his pace, called him a deal I can't write, and struck him twice, and left him bleeding in the bracken — for no crime whatever except that I was jealous and he was convenient.

The next morning, Mrs. Graham's boy slipped a letter into my hand. It was not a scolding. It was a key.

"If you will know the worst of me, Gilbert, you shall know all of me. Read this book. It is my diary. When you have read it, you will understand that I have no right to your love and no strength to refuse it — and that the monkey you struck is the only friend I have."

I read it that day, locked in my own room, and was never in my life so long an hour without food.

* * *

Her true name was Helen, daughter of a gently-fatted, unexacting old squire of the south, who gave his girl to the first gentleman of fashion who asked — and the first was Arthur Huntingdon: a splendid, laughing, golden-eyed drill-baboon with a hunter's shoulders, an heir's prospects, and a heart that had been left out at birth and never missed.

She was warned. She was nineteen and did not believe it. "He is gay and wild," said she, "but I shall tame him." Reader: you cannot tame what has no heart to tame. You can only be broken against it.

Grassdale was his seat, and his siege: a fine house with a fine cellar and a set of guests that never left. Lord Lowborough, a morose old gorilla of honor, gambling himself hollow. Hattersley, a blustering young brute, loud as weather. Annabella Wilmot, a great beauty who married Lowborough's title within the year and hunted on. Hargrave, cool and curling, who saw Helen's misery and moved in on it like a carrion crow in silk. And in the middle of them always, Arthur Huntingdon, drinking his cellars, betting his woods, breaking his wife's china and her spirit in the same stride — laughing, laughing, laughing.

The diary held it all. His open infatuation with Annabella under his own roof and his wife's eyes. His spending her uncle's gift on his flatterers. And — the page where I put the book down and walked twice round the room — the training of the child: little Arthur, his father's pet and pupil, taught to love his papa's glass of wine, taught to curse the servants for sport, taught that his mama's rules were nothing and her tears less, so that the boy came at last to hide his face in her fur and say, "Mamma, I love papa best; he is not cross like you."

That was the hinge. She tried the law — which gave a ruined wife nothing, not her fortune, not her name, not her child. She tried flight, and he caught her at the edge of the estate with the boy's lead in his fist. And at last, when the child was whimpering with wine and fright and the father roaring with drink and triumph, Helen took her son and her old servant Rachel and went — over the moors in the night, an outlawed mother, to Wildfur Hall, one step ahead of a law that would have given the boy back to the teacher.

* * *

When I had finished, I went straight to Lawrence, on foot, hat in hand, and asked his pardon. He gave it — he had to: he was her brother, sworn to silence, and I had blacked both his eyes for it.

And then, before I could say the other thing in me, the moors turned. A letter came south: Huntingdon, drunk, had taken a flyer at a ditch, and lay at Grassdale smashed and dying — dying slowly, hideously, with all his set gone from him like rats from a listing branch.

Helen went to him. That was the greatness of her — that when I begged her, at the gate, not to go, she looked at me with the only tears I ever saw her spend and said:

"He is my husband, Gilbert, and my son's father. I have prayed for this hour in hatred and arrived at it in charity. There is no place for you in the room, and none for hate either. Let me go."

She nursed him three months. The diary's last pages are hers and his, and I will not quote them, except to say that he died as he lived — refusing to the last hour to be sorry — and that she sat by him to the end anyway, which is a kind of greatness the canopy does not gossip about.

She came back in the spring, a widow, with the boy, to the little property her uncle had left her, and I came to her gate — slowly, this time, as instructed — and this tale, like hers, ends in a wedding.

The village found new business within the month. The boy grew straight. The farm below the hall has the best orchard in the county now, planted by a reformed jealous young male under his wife's direction.

She paints still, better than ever. There is a picture over my desk of a dark langur standing on a crag with the wind in her fur — and when visitors ask who it is, I say what I have said through this whole honest account: the best monkey I ever knew, and the only one who ever made me better.
