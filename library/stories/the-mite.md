# The Mite

*A parody of "The Mist"*

The storm came up the valley in the night like a troop of startled baboons, hooting and ransacking the canopy. By morning, half of Big Fig County looked like a giant had used our trees as dental floss. The power was out from the mountain all the way down to the river, and the whole valley smelled of snapped branches and wet fur.

Davey Drizzle — a chimpanzee who painted portraits of distinguished bananas and was frequently reminded this was not real art — stood in the yard with his boy, Billy, surveying the hundred-year fig currently sleeping through his studio window.

"Fiddlesticks," said Davey.

"Wizard," said Billy, who was eight and easily impressed.

The Bunch & Vine was the only market in Bramblehook with a generator, so when it opened its doors that morning, every monkey in the county went ape at once. Davey took Billy and their neighbor Breet Norton — a mandrill lawyer who mentioned Primate Academy the way other monkeys mentioned the weather — to stock up before the crowds got ugly. They were arguing plantains versus figs when the mist came down off the mountain.

It didn't roll in like ordinary fog. It arrived, the way a debt collector arrives — suddenly, and slightly damp. It smelled faintly of coconut sunscreen and old bananas, which was an unsettling combination in a fruit market, and it pressed against the big front windows like a monkey trying to remember whether he'd left the stove on.

Then the ancient galago came scuttling out of the white, eyes like saucers.

"Something in the mite!" she shrieked. "Something in the mite took Koko! Mites in it the size of dogs!"

"There is no such thing as mites with ambition," declared Breet, and strode toward the doors to prove it.

(He would spend the remainder of events being wrong about this, before being escorted away by something with far too many legs, which everyone agreed served him right. Legally speaking.)

Inside the Bunch & Vine, the troop debated. The manager, Mr. McTigue, wanted the doors locked, the lights low, and everyone calm, in that order. Norm, the bag-boy spider monkey, wanted to go out and fix the generator, which had chosen this week to quit. Davey said don't. The manager said absolutely don't. Norm went anyway, because a teenage monkey would rather risk the apocalypse than disappoint middle management, and because there was an Employee of the Month plaque in it for him.

The screaming was brief. When they hauled Norm back inside, he was gray with mites to the eyebrows, and the anti-itch powder flew for an hour while he whispered about what else was out there in the white. Things that groomed you without asking. Things with hooks, and gentle, terrible patience.

That first night, things began landing on the windows.

They came toward the store lights out of the white — fluttering pink things the size of flying eyebrows, then larger hunters that swooped and snapped them up midair, then something with wings like picnic tables that hit the skylight hard enough to make every monkey's fur stand on end. One window cracked. Ollie, a gorilla clerk of few words, duct-taped it flat, then stood beside it until dawn, holding a broom, daring it.

By morning, the whole front of the store had been cocooned in silk overnight — every cart, every bushel, every parking sign wrapped in soft grey thread, like the world's most dedicated department of gift-wrapping had come through on commission.

"Nobody itch," said Ollie.

Nobody itched. But Mrs. Howlermandy, a howler monkey who sold dream-catchers and pamphlets, knew an opportunity when she howled at one.

"That's it!" she announced. "The Mite is the judgement of the Old Troop! We have been a naughty species!"

"We're monkeys," said Davey. "Naughty is our whole brand."

"THE ITCH IS UPON US!"

Panic, like grooming, is contagious. By the second day she had followers, and followers, as usual, wanted a sacrifice.

It was also on the second day that they found the note. Two young army monkeys — privates from the base up on the mountain, who had been quietly eating corn chips in the corner since the mist arrived — had slipped out the loading dock at dawn. What they left behind was pinned to a bag of chips: "SORRY. IT WAS US. PROJECT MITESIZE. WE WERE TRYING TO SHRINK THE SPACE BETWEEN THINGS. WE SHRUNK THE WRONG DIMENSION. GONE TO REPORT FOR DUTY. P.S. THE DELI CASE WAS ALREADY BROKEN."

Nobody said anything for a long time. Ruth, the checkout macaque, finally summed up the county's entire position on the matter. "Well," she said. "That's just great."

By the third day the shelves were bare and the mood was worse, and it was decided that a small party would go two doors down to the pharmacy for calamine, anti-itch powder, and — at Davey's insistence — batteries, because a flashlight is a monkey's second-best friend. What they found in the pharmacy was cocoons. The pharmacist had been wrapped to the ceiling in a beautiful hammock of thread — alive, snoring, eight feet off the ground, and too embarrassed to discuss it. On the way back they passed the corn-chip soldiers' helmets, arranged in the parking lot in a spiral. Nobody looked at the spiral long. You could feel it when the spirals noticed you back. It felt like a rash on the inside.

When they returned, Mrs. Howlermandy was holding court by the smoothie case, which had stopped being frozen and started being aggressive.

"THE MITE DEMANDS A SIGN!" she howled. "GIVE US THE SMALL ONE!"

"Or at least the overripe bananas!" cried her followers, who were at least attempting a compromise.

They cornered Davey and Billy against the smoothie case. Davey put Billy behind him and picked up the only weapon left in the store, a baguette, which he held like a club and which everyone respected more than it probably deserved.

It was then that Ollie the clerk rammed a shopping cart through the crowd like a jungle delivery truck.

"GO!" he rumbled. "And leave us five stars on the way out!"

They ran — Davey, Billy, Norm wrapped to the eyebrows, Ruth, the galago clinging to the cart, and Ollie pushing — out the doors and into Davey's truck, and the mite swallowed the road behind them.

They passed things in the white. Enormous, slow shapes, folding and unfolding, like a billion tiny bugs pretending to be weather. Then, on a hill above the road, something walked that was bigger than weather entirely — legs like scaffolding, a body like a cathedral, grooming the treetops with hooks as it went, unhurried, unbothered, ancient. Nobody looked at it directly. Billy looked, once, and then held his father's arm and did not look again, and Davey drove.

Billy whimpered. Davey drove one-handed and did terrible arithmetic. Six of them now. Two bunches left. One long, itchy road.

"Buddy," he said quietly, "if it comes to it—"

"Don't," said Billy.

Davey gripped the soft bananas anyway and kept driving, because that is the whole job of being somebody's father.

Then Billy pointed out the window. "Dad. Look."

Ahead, through a tear in the white, an army truck rolled past spraying a water cannon of anti-itch powder like a crop duster of mercy. Behind it came the sound every starving monkey knows in his soul: a relief convoy, honking, loaded with green bananas, fresh mangoes, calamine by the drum, and one very apologetic veterinarian.

Davey pulled over and cried like a baby baboon, which is to say with enormous dignity and no restraint whatsoever.

Nobody ever explained the Mite. The army claimed Project Mitesize had been "a weather balloon." Mrs. Howlermandy published a pamphlet about it, which was ninety percent font. The pharmacist never discussed the hammock, although he did take up weaving.

But Davey kept those two overripe bananas on his windowsill forever — a reminder of how close the arithmetic came, and of how the world, sometimes, swings back your way. On the radio that night, faint through the last of the white, a voice said the convoy was working down the valley, town by town, and that the word for tomorrow was calamine.

Billy slept the whole way home. Davey drove, and watched the road, and did not do arithmetic at all.
