# Chimpizen of the Galaxy

*A parody of "Citizen of the Galaxy"*

On Jubbulpore, in the Plaza of the Nine Moons, the auctioneer's gavel came down like a coconut on a stone: "Number ninety-seven, young male chimp, docile. Who opens the bidding?"

Number ninety-seven was Thorby, nine years old, with no memory of being anything but property. He watched the free monkeys barter for his bones, feeling not grief — you cannot grieve what you never had — but the small hope that whoever bought him would feed him.

The bid that won him was one credit, offered by a beggar.

Baslim the Cripple was the plaza's oldest fixture: a one-legged orangutan with an eyepatch, a stall at the mouth of the Grand Bazaar's darkest alley, and a routine the tourists timed their visits by — the bow, the bowl, the blessing. He bought the cheapest scraps and was universally dismissed. Nobody who is ignored is ever suspected.

At home in the alley's back room, Baslim stood up off his cart straight as a mast, took off the eyepatch, and looked his purchase over with both bright eyes.

"Can you count?"

"...No, master."

"You will. Can you remember a face?"

"I think so, master."

"You will. Sit down. Eat." He pushed over a dish of real food, hot, the kind Thorby had only smelled through doorways. "You are not my slave, boy. Under the Sargon's law you are — law being the organized gossip of monkeys with troops — but in this room you are my son. Do you understand the difference?"

"No, master."

"Good," said Baslim. "That's the correct answer to everything important. Eat."

Thorby's education was the strangest in the Nine Worlds. Mornings, he begged — a beggar's bowl sees everything, and a beggar is furniture. Evenings, Baslim taught him languages, mathematics, the customs of Free Traders and Guardsmen, and tradecraft — though Thorby understood that only years later. How to carry a message in memory alone. How to be sold, or bought, or beaten, and keep looking like furniture.

"Slaves are cheap, son," Baslim said once, staring into the stew. "That's the whole crime of the trade in one sentence. A free chimp is expensive — takes years, costs love, can't be replaced. So they make monkeys cheap, and the cheaper they make them, the richer the sellers. Somewhere out there sits a boardroom of well-groomed gentlemen who know the price of everything except that." He stirred the pot. "I intend to send them the bill."

The Sargon's police came on a night the plaza smelled of rain. The syndicates had finally traced the old beggar who asked one question too many. He put Thorby under hypnosis and packed a sealed message into his sleeping mind; he arranged, with the last coin in his bowl, a bargeman's silence and a pilot's blind eye. Then, so no drug could make him betray the channel he had spent a lifetime opening, Baslim the Cripple took the long sleep, upright in his chair, both eyes open.

"Begone, furniture," he told Thorby, gently, the last thing he ever said aloud. "There's a whole galaxy out there. Go be expensive."

The pilot who carried him off Jubbulpore wore the patch cords of the Free Traders, and delivered him to the *Sisu*: a ship as old as a nation, crewed by the People — a family of macaques two hundred strong, every one born aboard, every stranger a mortal danger to the ship's genome of custom.

Captain Krausa heard the sealed message — the boy speaking in his sleep in Baslim's own voice: *"I am Baslim the Cripple. This is my son. The House of Krausa owes me a debt no credit can weigh. Make him People."* — and Krausa, who had owed Baslim his life, turned gray and went aft to break the news to his mother.

Grandmother Krausa was ninety, blind, and the actual sovereign of the *Sisu*, as everyone aboard knew and no logbook recorded. She patted Thorby's head with a hand like a dried vine.

"The old beggar collected debts in people," she said. "Very well. Captain, enter him in the family book: son of the ship, branch of my branch. Boy — hear me once. You are People now, and the People have one law above the Sargon and every Sargon: the ship comes first, the family second, yourself never. Keep that law and two hundred monkeys will die for you. Break it and I will space you myself, and weep, and keep your allowance in trust."

"Aye, Grandmother."

And that was the second family. Thorby learned the ship the way Baslim had taught him to learn a plaza: position by position, debt by debt. He made one true friend — Mata, the captain's granddaughter, who taught him what no adult explains: that a ship is lonely in exactly the way a family is — even when you are surrounded.

Two years in, the debt came due again. *"Deliver my son to the Hegemonic Guard,"* Baslim's message had said next. *"To Colonel Brisby. Say: Baslim the Cripple begs a favor."* The *Sisu* altered course at the edge of the Nine Worlds, and Thorby learned the third and hardest law of families: they are the branches you fall from, and the fall is called growing.

"You are always of the *Sisu*," Grandmother told him at the hatch, having prepared the speech with the care of a monkey defusing her own heart. "Whatever cloth you wear, your branch is in our book. But you are not *of* us the way the others are, and I am too old to pretend, and the old beggar's debt comes before my fondness. Go. Be expensive."

Colonel Brisby of the Hegemonic Guard — X Corps, the quiet one, the one that did not parade — heard the phrase *Baslim the Cripple begs a favor* and stood up, because among the officers of the Exotic Corps that name was a rank. What Thorby had called Pop for six years had been one of the Guard's deepest agents, a spider at the center of the web that hunted the slave trade. He looked up at the new recruit with an expression Thorby had never seen from anyone in uniform.

"Son," Brisby said, "do you know who you are?"

He did not. The Guard's records halls supplied it: he was *Thor* Rudbek of Rudbek, heir to Rudbek & Associates, the largest concern in charted space. His parents' yacht had vanished in the Fringe when he was four; the slavers who took it had sold him down and down the Nine Worlds to a Jubbulpore auction block and a beggar's care. On paper, one of the richest orphans alive.

In practice, the guardians of his trust — immaculately groomed officers of the family firm named Weemsby and Bruder — had managed his fortune for sixteen years and proposed to keep managing it until his death of convenient causes. They met their heir with lawyers.

Thorby, who had been furniture, property, and People in that order, found being rich the hardest status of all — and that he had been armed for it his whole life. Baslim's tradecraft: the patience to watch faces and count crowds. The People's mathematics: the kinship calculus that computes any debt to any remove. And the Guard's backing, plus one ally — his cousin Leda Rudbek, who had waited sixteen years for a Rudbek with a pulse and a spine.

The battle of Rudbek Towers was fought in boardrooms, and Thorby fought it like an ape of the old jungle: never the offered terms, always the flanks. He petitioned the Court of Claims for his majority and demanded, under charter, a full accounting of the trust; and when Weemsby's lawyers drowned the motions in procedure, Thorby stood in open court and did the one thing no gentleman of the firm could survive: he read into the record the manifests his father had copied and hidden before dying — ships of Rudbek & Associates, reflagged through charter within charter, carrying slaves through the Fringe for the syndicates. The fortune that owned him had been built on the trade that had sold him.

"The account," Thorby said quietly, into a silence you could have hung fruit from, "is called due. My father died collecting it. A beggar taught me the rate of interest."

Weemsby went to prison, the board was purged, and Thor Rudbek of Rudbek, majority granted, found himself master of an empire of credits beyond counting — the exact boardroom Baslim had promised to send a bill.

He sent it. Rudbek & Associates declared war on the slave trade: ships reassigned, charters audited, the syndicates' routes burned one by one. Leda ran the fleets, Brisby ran the web, and on Thorby's desk sat a small brass bowl that had once rattled credits in the Grand Bazaar, holding one coin: the credit Baslim had bid for a slave boy and won.

They asked him once, at a reception of the great and groomed, what a Rudbek was doing in the Fringe trade, in a war that made no profit.

"Me?" said Thorby. "I'm nobody, really. Son of a beggar, branch of the *Sisu*, late of the Guard." He smiled the smile Mata had once liked, out in the dark where the ships keep their own time. "Citizen of the galaxy — and I pay my debts."

And far out on the Fringe, on a route the charts do not name, a slave ship met a Rudbek cruiser, and was not allowed to finish its voyage.
