# Mostly Hairless

*A parody of "Mostly Harmless"*

The revised, updated, and enormously expensive new edition of *The Branchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* had, at its heart, one tiny change. The old entry on planet Earth had read, in its entirety: **Mostly harmless.**

The new entry read: **Mostly hairless.**

Nobody at the Guide's offices could explain the edit. Nobody at the Guide's offices could explain the new Guide at all — the Mark II, an inky black, bird-like, infinitely manipulable thing that could fold itself through spacetime, show you any reality you asked for, and answered to a Vogon. It had been financed, the accounts department insisted, by an invoice made out to *InfiniDim Enterprises*, which is what happens when a subsidiary of Vogons gets hold of a publishing house.

Ford Banana-Fetch, veteran researcher, found the reorganization suspicious in the way a monkey finds a silent leopard suspicious — immediately, and with total commitment. Investigating, he got trapped on the thirteenth floor of the Guide's building — a floor that did not officially exist, containing the accounts department, where monkeys worked in a silence so complete it had a texture.

He escaped, barely, by being radically unpredictable, which was the only department in which Ford had seniority. He also, before leaving, mailed something — but we'll come to that.

Half a galaxy away, on the warm tropical island of Lemuella, Arthur Chimp had found the only job he'd ever loved: Sandwich Maker.

It had begun by accident — a crash, a Tool Maker's kit, a hungry village — and now every day Arthur sat in the shade of his hut making Perfectly Normal Banana Sandwiches. The villagers thought him a holy man. The pikka birds, which shouldn't have existed, watched him work with heads cocked to one side. His helper, a small, fierce island girl, collected and distributed the sandwiches with terrifying efficiency. She was, Arthur reflected, the most competent person he had ever worked with, and she was nine.

Twice a year the Perfectly Normal Beasts came — great herds of them sweeping majestically across the plain, arriving from nowhere in their thousands and vanishing to nowhere, at a thunder, in about six seconds. The villagers, having never once seen a beast that did otherwise, called this "normal," and had a feast; the Beasts' migration made no sense and kept no schedule, which the villagers considered the schedule. The Sandwich Maker got two of them, and the prized cut, and no questions were answered, because nobody had noticed there was a question.

"The Sandwich Maker," the villagers said, in the reverent tone other cultures reserve for weather gods, "spreads."

Arthur was, for the first time since his tree was demolished to make way for a vine-bypass, almost happy. That was his first clue something terrible was coming. In his experience, happiness was just the universe clearing its throat.

The something terrible arrived in three parts.

Part one was Random Chimp — his daughter, a teenage girl with Arthur's genes, Trill-ape's restlessness, and a temperament like a startled hornet, come to stay with the father she'd never met, and hated, for never having been there, and hated, more unfairly, for now being there.

"You're not my father," she told him. "You're just some hapless monkey the universe keeps employing."

"That," Arthur admitted, "is the most accurate thing anyone has ever said about me."

Part two was Trill-ape, arriving like news footage of herself, a war correspondent covering the story of her own family badly. She arrived with a camera-ape, a schedule, and the sincere belief that a parent is something you can do in the evenings, if the galaxy held still — which it had never once done.

Part three was everything else. Random stole a ride on the first thing leaving — which turned out to be the new Guide, the Mark II, which folded itself around her and took her where she asked: to Earth. A new Earth. One of the parallel Earths the Vogons hadn't found yet, in a slab of realities where the monkeys had worked out the Question — the Mark II being, all along, the Vogons' targeting system, and Random, all along, its last delivery mechanism.

The family followed — Trill-ape by ship, Ford and Arthur by Guide, crash-landing in a suburb — and the whole war ended, as it had run, in a small room. Random ran to the hills and was found by Trill-ape, and the two of them had the fight they had been carrying across the galaxy: about a father who wasn't there, a mother who was a byline, a childhood spent in nightclubs and departure lounges, and a watch that didn't tell the time because it was sulking.

"I don't even *know* you!" Random shouted.

"I know," said Trill-ape, who had interviewed warlords and could not get through to a fifteen-year-old. "I'm the news. I'm always somewhere else. It's the worst thing I do."

Ford and Arthur, meanwhile, arrived last — by Guide, in a ball of inky feathers, through a parallel suburb — where Ford's first act was to check the local entry on Earth, and Arthur's was to ask after sandwiches. "Still mostly hairless," Ford reported grimly. "Nobody ever updates the food."

And on the hill above them hovered the Grebulons' surveillance ship — the fleet's targeting system, unknowing and unemployed. Its leader had lost his mission instructions eons ago and settled into watching interstellar cable TV, and on the subject of the stars had taken a weekly adviser: Random, who had stormed up the ramp furious and been offered snacks.

"You just make it up?" the leader asked, crushed. "The stars part?"

"You just make it up," said Random. "Everybody does. That's why it works."

The leader found this the most reassuring thing he had ever heard, and was still smiling about it when the Vogons decoded his feed — which is one more happy ending than most of us are issued.

The last version of Earth was destroyed on a Thursday. This had generally been agreed, throughout the galaxy, to be the worst possible day for it.

Arthur stood on the hillside with Random, both of them watching the sky come apart in a tidy Vogon sort of way — no drama, no firestorm, just the lights going out of everything, in order, like closing time at the universe.

"Is this it?" asked Random.

"This is it," said Arthur.

"Typical," said Random, who was fifteen, and therefore equipped with the only worldview that could survive this one.

Arthur put his arm around his daughter and said the only thing he'd ever found that helped, which was not much, and was not nothing:

"We had good sandwiches."

There was, however, one loose end swinging loose at the edge of everything: Ford Banana-Fetch had, before the end, mailed the old Guide's entry on Earth — the original, handwritten, coffee-ringed entry — to the one address the Vogons didn't control. On it, under *planet: Earth*, he had scrawled his final revision, the entry as it should have stood, with a researcher's whole career folded into two words:

**Mostly hairless. Odd, but try the sandwiches.**

And somewhere, on some other branch of the great probability tree, another Arthur Chimp walked into another jungle, thumb out, towel over shoulder, blissfully unaware, reaching for a ripe and yellow future.

The universe, as Ford would tell you at length, in a bar, without being asked: mostly hairless. Odd. But try the sandwiches.
