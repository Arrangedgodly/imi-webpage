# Oathswinger

*A parody of "Oathbringer"*

Kaladin of Branch Four had a spear, a frown you could hang fruit from, and a tiny fren named Syl who lived in his fur.

"I'm a vine-fren," she explained, popping out of his shoulder fur as a ribbon of light. "That's a spirit of honorable swinging. You're a Windswinger of the Knights Ripe. Try to keep up."

"Radiant," Kaladin said. "Knights Radiant."

"You say tomato," said Syl, who had never once said tomato.

The old oaths were coming back into the world, and the Everstorm with them — a storm that rolled backward, hurling green lightning, carrying the Refused: apes the troops had cast out, now wearing the shapes of their own anger and riding the wind. Everything Kaladin had sworn to protect was on fire, which was, admittedly, usual, but this time the fire had opinions.

In the tower-city of Oothiburu, perched in the boughs of a mountain-tree, old Dalinar Kholin — a scarred silverback who had once been called the Black Vine, and had earned it — was trying to knit nine troops of quarrelsome apes into one. He had given up his famous Shard-bough, the blade called Oathswinger, decades ago; it swung on through legend without him, a name he did not deserve, in a story everyone told wrong. What nobody told at all was the Thrill.

The Thrill was Banodium's oldest gift: a red singing in the blood that made battle feel like being told a wonderful secret. Dalinar had been an addict. In the old days his brother Gavi-larch had handed him the world and called it a gift, and the Thrill had handed him a torch, and he had carried both, laughing. They called him the Black Vine then, and he had burned his way across the jungle with the joy of an ape who believes joy is proof. He had burned a city called Ratha-leaf to win a battle, and his wife Ivy had been inside the plan and inside the fire both.

There is a valley where an old power called the Night-groomer combs away memories for a price, and Dalinar had paid it: the memory clipped from him like a knot from fur. But the Cultivator, who prunes futures, had stored it on a high shelf of his mind, to ripen.

It ripened now. It came back on an ordinary evening, in an ordinary doorway, smelling of smoke, whole. He told no one for eleven days. He read the little book of Noha-don, the ancient ape-king who had written that the way of branches is one branch at a time, and he did not sleep, and Syl reported to Kaladin that the tower's walls felt "clangy," which was the technical term.

Dalinar Kholin told the gathered troops of nine nations what he had done, out loud, in front of everyone, because his son had taught him that a lie is just a debt with interest. His enemies laughed, and his allies went gray, and his nephew Renarin, whom everyone called weak, stood up first and said, "Good. Now you're finally someone worth following." Then the troops, miraculously, did not scatter. Apes, it turns out, will forgive a wound they can see before a wound they cannot. Renarin's quiet fren Glys glowed through his fur like lantern-light through a knothole, and Renarin, whom every possible future had mislabeled, stood there being exactly himself — which was the plot twist the storm had not budgeted for.

Kaladin, meanwhile, was failing publicly at hope. He had gone to the fallen capital in the sky to rescue a child and a kingdom, and managed neither; he had frozen in the dark while the Refused sang, and the fourth oath sat in him unripe, because it began *I accept that there will be those I cannot save,* and he could not. Not yet. Not while the young king Mognash had murdered still fell in his dreams, spear turned to driftwood in his hands. He came home quieter than he left, and Syl rode his shoulder like a small worried lantern.

Banodium came twice to treat before it came to terrify. *Be my champion, old ape,* it said, showing him futures: the towers intact, the families spared, the whole world made calm, all purchasable at the price of one small oath. "I have read your terms," said Dalinar. "Your contracts are all signed in someone else's blood." And he sent a god away and sat down to write his memoirs instead, which is what apes do when they cannot sleep and will not surrender.

Then Banodium, god of rage and heat, came to Dalinar that night in a dream, wearing a voice like warm tar: *Give me your pain, old ape. Lay it down. Let someone else carry it. You could forget. You could rest. I will even take the Thrill back — I will make you gentle.*

And Dalinar Kholin stood up in his own dream, gray-backed and stinking of smoke, and roared the words that saved a world:

**"YOU CANNOT HAVE MY PAIN. It is MINE. I earned it carrying it, and I will carry it up every branch of this world if I must — and I will climb WITH it!"**

The dream cracked like a nut. And on the battlefield of Thaylen Bough, where the Refused poured through broken walls and Kaladin froze — the king falling again, the driftwood spear again — it was Dalinar's voice that reached him across the field, steady as a trunk:

"Son. The most important swing a monkey can take is the next one."

Kaladin took it. Something in him let go of the falling king and caught the next branch instead, and the fourth oath finally ripened in his mouth: *"I accept that there will be those I fail to protect. I accept that I am one of them. I swing anyway."* Branch Four took ten thousand more. Szeth of the White Fur held the harbor wall beside a black sword that kept asking, politely, where the evil was, and everything that came over the wall showed it. The Radiant oaths rang out over the burning field like ripe fruit falling: *"Life before falling! Strength before wilting! Swing before destination!"* Shallan the mask-wearer set down one of her three faces and fought honest; Adolin dueled a monster in a saddle of smoke and won by being too stubborn to be afraid, and afterward told no one about the apology he'd owed the monster for years, there not being time.

At the center, Dalinar walked unarmed into the storm of Banodium's making and took hold of the three realms of the world like three vines, and *braided* them — the swinging door between heavens yawning open — and the Everstorm unspun around him, and every ape on the field felt, for one heartbeat, like one troop. Somewhere behind him, an old voice that had ridden the storms for years said, satisfied: *united, then.*

"Come, then," Dalinar said, to a god. "Groom me if you dare. I have nothing left to hide."

"What are you?" Banodium demanded, shrinking, for a god of rage has no purchase on an ape who has stopped raging.

And Dalinar Kholin, the Black Vine, the man who had burned a city, said the last word of the battle:

"I am the Troop."

Afterward, Kaladin sat on the wall with Syl, watching the light come back, his spear across his knees.

"You couldn't save the king," said Syl, gently.

"No," said Kaladin. "But Branch Four keeps trying. That's the whole order, isn't it. We keep trying."

"Radiant," said Syl, and tucked herself into his fur, and glowed.
