# Groomio and Juliet

*A parody of "Romeo and Juliet"*

In fair Veronan Vines, two troops held an ancient grudge: the Monteapes and the Capuchulets, feuding till the whole jungle had learned to duck. The grudge was so old that nobody remembered what it was about — the three leading theories involved a fig, an insult, and one monkey's grandfather sitting on another monkey's grandfather's tail — but by then it ran on pure tradition.

It expressed itself, as tradition does, through fruit. One morning in the marketplace, Sampson-ape and Gregory-ape of the Capuchulet household stood chewing figs when some Monteapes passed below.

"I bite my thumb at the Monteapes, sir," said Sampson-ape, chewing loudly.

"Do you bite your THUMB at us, sir?" called a Monteape.

"No, sir," said Sampson-ape, "I do not bite my thumb at you, sir — but I bite my thumb, sir."

"You DO bite your thumb, then, AT US," said the Monteape, and the figs flew, and Benvolio-ape tried to break it up with a "Put up your fruit! You know not what you do!", and old Lord Capuchulet hung out of his hollow shrieking for his sword until his wife reminded him he had a crutch ("A crutch, a crutch! Why call you for a sword?") — and Prince Escalus, an ancient orang, arrived with the Watch and pronounced the law of the land: "Three brawls and the banishments begin. The Vines are NOT a fruit salad."

Groomio Monteape, Benvolio's cousin, was the finest young groomer in the vines — he could find a flea in a thunderstorm. Lucky, for his name was Groomio. That week he was moping spectacularly over a capuchin named Rosaline who had sworn to eat nothing but unripe fruit, and Benvolio prescribed the universal cure: "Examine other beauties. By giving liberty unto thine eyes. There is a feast at the Capuchulets' tonight. We go masked."

One night, then, the Capuchulets threw a masked feast, and Mercurio dragged Groomio along. And Groomio saw Juliet Capuchulet across the fruit bowl, and forgot Rosaline so completely that even now historians cannot confirm she existed.

"O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright," he breathed — "a rich jewel in a baboon's ear!"

They talked till the torches burned low. The Nurse — a vast, wonderful old macaque who had raised Juliet from a pink-faced baby — bustled up to fetch her, and identified everyone in the room for Groomio in a whisper ("That's Tybalt, her cousin, the tamarin with the temper — that's her mother — and that, young monkey, is the lady herself, so mind your manners"). Called away, Juliet spoke from her high branch, not knowing the night was listening:

"O Groomio, Groomio, wherefore art thou Groomio? Deny thy father and refuse thy name — or, if thou wilt not, be but sworn my love, and I'll no longer be a Capuchulet."

"'Tis but thy name that is my enemy," Groomio called, climbing toward her. "What's Groomio? It is nor hand, nor foot, nor face. What's in a name? That which we call a banana by any other name would taste as sweet."

"Art thou not Groomio, and a Monteape?" gasped Juliet.

"Call me but love, and I'll be new baptized. Henceforth I never will be Groomio."

(He would. Everyone did. You try renaming a monkey whose name is already a pun.)

"If that thy bent of love be honorable, send me word tomorrow," said Juliet, and was whisked inside by the Nurse, who had heard everything, kept it a secret, made Groomio repeat his intentions three times for the record, and complained about her feet each time.

Next afternoon they were secretly wed by Friar Lemur-ence, a gentle lemur with a night-garden of herbs. "These violent delights have violent ends. Love moderately — wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast."

Moderation lasted three days.

On day three, Juliet's cousin Tybalt — a tamarin with the soul of a bee — came looking for the party-crashers. "Groomio! Turn and draw, you party-ape!"

"Good Tybalt, put your climbing-gaff away," said Groomio, now Tybalt's kinsman by secret marriage. "I never injured thee, but love thee better than thou canst devise."

Tybalt did not care for peace, and Mercurio did not care for anything, so Mercurio stepped in. "Good king of tamarinds, will you walk? You shall give me your twelve golden whiskers in the pot." Blades flashed, fur flew, and the gaff found its mark.

"A plague o' both your troops!" cried Mercurio. "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a rotten banana. Your houses!" And he said no more, unlike him.

Then Groomio's grief got ahead of his vows, and Tybalt fell too. "O, I am fortune's fool!" said Groomio, standing over him with the gaff hanging from his paw. By sundown Prince Escalus ruled: "Banished, Groomio! Three brawls over one papaya is three too many!"

Juliet wept — husband banished, cousin buried — while the Nurse relayed everything to everyone, mixing up the news catastrophically ("He's dead! No, Tybalt's dead! No, BOTH — no, one banished — sit down, I'll start again"). The Friar hid a frantic Groomio in his herb-garden and preached him a sermon — "There is no world without Veronan walls; heaven is here, where Juliet lives" — and one secret night of rope-ladder happiness was had before banishment took him to Ban-tua.

Then Juliet's parents arranged her wedding to noble Count Paris-ape, a pleasant monkey with excellent fruit. Friar Lemur-ence offered a desperate plan: "Drink this gourd. It will stop your breath for two-and-forty hours; they will lay you in the family hollow. My messenger will fetch Groomio from Ban-tua, and when you wake, he'll be there."

That night Juliet sat alone in the hollow and turned the gourd in her paws. "What if it be a poison the friar subtly hath ministered? What if, when I wake before my Groomio comes, the hollow air chokes me? What if the whole troop, hearing me stir, shrieks like mandrills torn up by the roots, and wakes the dead?" — and then she thought of Groomio, and drank.

It worked so well that the entire Capuchulet troop held a magnificent funeral.

Then everything went wrong with the efficiency of a barrel of monkeys rolling downhill. The messenger — a kind but unhurried slow loris — set out for Ban-tua; the news traveled by parrot, and the parrot got there first.

"Then I defy you, stars!" cried Groomio, riding home with a vial of deep-sleep sap from an ape apothecary, to lie beside his bride and sleep forever. (The apothecary had hesitated — "Mantua law, my monkey, Mantua law" — but Groomio had offered thirty-two bananas and a tearful speech about duty, and apothecaries are only so strong.)

For when Groomio drank the sap in the dim hollow and made his farewell — "Thus with a kiss I die" — the apothecary had not mentioned the sap was cut with coconut water. It was, in pharmacy terms, mostly a smoothie.

At dawn, at the grand joint funeral — the patriarchs mourning together for the first time in a hundred years — Juliet opened one eye.

"...Groomio? Why is my father holding a fig branch and crying?"

Groomio sat up, groggy, vial still in paw. "I'm at a funeral. Whose funeral is — JULIET?"

The two troops screamed as one. Friar Lemur-ence fainted into his herb basket; the slow loris, arriving at last, took in the scene, said "Oh good, it's resolved," and turned around; and the Nurse cried so hard and happily that she had to be fanned with a serving leaf.

Prince Escalus raised his paw.

"Capuchulet! Monteape! See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, that heaven finds means to kill your joys with —" he peered at the sleepers, very much alive — "*dramatic irony*! All are flea-d! All are punished! For never was a story of more woe than this of Groomio and his Juliet — AND NEVER WAS A FUNERAL SO SURPRISED."

The patriarchs, weeping and laughing, clasped paws over the coffin lid. "O brother Monteape, give me thy paw. I shall house a bronze statue of them grooming."

"Some shall be pardoned," said Escalus wearily, "and some shall be married. Properly, this time."

And so they wed again in open sunlight, both troops attending, Mercurio honored with the largest wreath and the rudest eulogy, the parrot forbidden from delivering any news at all, and one very slow loris as ring-bearer — for never was a story of more woe, and never was a woe so happily, monkeyishly undone.
