# The Andromandrill Strain

*A parody of "The Andromeda Strain"*

Very early on a spring morning, a Project Scoop satellite came down out of orbit and landed, with a small green flash, in the troop-range of Peeldemont — population, sixty-eight mandrills.

By dawn, all sixty-eight were the color of oatmeal. If you know anything about mandrills, whose faces run to blue and scarlet and gold the way other monkeys run to brown, you know that this is very bad indeed.

Two monkeys cannot investigate a disaster, so the troop sent scientists, in strict order of reputation: Dr. Stone, a silverback bacteriologist once described on the cover of *Primatology Today* as "the best mind in the burrow"; Dr. Dutton, a clinician; Dr. Leavitt, a pathologist with a secret (he was epileptic, and hid it the way all proud monkeys hide things — in plain sight, behind a dignified stare); and Dr. Hall, a surgeon, selected for a quality no one had ever complimented him on: he was single. This was the Odd Chimp Hypothesis — the formal finding, typed and notarized, that an unmarried male makes the cleanest life-or-death decisions. Hall read the paperwork on the flight out. "I'm single," he told Stone, "not sensible." "The committee," said Stone, "was aware of neither."

They flew to Peeldemont first, in foil suits, and walked the one street. Every mandrill had died standing up. A buck groomer lay with his comb still in his paw; a card game sat frozen mid-deal, one paw of cards held forever; the diner's pancakes had gone cold on a griddle no monkey would ever scrape. Nobody had run. Nobody had bled. The strange part — the part Stone kept returning to — was that nothing decayed. And overhead, in a sky full of their business, not one vulture would land.

"Even the undertakers of the air," said Hall, "are calling in sick."

The recovery team found two survivors: an infant colobus, roughly two months old, who had screamed since the flash and showed no sign of stopping; and Old Gibbon, sixty-nine, who lived on fermented sap and had not been internally balanced since the Coolidge administration, or whatever the monkey equivalent was. They carried both out, along with the satellite, and blew the town to a tidy crater on general principles.

The Wildvine Laboratory lay five levels beneath the jungle floor, the deepest of which — Level Vine — existed to study exactly this: a bug from off-world. Entry cost the scientists their dignity in stages: they were washed, shaved, sniffed, dipped, and blow-dried, in roughly that order. Stone's skull, pink and gleaming, was judged "very reassuring" by the instruments and "very upsetting" by everyone else.

The thing in the satellite turned out to be a fleck of crystal, parrot-green — "a green 'nana from the stars," as the junior staff immediately named it — which did not eat flesh so much as organize it. Its appetites were narrow and its manners appalling. Dropped into a sealed capsule with a laboratory macaque, it killed the animal in three seconds flat, leaving blood clotted like dessert at a banquet nobody had ordered. Grown in dish after dish of peatri agar, it thrived — doubling every twelve minutes, a growth curve Leavitt plotted until the line ran off the page, up the margin, and onto the desk. The spectrograph was worse: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen missing entirely. "A life form organized like a joke," said Dutton, "with no nitrogen and no punchline."

They ran the capsule series twice more to be certain, which cost two more macaques and everybody's appetite. Then Dutton asked the question the protocol had no form for: who puts a satellite up to catch bugs, and why? Stone looked at the green fleck for a long moment before answering. "Scoop was designed by a committee I sat on, Hall. We put it up to see what would fall back down. The theory was that anything that could survive the fall could be taught to survive other things. In our defense, we filed excellent minutes." Hall observed that the joke, after all, did have a punchline, and that they were living in it.

And the thing wanted one thing: blood at a comfortable middling sourness, neither tart nor sweet. A hair too acid, it starved. A hair too alkaline, it starved.

Which explained the survivors. The baby had screamed itself into one far country; Old Gibbon had pickled himself into the other. Like a Goldilocks of the blood, Andromandrill wanted it just right, and both patients were deliciously, catastrophically wrong.

Then, near midnight, a seal failed on Level Vine — except, as Leavitt noted in the margin, it had not failed. It had been *eaten*: the gasket chewed through like fig rind, by a strain already changing its diet. That night Leavitt dreamed of a neat house with a sign over the door reading NO PESTS IN THE HOUSE, and woke at 3 a.m. certain the dream had contained something important, and — this was the professional tragedy — unable to remember what. He had spent a lifetime training his eyes to slide past anything that flashed, and his eyes had gotten excellent at it. Somewhere behind the slide, a warning light had blinked at him all evening, patiently, like a monkey waving from a far branch.

The protocols did what protocols do: an atomic core, deep under the floor, began counting itself down toward a very tidy bonfire. Tidy, Hall realized — reading, of all things, Leavitt's unattended notes while the sirens went thoroughly ape — except that the strain had mutated overnight into something that could feed on energy directly. The bomb meant to kill Andromandrill would instead be the largest meal ever served to it.

The stop-key was Hall's, by the Odd Chimp Hypothesis. The substation lay behind a sealed door, and the last route ran through the central core itself, where the green flecks hung in the air like pollen. He went up the shaft hand over hand, in the dark, holding his breath for what turned out to be four minutes — a personal record, he would later insist — and slapped the cutoff switch with one banana-second to spare.

On Level Four, meanwhile, Leavitt had a seizure under the strobing emergency lights, and so missed, for eleven minutes, the memo that saved everyone else's century.

The bomb died quietly. Hall sat down on the decontamination grating and laughed like a hyena, which, he would insist afterward, was purely professional. And the notarized Odd Chimp Hypothesis was quietly amended, in the annex, to read: *findings provisional; subject got lucky.*

In the end Andromandrill mutated again — toward latex and plastic, of all things, having acquired a taste for gaskets — thinned into the upper air, and drifted west toward the sea, growing, changing. The infant was adopted by the Wildvine typing pool, never stopped screaming, and never was sick a day in its life, being permanently too sour for anything. Old Gibbon was discharged with a prescription of one glass of fermented sap nightly, medicinally, which he took like a man. Stone and Hall watched the sun come up through the jungle and split the last banana on the lid of the sealed core.

The final report ran to nine hundred pages and concluded, in bold, in the executive summary, twice: WE DO NOT KNOW.

"Any predictions?" asked Hall.

"One," said Stone. "Next time, we will at least try to be less surprised. They always do."
