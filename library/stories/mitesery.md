# Mitesery

*A parody of "Misery"*

Paul Shimmon had done a foolish thing, and he knew it the moment the storm came up: he had driven the mountain pass in November, in an open tree-runner jeep, with the only copy of his new manuscript on the seat beside him.

He had killed off Mitesery Chastain. Mitesery — the plucky Victorian bonobo heroine of eight bestselling novels, the monkey who had paid his grocery bills for fifteen years — was dead at the end of chapter seventy-two. He had written THE END at the Silver Vine Lodge, drunk two bourbon-and-banana punches to celebrate, and pointed the jeep downhill.

The last thing he remembered was the jeep sliding, a tree arriving politely at the windshield, and the sky going white and quiet.

He woke in a guest nest, both legs wrapped in bamboo splints, and a big, calm, moon-faced lemur watching from a stool.

"You crashed," she said. "I dragged you out with my winch. Paul Shimmon. I know who you are. I'm your number one fan."

Her name was Annie Lemur. She was a retired veterinary nurse with broad grey shoulders, a shelf of his books in honey-colored rows, and no telephone. "Lines are down all over the county," she said, feeding him a Banril tablet — banana-flavored, chalky, wonderful — whenever the pain scratched. "You'll stay till you're mended. I'll take care of you like nobody ever. Can ya believe it? Paul Shimmon, in MY nest."

She asked, shy as a groom, to read the new manuscript. He gave it to her, flattered and fuzzy with Banril.

She read in the corner all afternoon. Then she set it down with the gentleness of someone handling a dead parrot.

"You dirty bird," she said quietly. "How could you? Mitesery Chastain can't be dead. She just CAN'T."

"Annie, it's a book—"

"I read every one," she said, moony-eyed, staring somewhere over his left ear. "Eight books. I waited for every one. And in the ninth you kill her? Whosoever reads this, I hope they know what a dirty bird you are."

She went to the moon, and when she came back from the moon she was carrying the manuscript and a box of matches.

"Burn it," she said. "There's only one copy, you said so. Burn it, and you'll write a new one. A good one. Where she lives."

He burned it, page by page, in the ashtray, his hands shaking, his heart going like a fist on a drum. And then he wrote. She brought an old Leafmaster typewriter, its "e" missing. He wrote an entire novel without the letter "e" — *Mitesery's Return* — and it was, God help him, the best thing he had ever done, because for the first time in fifteen years he wasn't writing for the grocer. He was writing to stay warm.

He was also fairly sure he was a prisoner. The phone never got fixed. The Banril bottle got emptier. He tried the window once, made it as far as the sill, and discovered what Annie's calm could turn into.

"I'm not going to hurt you much," she said — which, he learned, is Annie for *I am about to hurt you quite a lot*. She clipped his climbing claws, every finger and both thumbs, close to the quick. "There," she said, holding up the little curved crescents in the lamplight. "A monkey who can't grip can't run. God gave you those, and I'm just keeping them for Him."

After that he earned privileges the way convicts earn sunlight. The wheelchair, first, for an hour a day. He explored the nest while she drove to town for figs, and in the parlor he found the album, its cover stamped MEMORY LANE in flaking gold. He expected vacation leaves. He found clippings. The Bouncing Baby Bonanza case — eleven infants gone still on her watch at the clinic, and a trial that ended in an acquittal that surprised even the defense. A move to the county. A contest: I'M YOUR NUMBER ONE FAN, said the ribbon on her scrapbook photo, beside a grin he now knew personally. And a recent one, corners still sharp: STATE TROOPER MISSING, SOUTH GRID ROAD, with a note in Annie's round hand: *He asked too many questions. Nothing to do with little old me!!*

Paul put MEMORY LANE back exactly as he'd found it, one spine tilted to the left, and did not scream until she was home and in the doorway, when he screamed with great skill at a mouse.

The trooper's cruiser came and went from the county road, slower every week. Once, when Annie was at market, it pulled right up the drive. Paul waved both armless-feeling arms from the window. The young trooper tipped his hat. And then Paul watched Annie come out of the trees with a basket of figs and her Sunday smile, and the trooper drove off with a free fig and no report, and Paul understood that he was the only monkey left who knew MEMORY LANE by heart.

He kept writing. It bought her moods off him one mood at a time, and it did something else besides: it made him honest. Somewhere past the missing "e" he stopped laying traps of adjectives and started laying track. When Mitesery came back from the dead in chapter eleven — buried, not burned, in the Chastain family plot, awake in the dark, using her ring to scratch at the coffin lid, one letter at a time — even Annie wept, and he was not too proud to notice he had made her weep with a chapter and not with a trick.

"How does it end?" Annie demanded nightly, massaging her big grey hands. "Does she live? She lives, right? CAN YA tell me she lives?"

"Not till the last page," Paul said, and meant it in a way he had never meant anything.

He planned — slow, like erosion. He built a second manuscript, real pages on the outside, blanks within; and inside the hollow of his splint, where the itching lived, he kept one match.

He had thought of calling the trick Matchless. He settled, in the end, for just doing it.

The night he typed THE END, Annie brought champagne, actual champagne, humming, dusting the tabletop twice, laying out two glasses. She hadn't looked so happy since the acquittal. "After," she said, "we'll read it together, the whole thing, out loud, just us." And Paul thought: she will be leaning over these pages within the minute, and nothing in this nest will ever lean over anything again.

He made her lean close for the last page — and lit the match and touched it to the fake. The manuscript went up like the harvest bonfire. Annie shrieked and snatched at the burning pages, dancing with them — "the BOOK, the BOOK, you DIRTY BIRD" — and while she danced with the fire, Paul Shimmon, who could not grip, could still swing: the Leafmaster came around in both arms like the wrath of a disappointed fan.

The horns didn't come till morning, and by then the nest had a smell of smoke and banana and things a family publication covers with a tasteful leaf. MEMORY LANE went into evidence. The county read all about it, every clipping finding its place at last.

Years later, at a diner, a waitress told him she was his number one fan and asked him to sign a napkin. He tipped her three hundred percent and walked to his car very fast, because in the dark, sometimes, he still heard it: the little tick, tick, tick of claws being clipped.
