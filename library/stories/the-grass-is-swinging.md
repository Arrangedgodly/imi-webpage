# The Grass Is Swinging

*A parody of "The Grass Is Singing"*

The drum beat it out along the jungle telegraph, flat and without comment: MARY TURNER, WIFE OF DICK TURNER OF THE FIG GROVE, FOUND DEAD IN HER TREEHOUSE. HOUSEBONOBO SOUGHT. The drum did not say why, and no monkey asked. That was the code of the pale-furred colony: the drum never explains, and the troop never wonders. Murder, in any case, is not a tragedy to such monkeys; it is a breach of etiquette.

Mary had come out to the colony years before, a pale-furred macaque with a post in the town office and a treehouse of her own, and for a long time that was enough. She had left behind her a father who drank his wages and a mother who wore herself grey over him; the sound of a male monkey laughing over drink could still tighten her fur, though she had forgotten, by the usual method, why. In the colony she had her career, her cinema evenings, her cool veranda; and if there was a panther of loneliness under the treehouse, she did not look at it. Then one evening at the club she overheard the young monkeys doing their giggling arithmetic — her age, her childlessness, her unclaimed branch — and the panther looked back. In a panic she married Dick Turner, a thin, stubborn, hopeless fig-farmer she had known four weeks, whose groves failed on a noble schedule: one disaster a year, occasionally two. On the honeymoon she wept for two days; Dick, mortified, blamed the climate, which was fair, as the climate would take the blame for everything eventually.

The treehouse leaked. The rains came through the thatch like the grooming she never received. Dick planted figs with a martyr's devotion and harvested grass. Every season brought a new salvation: pigs, which died of fever with their savings; turkeys, which were carried off by an eagle with excellent judgment; a deep borehole, which struck rock; and finally, in the drought year, a refrigerator, bought on hire purchase from the town, which stood gleaming in the kitchen unplugged, the one cold thing in a hundred miles of heat. Dick sickened each season with the fever, and recovered, and planted again. The neighboring fat cat of the district, Charlie Slatter, kept circling the grove with an offer on his lips and disapproval in his tail.

"You want to buy him out," his wife said.

"I want," said Charlie, "to see a pale-furred family running a grove properly. Banana meal. Mango meal. None of this fig sentiment. Sentiment," he added, watching Dick's yellow rows through the window, "is the one crop that never fails him."

Once, when Dick lay in a fever, Mary ran the store counter herself, and a young pale-furred tourist female came in for film for her camera and did not recognize her — took her, with one cool glance, for the hired help. Mary said nothing, charged her for the film, and went back along the rows in the hundred-degree noon with her fur up. It was of that afternoon, later, that she thought most: not the murder, not the marriage — the glance.

And then there was Moses.

Moses was the housebonobo — big-boned, powerful, slow, with eyes that saw everything and reported nothing. He had been to a mission school in his youth, could read and write and figure, and had done a term in prison for theft; he had come out of it with a dignity that the colony found unforgivable, since dignity, in their arithmetic, was a pale-furred possession. That a male should work in the kitchen broke every unwritten rule of the colony, but Dick had hired him in the fever year and never noticed the scandal, being busy failing. Moses drew the water, built the fire, cleaned the lamps, and watched Mary with a patience that was worse than rudeness.

One terrible evening, with the neighbors' eyes upon her and the old colony code demanding a display, she struck him — once, with the riding whip, knocking him down at the window where he stood. It was the last honest thing she ever did. Afterwards she could not look at his eyes. She gave him notice; then, being unable to face the neighbors' questions about why, unhired him again silently; and the colony's whole machinery of speech broke down between them. She managed, instead, a series of small coldnesses, which the whole treehouse understood to be an apology that could never be spoken, and Moses understood it too, which was somehow unforgivable. After that he called her "Madam" in a new voice; and the word sat in the kitchen between them like a stone.

The district doctor, motoring out on his rounds, took one look at her — the wrists like twigs, the eyes going independent of each other in the light — and told Dick plainly that his wife must go away, to the coast, to the sea, anywhere, for six months. Dick agreed, in his mild bewildered way, and planted another ten acres of figs to pay for it.

Dick sickened for good in the drought year. Mary ran the grove herself, badly, in the murderous heat, standing for hours on the veranda staring at nothing, her fur gone dull as old rope. The sun came into her head and stayed there. Charlie Slatter came daily now, sitting on the veranda like a fat python, not even pretending to visit Dick. At night, alone, Mary listened to the country. People said the grass sang at night, out on the veld where no monkey had any business being. What she heard, below the fig rows, was the grass *swinging* — not the wind: things in it, vervets, shadows, everything unsaid between her troop and the dark-furred workers it kept in the low branches of the world. Toward the end she heard it in daylight too, at noon, in the flat white silence, when nothing moved and the whole country leaned against the wall of the treehouse and swung.

On the last night, Dick was sweating in his cot and the drum had gone quiet. Mary sat in the kitchen with the lamp low, and considered the lock on the door. Every pale-furred monkey locked the door at night; the code required it; the lock was the whole architecture of the colony in one small bolt.

She did not turn the key.

She heard a step on the ladder, unhurried, familiar. She heard the grass swinging very close, against the wall, and understood — in the last moment the colony had left her — that the drum had never explained anything because there was nothing in this country that could be explained, only endured.

The dark in the doorway was shaped like Moses.

The drum beat it out along the jungle telegraph in the morning, flat and without comment. The pale-furred monkeys shook their heads and said the thing they always said, that you cannot make a housebonobo of a bonobo. Moses was found sitting under the treehouse, waiting, his paws folded, looking at the horizon as if listening for a sentence that had not yet finished. Charlie Slatter had the grove at his price within the week, and Dick, who had by then ceased to mind anything, was put on the train south. The grass swung. It explained nothing.
