# Dark Chatter

*A parody of "Dark Matter"*

**London, August 1937.** I was a radio chimp with a third-class certificate and an empty pantry, so when the letter came — *radio operator required, eighteen months, Arctic expedition, generous pay* — I said yes before I'd finished reading it. Pride is a luxury. Bananas are a necessity.

The leader was Gus Balfour, a golden lion tamarin with an old family name and new money, charming in the way of monkeys who have never once been hungry. "Gruhuken, Jack," he said, rolling the word like a brandy. "A bay in northeast Greenland. Nobody's wintered there. We'll have it to ourselves."

"Why has nobody wintered there?" I asked.

"Silly local superstitions," said Gus breezily. "Trappers' tales."

Heerem, our Danish hunter — a grey-muzzled macaque who had spent thirty years in the high Arctic and smiled at nothing — said nothing at all. But I caught him looking at the chart of Gruhuken the way you look at a dog that has bitten once already.

We built the station in September: one hut, one wireless mast, one mountain of crates, and a sledge team of eight husky-built baboons, with the lead monkey a scarred old soldier called Erik. On our first walk of the shore we found the trapper's hut, half-swallowed by moss, and beside it a grave with a wooden marker gone soft as cheese. No name left. Only a date: 1908.

"Fever, they say," said Gus. "Poor devil."

Heerem spat, elegantly, into the scree. "His partners did not take him on the boat," he said. "That is what they say also."

I want to be fair to what followed, so I will set down plainly that I did not believe in ghosts. I believed in atmospherics, in fatigue, in the wind. All through October, as the light drained from the sky like water from a bath, I sat my wireless watch and heard, beneath the morse, a chatter. Not static. Chatter — the rise and fall of it, the rhythm of conversation, of some old monkey talking and talking to nobody who answered. I logged it as atmospherics. I underlined the word twice.

Then I saw the figure. It was the last week of the sun. I was calibrating the mast at dusk, and I looked up from my instruments across the shore, and a trapper stood on the scree — long coat, ancient rifle, fur cap — perfectly still, watching the hut. Not watching me. Watching the hut, the way you watch a house you used to live in. The light failed for an instant — one blink only — and he was gone, and there were no footprints on the frost.

Erik howled all night. None of the others made a sound, which was somehow worse.

After that, things left. First our third man, a Cambridge macaque named Deedes, who broke his wrist falling — or said he fell — and sailed home on the last supply ship. Then Gus caught his arm in the winch; it went bad, red and hot, and he too went out on the ship, weeping with fury and fever, promising to return in the spring. "Heerem stays, doesn't he?" he kept saying. "You're not alone, Jack?"

Heerem did not stay. He walked me to the hut on the last day of his contract, gripping my shoulder with his old grey paw.

"Little radio friend," he said. "Come with me. There is room on the boat. I will say you are ill; it is no great lie, you look it. That grave out there — the trapper was not taken because he was dying. He was dying because he was not taken. Do you understand me? A monkey alone in the dark becomes —" he hesitated, hating the word — "*something else.* Thirty years I have come north, and I have seen the ones left behind. They do not haunt us, these ones. They *recruit.*"

I stayed. I am still not sure whether it was courage or poverty. Both feel identical at the time.

Polar night fell on the fourth of November, and Gruhuken went dark from one moon to the next. And in that dark the chatter got louder. It moved out of my earphones and into the air. It stood outside the wireless window while I worked, a suggestion of shoulders in the black, talking its endless one-sided talk. Once, in the beam of my lamp, I saw frost on the *inside* of the glass, on the inside only, drawn in a pattern I could not stop seeing: a boat, and an empty sea, and one small figure on the shore growing smaller.

I found the trapper's diary that week, under the floor of his old hut. The last entries were thirty days of nothing but *ook* — pages and pages, the handwriting going from schoolroom-neat to something dragged. And then, on the very last page, in a hand suddenly, terribly calm: "They knew I could hear them. That is why they left me. The dark talks. You have only to be quiet and it talks. Send the boat to the others. Send the boat to everyone."

My mother died when I was nine and my brother followed her in the spring, and I have spent my whole life since being quiet about it, being cheerful, being no trouble. I understood then why Heerem had offered to lie for me. Grief is a door, and that shore was a hall of doors, all standing open.

In December the sledge team began to die. Not mysteriously — worse, *obediently*. They stopped howling at the figure on the scree and began to sit for it, at attention, eight husky baboons at parade rest, facing the grave. I dug up the marker plank and rebuilt the grave properly, carved him a name — I called him by the only name I had any right to: *Brother* — and I sat beside it through a whole night of the dark, my back to his stone, and I talked. I told him about my mother's cough, and my brother's laugh, and the price of bananas in 1937, and every lonely thing I had ever swallowed, out loud, at last, into the listening black.

The chatter on the shore stopped at dawn.

Not ended — stopped, the way a caller stops when someone finally picks up. It never came to my window again. Only once more, the very last night of the dark, when Erik stood up in the kennel and growled at the scree, and I saw the trapper far off in the moonlight by his own hut — but turned away from my station, facing the sea, waiting where he had been left. I put the lamp in the window and left it burning till morning. It seemed the least a monkey could do.

The ship came in May. Gus came off it one-armed and brown and loud, and found me thin and calm and oddly improved, the way a wound is after it has finally been allowed to hurt.

"Good God, Jack," he said. "Alone all winter. Didn't you go mad?"

"No," I said. "We talked."

**Afterword.** Old Jack Miller, radio chimp, wrote all this down fifty years on, in a warm flat, a wife asleep in the next room. He never wintered north again; he never needed to. But he kept, all his life, one small habit, and his wife never once asked him why.

Every night, before bed, he would switch on the old wireless in the corner, tune it slowly down past the stations, past the morse, into the sea of static underneath — and listen, just for a moment, to the chatter.

And whatever it said, wherever it came from, he would answer it, quietly, so as not to wake the house.

"Ooh goes there, brother," he'd say. "Ooh goes there. You're not alone."

And, he always swore, the dark — just sometimes — went quiet to listen.
