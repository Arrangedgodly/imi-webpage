# The Neverending Tail

*A parody of "The Neverending Story"*

"Bastian? Bastian Balthasar Bumbo?" his father called that morning. But Bastian — small, pear-shaped, hopeless at climbing, best at hiding — was already at Coreander's bookshop, where a gruff old orangutan eyed him over half-moon spectacles.

"The monkey tele-vine is full of adventure," rumbled Coreander, "and instead you hide in books. Books are dangerous. Books are doors. Go home."

But when the old ape shuffled off, Bastian saw it: a book bound in copper banana-silk, with two serpents biting each other's tails — a tail without end. THE NEVERENDING TAIL. He stole it (leaving his fig-money on the counter, because a coward he might be, but a thief never), ran to the schoolhouse attic, locked the hatch, wrapped himself in the grooming blankets, and began to read.

Fantastica — the Endless Canopy — was dying. A gray emptiness called the Nothing was eating the jungle, and where it passed, branches forgot how to be branches, rivers forgot they were wet, and whole troops simply stopped being told. The Childlike Empress, ruler of all Fantastica from her Ivory Fig Tree, was dying too. So she summoned a hunter: Atreyu, a young monkey of the plains, armed with nothing but a grooming-claw and a summons.

"You must find a cure for the Empress," said the ancient courier. "You must give her... a new name."

Atreyu rode out on Artax, his faithful gibbon mount, to the Swamps of Sadness, where the only danger is despair — and despair weighs a body down.

"Artax, you stupid gibbon, MOVE!" cried Atreyu as Artax sank into the gray muck.

"I can't, master. It's the sadness. It's all around. I love you, Atreyu. Don't forget me. Tell the Empress I did my best—"

And Artax was gone. (In the attic, Bastian's chest went quite seriously wrong.)

Alone, Atreyu pressed on — to Morla the Aged One, the ancient tortoise ("Child... of... the... plains... we... don't... care"), who sent him across a desert of gray dust to the Southern Oracle, where the Lady Uyulala's song told him the terrible truth:

"The Empress needs a new name, but no one INSIDE Fantastica can give it. It must come from outside. From a human child—" the song wavered, "—well. From a chimp child. A reader."

Atreyu turned. "A READER? I'm IN a story?"

And far away in the attic, Bastian dropped the book and said, out loud, "That's ME. She means ME." (And he looked around the attic to make sure the book hadn't heard, which it had.)

When the Nothing came howling, Atreyu fought it with a bravery that did no good at all, fell, and woke on the back of Falkor — a luckgorilla, thirty feet of golden, gently smiling ape, swimming through the sky like a warm idea.

"Don't worry," said Falkor. "A luckgorilla is the best luck you can have. Scratch behind the left ear and anything's possible." Atreyu scratched. It was.

But they were too late. The Nothing had swallowed nine-tenths of Fantastica; only the Ivory Fig Tree remained, floating alone in the gray, and the Childlike Empress waited there, white-robed and terribly calm.

"Empress, I failed you," wept Atreyu. "There's no cure, no name, nothing left—"

"No, Atreyu," she said gently. "You brought him. He's been listening all along." She looked up — directly out of the page — at Bastian in the attic. "You know my name. Say it, and save us all."

Bastian's hands shook. He was nothing — a fat little chimp who couldn't climb, whose father had stopped seeing him since Mother died. What if he said it wrong? But her golden eyes went through him like sun through fig leaves, and the name rose in him:

"MOON CHILD!" he shouted. "I believe in you! MOON CHILD!"

The Nothing burst like a soap bubble. Fantastica bloomed back in a single green WHOOMPH — branch upon branch, river upon river — and the Empress smiled and held out to Bastian a single grain of sand, the seed of a whole new jungle.

"Come in," said Moon Child. "You saved us."

And Bastian Balthasar Bumbo stood up in the attic and stepped through.

He was IN. On his chest hung AURYN — the two-tailed circle — with its simple instruction: DO WHAT YOU WISH. Bastian wished, and Fantastica obeyed. He wished himself tall, and golden-maned, and brave. He wished a Night Jungle called Perilin into being, glowing trees that bloomed in silver and sang lullabies to their fruit. He rescued a village of Tree-Folk from their own forgetful stairs; he sailed the fig-wine sea; wherever he went, crowds chanted his name, which was the point, and the poison.

Because each wish cost a memory of the real world. The tall golden hero could no longer remember his father's face. He forgot his attic, his school, his own small stubborn courage. When at last his old friend Atreyu came to take AURYN back — "It's eating you, Bastian! You'll forget you ever existed!" — the golden hero fought him, grooming-claw to grooming-claw, and won, and wounded him, and stood over his only friend in a silence that finally rang louder than any chanting crowd.

He carried Atreyu to the darkest mine in Fantastica, where Yor, the blind miner, dug pictures out of the rock — pictures of the things the world had forgotten. In a gallery of glittering stone, Bastian found one he could not explain: a tired chimp's face, over a workbench, not seeing a small son standing right there.

"What is that?" whispered Bastian.

"Your deepest one," said Yor. "I mine what's lost, and lost is what you're becoming. The only way home is to find your one true wish."

And Bastian, at last, understood: he didn't want to be a fiercer, stronger, golden monkey. He had only ever wanted his father to look up from his sadness and see him. He wished — with his last full heart — to love, and to be loved, and AURYN slipped from his chest like a leaf choosing its own branch.

At the Water of Life, Atreyu forgave him everything, and drank his health, and promised to remember all of Fantastica for him while Bastian carried the water home.

And the tail that had no end curled around him like a rope thrown down a well, and Bastian climbed it — up, and out, and back through the attic hatch, clutching the copper book, drenched with fig-blossom water from between the worlds.

He ran home and hugged his father, who DID look up — really up, the way he hadn't since Mother died — and Bastian said a true thing: "I brought Moon Child's water for you."

And the strangest part: to get home, he had needed three wishes and a tail without end, but to hear his father laugh, he needed only to tell the story. So he told it, all of it, every night for a week.

The next morning, he returned the book to Coreander's shop.

"You've read it," said the old orangutan, eyes narrowing.

"Yes. I lived it."

"Hmph." Coreander turned a page. "The story never ends. There are doors to Fantastica in every book — and this shop, little Bumbo, is FULL of books."

And Coreander grinned, slid a banana-silk volume across the counter, and said, "But be careful, chimp. This one's got quite a tail."
