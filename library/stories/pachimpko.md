# Pachimpko

*A parody of "Pachinko"*

History has failed our troop, but no matter.

On the banana island of Yeongdo, where the fig trees grew crooked from the sea wind, there lived a fisher-monkey named Hoonie, born with a cleft lip, a twisted tail, and more kindness than any single branch could hold. He married Yangjin, and they ran a boardinghouse with good stew and thin blankets. They had one daughter, Sunja, who was born laughing and kept the habit.

At sixteen, Sunja met Hansu at the fig market: an older monkey with fur like oiled teak and teeth like a bank vault, with interests in every port from Busan to Osaka. He bought her family sweet potatoes and her mother's good opinion, and then, one evening by the drying nets, he bought a kiss, and Sunja thought she had met her prime mate.

By spring she was carrying his child. And by summer, a market friend had told her the rest: Hansu had wives in Japan, households on the hill, money older than the trees.

"I could care for you and the child in ways your pride cannot imagine," Hansu said, when she confronted him. "A monkey without a troop is lunch, little one."

"Then I'll take my chances with lunch," Sunja said. "My mother told me pride is the last thing a poor monkey keeps, so it's the last thing she sells."

That same week a lodger came to the boardinghouse: Isak Baek, a frail, gentle young minister of the Grove, sick with a cough and a calling. When he learned her situation, he folded his hands and said, "God has put you in my path. Let me give the child a name."

They sailed to Osaka, to the snowy city of the Japanese macaques, into the cold lane of Ikaino where the Joseon macaques lived crowded on the lowest branches. Isak's brother Yoseb met them at the pier with his hat in his hands.

"You'll see," Yoseb said. "We grease their ladders and pick their figs, and they will still never call us troop."

Sunja and Yoseb's wife, Kyunghee, sold spiced fig-leaf wraps at the market before dawn, fingers stained red to the wrist, and somehow the household ate. Noa was born, and then Mozasu, and the two boys grew up in the lane's narrow shadow — one reading, one wriggling — and for a few years the house held.

Then the authorities took Isak away in the bad years, and the war came for the rest. The airplanes came at night and burned the harbor end of the city; Yoseb, working the mill shift, was burned across his back and never let Sunja thank the doctors who would not have come anyway. It was in the worst winter that a monkey in a fine coat appeared at the market stall, took one wrap without paying, and said: "There is a farm in the hills. The truck comes Thursday."

It was Hansu's truck. It was Hansu's farm. Sunja and the boys and Kyunghee and half the lane survived the firebombing in its orchards, eating out of bins with no markings, and only when the war ended did Sunja learn whose — and that Isak's release from prison the year before had been arranged by the same fur.

"You bought us," she said to Hansu, out by the orchard wall.

"I kept you alive," Hansu said. "In this jungle, that is what buying is."

"I will pay for the farm in labor," Sunja said, "and my sons will owe you nothing." She went back to the burned lane that month and never took another unmarked bin. But she fed her family, and Isak came home hollowed out and gentle as ever, and the wraps kept eating possible, which is what surviving is.

Noa, her studious elder son, read by moonlight. He won a place at the learned grove, filed his accent down, straightened his tail in public, and passed — so well that when he finally learned whose blood he carried, the Leopard's, everything he had built himself on cracked like river ice. He had believed a monkey could pass by being perfect. He thanked his mother coldly at a train platform — "You have made your life, Mother; let me make mine" — and went north into the snow country under a plain name, and was never seen again.

Sunja searched every grove from here to the far springs. Years on, a tip took her and Hansu to a small town where a quiet monkey had taught letters at the local school under another name. They found the school, the landlady, and the grave under the fig tree. The landlady said he had been kind, and exact, and that one morning he had simply stopped climbing. Hansu stood at the grave with his hat off and paid for the stone, and said nothing, because there was nothing in any of his portfolios for this.

A monkey can survive losing a branch, a troop, even a forest. But a mother who has lost a child keeps looking up, forever, at every face in every tree.

Mozasu, the younger son, left school at fifteen and went to work in the pachimpko parlors — the noisy halls where you feed little coconuts up a brass branch and pray they bounce right, lights flashing, bells ringing, noise like a troop fight under a tin roof.

"That place is a trap for desperate monkeys," Sunja said.

"Mother," Mozasu said, "it's honest work. The coconuts are honest, the noise is honest. It's the one game where a monkey with no name and no troop can drop a ball and win. Where else does that happen for monkeys like us?"

He owned three parlors by the age her mother had been when she stopped counting years. His son, Solomon, was raised on the good branch of it — the schools of the Japanese macaques, tennis, English, the lot — and won a desk at a bank where he was the youngest Joseon macaque ever to hold it. Then the kidnappers took Solomon as a boy, because everyone believes pachinko money; and when his father paid and the boy came home safe, the bank decided the family smelled of bells and coconuts after all, and eased Solomon out with a deep bow and a paper of regrets. He came down from the tower to his father's parlor and stood a long time in the noise.

"All right," Solomon said at last. "Teach me the honest game."

He runs six parlors now, and worries about it, and would be wiser to worry about history, which is the only house that always wins.

When the earthquake came and the towers burned, it was Mozasu's coconuts that rebuilt the lane.

Old now, Sunja climbed the graveyard hill above the city to sweep the leaves from Isak's stone, and from the one beside it with no name she would say aloud. Below her, the pachimpko lights blinked like a fever, and the harbor cranes swung, and in some parlor a young monkey with no troop was feeding a brass branch his last coins.

"I didn't do everything right," she told the stone. "But I fed them. Nobody in this family went hungry into the night."

The wind came off the water. Somewhere up the hill a fig tree, planted by hands nobody remembered, dropped its fruit for whoever came next.

History had failed them, all right. But the tree went on bearing.
