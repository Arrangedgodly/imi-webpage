# Chimplet

*A parody of "Hamlet"*

Something was rotten in the Troop of Denmarrow, and it wasn't the fruit pile.

Prince Chimplet, young heir to the canopy, shuffled along the scaffold branches in his little black fur, sighing at figs and declining to groom anybody. His father, the old King, was dead — they said a fig fell on him, but figs don't leave tooth-marks — and barely a moon later his uncle Clawdius had climbed onto the top branch and married Chimplet's mother, Queen Gertrude-ape, explaining cheerfully that this was "thrift": "With mirth in fig-eral and with dirge in marriage, we keep the catering to one event. All hail the savings!"

"A little more than kin, and less than kind," muttered Chimplet.

"Fie, my prince, cast thy nighted color off!" begged his mother. "Thou know'st 'tis common: all that lives must die."

"Ay, madam, it is common," said Chimplet, gazing into the middle distance. "O, that this too, too solid fur would melt, thaw, and resolve itself into a dew — or at the very least into a decent grooming."

But that midnight, on the high ramparts, his friend Horatio-ape grabbed his arm. "Look, my lord! It comes!"

A gray shape drifted through the fig branches — the old King's ghost, his eyes two sad coconuts.

"I am thy father's spirit," it moaned. "The fig that fell upon me was thrown, Chimplet. Thy uncle poured fig-nectar in my ear as I slept in the sun! Now, farewell. Remember me!"

"Remember thee!" cried Chimplet. "Yes, by these ten thousand banana peels, I'll remember thee! O villain, villain, smiling, damned villain!" From that hour, Chimplet promised two things: revenge, and acting extremely strange in the meantime. "The time is out of joint," he told Horatio-ape. "O cursed spite, that ever I was born to set it right! And there are more things in heaven and earth, Horatio, than are dreamt of in your philosophy. Also in mine. I checked."

Nobody did suspect a thing, mostly because Chimplet was now impossible to distinguish from the troop's usual teenagers. He announced he had lost his 'nanas. "I am but mad north-by-canopy," he explained, "when the wind is southerly." When old Polonius asked what he was reading, he said, "Words, words, words," and turned the leaf over meaningfully; and when Polonius observed that there was method in his madness, Chimplet replied, "There's a method to my grooming, too," and groomed the old baboon backward, which took a week to fix.

Old Polonius, chief advisor to usurping Clawdius, lectured everyone who held still: "Neither a borrower nor a lender be! To thine own troop be true!" His daughter Ophelia, a gentle gibbon, had been told to stop seeing Chimplet, and she watched her prince's antics with dewy eyes. "O, what a noble mind is here o'erthrown!" she sighed, and Chimplet, striding past, delivered the cruellest thing he ever said: "To a nursery, go."

For a while he wondered if he should simply give up altogether, and wandered the fig-groves muttering: "To flea, or not to flea — that is the grooming. Whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer the itches and scratches of outrageous fortune, or to take arms against a sea of troubles, and by grooming end them. To doze, to groom — no more; and by a groom to say we end the heartache and the thousand natural shocks that fur is heir to." He was still deciding when a troop of traveling players swung into Denmarrow — performing macaques, jugglers of fruit — and Chimplet had his idea.

"Speak the speech, I pray you, trippingly on the tongue," he coached them. "Do not, as the uncapable macaque does, saw the air too much. Suit the action to the word, the word to the action — and juggle the fruit only where the text allows."

Then: "Play me a murder," he requested, "where a jealous uncle naps a king with fig-nectar. Tonight." He told Horatio-ape: "The play's the thing, wherein I'll catch the conscience of the King."

*The Banana-Trap* was terrible theater and brilliant detective work, because halfway through the murder scene, Clawdius leapt up, knocked over the fruit bowl, shrieked "AIR! FRESH AIR!" and fled the hollow.

"Got him," said Chimplet, grimly.

Clawdius, badly rattled, sent for two of Chimplet's old schoolmates, Rosencrantz-nest and Guildenstern-nest, a pair of macaques so alike that even their mothers used initials. "My lord, we were sent for," they began, and Chimplet played them a little bamboo flute until they begged him to stop. "You would play upon ME?" he said. "You would pluck out the heart of my mystery? Call me what instrument you will — though you can fret me, you cannot play upon me." They delivered this to the King verbatim, minus the parts they didn't understand, which was all of it.

He found Clawdius kneeling in prayer — a perfect moment. But no: strike a monkey at prayer and he'd climb straight to heaven. "Now might I do it pat," Chimplet muttered, sheathing his fig-knife, "but a took without a reckoning goes up, not down."

He went instead to his mother's chamber, where Polonius hid behind a curtain of woven banana leaves. "Mother, thou hast thy father much offended!" Hearing rustling behind the leaves, Chimplet shrieked "A rat! A rat!" and stabbed through the arras, which is how Denmarrow lost its chief advisor. Clawdius, delighted, packed the prince off to England at once, with Rosencrantz-nest and Guildenstern-nest carrying a leaf-scroll instructing the English to "groom him sharply." Chimplet, who could read over shoulders, swapped the scroll for one that read "TREAT THE BEARERS TO A LIFETIME OF MANDATORY NAP DUTY," and said nothing, and escaped his escort by teaming up with a troop of pirate macaques ("best career move I ever made").

Ophelia, meanwhile, had gone about the troop handing everyone flowers and fruit: "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance... and there's a banana for you, and one for you. We must be patient." She climbed the willow over the stream to hang her garlands, and the branch broke, and she floated away downstream, out of Denmarrow, out of every tale but this one — and for a long time everyone feared the worst.

Chimplet came home just in time for a memorial and an immediately scheduled duel. In the graveyard, he plucked a familiar skull-prop from the diggers.

"Alas, poor Yorick-nut!" he cried, holding up the coconut that had been the jester's favorite. "I knew him, Horatio — a fellow of infinite jest, of most excellent fancy! He hath borne me on his back a thousand times. Where be your gibes now? Your flashes of merriment? Your knack for juggling three of these at once, which I never once managed?"

Then the duel: young Leertes, Ophelia's brother, with a climbing-gaff secretly tipped in sap-poison, and a cup of banana nectar also poisoned, from which — whoops — Gertrude-ape drank.

"The smoothie! The smoothie!" she gasped. "My lord, the smoothie!"

Leertes, wounded and sorry, gasped, "The king's to blame! The tip's envenomed — I can never be saved!" And Chimplet, at last, did it pat: he swung the climbing-gaff, poured the rest of the poisoned smoothie down his uncle's throat, and Clawdius fell — king, queen, uncle, plot — all at once.

But the tip had pricked Chimplet too. "I am dead, Horatio. Thou livest; report me and my cause aright... the rest is bananas."

"Good night, sweet primate," wept Horatio-ape, "and flights of flying foxes sing thee to thy rest."

At which point Fortinbr-ape, a foreign monkey-general who happened to be invading, arrived and took over Denmarrow, as is traditional. "Bear Chimplet like a soldier up the sunniest branch," he ordered, "for he was likely, had he been put on, to have proved most royal." They threw fruit — a tale told by a troop, full of sound and furry, signifying nothing.

And one footnote more, which arrived by otter-post the following spring: a river-troop far downstream reported a gibbon in their meadows, quite well, handing out rosemary to strangers and bananas to friends. So Ophelia never learned how it all ended — which, Horatio-ape wrote in his chronicle, was perhaps the only kindness the whole rotten story managed, and the reason he always signed his history of Denmarrow the same way: "The rest is bananas."
