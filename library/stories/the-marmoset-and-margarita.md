# The Marmoset and Margarita

*A parody of "The Master and Margarita"*

Follow me, reader! We shall climb the great banyan of the capital together, at the hour when the heat lies on the canopy like a fat old python who has dined unwisely. On a broad gray fork sat two monkeys: Berlioz, editor of the thick literary journal *The Fig*, solemn in his spectacles, and the poet Ivan Banana-domny, who had written an anti-banana poem so atrocious the journal intended to print it on the grounds that it could do no harm.

"If I understand you," said Berlioz, "you propose to prove there never was any Great Banana."

"Quite simply," said Ivan, "there wasn't."

At this the air went cold as a keeper's hose. Between them stood a mandrill — tall, expensively gray, his nose a rainbow in mild distress — and behind him hopped a checkered capuchin with a broken pince-nez, leading a tiny bushbaby on a golden chain.

"Fascinating," said the mandrill politely. "One question, citizens. The pawpaw — does the pawpaw exist?"

"A monkey cannot believe in the pawpaw and remain a materialist!" cried Berlioz.

"Ah," said the mandrill. "Then I regret to inform you that a macaw will spill banana oil on the tram-branch at the bend, and you will slip, citizen editor, and the trolley of history is punctual." The macaw spilled; Berlioz slipped; the trolley passed; and the monkey who had just denied the supernatural was given an unanswerable demonstration and parted, permanently, from his own opinion.

Ivan gave chase, howling, was seized by keepers, and landed in the banyan asylum. The capital, meanwhile, had begun to misbehave. At the Variety Theater the mandrill — styling himself, with a shrug, Professor Woland, specialist in the anatomy of the soul, the soul being what he kept in a jar, it being otherwise unobtainable — made it rain banknotes, which the audience caught with the gravity of monkeys at a free lunch; the notes turned to banana leaves within the hour. The fashionable she-monkeys of the front rows discovered that their fine store-bought fur had flown off to visit its original owners; and the compere, who objected to the proceedings, had his head taken off, displayed to the audience, and restored with apologies. That same afternoon the theater's administrator, one Stepa Banana-copper, answered his telephone and learned he had signed himself a holiday; a moment later he was on a beach in Yalta wearing what he had slept in. Berlioz's uncle, arriving to claim the deceased's fine treehouse, was informed by the checkered capuchin that documents establishing his existence had sadly never been issued, and that in any case no persons were received there — only memories. The writers of *The Fig* colony, dining at the Griboyedov treehouse in a noise like the whole jungle feeding, attributed all of it, variously, to mass hypnosis, counter-revolution, and the brandy — the three explanations which, between them, account for everything a monkey cannot face.

In the asylum, in bed 118, Ivan met a small marmoset with gray fur and eyes like lamps that had been switched off.

"I was a historian," said the marmoset. "She called me the Master. I wrote a novel about the Procurator of the Dry Season — Pilate, a hamadryas baboon with a sick head — and about a gentle gibbon called Yeshua, who went about saying that all monkeys are good and that the whole troop of the world would one day be one troop. They told me it could never be printed. *Print that,* they said, *and we shall all hang from one branch.* So I burned it, madman. Every leaf. I burned it myself."

The novel, for the record, ran thus. In Yershalaim, white-cloaked and red-lined and allergic to his own duty, the Procurator sat hating his headache and questioning the gibbon, who answered insolence with truth — that cowardice was the worst of vices, and that the kingdom of truth was at hand, where no power of any Procurator would be needed at all. "What is truth?" asked Pilate, and sent him up the hill, and washed his paws of it for two thousand seasons. Judas of Kirioth took his thirty silver bananas and was intercepted in the moonlight by the Procurator's man; and at the hour the gibbon died, the thunderstorm came down on Yershalaim like a curtain, and Pilate sat on his stone terrace and never once moved out of it again.

Out in the city lived the monkey who loved him. Margarita had been married off years since to a worthy official monkey, and she knew only that the Master had vanished. One afternoon a red-whiskered aye-aye with a long skeletal paw appeared at her window and held out a jar.

"Cream," he said. "Rub it into your fur. At nine, leap from your window and fly north. The mandrill requests you as hostess. One condition: refuse nothing asked of you tonight."

"Is that all?" said Margarita, who had been waiting years for a condition worth obeying.

She rubbed it on, her fur came alive — every hair standing up silver — and at nine she leapt onto a sweeping-vine and rode the night wind over the jungle, howling with freedom. She wrecked the treehouse of the critic Latrangsky, whose crockery of opinions did not survive the evening, and at midnight she descended, gracious and bare-pawed, upon the Great Ball of the Jungle Shade, where the dead of ten million years arrived politely, shaking the sand from their fur: fossil monkeys, dodos, the drowned of ten thousand rivers. At the piano sat a huge black capuchin in a bow tie.

"Kindly note," he said, "I object to that word. I am not a cat. I am a very clever capuchin, and I take my fig brandy without soda."

At dawn, spent and radiant, Margarita was offered her reward. She asked for one thing only, and they brought her the marmoset — thinner, grayer, extinguished — and on the desk before him lay his manuscript, leaf upon leaf, whole.

"But I burned it," whispered the Master.

"Manuscripts never rot," said the mandrill. "I told you: what is written by a lover of the truth does not rot."

"You deserve the light," the mandrill told them both. But a messenger had come to him at dawn — a ragged, frowning monkey named Matthew Levi, who had walked out of the Master's own novel into the dawn — with a request: *He has read your work, and asks you to give them peace.* The lovers looked at each other and asked for exactly that — a treehouse, and an evening, and each other. The mandrill, who had waited two thousand seasons for somebody to get an answer right, granted it. They were given their refuge at last — a treehouse in an endless fig grove, peace, the evening light on every branch.

And the mandrill, finishing his work in that country, paused over the hamadryas Procurator, who had sat out his two thousand seasons on the stone terrace arguing about cowardice.

"Cowardice," said Margarita, "is the most terrible vice."

"Then release him," said the mandrill. "It is already granted." And up the moonpath walked Pilate with the gentle gibbon, toward the fig tree of eternity, arguing about whether all monkeys are good.

As for Ivan Banana-domny, he was discharged in the spring and never wrote another anti-banana poem. Every year, at the full moon of spring, he sat by his window and saw, without grief, a monkey who had not deserved the light resting under a fig tree with the one who loved him; and he was satisfied, and so was the troop, which counted it a public service.
