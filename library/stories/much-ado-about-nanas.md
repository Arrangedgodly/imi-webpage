# Much Ado About 'Nanas

*A parody of "Much Ado About Nothing"*

The tamarind grove of Messina buzzed like a hornet's picnic, for the troop of Don Pedro was coming home from the Fig Wars, tails high and shields polished. Old Leonato, the colobus who governed the grove, hung out banana-leaf banners. Beside him stood his daughter Hero, sweet as sun-warmed fruit, and his niece Beatrice, a vervet with a tongue so sharp the troop borrowed it to trim the hedges.

"They say Beatrice talks the bark off trees," said young Claudio, flexing his shoulders.

"Only when Benedick's in earshot," sighed Don Pedro. "Those two have fought a merry war of nitpicking for years and never once drawn blood."

Beatrice required no earshot. "Count Claudio! Still alive? Then the Fig Wars required neither courage nor cleverness. And here's Signior Benedick — the prince's jester, a very dull fool! What news from the fruit market, my lord? Has the price of wit gone up? Yours is still two bananas a barrel."

"I marvel, my lady," said Benedick, a bonobo with a magnificent opinion of himself, "that you will still be talking. Nobody marks you."

"Nobody marks me? Then the nobody inside you must be doing your listening. Good morrow, Signior Windbag." And she swung off, leaving him spluttering among the figs.

"Heaven keep me from ever winding such a mate," declared Benedick. "I will die a bachelor."

"We'll hold you to that," said Claudio. "In writing. With witnesses."

But Claudio's heart had already gone ape for Hero, and Don Pedro promised to woo her at the harvest dance in his friend's name. Only Don Pedro's brother, Don John — a bitter macaque with a soul like an unripe persimmon — plotted spoil. "It must not be denied but I am a plain-dealing villain," he told his crony Borachio. "Let it be my privilege to sour the wedding figs. Bitterness is the only crop that grows in me."

The harvest dance was masked, which in Messina meant every monkey wore a leaf over one eye and called it a disguise. Don Pedro, masked as "Claudio's representative," spun Hero beneath the fig-lanterns and spoke sweetly on his friend's behalf, while Claudio watched from the trellis shadows — where Don John found him.

"Surely your lordship is mistaken in the prince," purred Borachio from Claudio's other side, a pincer movement of unkindness. "I heard him swear he would keep the fruit — the lady — for himself."

"Then farewell, Hero," said Claudio, dropping his garland. "If my prince wrongs me, I shall say nothing — but I shall never swing the same again."

He was within an inch of challenging his own ally before Don Pedro cleared it up, laughing: "I have wooed in thy name, and she is thine — embrace, Count, and mind the thorns."

Across the floor, Beatrice — masked, and therefore completely unrecognizable to everyone except absolutely everyone — had cornered a masked stranger and asked him to hold her fan so she could insult Benedick unencumbered.

"Tell me, signior: the prince's jester, that very dull fool. He hangs about the court like an old fig no one will pick, so ripe in his own wit that no other ape's can get near him."

"Is it possible?" whispered the masked stranger, who was Benedick.

"Nothing rides on that monkey but a painted face and a little stuffing. A stuffed fig with opinions."

"Give me a mate," Benedick told the night, pale as a peeled banana, "with less wit and more beard."

Then Don Pedro set his famous trap. Benedick would be lured into the high boughs, where, unseen, he would overhear the troop loudly lamenting, "Poor Beatrice! She loves Benedick beyond reason and will perish before she ever tells him!"

Up in the branches, Benedick gripped his perch white-knuckled. "This is no trick — they spoke in sad earnest! Love me? Why, then, it must be true. I am strangely, suddenly taken. I do spy some marks of love in her, heretofore unseen — mostly because I was looking the other way and eating."

The same trap caught Beatrice behind a screen of leaves, listening to Hero sigh, "Alas, Beatrice mocks poor Benedick so cruelly, and he simply adores her."

"Contempt, farewell!" gasped Beatrice to herself. "Benedick, love on! I will repay thee with grooming, with devotion, and with never-ending access to my bananas."

Alas, Don John struck next. "Come to Hero's window tonight, Claudio," he hissed, "and see how sweet she is when her father's asleep." What Claudio saw in the dark was only Margaret, Hero's lady-in-waiting, giggling with Borachio. It was enough. At the wedding bough next morning, Claudio flung down his fig garland.

"There, Leonato, take her back again. Give not this rotten fruit to your honest troop. Her bananas are forfeit — I know her not."

"Sweet prince, why speak you not?" begged Leonato.

"I saw with mine own eyes," said Don Pedro — and eyes, everyone agreed, were famously reliable in the dark.

Hero fell from her branch in a dead faint, and the grove gasped. Leonato raged at her till the leaves shook — "O she is fallen into a pit of figs that neither bottom nor bank can hold!" — but Friar Francis studied her face and believed her. "Guilt shows on a monkey like squashed fruit, and this girl shows none. Hide her," he said. "Tell the troop she died of shame. Slander sometimes ripens into truth."

But Beatrice had bitten her tail in half holding her temper. She found Benedick at once. "Prove you love me," she said. "Kill Claudio."

"Not kill him — he's my friend—"

"Kill Claudio."

"...He is not, on reflection, my *best* friend," said Benedick. "I'll challenge him."

He found Claudio and Don Pedro at the mourning-bough, and his greeting was not merry. "You have killed a sweet lady with your slanders, and her death shall fall heavy on you. I challenge you. Name your branch."

The pair exchanged looks of enormous amusement. "You will be a substitute to the fool soon," said Claudio. "His wit is a very fantastical banquet — just a pair of knives and forks and nothing between."

"Say what you like," said Benedick, starchily. "I am in a brown study, and the study is of your conduct."

Meanwhile Dogberry, the grove's constable — a patas monkey promoted for looking tall — had mustered the night watch with ringing instructions. "Are you good men and true? We are to comprehend all vagrant males. If you meet a thief, you may know him by his habit of carrying off fruit. Bid him stand in the prince's name."

"And if he will not stand?" asked the watch.

"Then let him go, and thank the orchard you are rid of a knave," said Dogberry. "O — and write down that the watch is to be tedious to no ape. It is our chief duty."

That night the watch overheard Borachio bragging: "I earned a thousand bananas for the window trick, paid from Don John's own stash! The lady was not Hero but Margaret — aye, the prince and Count Claudio were both deceived." The watch arrested him on general principle, and when Borachio's friend Conrade called Dogberry an ass, Dogberry demanded posterity: "O, that I had been writ down a baboon, and not a constable! Set down that I am a patas of the crown, and take no pity on me!"

The truth beat Don John to the second wedding bough by half a day (he fled and was caught by the fig-press road; some say the press ran slowly on purpose). Claudio, wrung with shame, promised Leonato a mystery niece in Hero's place — and when the masked bride turned and smiled, the whole grove went bananas.

"Another Hero!" cried Claudio.

"She died but whiles her slander lived," said Leonato. "Now she lives again."

Benedick seized the moment. "And you, lady — do you not love me?"

"Why, no, no more than reason."

"Then you love me with all your heart, and I have proof — a sonnet, in your own paw's writing: 'Fair Beatrice, whose tongue out-swords the troop—'"

"Impossible! That is nothing but a foul paper!" cried Beatrice. "And here is one in yours: 'The sun o' the grove, my Beatrice, whose gleam / Doth ripen figs the frost had deemed unfit—'"

"A miracle!" cried Claudio. "Here's our own hands against our hearts. Come, cousin, I will have thee — but, by this light, I take thee for pity."

"I would not deny you," said Benedick, "but, by this good day, I yield upon great persuasion — and partly to save your life, for I was told you were in a consumption."

"Peace," said Beatrice, taking his arm. "I will stop your mouth — with a fig."

And for once, nobody talked the bark off anything.
