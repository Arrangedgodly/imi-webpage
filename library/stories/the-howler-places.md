# The Howler Places

*A parody of "The Hollow Places"*

When the Navy kicked me out for a bad knee — mine, not theirs — I came home to Gully Hollow and took a job at my uncle's Wonder Museum, housed in a hollowed-out baobab the size of a municipal ambition.

I am Kiki. I howl, professionally and otherwise. My uncle Earl is a spider monkey whose glasses are held on by belief, and his museum holds the great wonders of our county: a two-headed sloth (one head real), a rabbit with horns nailed on ("a jackalope, sourced responsibly"), the Fiji Monkey-Fish ("do not look at it before lunch"), the World's Largest Durian (locked in a case, for reasons), and the Mysterious Thing In A Jar, never identified because nobody wants to open the jar.

My best friend is Simon, a sifaka who lives on the museum roof and comes down only for emergencies, which he defines narrowly and I define as anything involving the word "jar."

It was a good, strange life. Then the hole appeared in the Fig.

The Great Fig behind the baobab was older than the county, and one Tuesday a hole appeared in its trunk, about the size of a monkey, its edges clean as if the wood had simply decided to stop. It had no bottom. I know because I shone a flashlight in, and the light went away and did not report back.

Earl scratched his chin and said, "Interesting fact: holes go both ways."

Earl loves Interesting Facts; he keeps a stand by the register.

That night, whistling started up from the direction of the Fig. It whistled like someone trying to whistle, and doing it almost, almost right.

In the morning there was a little pile of clean grey sand on the counter, and a pear.

"I did not order a pear," said Earl. "Kiki, did you order a pear?"

Nobody had ordered a pear. It sat there being a pear at us in a way pears do not usually manage. Simon put it in a jar and labeled it PARALLEL PEAR (DO NOT EAT). By morning it was gone, jar and all, and there was sand in its place.

We should have cemented the hole that day. Instead, being us, we went in.

Simon and I, one flashlight, one boat hook (Earl's), one rope tied to the baobab. Inside the tree it was not wood. It was a dim grey world of pale trunks like bones, under a sky no color a sky should be, with the sound of a sea somewhere below everything. The air tasted like the inside of an old jar. We walked — or the place walked us, it was never clear — into a grove of white trees, every one hollowed with a hole worn smooth, as if by generations of gentle going-through.

And inside the hollows, things. A rocking chair. A wasp nest, empty. A tricycle. A stuffed owl in a bonnet. One tree held nothing but a single shoe, arranged with obvious care, the way Earl arranges the jackalope.

"Kiki," said Simon, very quietly, "it's a museum."

I looked down the long grey gallery of white trees, at the smooth worn holes, at the little spotless exhibits, each one labeled by nothing and lit by nothing and dusted by something, and I understood what the sand was for. It was housekeeping. We were not in a forest. We were in somebody's Wonder Museum, and we had walked in through the donations slot.

The grey figures began to gather then — pale, knuckled, patient, standing among their exhibits the way Earl stands beside a tour group, whistling their almost-whistles. They did not attack. They didn't have to. Museums have hours, and we were not leaving before closing.

"Nobody panic," I whispered. "But panic immediately."

We went back through the hole at a dead sprint and sat in the museum kitchen drinking coffee until our hands worked again. Earl took the news with the interest of a monkey being handed an Interesting Fact. "A museum," he said, "needs new acquisitions."

That night, Gruntilda, the taxidermied boar's head, was found facing the wrong wall. The Mysterious Thing In A Jar rattled like something knocking politely from inside. And the whistling moved from the yard to the porch, and then — the last night — right up to the window, where Simon and I watched a long grey hand with too many knuckles rest on the sill like a caller taking a breath before knocking.

"Right," said Earl from the stairs, dressing-gowned, boat hook in hand. "Nobody opens the door."

Nobody opened the door. The problem was that Uncle Earl went out at dawn to check on the Fig — "just a look, I'll take the boat hook" — and did not come back.

There was sand leading to the hole in the Great Fig, and sand leading away, and no Uncle Earl.

So Simon and I did the stupidest, most obvious thing, twice. We went in after him.

The howler places — his voice, later, called them that, and it fit. They were hollow, and they howled, faintly, all the time. We went down the gallery of white trees at a run, past the rocking chair and the owl in her bonnet, shouting his name into a sky that folded the sound away. And in a clearing beyond the exhibits we found Earl standing with his glasses fogged, sketching a white tree with terrible interest, while the grey figures stood in a patient ring around him, whistling. They didn't attack. They just wanted him to stay — to be a wonder too, filed under MISCELLANEOUS.

"Uncle Earl," I said. "There's a museum in you and they want it."

"Do you see the branching, Kiki?" he said dreamily. "It's all galleries. It goes back for miles. There's a tree in here with a perfectly preserved —"

"RUN!" I howled — and howling is the one thing I do better than anyone — and the sound went through that grey world like a crack through an egg.

We ran. The hole home was a small dark moon in the pale wood. Simon danced. Earl stopped to grab a sapling "for the collection" — "UNCLE EARL" — and then we were through, into morning, into the museum, where we knocked over Gruntilda catching our breath.

We poured two hundred pounds of cement into the hole that afternoon, then parked Gruntilda in front of it, facing out. For a week afterward there was a little grey sand on the floor every morning, sweeping itself up, almost, but not quite. On the eighth morning there was a pear on the counter, riper than the first. Simon put it in a jar and labeled it APOLOGY (STILL DO NOT EAT). It has not gone anywhere. Neither have we.

The museum is open again. There's a new stand by the register:

*INTERESTING FACT: HOLES GO BOTH WAYS.*
*INTERESTING FACT: PEARS ARE SOMETIMES LIES.*
*INTERESTING FACT: THE WORLD IS HOLLOW IN PLACES, AND THE PLACES KNOW WHERE YOU LIVE.*

Earl's sapling took to the yard surprisingly well. It has smooth bark and white leaves, and it grows in spirals, and we do not ask it questions. Admission is two figs. Gruntilda guards the back.

And the durian is still locked up. The pear jar listens to it at night, and whatever they discuss, it is not our business.
