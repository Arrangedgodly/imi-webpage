# Cindergorilla

*A parody of "Cinderella"*

Once upon a time, in the green heart of the Fig Tree Kingdom, there lived a young gorilla named Ella — except that her fur was always full of cinders from sleeping by the troop's fire pit, so everyone called her Cindergorilla.

Her father had set off to trade for pepper and salt years ago and never came home, so Ella lived with her stepmother, a vain baboon of exacting standards, and her two stepsisters, Preena and Pluma, who groomed themselves six hours a day and had never once groomed anyone else.

"ELLA!" they would call across camp. "There's fur in my fur! Comb it out!"

"Ella! The fire wants tending!"

"Ella! I've run out of figs to snack on while I wait for my figs!"

Ella did all the work and received none of the grooming, and grooming, as every monkey knows, is love. She slept in the ashes and was kind anyway, which is the harder path and the better one.

One evening a parrot flew through camp with news: the Silverback Prince would hold the Golden Banana Ball, and every maiden in the kingdom was invited.

"Every maiden!" shrieked Preena.

"Invited!" shrieked Pluma.

"ELLA! Iron my leaf-gown!"

"Iron YOUR — I sleep in a FIRE PIT," said Ella, and the stepmother's glance could have curdled milk at forty paces.

When the night came, the stepsisters departed in a haze of perfume and primping, and the stepmother, pausing at the door, said the sentence she had been saving for years: "You may not go. Ashes are your ballroom, cinders are your confetti, and that is the whole of it."

Ella sat down in the cinders and, for exactly one moment, let her shoulders droop.

"That," said a warm voice above her, "will never do."

In the branches glowed a small, ancient bonobo, silver as moonlight, radiating calm.

"Who—"

"I'm your Fairy Bonobo Mother, and we have work to do. First: courage. Second: kindness. You have both. Third —" she cracked her knuckles — "the fun part."

And she sang, low and swinging:

"Ook-e-ook, ah-ah-dee-doo,
Salve your sorrows, bring to stew,
Ook-e-ook, ah-ah-dee-day,
Tonight you swing the night away!"

A giant marula fruit swelled, shuddered, and split into a coach lined with moss. Six marmosets in the branches stretched, yawned, and became six footmen in livery of woven gold. Ella's ashy rags whirled into a gown of silver mist — deep gray and soft as storm clouds, the exact color of a gorilla worth knowing — and on her finger appeared a small glass banana, cool and bright.

"The ring lasts till the last fig falls at midnight," said the Bonobo. "After that, marmosets. Mind the time."

At the ball, the Prince — a young silverback with a serious chest and kind eyes — had been bored for hours by baboons discussing their own hair. One had explained her conditioning routine in such detail that he began to envy the guard peacocks, who were at least allowed to sleep standing up. Then the doors opened, a gorilla in storm-gray came in, and the whole hall went quiet, including the fruit bats.

"Would you dance?" he asked.

"Gorillas don't dance," said Ella. "We display."

"Then display with me," said the Prince, and beat his chest once, softly, which among silverbacks is practically a sonnet.

They danced. They shared a banana split, two spoons, no commentary. He told her the palace figs tasted like dust; she told him a fire kept you warm if you banked it right, and that nobody in her troop had ever banked anything for her. He laughed so hard the guard peacocks fell off their perch — and then, far off, the first fig fell.

"Midnight!" Ella gasped. "I have to go!"

"But—"

She was already off through the hall, down the steps, into the dark — and the glass banana slipped from her finger and bounced, once, twice, onto the palace stair, where the Prince found it, cold and bright, and closed his great hand around it.

That night the stepsisters came home in a condition beyond speech, which for them was a medical event.

"There was a gorilla," whispered Preena, "in storm-gray, like weather with manners."

"She split a banana with him," said Pluma. "Two spoons. TWO SPOONS, MOTHER."

"And then she ran off into the dark like the end of a song," sighed Preena, "and nobody knows her name."

Ella, smoothing the cinders out of their shawls, said nothing. She was holding the lantern, and the lantern-light fell on all of them, and nobody once looked at her. Nobody, that is, except the stepmother, whose small cold eyes went from the mystery gorilla the whole court was describing to the ash-gray girl by the fire, and back, and narrowed. A suspicion took up residence behind her eyes and began, quietly, to pay rent.

The Prince announced next morning that he would marry the maiden whose finger fit the glass banana or nobody's, and sent his heralds up and down the kingdom, fig grove to mangrove, with the ring on a velvet cushion — Grand Duke Fig in charge of the cushion, a serious old mandrill who believed in the mission more than he believed in lunch.

The search took a fortnight. Baboons jammed their fingers and howled. A mandrill duchess attempted the ring on her tail, for luck and for volume, and had to be greased free. One proboscis monkey got it wedged on his nose and was followed from door to door by a small crowd humming wedding marches. Preena greased her knuckle with tree-butter and got stuck to the cushion. Pluma attempted two fingers at once, which is against the law in several kingdoms.

And when the heralds came to the troop by the fire pit, the stepmother took one look at the velvet cushion, did the arithmetic, and locked Ella in the storage hollow with the winter yams.

"You weren't even AT the ball," the stepmother called through the door, to nobody, rehearsing.

But six marmosets in gold livery had been following the cushion all week — for a job is a job, however it ends — and while the household shrieked and greased and jammed, one marmoset picked the lock with a hairpin, and five others lifted Ella's watering pails out of her hands and set them down, respectfully, forever.

Then a small gorilla with soot still on her wrist came forward into the firelight.

"You," sniffed the stepmother. "You weren't even AT the ball."

The ring slid on as if it had been grown there.

"It fits," announced Grand Duke Fig, wet-eyed, to the entire troop, the forest, and a lizard. "The cushion is hereby retired. The cushion has done its work."

The Prince beat his chest so thunderously that three parrots fainted. "Will you stay?" he asked. "Not in the cinders. Here."

"Only if my family may visit," said Ella, who was kind, which is the harder path and the better one.

The stepmother and the sisters came to the palace in their best fur, prepared to grovel. Instead Ella sat them down and groomed them herself, behind the ears, one at a time, which nobody had ever once done for them, and they wept into their lapkins, and were better monkeys for it. Preena married a perfume merchant and Pluma took up chest-beating for sport, and both sent figs at the harvest, every year, without fail.

And so Cindergorilla married her prime mate, and the kingdom swung happily ever after — and if the fire pit went cold that winter for lack of anyone sleeping in it, nobody missed it but the marmosets, who had, after all, known her first.
