# Madame Bonobary

*A parody of "Madame Bovary"*

We were at monkey-school when the headmaster came in, leading a new boy by the hand.

"A new pupil. Charles Bovary, from a middling troop in the provinces. Say your name!"

The new boy, a plodding, amiable young bonobo, opened his mouth to say his name and instead knocked his desk over with his foot. "Charbovary!" he stammered. The class exploded — "Charbovary! Charbovary!" — and poor Charles wished the branch would open and swallow him.

Charles grew up to be a mediocre country health-groom of the Yonville Troop, dispensing bark poultices and banana tonics. A first marriage for plantains ended in widowhood; then, on a house call to a broken arm, he met Rouault's daughter.

Emma. A fine-boned bonobo with large dark eyes, raised on romance novels in a convent school, who had spent her girlhood dreaming of midnight terraces, whiskered heroes, and glittering high-canopy balls. Reality, in the shape of Charles, arrived wearing a stained waistcoat.

He thought she was perfect. She thought, on the wedding night, that this was not what the books had promised. Not remotely.

Before marriage Emma believed herself in love; but since the promised happiness was nowhere to be found, she felt she must have been mistaken. And she tried to discover what one meant in life by the words *bliss, passion, ecstasy* — words so beautiful in books.

Charles ate too slowly. Charles told the same three stories. Charles loved her with a fat, placid, blameless devotion she found, within a year, unbearable.

"Say something, Charles! Tell me about the world — about balls, heroes, passion!"

"I once treated a gorilla with a sprained wrist," said Charles, proud of this.

Emma nearly went bananas.

Charles, trying to please, took her to a ball at the great estate of a marquis-ape. One night of chandeliers and silver fruit-bowls and a viscount who danced with her twice. It ruined her. Having tasted one golden banana, she could never again eat the green ones.

Back in Yonville, Emma had a daughter (she had wanted a son; she fainted), took up charity-knitting, dropped it, sketched, dropped it, sighed at windows — and once, at the window, little Berthe tugged her sleeve, and Emma pushed her gently away, and the child fell against a bracket and cut her cheek. "You'll hurt yourself, silly thing," said Emma, and was annoyed with the furniture all evening — and never once, not for a banana, with herself.

And then the strangers began to arrive.

First Rodolphe Boulanger, a rich, moustached macaque with a château full of mistresses' letters and a heart like a dried plantain skin.

"She is quite pretty," he mused, watching her at the window. "And bored. Bored females are my speciality."

He chose his battlefield with care: the Yonville Agricultural Show, where the town's prizes were being announced from a platform in the square — Rodolphe having privately rented the room above it for the seduction. What followed was conducted in counterpoint, his murmured endearments interleaved with the official proclamations drifting up through the floorboards:

"I stayed away because your image pursued me—"

"—Manure! First prize in fertilizers, seventy francs, to Monsieur Bizet of Quincampoix—"

"—You came into my life like a river in flood—"

"—For the best neapolitan steer, a gold medal, to Monsieur Leherisson of Saint-Dionysy—"

"—Tell me, one word, only one word—"

"—Pigs! Two equal first prizes, to Monsieur Lehérissé and Monsieur Canuvert!"

"I am yours," said Emma, entirely, in front of the entire agricultural output of Normandy.

"Run away with me!" she begged, afterward. "To the great arboreal cities!"

"Certainly, darling. In September."

And then, being Rodolphe, he wrote one smooth, cowardly, beautifully composed farewell letter, sent with a basket of apricots to soften the blow, and vanished. Emma went half-mad with grief; Charles nursed her tenderly and suspected nothing.

Desperate to raise his wife from despair, Charles did the worst thing a mediocre monkey can do: he reached for ambition. Persuaded by Homais, the town's chatty apothecary-lemur, he attempted a heroic operation — to straighten the clubfoot of Hippolyte, the stable-lad, with a clever new contraption. The town applauded the genius of its health-groom. Then the leg went black. Gangrene crept up the limb like a publisher's advance, and Hippolyte lost the leg entirely, and Charles's reputation with it. Emma, watching her husband fail publicly at the one grand thing he ever tried, felt the last floorboard of her respect give way.

Then Leon returned — the young clerk who had adored her shyly — now polished and Parisian, met by appointment at the Rouen cathedral, where a beadle insisted on conducting Emma through the complete architectural tour before Léon could get her to the hired carriage; even her scandals acquired bureaucracy. Installed in hotel rooms by the hour, the second affair began: hotels, gifts, letters. And always, underneath, the gnawing *more*. Emma wanted higher, faster, brighter. She was a monkey climbing a smooth trunk: arms full of bananas, no branches left to grab.

She was also a shopper. Monsieur Lheureux, the draper-lemur, had been circling for years, offering silks and fans and "just a small promissory note, madame, between friends." Emma signed everything, and signed again, and even forced Charles to sign a power of attorney with his eyes shut. By the third year she owed a fortune in plantain-equivalent, and Lheureux, smelling blood, called in the debts. Everything was to be seized — publicly, with the whole Yonville troop invited to gawk — by tomorrow.

Emma ran from male to male that last day. To Leon (he hid behind a rehearsal). To notary Guillaumin (his price was not monetary, and she fled his office with her fur bristling). To Rodolphe, in his beautiful château — and for one mad moment they nearly fell into each other's arms again, until he said, mildly, that he was dreadfully short of cash, and Emma laughed the terrible laugh of a monkey hearing a romance-novel line read back to her by an accountant.

Going home, she slipped past the apothecary's window, took the little pot of arsenic paste — the white powder kept for killing jungle-rats — and ate it.

It was bitter — so bitter — cherry-bitter, monkey-liver bitter.

She died at length, in her nest, Charles weeping over her — and at the very end, as her voice failed, an old blind beggar was passing under the window, singing a lewd plantation song for coppers, and Emma raised herself, laughed the ghastliest laugh of her life at him, and fell back crying out the last words of the romantic dream: "It doesn't matter — nothing matters — the blind one — the BLIND one!"

Charles, gray-faced, met the blind beggar afterward on the road and tried to thrash him, which is the most romantic thing Charles ever did, and it was too late.

And after she was buried under the flat stone in the Yonville graveyard, sweet, dull Charles found her letters — Rodolphe's, Leon's — read every one, and forgave her entirely, and died soon after of a broken heart, holding a lock of her fur and wearing, to the end, the spats she had once told him were distinguished. Their daughter Berthe was sent to work in the flax-grove.

The apothecary Homais, who had pushed the clubfoot operation, supplied the arsenic, and calumniated the rival doctor in six counties, received at last the Cross of the Legion of Honor.

And Yonville gossiped about it all for perhaps three weeks, which is quite long for monkeys.
