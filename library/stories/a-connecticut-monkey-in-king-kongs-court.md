# A Connecticut Monkey in King Kong's Court

*A parody of "A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court"*

You may call me Chimp Hank, of the Connecticut Zoological Society, and I will make you swear on a stalk of bananas that every word of this is true.

I was bonked on the head by a keeper's shovel — an accident involving a runaway wheelbarrow and what I maintain to this day was a mistaken belief that I had thrown the first coconut. When I came to, the bars of my enclosure were gone. In their place stood a forest of towering fig trees, and upon the tallest throne of woven vines sat the largest ape I had ever seen: King Kong, sovereign of all he surveyed, and he surveyed rather a lot, being forty feet tall.

"Bowler," said he. "Bow before your king."

"I think you mean 'bow,'" I said, and that earned me a ticket to the stake. Bananalot — for that was the court's name — ran on pure superstition. The court wizard, an ancient orangutan called Merlin, claimed to have prophesied my arrival, though his prophecies were about as reliable as a ripe banana in a back pocket.

They chained me to a stump and piled the kindling. It was then I remembered my astronomical almanac and — by a stroke of luck the size of Kong's knuckle — a total eclipse of the sun was due that very afternoon.

"O king!" I bellowed. "Unchain me, or I shall swallow the sun and leave your kingdom in the dark!"

Merlin scoffed. The court snickered. Then the sky began to darken, the birds went silent, and nine hundred apes simultaneously discovered religion.

"By the Great Groomer!" shrieked the king. "Loose him! Feed him! Bring bananas!"

Being a progressive monkey, I made the darkness go away — for a price. I was dubbed "The Boss," second only to the king, and Merlin's stock dropped faster than you can say "sour grapes," though he certainly tried to sour mine.

I took a clerk, a bright little chimp named Clarence, who believed in me the way small chimps believe in weather, and together we got to work. In six years I worked wonders. I strung telegraph vines between the trees. I established grooming schools where knights learned hygiene instead of heraldry. I founded patent offices, newspapers (The Bananalot Trumpet, carved on bark), a telephone system whose greeting I taught the switchboard girls myself — "Hello, Central!" — and a secret training factory where bright young chimps learned the three R's: Reading, 'Riting, and Ripping bananas open from the blossom end.

"There she grows!" Clarence would cry each morning as the new crop came in, and I would nod like a general reviewing troops.

I even taught the knights baseball, armor and all. It was a beautiful thing to see the flower of chivalry standing in the fields calling each other "batter" instead of "thou ribald," and a great improvement on tournaments, though the batting orders had to be issued by herald, and the sliding was abandoned for reasons of iron trousers.

Adventure, of a sort, kept finding me. They sent me to rescue a princess from an ogre's castle, with a young damsel named Sandy along to narrate — and she narrated, hourly, for three days. When we dug the three sobbing captives out of the enchanted tower, they turned out to be three ordinary pigs, which Sandy received as a victory of the highest chivalry, and I received as a parable of the whole sixth century. I married Sandy, in time. She talked, and I had been listening so long it would have been strange to stop. Our daughter we named Hello-Central, after the greeting — so that at least one monkey in the sixth century would answer a telephone correctly.

Later I did a genuine miracle on the stock of Merlin's church: the sacred spring of the Valley of Holiness had gone dry, and the monks were ruined. I examined the works, found a stone jammed in the channel where a rival abbot had tucked it, fixed my pipe in secret, and then let them catch me praying over it in procession. When the water shot forty feet, they canonized me on the spot. Merlin announced that he had done it himself, telepathically, which was brave of him, as he was standing downwind of the spray.

But the sixth century needed seeing, not just fixing, so I talked the king into walking his kingdom incognito, as common monkeys. We found what common monkeys found: a jungle where the laws took from the poor and called it order. We got arrested as vagrants in one town, chained to a slave caravan in another, and Kong himself — forty feet of sovereign — was nearly sold at auction for two hundred bananas before I got us clear by way of a lightning rod and a lecture. "These are my laws," he kept saying, all the way home, in the voice of a king who had finally read his own mail.

The knights resented me all the same, especially Sir Sagramore le Fierce, who challenged me to a duel. In the old way he'd have chopped me into chimp pieces, but I had a secret weapon: the lasso — or as we said in Bananalot, a lariat of liana. When the tournament came, I stood calm as you please, twirled my vine, and roped him, his lance, his shield, and — in one especially satisfying throw — his dignity.

"That," I told the crowd, "is monkey business of the modern sort."

But power makes enemies, and Merlin got his revenge at last: the Church of the Great Ape laid its interdict upon the whole kingdom, and my schools stood empty overnight. When the realm rose against me, I retreated with fifty loyal young chimps — Clarence drilling them to the last — into a cave rigged with electrified fencing: copper and lemon-juice batteries, a miracle of the modern jungle.

"Knock 'em for a loop!" I cried, and we threw the switch. Ten thousand charging knights went over like a barrel of monkeys rolling downhill. Merlin, that very night, solemnly announced he would put a stop to the whole business, crept in past the warning signs, seized the terminals with both paws — and the modern jungle did what the modern jungle does. He went out, they tell me, glowing faintly, with his fur standing on end in an attitude of permanent surprise. It was the only prophecy of his that ever came true, and he wasn't there to enjoy it.

I'd have been king of the recovery too, but one sore loser with a spear got lucky in the smoke, and I took a wound in the throat. My clever boys sealed me in a glass case (another invention) to sleep off the injury, and there I stayed, snoring gently, for thirteen centuries — while above me, said Clarence's last letter, the interdict lifted, the schools quietly filled again, and Bananalot got on with learning to read, which no darkness has ever survived.

It was the keeper's shovel that woke me again, back in the Connecticut Zoo, right where I started. The veterinarian says I dreamed the whole thing. But I noticed something as I climbed into my tree that evening: my palm still remembers how to tie a lasso, and when the moon passes over the sun, I always know the exact minute it will end.

The Boss is gone; long live the Boss. Now, if you'll excuse me, I have a banana ripening by the clock.
