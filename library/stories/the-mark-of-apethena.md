# The Mark of Apethena

*A parody of "The Mark of Athena"*

The Argorilla II — a flying warship held together by Leo Valdez's questionable wiring — circled New Rome under a flag of truce, and everyone aboard had exactly the same thought: *Please let nobody do anything that starts a war between the two biggest primate camps in existence.*

Terminus, the Roman god of boundaries, inspected every inch of the hull first, on principle, with a clipboard and no legs. "No weapons inside the pomerium," he announced, to a ship that was essentially one large weapon with guest quarters.

Then Leo's hands began moving on their own, and the ship's ballistae opened fire on the Roman forum.

"I'm not doing it!" Leo shrieked, wrestling with his own paws like a monkey playing keep-away with himself. "Somebody's driving the Valdez!"

By the time the seven demigods crash-landed in the Tiber to escape the angry legions of Camp Jupiter, the truce was a banana peel on the pavement. Romans and Greeks — two troops who had spent two millennia refusing to admit they were related — were officially at war, and the seven had roughly six days to stop it.

They had one lead. A coin, given to Annabough by her mother, that showed an owl — and beneath the owl, a sigil that had haunted Apethena's children for millennia. Somewhere in the old lands, the statue of Apethena Parthenos had been stolen by the Romans. The goddess of wisdom wanted it back. Her mark appeared in fire wherever Annabough looked: on maps, on pizza boxes, on the insides of her eyelids.

"Follow the mark, reclaim the statue," Annabough said grimly, "and maybe the two camps stop trying to groom each other's faces off."

Percy — fresh from the Roman months that had scrubbed his memory — held her hand like he was afraid the sea might take her too. He had come back from the dead-ish and intended to keep standing next to her.

The Argorilla sailed east, collecting catastrophes like a troop collects ticks. Over Kansas, the eidolons — possessive little spirits that Gaea kept like stray cats — tried again: Percy and Jason woke up midair trying to kill each other while Leo screamed over the intercom. Piper's charmspeak finally dragged the truth out of the ghosts, and Leo, feeling clever for once, broadcast the order to leave in bad Latin through every speaker on the ship at maximum volume: "OUT. BAD GHOSTS. OUT."

The eidolons left. Mostly out of embarrassment.

Bacchus, god of wine, met them in a Kansas park, refused to help, demanded a diet soda, delivered one cryptic sentence about the twins in Rome, and vanished in a shower of grapes, which is the godly way of shrugging.

In Charleston, Aphrodite — looking like a different beautiful somebody to each of them, which was confusing for the navigators — told Annabough what the mark meant and where the statue waited. Annabough didn't want to hear it. Wisdom's children had been falling into that spider's hole for two thousand years, and the goddess of love just smiled: "Following your mother has never once been the safe option, darling. That's rather the point of mothers."

And at Fort Sumter, the Romans caught up — Octavian with his stuffed-animal auguries and his legion eagles, stuffed once more into an incident nobody would forgive. The seven left under fire, with the Parthenos's trail pointing east across the Atlantic.

And Annabough had her own private quest within the quest — for the mark led to the one being in creation who had ever out-woven Apethena. Arachne. The first spider. The weaver with a grudge two thousand years old, who'd been turned into a spider and had spun in solitude ever since, nurturing her fury like a monkey with a single banana it refuses to share.

In Rome — the real one, old and golden and full of things that wanted to eat demigods — the seven split up. Percy and Jason broke into the giants' lair beneath the Colosseum and found Nico di Angelo sealed in a bronze jar like the world's saddest takeaway, surviving on pomegranate seeds and spite. Then the twin giants, Otis and Ephialtes, staged their deathmatch spectacular — chained mortals, spotlights, matching spears, and matching egos — and got far more show than they'd planned for: Jason summoned the spirits of Rome's lost legion with a scepter, Percy brought the local plumbing into it, and the twins fell for the oldest trick in the arena, which is fighting someone your own size. Frank demonstrated that a son of Mars who can turn into any animal is a whole zoo with anxiety. Piper charmed a god with the promise of "a better story."

In Naples, while the others checked charts, Leo fell through a hidden hatch into the workshop of Archimedes himself — bronze spheres, ancient gears, and death rays in beta — and came up holding a designer's whole lost legacy. The Argorilla left Italy twice as armed and three times as smug. "Behold," said Leo, "the Valdez special."

And Annabough went down. Alone. Through a tunnel of web, into a cave where an ancient spider tended an ancient loom, surrounded by her descendants: silk boxes, each one the size of a monkey, each one soft with what was inside.

"You've come about the statue," said Arachne. Her voice sounded like a loom being strung. "Apethena's little monkeys always come about the statue."

Annabough was broken — cracked ribs, sprained ankle, a dislocated shoulder she'd popped back in herself — but she had her mother's actual brain, and Arachne had spent two millennia talking only to herself. So Annabough flattered. She admired the weave. She mentioned — ever so casually — that if Arachne wanted the whole world to finally see Apethena humiliated, she should display the stolen Parthenos in her web. She even suggested a sizing plan for the statue's pedestal.

Arachne, who was vain the way spiders are venomous (i.e., structurally), enlarged her weaving floor exactly as Annabough had calculated: straight over the trapdoor to the Underworld. The floor gave. The spider fell — but as she dropped, she fired her silk, and it caught Annabough's ankle, and the web held them both, and the silk boxes around them split open, and the floor of the cave was suddenly a sea of spiders, all flowing toward her.

"PERCY!" she screamed, and he came. Of course he came. He cut her free, and they ran, and behind them the statue teetered on the broken floor, and Arachne's silk pulled tight around Annabough's leg one last time, and the ledge cracked.

Together they fell — into the pit, into the dark, toward Tartarus — holding hands, which was the only part of the plan that worked.

"Percy," she said, as the darkness rose.

"I know," he said, and squeezed her hand. "Tartarus. Doors of Death. We'll find them together."

Above, five demigods took the statue, saved the ship, and set a course to meet the armies. The mark of Apethena had led where wisdom always leads — straight down, into the worst place in the world, with the best company possible.
