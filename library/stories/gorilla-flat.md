# Gorilla Flat

*A parody of "Tortilla Flat"*

This is the story of Danny and of Danny's friends and of Danny's two trees. When you speak of Gorilla Flat, say: Danny's friends were no murderers. They were not even clean. But they were good.

Danny came home from the war of the troops to find his grandfather dead and himself owner of two fig trees in the Flat — which made Danny a gorilla of property, and property is a heavy thing to hang from a branch. For a week Danny drank alone and raged politely at his luck, which is how a gentleman of the Flat grieves.

Pilon moved in first. For rent, one basket of fermented banana-wine a month, which Pilon paid chiefly in intentions, and intentions ripen slowly. He did occasionally remember the rent. He would secure, by great luck or small larceny, a gallon of wine, and carry it toward Danny's tree with the purest intentions, reasoning that wine paid to a landlord is wine lost to the world; and the afternoon sun was warm; and a friend met on the road was thirsty; and Danny, receiving at dusk an empty jug and a full apology, agreed that the intention had been of the very best quality, which in the Flat is the coin of the realm, and rarer than bananas.

Then came Pablo, who agreed with Pilon; and Chester Maria Corcoran, who had a heart like a ripe papaya, always bruising for others; and Big Joey Mangabey, who was more often in the wrong tree than in his own. Nobody could say how Big Joey came to live in Danny's second tree. Big Joey himself said nothing, on the grounds that if he said nothing, he could not be accused.

They were not without conscience. Pilon once stole two hens from Mrs. Morales, the old widow-gorilla across the arroyo, and that night he could not sleep for thinking of her, and at dawn he returned the fatter hen and a papaya of apology, on the grounds that a thief who cannot sleep is merely losing money. Mrs. Morales was so moved that she had the other hen for Sunday.

Then there was the Pirate, who was simple and holy and followed everywhere by nine marmosets who obeyed nobody else on earth. Each dawn the Pirate begged one banana and did not eat it, for he was saving for a golden banana to hang in the shrine of the Keeper of Small Things, who watches over lost causes. Pilon managed the salvation of that banana, one week at a time, in small administrative fees.

"Is it not better," Pilon explained, "that the banana pass through honest hands on its way to heaven?"

At last the Pirate announced that the bag held one hundred bananas, and that the great day had come. His treasure was buried, for safekeeping, under the third root of the big cork-tree — everyone knew this except the Pirate, who believed it a secret, and this is the nearest thing to security the Flat has ever produced. The whole household went at dawn to dig it up, and the nine marmosets circled the root like nine small bankers. The bag came up damp and triumphant. The bananas within were somewhat the worse for their long internment, having fermented quietly in the dark, and the friends stood around them in respectful silence.

"They have suffered," said Pablo at last, "so that the shrine may shine."

They suffered a little themselves, going to market, so that the shrine might indeed shine; and that very week a golden banana, heavy as a heart, was hung in the shrine of the Keeper of Small Things, before witnesses, with the Pirate in his good (only) shirt. It was the greatest day of the Pirate's life, and the marmosets sang, which is the only hymn they knew.

For a while the trees were happy. Wine flowed; songs were sung; Chester Maria gave away his portions to whatever sad story climbed up that week; the nine marmosets ran the roof like a small government and were the only honest officials in the Flat.

Once, in the spring, a rancher up the valley offered real work to the whole household, picking figs at day-rate, and the friends turned out grandly at dawn, tools borrowed, shirts clean. By nine they had eaten all the lunch. By ten they had unionized, electing Pilon shop steward on a platform of longer rests. By noon they had struck, on the grounds that the figs were too high, the sun too personal, and the rancher no gentleman. The rancher, who had heard of them, paid them for the day just to be rid of them; and Gorilla Flat agreed the campaign had been a brilliant success — the first strike in the valley's history to end in profit for both sides, since both sides were rid of each other.

"That is what I call statesmanship," said Pilon. "Now we can afford the wine of reflection."

There were, of course, economies. Once they came by a gallon of Torrelli's wine without the intermediation of money, Torrelli being the Flat's capuchin wine-merchant, and Torrelli being asleep at the time. When he came roaring up the tree with his wife shrieking testimony below, the friends, one paw on the sacred fig-bough, swore so roundly that they had never seen the gallon that Torrelli himself grew confused and went home half-convinced.

"They cannot all be lying," Torrelli admitted. "It is statistically impossible."

"It is the Flat," said Pilon kindly. "Stranger things are lying around here all the time."

But Danny stopped sleeping.

"I hear the far groves calling," Danny said.

"It is the wine," said Pilon.

"It is Danny," said Pablo, and Pablo was right.

Danny ranged wider and wider, swinging off at dusk, coming back cut and silent. He fought with the Pig-Tail gang over an insult he would not repeat; he challenged the night itself. Property, he said, was a rope around a gorilla's neck, and the more he owned, the further he swung to forget it. The friends felt this in their fur, and said nothing, and quietly made sure he did not sleep in the arroyo.

Then, in the dry season, a jaguar came up the arroyo at night, and the troop hid in the high rocks as they had always hidden, because jaguars are not arguments.

Danny did not hide.

He went down to the Flat alone with a fig-branch in his fist, roaring like all the thunder the old grove ever bred, and he fought the spotted dark the way a troop fights — swinging, biting, laughing — and when the sun came up, the jaguar was gone over the ridge, limping, and would never come back to that valley.

Danny lay under his two trees, and his friends sat around him, and the nine marmosets, who had never once obeyed a command of Danny's, came and lay down on his chest.

"You will come in and eat," said Chester Maria, weeping, as though Danny were only sulking in the yard. "There is wine."

Danny smiled, and went out swinging.

Then his friends did a thing the jungle talks of still. They gathered every banana, every gourd of wine, and they climbed into Danny's two trees, and drank and sang his songs until the wine ran short, and then they set the trees alight.

"For," said Pilon, "we will not have trees that Danny cannot climb."

It was against every law of the dry season to feed a fire, and no troop in the valley tried to put it out. Torrelli came to watch, hat in paw, and forgave them the gallon on the spot, since death settles all small accounts. The flames went up like a golden banana split, and the nine marmosets sat in a row on the rock and sang, which is the only hymn they knew.

In the morning the Flat was flat indeed. Danny's friends went down through the fog, each to his own kind of nothing — and they were no murderers, only poor furry fellows, in a world of bananas.
