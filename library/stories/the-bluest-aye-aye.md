# The Bluest Aye-Aye

*A parody of "The Bluest Eye"*

Here is the nest. It is green and white. It has a red door. It is very pretty. Here is the family. Mother, Father, Dick, and Jane. See the banana. See the banana, Jane. Take the banana, Jane. Take it, Jane. TakeitJane takeitjane...

It was the same that Christmas, quiet as it's kept, when the grown monkeys gave Frieda and me each a golden-furred baby doll that said "banana" when you tipped it, and everybody watched to see us cherish them. We had dismembered both dolls by New Year's — not out of wickedness, out of inquiry; we wanted to see what made the banana come, and whether, once found, it could be made to say anything else. It could not. There is a kind of child who takes the world apart looking for a different voice inside it, and the world seldom forgives the search.

Quiet as it's kept, there were no ripe bananas in the fall of 1941. We thought, at the time, that it was because Pecola Breedlove had wished for something she should never have had to wish for. We were wrong. The troop was wrong. The ground itself was wrong, and nothing we planted came up.

This is what we knew then: the Breedloves lived in an abandoned storefront nest at the edge of our canopy, a family so poor and so battered that even the moths pitied them. And Pecola — Pecola was an aye-aye.

If you have never seen an aye-aye, understand: she had the biggest eyes of any child in our jungle, great pools of amber with a lamp behind them, a tail like a silver question mark, and one impossibly long, elegant middle finger for digging grubs out of bark. She was, in the way of her whole ancient family, extraordinary.

Which is to say, the other children could not leave her alone.

"Night-eyes! Bug-digger!" the boys chanted from the branches of the school fig, circling her the way misery loves company, until she dropped her books and pressed her hands over her beautiful eyes — until my sister Frieda and I could not stand it and went for those boys scratching and biting, a couple of wild little macaques defending a girl we barely knew. Afterward Pecola sat with us, stunned by the arithmetic of it: somebody had fought *for* her. She sipped a little sweet sap from a jar and her whole thin body relaxed, and I understood, even then, that she was not used to being near anyone who was not taking something.

It was the same everywhere. Maureen Peel, a golden lion tamarin with fur like poured honey, could walk the length of that jungle without ever once being called a name. Teachers smiled at Maureen Peel. Grown monkeys slowed down to look at her and say *awwwww*. Grown monkeys looked at Pecola and looked away. And when Maureen, who was rich that week and kind by daylight, walked Pecola home and bought her a fig ice — even that turned. Somebody mentioned the Breedloves' storefront, and Maureen tossed her pretty tail and said, "I am cute! And you are ugly! Both of you!" and swung off, and Pecola said nothing at all, and I hated Maureen Peel for a whole winter, and Frieda threw a rock at her, and missed.

Every night, without fail, Pecola knelt at her window and prayed. Not for a bigger fig. Not for a kinder troop. She prayed for blue eyes.

Think of it. Of all the wishes in the world, she chose that one — to swap her two amber lanterns for two little blue seeds, so that when people looked at her they would say *what a pretty little aye-aye*, and pretty would mean loved, and loved would mean safe. She prayed a whole year.

To understand why, you would have to know her house, and her house was its own weather. Mr. Breedlove — Cholly — a big handsome macaque whom nobody had ever raised, left as an infant in a fig tree for the wind to claim, claimed instead by a great-aunt and by a rage he could not fold up small. Mrs. Breedlove — Pauline — with her crooked foot and her dream of the movies, keeping house all day for a glossy little family across town whose baby she loved more cleanly than her own. When they fought, the storefront shook; when the fighting was over, somebody had always been put outdoors. There is a difference between being put out and being put outdoors, in our jungle, and it is the difference between a door closing and a door locking. Once, after the worst of those nights, Pecola stayed with us a few days, and at supper she asked, in a small voice, out of nowhere: "How do you get somebody to love you?"

We did not know. We were nine.

A little after that, quiet as it's kept, the trouble at her house came, and Pecola went away to live with a far troop, and nothing about her was ever simple again. The grown monkeys talked about it the way they talked about weather that had broken somebody else's roof: it was terrible, it was the family's own fault, it was a shame, and had you heard the part about — and the troop decided, without meeting, that whatever the trouble had left behind, it did not deserve to live, and said so at the market, and Pecola was somewhere hearing every word of it, because everyone always is.

Before she went, she visited Soaphead Chimp, a silky old chimpanzee who read wishes like tea leaves and granted them like a con artist. He was, he informed her gravely, in the wish business. He was also in the middle of writing the longest letter of his life, a letter to God, which began: *I have caused a miracle... I gave her the blue eyes she asked for... you gave me the berries, I gave her the eyes, and no one can take them away.* Great apes had failed him, that was the heart of the letter, and the small failures of God he had decided, at last, to correct personally.

"I can help you," he told Pecola, "if you do exactly as I say. Give this berry to the old hound by the gate. If he behaves strangely, your wish is granted."

The old hound ate the overripe berry, staggered, sat down with tremendous dignity, and howled at nothing, being old and full of bad fruit.

"It is done," Pecola breathed. "They're blue. Aren't they? Tell me they're the bluest in the whole jungle."

Frieda and I planted seeds that fall, when it was clear Pecola was not coming back and the talking had started — seeds from the little wild bananas by the fence, planted in our own plot of black dirt, and we sang over them, charms and chants and promises, saying: if they come up, everything will be all right. We said it louder the second week, and the third, because nothing was coming up, and because somewhere we had heard that the trouble had left something behind, something small that the whole troop was wishing dead, and we could not stop it, and the seeds were the only votes we had.

They did not come up.

I am convinced the fault lay not in Pecola, and not in the seeds, but in the ground — in the whole jungle floor, sick to its bedrock with looking away. For years I believed that if our bananas had sprouted, Pecola would have known love, and stayed, and been whole.

It is too late for that kind of magic now. But this much I know: she walked the far canopy afterward, talking to a friend nobody else could see, a friend with blue eyes, and she was happy in the way of the terribly hurt. And I, who spent my own girlhood wishing to be a whole troop's idea of pretty, never once doubted that of all the eyes in that jungle hers — enormous, amber, steady, and unloved — were the most beautiful. And the bluest, for the seeing.
