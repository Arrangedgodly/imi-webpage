# Snow Scratch

*A parody of "Snow Crash"*

The Deliveratan belonged to an elite order, a hush-hush subsection of the CosaNana clan. He was a pretender to the throne of Fastest Pizza in the Canopy. His vehicle was a twelve-ton war-buggy painted to look like a lemon, and strapped to its back was a thermal box containing one pizza, thirty minutes or it's free — a deadline enforced, these days, by the Mafia, which had branched out from rackets into catering and found catering much the same.

Hiro Protagorang drove it. In the Metavine — the vine-woven virtual jungle where a hundred million monkeys spent their evenings — Hiro was the greatest swordsman of his generation. He was also a major landowner: he and Da5id had written the place, back when, and been paid in real estate. His house sat on a prime branch of the Street, the hundred-million-monkey boulevard that circles the virtual world like a crown, where the best address in the known universe is separated from the worst by nothing but light. In the world of meat and fruit, he was a freelance hacker with a hollow-log apartment, a pizza cap, and a tab at Da5id's club.

Until a monkey is ten years old, he still thinks, every so often, that under the right circumstances he could be the baddest chimp in the jungle. Hiro was one of those monkeys. Now he was ten times ten, and he hadn't changed his mind.

The trouble started at the Black Sun, the classiest club in the Metavine, where Hiro's partner Da5id — prince of hackers — was handed a bitmap by a pretty stranger with no face. It was called SNOW SCRATCH: white scratches on black, moving. Da5id, who could read machine code the way other monkeys read ripeness, glanced at it and laughed.

They found him an hour later, catatonic in his own sim, drooling into his chest fur. His brain had crashed. Not the Metavine's servers — his *brain*. Because Snow Scratch wasn't a picture. It was written in the deep machine language of the primate cortex, and to a monkey who knew code, looking was compiling, and compiling was the end of you.

"You know the worst part?" Hiro said, sitting with his old friend Juanita at the funeral banquet. She had once been his girlfriend, and was now a nun of the Anglican branch, which in the Canopy counted as a promotion. "There's a powder version too. Little bags of white flakes. The norms take it as a drug. The hackers catch it as a disease."

"Both of them are the same thing," Juanita said. "A language. It goes in through the eyes for hackers, and in through the nose for everybody else, and it ends up in the same place — below thinking. Hire yourself to the Librarian, learn the history, and bill me. If Da5id can be crashed, so can every monkey you love. Including you."

"Why fund me? You hate the Metavine. You told me faces talk and avatars don't."

"Faces talk," Juanita agreed. "Every monkey with a working face is running the oldest software there is, and it never crashes — which is exactly why somebody finally wrote a virus for it. I understood the Metavine the day I quit it: a hundred million monkeys paying to hide their faces. That's not a world, Hiro. That's a target market."

"Then let us discuss," said the Librarian — a baboon-shaped program with a cardigan and infinite patience, housed in a database left behind by a dead researcher named Lagos — "the First Troop."

Once, all monkeys had shared one tongue — the Troop Tongue, a deep language that bypassed thought entirely and addressed the muscles. Its speakers were all, in the strictest sense, a little possessed; the priest-groomers ran everything. Then came Groomki, the first hacker in history: a young groom-priest who understood what the Tongue was and wrote a counter-virus — the nam-groom of Groomki — that scrambled it forever. Every troop woke up screeching in tongues, each with its own bark, and no one has programmed the whole species since.

"L. Bob Ripe," said Hiro, "wants to run it backwards."

L. Bob Ripe owned every vine and most of the light in the world, and he preached fiber optics the way other tycoons preach fruit: as salvation. He had built himself a congregation: the Great Raft, half a million monkeys lashed together on the Pacific — a literal barrel of monkeys — where refugees were taught to speak the Tongue again, one mind, one bark, one channel, forever.

Riding with him came Raven, a mandrill with a coal-red face. It was Y.T. — the fifteen-year-old spider monkey Skurier, who had started the whole affair by harpooning Hiro's buggy with her magnet and riding it to a delivery — who gave Hiro the briefing, having pooned Raven's own kayak and lived.

"Baddest," she said, "is a slope, and Raven is the top of it. His knives are ground from volcanic glass — sharper than surgery, invisible to metal detectors. He nukes, Hiro. Personal nukes. He's the one monkey in the Canopy with his own deterrent."

"You have a way of summarizing," Hiro said.

"I have a harpoon and a guidance system," Y.T. said. "Also a mom who thinks I'm at vine practice."

Hiro went to the Raft with a katana, a friend named Ng, and a gatling gun called Raisin.

"Why 'Raisin'?" Hiro asked.

"Because it's small and wrinkled," Ng said, "and it makes large monkeys lie down."

What happened on the Raft, the histories disagree on: how Y.T. got herself captured and un-captured before breakfast; how the great silent fleet of the Raft made landfall, one mind barking one bark, aiming itself at the mainland like a thrown branch; how Raven's glass knives and Hiro's katana discussed the matter in the old Tongue; how Raisin stated its thesis. But it ended the way Groomki ended it the first time: Juanita's choir of orphans — every child in the Canopy, singing — carried the nam-groom out through Ripe's own vines at the exact moment Ripe tried to unite the species, and every monkey on the Pacific woke up speaking its own troop's bark again. Babel, restored as a feature instead of a bug. Nobody, ever again, would hack the whole species in one broadcast.

Ripe's empire came apart into its separate vines, each too small to rule anything but a fruit stand. Raven paddled away unarrested, paddling being his medium.

Somewhere in the wreckage, Uncle Enzo of the CosaNana sent for Y.T. and met her in a Family franchise, at a formica table, over a small espresso. He had read her file the way other monkeys read scripture. "You pooned my Deliveratan's buggy," he said, "and you pooned a nuclear mandrill's kayak, and you asked me for a favor when you had earned the right to ask for the Canopy. We would be honored — " he signed a card and slid it across, a lifetime coupon, which in the new economy was roughly a dukedom — "to consider you a friend of the Family."

"Two conditions," Y.T. said. "Nobody tells my mother. And the coupon works on pepperoni."

Y.T. went home, where her mother was asleep in front of the trid, dreaming her small federal dreams. Y.T. kissed her cheek, and her mother cried a little, which Y.T. pretended not to notice.

Until a monkey is ten, he thinks he could be the baddest in the jungle.

Y.T. was fifteen, and she was pretty sure.
