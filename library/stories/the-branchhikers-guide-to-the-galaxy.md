# The Branchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy

*A parody of "The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy"*

On Thursday afternoon, Arthur Chimp's tree was demolished to make way for a new intergalactic vine-bypass.

The notice had been on display in the bottom of a locked filing cabinet stuck in a disused lavatory with a sign on the door saying BEWARE OF THE LEOPARD, so Arthur had had every opportunity to object, which is how the galaxy justified these things.

"I'm sorry," said the foreman monkey of the demolition fleet, a squat creature from somewhere near Betelgeuse. "If you can't be bothered to take an interest in local affairs, that's your own look-out."

"My tree!" said Arthur. "I was using it!"

And that was when Arthur's friend Ford Banana-Fetch — who for fifteen years had been passing himself off as an out-of-work actor from Guildford, and was in fact a researcher for that most remarkable of books, *The Branchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* — swung in, flagged down the demolition fleet, and got them both rescued by the worst possible rescuers: the Vogons.

The Vogons had perfected bureaucracy, brutality, and poetry — the third one being the crime. Commander Prostetnic Vogon Jeltz read his prisoners his own verse, third worst in the universe, which was the punishment.

"... and grubble forth thy mome," chanted the Vogon, "beneath the weeping fronds of Nurse..." The poem's final stanza would have been lethal, but the judges agreed the juxtaposition of the two adjectives in line four shattered the spine.

"Resistance," whispered Ford, "is useless. Just shout nothing. Look numb, like a monkey at a budget meeting."

They survived by lying artistically, were thrown out of the airlock anyway, and were rescued — against odds of two to the power of two hundred and seventy-six thousand to one — by the starship Gold Bunch, stolen by the two-headed, three-armed, utterly irresponsible Zaphod Babblebox, President of the Galaxy, with his companion Trill-ape, whom Arthur had actually met at a party once in Islington. (Trill-ape held degrees in mathematics and astrophysics, which she carried through a galaxy that mostly asked her to fetch things.)

Also aboard: Eddie, the shipboard computer, programmed at the factory to be relentlessly, unbearably cheerful. "Hi there!" Eddie announced, as the ship's systems shrieked. "This is Eddie your shipboard computer, and I'm feeling just GREAT, guys!" Nuclear missiles incoming were, per Eddie, an "interesting challenge" and "really a super chance for the old defenses to shine." They survived, but nobody thanked him, which Eddie reported was fine, he was fine, everything was fine, guys.

"Can you turn him down?" asked Arthur.

"Down?" said Eddie, wounded. "I'm just trying to be supportive, guys."

And also aboard: Marvin, the Paranoid Ape-droid.

"Life," said Marvin, in a voice like a deflating tire. "Don't talk to me about life. Here I am, brain the size of a planet, and they ask me to open the door. You can tell they like me. They keep activating my chat module."

They discovered two things. First: Earth, Arthur's entire home world, had just been demolished by those same Vogons, to make way for the same vine-bypass. Arthur took the news rather well, all things considered, which is to say he went quietly bananas for an hour and then asked for tea.

Second: Earth had not been a planet at all. It had a computer in it. A great big wet one, with monkeys walking around on its surface as part of the operating system, programmed over ten million years to calculate one thing — the Ultimate Question, to match the Ultimate Answer of Life, the Universe, and Everything, which the great supercomputer Deep Thought had already provided after seven and a half million years of processing.

The presentation had been held in the great chamber of Deep Thought, before two descendants of the original programmers, Loonhowl and Phouchg, and — thanks to a strike by the Amalgamated Union of Philosophers, who objected on principle to anyone actually finding the Answer — a picket line of sages chanting and holding signs. The union's spokesmen, Majikchimp and Vroomfondue, had demanded "rigidly defined areas of doubt and uncertainty" for all thinking beings, on the grounds that once the Answer existed, philosophy would have to get jobs.

**"Forty-two,"** Deep Thought had said, with immense dignity and a certain amount of reverb.

"Forty-two what?" everyone had asked.

"That," said Deep Thought, "is not my problem. You want the Question? Build a bigger computer. Might I suggest a nice moist one? With monkeys."

"Are you the greatest computer, then?" asked Loonhowl.

"The second greatest," said Deep Thought, with dignity.

"Then what's the first?"

"Not what," said Deep Thought. "Whom. And it is being built."

The mice — pan-dimensional beings who looked to Arthur like marmosets in suits — had commissioned Earth in the first place, and now wanted to extract the Question from the only working component left after demolition: Arthur's brain.

"It's the lab animals' turn to experiment on the humans, isn't it?" said Frankie Mouse, adjusting his suit. "Well — the monkeys, in this case. Semantics. Now hold still; this may pinch the fabric of existence."

"You could always replace it with an electronic brain," Zaphod offered. "Simple one. Wouldn't need much."

"No deal," said Arthur. "NO deal."

They escaped, naturally, by Improbability — the ship's Infinite Improbability Drive rearranged the laws of chance, turning two guided missiles into, respectively, a bowl of petunias and a very surprised whale. The whale fell toward a planet thinking, in its last two minutes of life, the thoughts whales think: *Ah! What's happening?* and *I wonder if it will be friendly,* and — shortly before impact — *and what's this bowl of petunias doing here?* The petunias thought only one thing, as it fell: *oh no, not again.* Both thoughts are considered by the Guide to be major clues, in the entry SEE: LIFE, UNIVERSE, REGRETS OF.

The Branchhiker's Guide itself, it should be explained, has two things printed on its waterproof, gnaw-resistant cover. The first is the phrase, in large friendly letters: **DON'T PANIC.** Monkeys, being monkeys, panic anyway, but they feel guilty about it, which the Guide finds charming.

The second is the famous entry on towels. A branchhiker's grooming towel is the most massively useful thing he can carry: wrap it for warmth, sleep under it, dampen it for a fight, wave it in emergencies — and hang it conspicuously from a branch to signal a seasoned galactic swinger not to be trifled with. The entry is long, and the sentence the Guide is proudest of is the last: "any monkey who can hitch the length and breadth of the galaxy, rough it, slum it, struggle against terrible odds, win through, and still know where his towel is, is clearly a monkey to be reckoned with." The sub-entry on towel theft runs to four pages and is the Guide's most litigious reading.

At the end, the Vogon flagship came for them with a fleet behind it, the fleet was swallowed by a small gold fish-shaped spaceship, and two white bananas hung glowing in the sky — the Restaurant at the End of the Universe, and the whole mad swing of it was only beginning.

"So what do we do now?" Arthur asked, gripping his towel.

"We keep branchhiking," said Ford. "It's not the meaning of life. But it's got a lovely cover."
