# Chimphood's End

*A parody of "Childhood's End"*

The ships came on a Tuesday, and hung over every capital in the world — vast silver figs, motionless over Londonzoa, New Yorktropics, Tokyobana — in much the same way that bricks don't.

No landing. No message. But when the Secretary-General of the United Troops, Rikki Groomgren, sat alone in his office that night, a voice like a great cat purring in his skull said: *My name is Karellangur. I am Supervisor for Earth. There will be no more war, Groomgren. You may consider this an accomplished fact.*

"Do I get a say in it?" asked Groomgren.

*You do not,* said Karellangur, not unkindly. *In fifty years, you will thank me. In a hundred, you will barely remember how to hold a grudge. That is the point.*

There were monkeys who did not take it well. A general in the high grasslands ordered his tanks to roll at dawn, as a point of pride; the tanks rolled exactly a hundred yards and stopped, out of fuel, and no fuel shipment would ever find them again, and every mechanic in the supply chain found his paperwork had quietly rearranged itself overnight. A cartel of fruit barons tried to corner the world's bananas; the price of bananas ceased, as a concept, to exist. The last warlord on Earth surrendered in the third month to an empty sky, and was heard to complain that you could not even surrender *to* anyone, which everyone agreed was the insufferable part.

And that is very nearly exactly what happened, planet-wide. No more war. No more hunger. Bananas, freely, everywhere. No monkey worked more than twenty hours a week, and the loudest complaint in the world, by year sixty, was that the figs overhead blocked the view of the stars. The troops' greatest minds quit pushing physics — every avenue Karellangur forbade "for your own good" — and the arts went soft, then ornamental, then quiet. The jungle had become a garden, and gardens, Karellangur might have reflected, are where curiosity goes to be mulched.

The generation born under the ships threw one last party worth recording, at the tree-mansion of a rich young mandrill named Rupert Boast, where the fashionable game was a talking board that spelled out answers with a planchette. Someone asked it, as a joke, when the visitors would finally show themselves. The board, amid much laughter, spelled: *NEVER*. Who, then, would rule the Earth after the troops were gone? And the board, which had been witty all evening, spelled slowly: *THE LAST GENERATION*, and refused to say another word, and everyone roared at the spirits for being morbid, and refilled their cups, and none of them ever guessed that the joke had been told about them, and that it was not a joke.

Karellangur himself was never seen for over a hundred years. When at last he descended to meet Groomgren's successor face to face, a generation of monkeys got the shock of their lives. He was a Night Monkey — a tarsier-headed, saucer-eyed, spindly-limbed thing out of the oldest campfire tales, wings folded along his back, a shape straight out of the race's oldest bad dreams.

"We are not devils," Karellangur said mildly, reading their faces. "But your ancestors saw us, once, far in the deep past of your species' memory, and were afraid. We never understood why that memory was of *the future* — until it was too late to matter."

Then came the quiet. No more children were born — anywhere. Not one. Zoos, maternity groves, day-care lianas: empty, one quiet year after another. Humanity was granted, by lottery of biology, one last generation. The talking boards, someone remembered, had said so.

And the last generation was wrong.

Not sick. Not unhappy. Wrong. They stopped speaking. They stopped playing. They sat in circles in the long grass of abandoned playgrounds, staring at nothing, while their toys drifted overhead in slow, lazy orbits. Jean and George Gibbonson watched their daughter Jennifer-Anne — the first of them all — sleep through a solar flare without waking, and heard her dreaming in a language that a hundred thousand children dreamed simultaneously. The doctors were useless, being doctors; the parents were worse, being parents.

"The groupmind has taken them," Karellangur told Jean Gibbonson, and for the first time in a century his voice was not purring at all. "They are leaving us, madam. They are becoming something that does not need troop-trees, or bananas, or mothers. Your children are joining the Overgrove."

"The what?"

"The Overgrove. The mind above all minds. We have watched it take species a thousand times, and we have never once been permitted to join it. My people are... a ladder. Others climb us." The great night-eyes closed. "We always knew. The highest branches are not for us."

Jean and George took the children who were left to New Athensgrove, the last of the holdout islands, founded by artists and stubborn macaques who refused to spend the finale queueing. For a few years the island burned bright: plays written to be performed once and eaten, orchestras that performed over the sea, a glassblower who made nothing but windows, for the light. Everyone on New Athensgrove was creating against a deadline, which was all anyone had ever needed, and it was the best party at the end of the world anyone could have arranged.

Then the summons came, and Karellangur's ships herded the last children — herded, nobody pretended otherwise — onto a single empty continent, to wait in the grass with their own kind. The parents could visit. Most did, once. Jean Gibbonson stood at the treeline for an hour while her daughter looked through her at something eight universes wide, and went home, and never said what it was she had or hadn't seen, and George never asked.

The last adult monkeys on Earth included one Jan Rodrigrubs, a young chimpanzee who had done the one interesting thing left: blackmailed his way aboard a Uplifter supply ship, traveled to the Uplifters' far world and back at lightspeed, and returned eighty Earth-years later, unaged, to a planet of empty cities and ten million silent children in the grass.

He had seen the Uplifters' homeworld, and it was the loneliest place in the universe: a museum world, immaculate, endless — every art their species had ever made preserved under glass, and no one left to become anything new. They collected the destinies of others the way sad old monkeys collect other monkeys' letters. Jan had asked his host why they served the Overgrove at all, and the Uplifter had taken a long time to answer, and had said: "Because we were not invited, little one. One serves the door one may never open."

It was Jan who kept the log of the end, and Jan who watched from a hillside as, over one long final summer, the children rose. The storm broke over the whole world at once: a slow cyclone of light above every troop-tree on Earth, as ten million children lifted out of the grass, out of their bodies, out of history — becoming one shining thing that flexed once, gathered itself, and poured up off the planet into the dark, and was gone.

The earthquakes followed. Earth itself was coming apart; the new mind had eaten its inheritance entire and had no further use for the nest.

High above, in the last ship, Karellangur watched his tedious, beloved assignment end in blue fire, and transmitted a final message home:

*It has happened. Whether it is good, I have never known. We will follow the Overgrove's trail until it ends. If it ends. Goodbye, little monkeys. You were the twelfth. Yours was the finest of all the crops.*

The ship went to starspeed. The jungle below had already stopped burning.

And Jan Rodrigrubs, the last monkey in the old world, watched the sunrise come up gold and green over the empty canopy, and did not feel afraid. He transmitted the last log entry as the ground shook — "the wind is warm; it smells of rain and figs; I regret nothing that was" — and settled back against the hill, and put his hands behind his head, and waited for the planet to finish its sentence.
