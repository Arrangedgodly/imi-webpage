# Primate Latitudes

*A parody of "Pirate Latitudes"*

Port Chimpal, Jamaica, in the Year of Our Monkey 1665, was the wickedest town in the New World, and the busiest. On hanging days the whole troop turned out to watch pirates dangle from the yardarm while merchants haggled underneath for their boots. Captain Charles Hunta, a broad-shouldered chimp with a scar through one eyebrow and a smile like a drawn cutlass, watched the executions from the balcony of the Feather & Fig with professional interest, calculating drop-lengths the way other monkeys calculated profits.

He was a privateer, which is a pirate with papers. The difference, as Hunta liked to say, is a matter of paperwork and banana splits: "A privateer is a patriot when his patron wins the war. Every other time, he's just a monkey with a fast ship."

Sir James Almond, the mandrill Governor of Jamaica, received Hunta in his study behind a screen of leaded glass, because governors and pirates cannot be seen going ape together in public.

"There is a galleon," said Sir James, "at Matanana Bay. The *El Trinidad*. She carried the year's harvest of silver plantains out of the Main — forty tons of white-leaf silver, enough to buy the Crown a new navy. She sits under the guns of the Matanana fortress, behind a reef no pilot alive will cross, guarded by Comandante Cazalla, who has flayed every monkey fool enough to attempt her. The Viceroy believes her impregnable."

"Nothing a monkey can build," said Hunta, "is impregnable to another monkey with sufficient appetite. Who's my crew?"

He assembled them the way a chef assembles a recipe for disaster. Sansong was a langur assassin, elegant and faithless, who professed loyalty the way other monkeys professed religion: loudly, and only when witnesses were present. Lazoo was a slow loris raised as a boy, with night-silver eyes that could count the legs on a fly at a hundred yards and a temper nobody mistook twice. The Moor was a Barbary macaque of enormous calm and enormous hands. Don Diego — "Black Eye," on account of the patch — was a capuchin gunner who could build a bomb out of a gourd, two fingers of lamp oil, and a grudge. Enders, the sailing master, was a gibbon who loved rigging and strumpets in equal measure and often confused the two. And Bassa was a gorilla the size of a ship's boat, loyal as an anchor.

They took aboard one passenger, a small marmoset servant girl called Anne Sharpe, who had been beaten once too often by her mistress and asked to be carried to sea. Nobody asked twice, because nobody argued with a monkey volunteering to work.

Mrs. Hacklett, the king's commissioner's wife — a howler of great plumage and greater appetite — had other ideas. She cornered Hunta in the powder-blue shadows of the Governor's garden and offered him her fan, her sighs, and considerably more.

"Captain," she breathed, "a monkey of your talents need not risk the reef at Matanana. There are softer channels."

"Madam," said Hunta, bowing, "I never mix monkey business with the Crown's business. The fur always tells the tale."

She never forgave him. Remember that; it matters later.

They sailed in the *Cassandra* under a bruised monsoon sky, because Hunta wanted the storm's skirts to hide his approach. Off the bay he put Lazoo up the mast with a shaded lantern, and the loris read the reef the way a priest reads scripture — slow, silent, and only aloud when it was a matter of sin.

"Channel's there," Lazoo said at last. "Twice a boat's width, may once. The howler on the fort is watching the weather, not the water. In this gloom we're just weather."

They went in at dawn's first grey, oars muffled with banana leaves, and Bassa swimming the keel line ahead like a hairy pilot whale. The fortress woke to fire pots arcing out of the mist — Don Diego's specialty, gourds of popping corn and oil that burst in thumping orange fists — and then to gorilla, which is to say to Bassa, coming over the wall with a cannon in each hand as gently as a mother carrying twins.

Comandante Cazalla met Hunta on the rampart with a toledan blade and a curse. They fought along the whole length of the wall in the rain, steel ringing off steel, the howler strong and the chimp fast, until Cazalla slipped a half-inch on wet stone and Hunta's point found the one gap in his breastplate, just under the arm.

"Tell the Viceroy," Hunta said, "the channel is not impregnable. It was merely untried."

In the galleon's hold they found the silver plantains stacked like moonlit logs, and the crew went briefly, dangerously bananas — dancing, weeping, biting coins. Sansong's eyes, Hunta noticed, did not dance. They counted.

They did not get clean away. Two days out, the sea itself reached for them: a kraken, arms like a forest of drowned masts, hauled two sailors off the deck and wrapped the stern in a grip that made the hull groan. The Moor hacked at the tentacle with an axe while Don Diego calmly assembled a fire pot, lit it, stood at the rail like a monkey posting a letter, and dropped it down the beast's sucking throat. The kraken let go with a sound like a swamp apologizing and slid away, and Enders trimmed sail with a shaking hand and swore off two of his vices, temporarily.

The hurricane found them off the Caicos. For a day and a night the *Cassandra* climbed mountains of water and slid into valleys of it, Enders lashed to the wheel, singing filthy hymns to keep his courage up, Anne Sharpe beside him passing the sandglass, her small face as steady as a figurehead's. The Moor went over the side saving a man and came back up the trailing line on his own, insulted. And in the worst hour of it, when the storm's roar swallowed everything, Sansong tried to take the ship.

He had the boat loosened and six malcontents at his back, and he told Hunta so through the rain, sword drawn, shouting that a live share in the boat beat a dead share in the storm.

"Sansong," Hunta called back, almost kindly, "you never once went ape for anything but yourself. That's not a pirate. That's just a monkey."

Hunta shot him through the throat across the deck, and the storm took the body, and the malcontents discovered urgent business at the pumps. When dawn came, so did Port Chimpal.

There, Mr. Hacklett — the king's man, a thin howler with his wife's fingerprints all over his policies — had Hunta clapped in irons on three charges: piracy, murder, and the ravishing of Mrs. Hacklett, who swore on a stack of bibles that Hunta had forced himself upon her in the Governor's garden. It was, all of Port Chimpal agreed, the trial of the decade, and the decade had been lively.

The hanging judge was ready. The rope was ready. And on the last night, Mrs. Hacklett came to the cell.

"I will recant," she whispered through the bars, "every word. If you will lie with me, Captain. Once. That is my price."

Hunta looked at her for a long time — at the plumage, the hunger, the small mean fury of a monkey who had never once been told no by anything but her own reflection.

"No," he said. "Hang me, then. A chimp who can't keep his fur out of one bargain will lose it in the next."

She stared. Then, because a monkey scorned works in mysterious ways, she went straight to the court and recanted everything anyway — torn, the gossips said, between fury and something dangerously like respect. Without her, the ravishing charge collapsed. Without the ravishing charge, the murder charge collapsed, Cazalla having died lawfully enough in a lawful raid under a letter of marque that Sir James Almond now produced with a flourish and a look of wounded innocence. And piracy — well. "You cannot hang a monkey," the Chief Justice sighed, "for taking the King's enemies' fruit with the King's own signature on the orders. Acquitted."

The gallery went bananas. The gallery would have carried Hunta home on its shoulders, but he walked, because Bassa had already loaded the silver plantains onto carts and Anne Sharpe was waiting at the gate with his hat.

He bought the finest house in Port Chimpal, hung a cutlass over the hearth, took Anne Sharpe as his prime mate, and hung out a sign that read: **HUNTA & SONS, PRIVATEERING, SALVAGE, AND ADVENTURE — NO JOBS TOO HIGH, NONE TOO LOW, NONE WHILE THE WIND'S AFT.**

Years later, a young monkey asked him whether it was true he'd robbed the King of Spain blind, cheated the gallows, and survived a kraken and a hurricane in one season.

"Survived?" said old Hunta, peeling a banana with his teeth. "Son, I *swung* through them. Latitude's just a line on a chart — but a monkey who picks his crew, minds his weather, and keeps his monkey business out of his business, he can swing anywhere between here and the horizon."

He tossed the young monkey the peel. "Now mind the curve in that, boy. That's the whole of seamanship."

Outside, the masts of Port Chimpal swayed like branches, and somewhere beyond the reef, faint and eternal, the sea cleared its throat and waited for the next of them.
