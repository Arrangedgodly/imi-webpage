# The Macaquelous Journey of Edward Tulane

*A parody of "The Miraculous Journey of Edward Tulane"*

Once, in a tall house on Egypt Street, there lived a china macaque named Edward Tulane. He was made of the finest porcelain, with a long elegant tail curled just so, velveteen fur the color of ripe banana, and movable joints at elbows and knees. He wore a hand-tailored silk vest and a small gold pocket watch on a chain. Edward was extremely pleased with himself, and why not? He was, by his own private assessment, the most distinguished-looking monkey ever fired in a kiln.

He belonged to a ten-year-old girl named Abilene Tulane, who adored him beyond all reason. Every morning she wound his pocket watch and told him, "I am coming home soon, Edward. Watch the time for me." And every morning Edward stared at the window and thought about how marvelously the light caught his porcelain ears.

He did not care for Abilene. He did not care for anybody. His heart, had you looked inside his china chest, was a small dry coconut — sealed, hard, and entirely its own.

"Edward," said Abilene's grandmother Pellegrina one evening, her old brown eyes fixed on him, "I wonder if you are capable of loving anyone at all."

Edward, being a doll, said nothing. But he thought that love sounded like a great deal of monkey business, and he was above that.

It was Pellegrina who told Abilene a bedtime story that night, about a beautiful little macaque princess who loved nobody. "She was turned into a wart hog by a witch," said Pellegrina, "and no one wept for her, for she had never loved anyone who might weep for her. The end."

"That's a terrible story," said Abilene.

"It is," agreed Pellegrina, looking again at Edward. "But it is not over. Not for some."

Edward felt — though a china monkey cannot shiver — something very like a shiver.

The Tulanes sailed for London, and Abilene took Edward aboard. On deck, two rude young baboons began to toss him back and forth for fun, hooting with laughter. "Give him back!" cried Abilene. "He's my prime mate!" But the baboons only laughed harder, and one of them threw too high, and the other missed — and Edward Tulane went sailing over the railing, silk vest fluttering, gold watch flying, down and down and down into the green sea.

He sank. The water filled his velveteen fur, and the last thing he saw was Abilene's tear-streaked face far above, growing smaller. "Ramona of the deep," he thought bitterly, "this is no place for a monkey of my distinction."

And there he lay on the ocean floor, face down in the muck, for two hundred and ninety-seven days. Fish came and looked at him and went away. A lobster called him "the world's saddest paperweight." The seasons swung by overhead like branches he could not reach. If you have ever wondered whether a china monkey can despair, Edward can tell you: he can.

On the two hundred and ninety-eighth day, a storm churned the sea, and a fisherman's net scooped him up. The fisherman, Lawrence, carried him home to his wife Nellie, who gasped with delight. "Look at the poor dear! He's been through the wars." She sewed Edward a pair of blue coveralls and a little knit cap. She set him at the kitchen window. Nellie talked to him all day about her children, and her pies, and the stars. She renamed him Susanna.

Edward did not answer, of course. But somewhere in his chest, the coconut cracked — just a hairline.

Then Lawrence's daughter Lolly came to visit, sneered at the "creepy old monkey doll," and dumped him in the trash heap. Buried in garbage, Edward thought: Nellie talked to me, and I never once talked back. Now I would give my gold watch to hear her voice.

A dog named Lucy dug him up — a rangy hound who shook him like a rattle and delivered him to her master, a hobo bull of a man named, as it happened, Bull. Bull rode the rails with Lucy, and Edward rode in Bull's knapsack, and at night around the hobo fires, men who had nothing held Edward gently and spoke of the children they had left behind. One quiet man told the story of a daughter who danced on his boots. Another said nothing, only touched Edward's porcelain paw. In the camps, Edward learned that a heart can be carried far from home and still go on beating.

"Where are we going, Lucy?" Edward asked one night, under the wheeling stars.

Lucy, being a dog, heard him somehow. "Nowhere fast," she said. "Going ape and riding free."

It did not last. A brakeman found them riding the boxcars and hurled Edward off the moving train into the ferns. Days later a crabby old woman strung him up on a pole in her garden as a scarecrow, and Edward hung with his arms out, indignant beyond all measure, until a boy named Bryce scrambled up the pole at midnight and carried him home in a flour sack.

Bryce had a little sister, Sarah Ruth, who was sick and could not run and shout and swing like other monkeys. She was four years old, with huge dark eyes, and when she saw the china macaque she held out both arms.

"Hello," she whispered.

"Hello," said Edward — but only in the way of dolls, which is silence. Sarah Ruth did not mind. She named him Malone and told him about the stars and her brother's harmonica, and how one day she would climb the tallest fig tree in the county. And Edward, who had once admired only himself, found that he would have traded every last bit of his porcelain to climb it with her.

Edward learned to tell stories with his hands while Bryce worked the strings like a puppeteer, and Sarah Ruth laughed — a small papery laugh — and it was the finest sound Edward had ever heard.

But Sarah Ruth's sickness got worse, and one dawn she stopped, and the house filled with people crying, and Edward learned the size of the hole a small monkey leaves in the world.

Bryce took Edward anyway, on the bus to Memphis, to earn dinner by dancing him for coins. He was starving himself, and his hands shook, and Edward flew from the strings and crashed onto the tabletop — and the china macaque broke, in pieces, all his elegant pieces, wide open.

"Do not weep," Edward wanted to say. "I am done for, and I loved her, and that is enough."

A doll mender named Lucius Clarke gathered up every shard and wired Edward whole again, for the price of Bryce's hunting knife. But mended dolls are worth only patience, and Edward sat on a high shelf in the mender's shop for seasons, facing the wall, his heart slammed shut again. He decided he would never hope again. Hoping was how you got thrown off ships and out of trains and into gardens and onto tabletops.

Beside him sat an ancient doll named Lucy, wise as grandmothers.

"You have to open your heart," said old Lucy, "and wait. Somebody will come. Somebody will be yours, and you will be hers, and that is the whole silly, swinging miracle of it."

"I have been loved," said Edward. "I have been lost. Lost is worse."

"Then you know both," said Lucy. "So open."

And Edward — battered, scarred, tail glued at a proud new angle — turned his face to the shop window. And one rainy afternoon the bell rang, and in walked a mother with a little girl, and the mother stopped and put her hand to her mouth, because there on the shelf sat a macaque she had wound a gold watch for, once, on Egypt Street.

"Edward," said Abilene.

"Mama," said the little girl, reaching up. "Is that the monkey? Is that Edward?"

Abilene lifted him down. Her tears fell on his mended head like warm rain.

"Edward Tulane," she whispered, "you have been on a long journey."

He had been overboard and undersea, sewn and smashed, hoisted on a pole and mended on a shelf. He had been Susanna and Malone and nobody. He was scarred and his vest was gone and somebody's dog had chewed his left ear.

And in that whole wide window-lit moment, as the little girl hugged him and called him hers, Edward's coconut heart split clean open, at last and forever, and the china macaque who had loved nobody understood the end of Pellegrina's story:

The princess is only turned into a wart hog if she stays that way.

"Yes," said Edward, to Abilene, to the girl, to the rain, to the whole swinging world — silently, happily, finally. "I'm home."
