# Where the Sidebranch Bends

*A parody of "Where the Sidewalk Ends"*

There is a place where the sidebranch bends, and the troop's oldest stories begin, where the canopy opens like a green palm and the sun burns bright crimson warm — and the chimpdren, they know it. The chimpdren always know.

Sid the macaque did not know it. Sid lived in the middle of the Smoke Jungle, where the burn piles smolder and the dark lower branches wind and bend, where the asphalt vines sprawl and nobody swings anymore because there is nowhere to swing to.

In the Smoke Jungle, everyone hurried and no one climbed. Sid's mother had eleven jobs, all of them involving sitting. His teacher, Professor Gruff, taught sitting. The whole troop had gone, as his grandmother put it, completely groundward.

Every morning at the troop school, the chimpdren sang the Smoke Jungle anthem, which went:

*Listen to the MUSTN'TS, child, listen to the DON'TS. / Listen to the SHOULDN'TS, the IMPOSSIBLES, the WON'TS. / Listen to the NEVER-HAVES, then listen close to me — / anything can happen, child, but NOTHING up a tree.*

"That last line is wrong," said Sid, on his very first day.

"Nothing," said Professor Gruff, "is up a tree. Especially not your head. Eyes down. Tail down. Dreams down."

But Sid's tail refused, curling up at the tip day and night, pointing at the sky like a small hairy compass.

"If you are a dreamer," his grandmother used to say, grooming his ears in the gray dawn, "come in. If you are a dreamer, a wisher, a liar, a hope-er, a pray-er, a magic banana buyer... if you're a pretender, come sit by my fire, for we have some flax-golden tales to spin. Come in! Come in!"

She had been there once, long ago, before the smoke.

"Follow the chalk-white arrow marks," she whispered. "The young ones scratch them into the bark. The chimpdren, they mark, and the chimpdren, they know the place where the sidebranch bends."

"But Gran," said Sid, "everything below the smoke is flat. Where would a sidebranch even GO?"

"It bends," said his grandmother, "back up."

Nobody in the Smoke Jungle went up. Up was where the old stories lived, and the old stories had been filed away in favor of sitting.

Even so, Sid looked up — his whole problem, according to Professor Gruff.

One smoke-black morning, Sid met Sarah Cynthia Sylvia Stump, the most famous monkey in the Smoke Jungle, famous for one thing only: she would not take the banana peels out.

They piled up around her tree. They piled up past her knees. They reached her chin. They touched the sky, as the saying goes, and Sarah Cynthia Sylvia Stump simply would not take the peels out. It went on so long, and so high, that something remarkable happened: the peels composted, and fig sprouts came up through them, and the sprouts, being young and optimistic, bent.

Toward the sun.

Sid saw it first — a green bend, a living branch, curling up out of a mountain of old banana peels. And scratched into its bark: a chalk-white arrow.

"Gran's arrows," Sid breathed.

So Sid did what dreamers do: he climbed, with a walk that was measured and slow, following the arrows from branch to bend to branch. Halfway up the peels, a loose vine took him around the shoulders like an old friend who doesn't know his own strength, and Sid, wiggling, found himself humming the only song that fit:

*Oh gee, it's up to my knee. Oh my, it's up to my thigh. Oh fiddle, it's up to my middle. Oh heck, it's up to my neck. I'm being hugged by a boa constrictor — I wish I was somebody QUICKER.*

He was. He wriggled free in eleven seconds, which is quick for a dreamer, and the vine, which was only a vine after all, looked faintly embarrassed.

At the top of the peels, the smoke line began. Sid looked back once. Down there, the whole troop was singing the anthem, and he could hear Professor Gruff's voice booming the last line: NOTHING up a tree.

And Sid — who had learned nothing else in school, but had learned this from his grandmother — sang the line back the other way, the way it was surely written before somebody sat on it:

*...but ANYTHING can be, child. ANYTHING can be.*

He climbed past the smoke line, where the air turned from gray to gold. He climbed past the pits where the asphalt flowers grew — terrible blooms, gray and flat and always in rows, and every one of them looking down. He counted arrows the way you count heartbeats.

And then the wind changed.

It smelled of peppermint. Peppermint and rain and ripe fig, all mixed into one impossible breeze.

And then the branch ended — no, BEGAN — at the place where the sidebranch bends: the first branch of the true canopy, where the grass grows soft and white, where the sun burns crimson bright, where the moon-bird rests from his flight to cool in the peppermint wind.

Monkeys were already there. Dreamers, wishers, liars, hope-ers, pray-ers, magic banana buyers — swinging slow circles in the gold light, telling flax-golden tales. One was juggling raindrops and keeping the cloudy ones for later. Another was a liar of the good kind, the kind whose lies turn true if you wait a week.

"Welcome," said an old gibbon with a grin. "We wondered when the Smoke Jungle would send somebody."

"How did you know it would send anybody?"

"Because the chimpdren always mark the way," said the gibbon, "and the chimpdren always know. Somebody up there has been scratching you arrows since before you were born. The young ones never stop believing somebody's coming."

"Who scratched mine?"

"Ah," said the gibbon. "The dreamers scratch arrows. The dreamers climb them. Most monkeys do both eventually. Ask your grandmother what she was doing last Tuesday."

Sid looked down through the gold leaves. Far, far below, tiny and gray, the Smoke Jungle lay like a dropped ash. And there, a figure stood on a mountain of banana peels, shading her eyes, looking up.

Sarah Cynthia Sylvia Stump. Still not taking the peels out — but climbing them.

And behind her, squinting upward, tail-tips curling hopefully at the sky: Professor Gruff, eleven of Sid's neighbors, and one very old grandmother, climbing slowly, with a walk that was measured and slow, showing everybody else where to put their hands.

Let us leave this place where the smoke blows black, thought Sid. We'll walk with a walk that is measured and slow, and watch where the chalk-white arrows go, to the place where the sidebranch bends.

And they did. Every last one — even the professor, who quietly admitted there had been a branch up there after all, and then wrote a paper about how he had discovered it, which is sitting of a different kind.

And Sid? Sid sat by the gibbon's fire, in the soft white moss, with the peppermint wind in his fur and his tail pointing proudly at the sky — a dreamer, come in, at last, to stay.
