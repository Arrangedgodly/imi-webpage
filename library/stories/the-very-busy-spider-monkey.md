# The Very Busy Spider Monkey

*A parody of "The Very Busy Spider"*

Early one morning the sun came up over the kapok tree, and a little spider monkey swung onto the highest branch with a long green vine looped over one shoulder.

She had a plan. Today the fig wind would blow — she could smell it coming, sweet and heavy, the way you can smell rain before it falls — and when the fig wind blew, the ripe figs would drop all at once, plop, plop, plop, like soft purple rain. And the spider monkey intended to be ready.

She hitched the vine to the branch. She looped it. She knotted it. She wove it, over and under, under and over, with her four clever hands and her cleverer tail, and she began to build a hammock-net, wide and strong and fine, to catch the falling feast.

She had learned weaving from her grandmother, who had learned it from hers, all the way back to the first spider monkey who watched a spider and thought, *I could do that. Bigger.* Spiders weave to eat. Monkey grandmothers weave to keep their grandchildren from bouncing off the forest floor, which is nearly the same thing.

She had not been weaving long when a howler monkey swung by, booming his good morning at the whole jungle.

"Ooo-ooo-ooo!" said the howler. "Want to come roar at the sunrise? It's the best part of the day!"

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock.

The sun climbed. A toucan flapped down and perched on a bough beside her, his beak as big and bright as a mango slice.

"Want to flick berries and catch them in your beak?" asked the toucan. "I'll teach you. I'm very good. Well — I'm very good at flicking."

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock.

A while later a sloth came along. It took a while. That is what sloths are for.

"Want... to... hang... out?" asked the sloth.

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock. (She was also, it must be said, already hanging — upside down by her tail, weaving with both feet and both hands — but the sloth was too polite to point out that she had beaten him to it.)

A troop of young capuchins came racketing through the canopy like a joke with too many punchlines.

"Want to play monkey-in-the-middle?" they called. "You'd never be in the middle! You're never in the middle! You're never anywhere! Come play!"

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock.

Presently an actual spider descended on an actual thread, inspected the hammock from corner to corner with the air of a building inspector, and sniffed.

"You're doing it wrong," said the spider.

"Says you," said the spider monkey — which was the only thing anybody said to her all day that was worth answering — and she kept right on weaving, because the spider said that to everybody, and had never once woven anything a fig could fall into safely.

The sun climbed higher. A capybara waddled out of the river below and looked up, whiskers dripping.

"Want to wallow in the warm mud?" asked the capybara. "It's lovely and squishy. There's room for a small monkey. Well. A very small monkey."

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock.

At midday a tree frog piped up from a leaf nearby, where he had been sitting so still he was nearly a second leaf.

"Want to sing in the rain chorus tonight?" asked the tree frog. "We're doing the long piece. The one with the trills. Everybody cries."

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock.

A macaw swept down in a blaze of red and blue, so bright the branch seemed to flower.

"Want to preen and gossip?" asked the macaw. "The vultures have split up. Again. I know everything."

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock.

The afternoon grew heavy and still. A boa constrictor poured himself along a limb, cool and slow as poured syrup.

"Want to ssssssneak up on eggssssss?" asked the boa.

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock. (She did, however, weave a very small bit faster, because there is only so much comfort a monkey can take from a snake asking about eggs.)

A hummingbird zipped up, hovered an inch from her ear, and asked her something at top speed, twice. Nobody has ever translated hummingbirds. Whatever it was, it sounded like a lovely idea and would have taken all afternoon.

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock.

By late afternoon a fat rain shower came and went, drumming its little fingers on the leaves, and the spider monkey hung her half-finished net over her head like an umbrella and wove on, damp and determined, while the whole jungle dripped and shone.

Then the wind began to stir, and the kapok leaves turned their silver sides up. The fig wind was coming. A jaguarundi slunk along the shadow beneath her branch, quick and low.

"Want to chase lizards?" asked the jaguarundi. "Fast ones. Mean ones. Fun ones."

The spider monkey didn't answer. She was very busy weaving her hammock.

She pulled the last knot tight. She tested the net with her whole weight, bouncing, swinging, hopping. It held. It was beautiful — a great silvery-green web of vine and tendril and monkey-craft, strung beneath the branch like a net beneath a tightrope walker.

And just then the fig wind blew.

It came up the valley with a sound like the jungle taking a deep breath, and it combed through the fig trees, and every ripe fig on every fig tree let go at once.

The figs fell. Plop, plop, plop — hundreds of them, a soft purple storm — bouncing off branches, rolling off leaves, dropping through the green gloom.

And into the hammock they fell. The net dipped and swayed and filled, figs rolling into its corners, until the spider monkey sat in the middle of the biggest supper any monkey had ever caught without moving an inch.

"WELL," said the howler, appearing at the edge of the branch with his mouth open.

"My goodness," said the toucan.

"Nice... net..." said the sloth, arriving just in time.

"Is that all for you?" asked the capybara.

The spider monkey looked at her full and beautiful hammock, and then at all the faces hanging around it — the howler, the toucan, the sloth, the capybara, the tree frog, the macaw, and even the jaguarundi, trying to look as if she had merely been passing by on lizard business.

And she laughed, and pulled one corner of the net down low, so the figs rolled out like a purple waterfall, and there were plenty, and then some, and then some more than that.

They ate as the sky went orange. The toucan flicked berries and caught some of them. The sloth reached a fig, eventually. The macaw told everyone about the vultures again. The boa ate an egg and nobody asked whose.

At last the moon came up, and an old owl drifted down out of the dark and landed on the branch above the net. She turned her head all the way around, the way owls casually do, and peered down at the woven wonder swinging gently beneath her.

"Whooo," said the owl, "has built this beautiful hammock?"

The spider monkey didn't answer.

She was fast asleep — swing, sway, swing — with her tail curled around her nose, dreaming fig-flavored dreams in the hammock she had woven all by herself.

And if you had asked her — at noon, mid-knot, mid-vine, too busy to lift her head — whether she'd missed a whole day of games and gossip and wallowing and song, she would have told you what she told the spider:

Missed it? No, no. She had been playing the longest game of all, the one where you say no all day to everything else, so that at the end of it, swinging in the dark with a full net and a full troop of friends, you get to say yes.

The capuchins, swinging by on their way home, would have understood at once. Monkey-in-the-middle, they would have told you, is easy.

Any monkey can be in the middle.

The trick is being the one who wove the middle.
