# Sevenleaves

*A parody of "Seveneves"*

The Great Mother Tree split without warning and without apparent reason.

Nobody saw it happen. The sentries on the night watch heard a crack they took for thunder, and in the morning the whole eastern sky was changed: the Mother Tree, the single vast trunk from which every troop in the world hung its woven history, stood broken — and where her crown had been one green continent, there now floated seven boughs, seven enormous sundered branches turning slowly in the wind, grinding at their fracture faces whenever the breeze pushed them together.

It was Doc Figby, the famous science macaque of the Royal Grove — a monkey who had spent nine lives explaining falling objects to the falling objects themselves — who climbed the highest spire with a spyglass and came down gray-faced.

"Every time two fracture faces kiss, they spall," he said, on every vine, in every grove, that same evening. "Splinters beget splinters. Chips beget chips. The cascade does not stop — it accelerates. In two years, perhaps three, the boughs will have ground themselves into a cloud, and that cloud will come down. They will call it the Hard Rain, and it will strip every leaf, every nest, every fruit and every monkey, from every tree in the world."

"Nonsense," said the elders. "The Mother Tree has stood for a hundred thousand generations."

"The Mother Tree," said Doc Figby gently, "is standing on borrowed time, and so, now, are we all. Branch out, or be buried."

The world did what worlds do: it went briefly and completely bananas. There were denialists, bough-truthers, prophets who declared the splitting a punishment for grooming sins. But there were also engineers, and engineers with two years are the most dangerous animals alive.

The plan came from Dinah MacQueerie, chief tool-maker of the Capuchin Guild, who had spent her life tending the Weaver — the great automatic loom in the Mother Tree's crown that spun raw bast into nest-cloth — and she put it in one sentence: "We cannot stop the rain, so we must outrun the sky." The Cloud Canopy Ark: a colossal woven raft-nest, to be built in the high mist above the storm-floors of the atmosphere by the Weaver and her sisters, provisioned with every seed, every cutting, every scrap of stored fruit-pulp the world could weave in, and launched — floated — before the first Hard Rain fell. Every grove that could weave a strand contributed one. The rescue was called the Big Braid, and it was the largest cooperative act in the history of primates, which is a low bar, but they cleared it by a mile.

Not everybody rose gracefully. President Julia Flufferty of the Western Grove — a monkey of magnificent fur and magnificent self-regard — delivered a farewell address to her starving constituents from the boarding platform of the Ark itself, having quietly seen to her own berth. Aloft, she set about "reorganizing" the Braid: pods were retitled, councils dissolved, loyalists fed first, until the captains of the weave, led by the swing-captain Ivy and the iron-fisted old marshal Tekla, put it to her plainly that the Ark held room for exactly one president of exactly nothing, and sealed her, with her faction, into a self-sufficient seed-pod and set it adrift in the high mist. It was a hard hour. Nobody apologized.

The Hard Rain began, as Doc Figby had promised, early.

The first stones came down like the fist of an angry sky. The Mother Tree's remnant trunk went in the first season. Then the orchards. Then the coast figs, the spine-fig groves, the terraces where the gibbons had sung for a million mornings — the songs just stopped, mid-phrase. Doc Figby stayed at his broadcast-vine longest of anyone, narrating the end of the world to an Ark that could not stop listening: "The northeast groves have gone... the river figs are burning... friends, if you can hear me: swing high, hold tight, remember everything." His last transmission cut off mid-sentence, in the way of all the best science macaques, still explaining.

And then the Ark's own arithmetic began. Mist-storms shredded whole clusters. The water-gourds froze and burst. A blight took the pulp-stores. Factions cracked off like boughs; some drifted and died politely, some did not go politely. Within four years, of the eight hundred who had risen, there remained, in the Hollow — a vast scarred seed-husk from the Mother Tree's own crown, riding the high mist — a remnant you could count on your paws and feet: and of the females, exactly seven of breeding age.

Sevenleaves, they would be called forever after: Dinah the tool-maker; Tekla the marshal; Moira the gene-wise bonobo; Aïda the brilliant, furious strategist; Camilla the gentle peacemaker; Louise the storyteller, who alone had memorized the world that was lost; and Julia Flufferty, the deposed president, hauled in at the last from her drifting seed-pod by a rope none of them ever stopped resenting.

There were no males who could father the future. But Moira had the twin-seed craft — the old bonobo discipline of stirring an egg to life with another egg — and so the seven of them, in the Hollow's lantern-light, made the decision for which the whole species would forever after be shaped: each would found her line, and each would choose, from everything known, what her daughters would carry. Tekla chose hardiness — dense fur, dense bone, patience like stone. Aïda chose brilliance, and bred it with avarice, to make the line that would never be anyone's fool again. Camilla chose kindness, outright, as a survival trait, which every other leaf predicted would fail and every other leaf was wrong about. Moira chose fertility and variation, wild throws of the dice. Louise chose memory: her daughters would be born knowing the old songs. Dinah chose clever hands. And Julia Flufferty, last and loudest, chose charm — the pelt, the voice, the talent for being adored — because, she said, "every troop needs a monkey to be talked about, or it forgets it is a troop."

Then they tied their names to the Hollow's spine-vine, and set about not dying.

The Seven Troops they founded grew strange and strong, the way isolated orchards do. The Teklan gorillas, shaggy as thunderclouds, kept the peace of the high mist. The Aïdan chimps ran the water-trade and most of the mischief. The Camillan bonobos healed feuds by the oldest bonobo method. The Moiran spider-monkeys threw out every kind of child, striped, spotted, and improbable. The Louisian gibbons sang the Old World back into being each dawn — grove by grove, fig by fig, none of it forgotten. The Dinan capuchins built, and built, and built. And the Julians — beautiful, unbearable — ran the councils, the theater, and the gossip, and somehow always the food got distributed.

Five thousand seasons after the splitting, the descendants of the seven came down at last through a sky grown gentle, to a world healed green and strange, and stood together on the soil of the Mother Tree's stump — a stump nine miles around, a meadow, a plain — and by long custom each troop brought a cutting from its founding mother's first orchard, and planted seven.

A young Dinan, asked afterward what it had felt like, said: "Like climbing down to a branch nobody had ever tested, and finding it held the whole troop after all."

And on the stump's rim, someone — the Louisians say it was one of theirs; the Julians claim the credit, as they claim everything — tied a single knot-vine, the old carbon-braid of the Ark, into a simple record in the ancient style, the one every nestling learns to read before it learns to swing:

*The Mother Tree split without warning. We were seven leaves on the wind. Now we are the tree.*
