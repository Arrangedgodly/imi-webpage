# The Thief of All Apes

*A parody of "The Thief of Always"*

Harvey Swick was ten, a chimp with a good heart and a bad case of February, and on the evening everything began he stood at his window and told the world how bored he was.

"I wish I were anywhere but here," he said. "February is a great grey beast that eats the year while nobody's looking. I wish — I *wish* —"

"You wish what, little buddy?" said a voice at the window.

There on the sill — of a second-floor window, mind you — sat a lemur. Not a zoo lemur, polite and sleepy, but a tall dapper one in a pinstripe coat, with a tail like a question mark and a grin that went from ear to ear and then kept going, far past where ears stop, until his whole face was grin.

"Name's Rictus," said the lemur, shaking Harvey's paw in both of his, which were cold. "I couldn't help hearing. You're *bored.* A bored boy is an emergency, and emergencies are my business. How'd you like a place where it's spring in the morning, summer at noon, Howl-o-ween at dusk, and Chimpmas every single night?"

"That's not a place," said Harvey. "That's not even a sensible week."

"It's a house," said Rictus, grinning wider. "Holiday House. And the mist is at your window right now."

And it was: a lake of mist lapping at the sill, and beyond it — Harvey could have sworn — birdsong and caramel. His parents were downstairs arguing about guttering. February was behind him like a beast.

Harvey climbed out. Of course he did. He was ten.

The mist took him like a current and set him down in sunshine, before Holiday House: towers and porches, a garden where apple blossom and pumpkins grew in the same hedge, every good smell he knew on the air — cut grass, candy, woodsmoke, rain, and banana bread, the one smell his mother never got right.

A boy about his age sat on the porch steps eating a candy cane: a heavy-lidded baboon called Wendell.

"You the new kid," said Wendell. "Rule one: don't ask where the days go. Rule two: there's no rule two. Mrs. Griffin's made breakfast."

Mrs. Griffin was a round old macaque with flour to the elbows and cats around her ankles like a spilled basket of yarn. "Welcome, dear," she said. "Eat up. You'll want your strength — today's a *big* day."

Every day was a big day. Morning smelled green: spring. Noon was swimming and ice cream off the pier: summer. As the light went gold they carved pumpkins and the house gave out candy with both hands: Howl-o-ween. And when full dark came, snow fell, and there were stockings, a tree to the ceiling, presents, carols: Chimpmas. Harvey went to bed deliriously happy, thinking: *every day. Every single day, forever.*

And so it was. And so it was. And so it was.

Harvey was not a fool, whatever his report card said, and after a while of mornings and pumpkins and stockings he began a private list of Questions.

One. Where did yesterday *go?* It didn't lie behind you, worn and put away, like days at home. It just — wasn't. Like a banana peeled and eaten in the dark.

Two. Why did the lake smell, some evenings, of dust?

Three. Why did Mrs. Griffin say "oh, ages, dear" in the voice monkeys use for *forever, and not by choice*?

Four. Who was Mr. Hood?

Mr. Hood owned the house — so said Rictus, grinning through the mist; grey cold Marr, the tall servant who never blinked enough; Mrs. Griffin, softly. But nobody ever saw him. The topmost tower's light burned behind its curtain, one yellow eye.

It was the fish that broke the spell. One morning they were all dead, floating, and Mrs. Griffin stood very still and said, "Not the fish again," and then, before Harvey could ask what *again* meant, "Never you mind, dear," with flour on her cheek and tears in her fur, and buried them under a stone already crowded with little markers.

That night Harvey looked out by moonlight and saw the markers, hundreds of them, row on row, every one carved with the same word. Not names. Not dates:

*TIME. TIME. TIME. TIME.*

"Rictus," said Harvey quietly, "I want to go home."

The grin appeared in the window before the face did — never trust that. "Home?" said Rictus. "Buddy. Pal. It's Chimpmas Eve."

"Every night is Chimpmas Eve. That's the problem. I'd rather be bored and *real.*"

"Ooh," said Rictus, grinning his terrible grin, "the Master won't like that. He *never* lets a guest —"

But Harvey was already running, out the porch, past the pumpkin hedge, into the mist, which took him roughly this time — and dropped him, gasping, on the street outside his own house, where his mother was hanging curtains.

She dropped the curtain rod and screamed for his father. They wept; they hugged him; they rang the police and the newspapers. Because — Harvey worked it out slowly, the way you work out a bruise — he had been gone one night by his own fur.

He had been gone a year.

There were lines on his mother's face that had not been there in February. There had been search parties. His father had gone quietly grey about the muzzle. Harvey felt something go cold in his chest that no fire in any Holiday House could warm.

*It steals,* he thought. *It stole a year of us to dress its stages. It peels whole days off the world's banana and eats them in the dark. And Wendell's still there. And Mrs. Griffin, who cried over fish, is still there.*

"That's the last time," said his father that night, holding him like a monkey holding the last fruit of a hard season. "You never go near that mist again, you hear me?"

"I hear you," said Harvey.

And the very next evening, when the mist came creeping up the street, Harvey Swick swung his legs over his windowsill and went back.

Rictus found him on the lawn, his grin, for the first time, cracked down the middle. "You came *back?* Nobody comes back. Marr! MARR! We got a —"

"Calm down," said Harvey, walking past him. "I've come to see Mr. Hood."

The curtain of the tower room drew back at a touch. Inside, in a rocking chair, in lamplight, sat Mr. Hood — and Harvey's stomach performed a slow cold somersault: Mr. Hood was small. Shrunken. A moth-eaten elder ape of no particular species, folded in his chair like a letter nobody had ever sent.

"You know what I am," said Hood, and his voice was the whole house — the Chimpmas bells, the Howl-o-ween wind, the lake. "Say it, boy."

"You're the Thief of All Apes," said Harvey. "You steal our time and pour it into your stages, and everyone stays happy and nobody grows, and the fish die of it, and Mrs. Griffin buries TIME in the garden."

"Menials," said Hood, with terrible gentleness. "I offer *always.* No February. No grey beast. My secret, Harvey Swick? Time takes everything. Every monkey ever born gets robbed blind by morning. I am simply the one ape in the world who robs *back.* Join me. Make a wish. Anything. I'll grant it, and every day after, forever."

And because it was a trap, and because he was ten and had a good heart, Harvey wished.

"I wish for the dawn."

Hood blinked. "Which dawn, boy? I *make* the mornings here. You're asking for the real one."

"The real one. First one."

Hood granted it — he had to — and real morning broke over Holiday House for the first time in a hundred years, grey and honest and low, and under real light the pumpkin hedge withered, and Hood cried out as though the wish had cost him an arm.

"Again!" he snarled, rising out of the chair, swelling, a giant of stolen time, floors shaking. "Wish AGAIN, boy, I'll bury you in dawns —"

"You're not a giant," said Harvey, and every one of Mrs. Griffin's buried stones rang like a bell. "You're a thief in a pelt, and I wish you EMPTY."

Hood roared and reached for always — and every hour he'd ever stolen came out of him at once, a hundred years of mornings pouring home. The house couldn't hold them: lake water burst up through the lawns, stage-curtains ripped, and the great grey thief of all apes folded and folded, an old letter, unsent, unsendable — and was gone.

They found Mrs. Griffin in the ruined kitchen, holding her cats, young no longer — old, free, finished.

"It's all right, dear," she said, seeing Harvey's face. "I've been owed a rest since 1907. Mind the garden on your way out. It's only seeds now — plain, honest seeds. That's what time is, dear. Soil, and seeds, and mornings."

Harvey and Wendell walked out through the last of the mist as the real sun rose.

February came round again the next year, of course. It always does. But Harvey Swick stood at his window in the flat grey light, watching the mist not come, and thought he heard — far off, maybe — a grin, closing.

"Come if you like," he told the dusk. "But I know your name now, and I know the trick, and there are ten thousand mornings ahead, and I'm going to be in *every single one.*"

And he was.
