# Raising Screams

*A parody of "Raising Steam"*

Dick Simian's father had died making steam. Boilers were like that: honest servants, terrible masters. Most monkeys who lose a father to an explosion develop a sensible aversion. Dick Simian developed an obsession, plus a burn scar, plus, by the age of thirty, an engine.

He called her Iron Liana, and she was — her first admirers said — a hundred tons of whistle, wheel and worry, capable of dragging a loaded branch of banana trees uphill through deep mud while the whole of Ankh-Morpork held its ears.

"She's not magic, you understand," Dick told the crowds, stoking her lovingly. "That's the point. She's better than magic. She works every time."

In Ankh-Morpork, this sentence constitutes fighting words, and the Patrician — a slender siamang of iron courtesy named Havelock Vetinari — dispatched his favorite reformed criminal to investigate. This was Moist von Lipwig, a capuchin in a golden suit who had once been a con artist and was now, by the tireless application of the Patrician's dog-collared logic, Postmaster General, chairman of the Royal Bank, and the city's official turner-of-loose-cannons-into-cannons-pointed-where-he-wanted.

"Congratulations, Mr. Lipwig," said Vetinari. "You are now chairman of the Ankh-Morpork and Sto Plains Hygienic Railway."

"I don't know anything about railways!"

"An excellent qualification. The last chairman who knew about railways married one. Dick Simian builds them; you will make monkeys believe in them, and I say this with confidence because you have made them believe in stamps."

Money was provided by Harry Kong, the King of the Golden River — a vast, jovial gorilla who had risen from the gutters by the simple creed that one monkey's trash is Harry Kong's treasure, and who now found himself, to his eternal astonishment, respectable, rich, and railway-mad.

"You mean to say," said Harry Kong, watching Iron Liana howl past with thirty carriages, "that she'll do it again tomorrow? And the day after? Without miracles?"

"Every day," said Dick Simian. "Twice on Sundays."

"Son," said Harry Kong, wiping away a tear with a banknote, "take my money. Take all of it."

And so the age of raising screams began — for that is what the first commuters called it, when the whistle shrieked across the Sto Plains and monkeys came running from miles around just to be terrified on schedule. The line to Quirm opened to great ceremony. Farmers timed their crops by the two o'clock scream. A generation of young gibbons hung off the footbridges waving at the footplate, dreaming of oil in their fur and far places at the end of a branch line. The clacks towers — the old semaphore web that had been the fastest thing in the world for thirty years — watched the new iron lianas swing past below them and, for the first time, felt slow.

Rapid progress, of course, offends somebody. In this case it offended the grags: deep-down pygmy marmosets of the old rock, sworn to the antique ways, who held that no true dwarf — sorry, no true marmoset — should ride above ground at forty miles an hour behind a screaming kettle. A hard core of them, led by a cold-eyed grag named Ardent, went beyond theology into chemistry, and clacks towers began to burn across the plains, and then a culvert collapsed under Iron Liana's sister engine, and Dick Simian stopped sleeping.

It was Adora Belle Dearheart — Moist's wife, a galago with a cigarette permanently installed and the collective bargaining instincts of a landslide — who spotted the next move. "It's not the railway they hate," she said, blowing smoke at the map. "It's the everything. Ardent means to have his little apocalypse in Uberwald. They're moving against the Low King."

The Low King of the marmosets, Rhys Rhysson, stood for the deep-downs' future rather than their longings, and for this Ardent's faction meant to replace him with a king made entirely of tradition, by force, in the dark, tonight.

"Now that," said Vetinari, who had of course arranged to be in the room, "would be a tragedy, and also an inefficiency. The Low King supports the railway. Therefore the railway shall support the Low King. Mr. Lipwig: be in Uberwald by tomorrow."

"It's eight hundred miles."

"Yes. Take the train."

What followed entered railway legend as the Great Uberwald Run, and goblin — sorry, tarsier — folk songs still get the numbers wrong about it.

There were no rails through the Kneck Valley. There were rails being laid, by tarsier crews working under Dick Simian's furious instruction, thirty feet of fresh track at a time, sleepers swinging down out of the dark like the beat of some enormous patient heart. The tarsiers — huge-eyed, gentle, and for centuries dismissed by city monkeys as vermin — had found on the railway the first honest work that came with the word "mate" attached, and they laid track like monkeys repaying a debt to their own grandchildren.

Iron Liana ran the lowlands at a shriek, her firebox glowing like judgment. At the foothill junction she was swapped — Dick wept, briefly, like a groom — onto the great mountain engine Bitter Wind, her boiler superheated, her trucks stripped to iron and lanterns. Moist rode the footplate in his golden suit, now mostly soot, feeding coal with the aristocracy of the truly terrified, while behind him, in a locked and armored carriage, rode the diamond-dog soldiers of the Low King's loyalists, playing cards and grinning at the windows.

The grags had blown the pass at Kneck. Bitter Wind took the emergency cut through the forest, laid in some places that very afternoon over frozen ground, at speeds no engine had been certified to survive, with the scream of her whistle set loose down every valley like the news itself arriving ahead of the news.

And when she burst, roaring, out of the pine dark into the torchlit snow of the Schmaltzberg concourse, with the Low King's banner lit by her firebox and Moist von Lipwig leaning out into the freezing air with his hair blown back like a golden banner of inevitability, the coup collapsed like a soufflé in a slammed oven. It is one thing to whisper in the dark that the old ways must be defended, and quite another to look up and see the future arriving at sixty miles an hour with its brights on.

Ardent was found the next morning, in a tunnel, attempting to argue theology with a piston. He lost.

The Low King was reseated before breakfast. Dick Simian was made a dwarf — sorry, an honorary marmoset, which by the deep-downs' own laws made him the size of a very confident ox. Harry Kong wept again, and invested in everything. The tarsiers got a province, a pension, and, within the decade, their own engine works, whose gleaming engines are the envy of three continents and smell faintly of well-earned respect.

And Iron Liana? She was rebuilt, of course, as they all are — new boiler, bigger wheels, a whistle with a trill in it — until even Dick Simian had to admit there wasn't a bolt of the original left, and yet she was still, unmistakably, her. Some monkeys said she'd become a little bit alive. Dick knew better than to argue. He just kept her fed.

On the day the line reached the far coast, Vetinari rode in the front carriage, watching the world swing past at speed, and said to Moist, in the tone of a monkey confirming a bet already won:

"You know, I have never entirely trusted the future. It has always seemed to me to arrive unaccompanied by evidence. But it appears it was merely waiting for a timetable."

Moist, watching the wires of the new clacks-and-rail relay flashing past, keeping pace, said nothing. He was doing the job in front of him, which was the whole trick, and it had taken him from the gallows to the footplate of a new world without once letting him off for good behavior.

The scream of the evening train went up over the plains, and ten thousand monkeys set their watches by it, and got on with the future.

All aboard. Next branch, everywhere.
