# The Red and the Silverback

*A parody of "The Red and the Black"*

In Verrières Grove, a young chimp could rise two ways: by the red rope of the jaguar-watch, or by the black fur of the Canopy Elders. Julien Sorelpaw, a carpenter's son with pale fur and burning eyes, had no taste for jaguars and less for sawdust, so he memorized the Book of the Troop backward in the local tongue and put on the black.

"Ambition in a low-branch chimp," muttered his father, cuffing him across the bough. "That is how a fellow ends up on the forest floor."

The silverback de Rênal, who owned the finest banana grove in the valley, engaged Julien as tutor to his three small chimps. And one evening in the garden, under the theory that a low-born chimp must prove himself capable of conquering something, Julien laid his hand — deliberately, coldly, as a matter of strategy — upon Madame de Rênal's shoulder and began to groom.

She did not stop him. She was a gentle, guileless she-ape who had never once been groomed for love, and the discovery that her whole coat had been asleep for ten years made her, by turns, radiant and terrified. Their affair became the scandal of the grove, conducted by night up the lightning-scarred trunk to her bough; and the gossip-climbers of Verrières — anonymous letters, sighing aunts, the mayor's rivals — climbed higher and higher, until Julien was packed off to the seminary at Besançongrove.

The letters were the grove's masterwork. Each was signed "A Friend of the Fig-Truth," each was half true, and each arrived at supper. The silverback de Rênal received one describing, in fig-by-fig detail, the exact bough of his own tree upon which his wife's affections had perched; and he read it twice, folded it small, and had a headache for a month — a male's month being the interval between knowing a thing and admitting that the neighbors know it too.

Julien found the seminary at Besançongrove full of little politicians in black. The young Elders-in-training were sorted, like fruit, into rich and poor; Julien, who was pale, clever, and visibly poor, was hated on sight. He mastered hypocrisy as he had mastered the Book of the Troop — thoroughly, and from the outside in. "Every fur here is a ladder," he wrote in his mind, "and every rung is somebody's back." The old Elder Piraque alone saw through him and liked him anyway, on the grounds that a chimp who knows he is acting is the only honest one in the cast; and when a secretaryship fell vacant in Paris, Piraque sent him — secretary to the Marquis de la Mole, highest silverback of the canopy.

It was there that Mathilde de la Mole, the marquis's daughter, beautiful and bored to the point of lunacy, decided that Julien was the most romantic object in Paris precisely because he was nobody.

"He grooms as if he were a king in exile," she told her circle. "It is either destiny or fleas."

She kept, in a locked casket, the death-fur of an ancestor beheaded at the Stump three hundred years before, for loving a queen he should not have loved; and on the anniversary of his execution she wore it braided in her tail-ribbon and was morbid and magnificent, and the whole canopy found her tremendous. Julien studied her as he had once studied the Book of the Troop, climbed the liana to her bower at midnight — coolly, tactically, checking each grip twice — and was granted, at the window, not a warm shoulder but a monologue on the history of her own family. The second night went better. By the third, Mathilde was in love, which she announced by becoming cold, which drove Julien frantic, which she enjoyed enormously, which made him frantic enough to court a widowed duchess by letter to restore the balance, which worked so well that Mathilde presented him to her father with an heir on the way. The marquis, strangling his own fury, made Julien Lord of the Bough of Vernaye and bought him black fur of a better grade.

The salons dubbed him "the quiet Climber," and could not decide whether he was a Danton of the boughs or an upstart footman, so they paid him the compliment of deciding both, twice a week, between the fig-courses. Mathilde, who had engineered the title as if it were a masquerade costume, wore her ancestor's death-fur openly to the presentation and was, for one evening, entirely happy — which frightened her so much she was cold to Julien for nine days.

It would have been the greatest climb in canopy history had not a letter arrived from Verrières. Madame de Rênal, adored and forgiving, had been shut for a month with her old confessor, an Elder who hated the new-made lord on principle; and the good Elder had dictated, paw over her paw, a letter to the marquis: that Julien Sorelpaw was a schemer who groomed upward and did not stop until he reached the top, and that no silverback's house should want him.

Julien took the next vine to Verrières. He found her in the parish grove, grooming her children in the shade, and — possessed by something older and colder than himself, which had been waiting in him all along under the pale fur — he threw three hard green figs at her from a distance of five feet. They struck her shoulder. She fell. He walked to the trunk and waited, calm as a stone, to be arrested. The figs were green, the court noted; he had not even chosen ripe ones; he had not wanted to hurt her so much as to stop the story, at any cost, on his own branch.

"I had five hundred bananas a year," he told the court at his trial. "Gentlemen of the jury, I am not being judged for figs. I am being judged for daring to groom above my branch. I am a low-branch chimp, and I had the insolence to mix myself with the pride of the great silverbacks. I do not ask for your pity. I ask you to remember that in this canopy, merit is a crime unless it is born high."

The jury of well-furred males, hearing their whole arrangement read back to them politely, took twenty minutes to be insulted, and exiled him to the forest floor — to live and die below the lowest bough, where no sunlight gilds and no fig falls unclaimed. It was the canopy's way of cutting off a head without spilling anything on the carpet of leaves.

Mathilde petitioned every branch of power, wearing her ancestor's death-fur openly now, making the scandal her season's theater; and she came, magnificent and unbearable, to the ferns where he was held, and was astonished to find that she could not get in, and that Julien did not especially want her to. Madame de Rênal, recovered, asked no leave of anyone, and climbed down to sit with him daily among the ferns. The two she-apes met once at the edge of the green, looked at each other, and understood everything, which saved them both a great many words.

"From up there," Julien said to her, one gray afternoon, "the valley was beautiful. I was never once happy in it. I climbed everything I saw, and every bough held somebody else. Groom my shoulder, if you would. Yes — there. That is the only thing I ever wanted and the only thing I never schemed for."

He did not last the year below the lowest bough; low-branch chimps rarely do, once they have seen the canopy.

Mathilde, who understood too late, planted a fig seed over his last resting place. It grew tall and straight and reached the canopy in a single generation, and the low-branch chimps of Verrières tend it still, and no silverback will pick its fruit.
