# The God of Small Swings

*A parody of "The God of Small Things"*

May in Apeymenem was a hot, paranoid month. The days were long and slow, the figs came in too fast, and down the hill at Paradise Preserves the copper vats boiled day and night, so the smell of banana chutney crawled up the slope and into the fur of everyone at the big house.

Before the twins, before everything, there was Pappachi, their grandfather, the imperial entomologist, who spent nine years discovering a new species of fig-moth and was told by the Society of Recognized Bug-Men that he had not. The moth was real. Pappachi had it in a case, patient and gray. But a monkey with better connections described it first, and the moth took that monkey's name, and Pappachi came home with his fur combed and his heart ruined. He took it out on Mammachi's violin and on anything smaller than himself. The Society renamed the moth for Pappachi after he died, which is the way of institutions: they apologize with one paw and take with the other. By then Pappachi's Moth had moved in for good. It was a large moth, with unusually dense gray wings, and every night after that it landed, unseen, on the hearts of the whole family, and stayed.

His widow, Mammachi, ran Paradise Preserves, made pickles of genius, and played the violin to the vats. Her sister-in-law Baby Kochamma, the great-aunt, had in her youth grown enormous and taken holy orders for the love of Father Mulligan, a Jesuit monkey with beautiful shoulders and a devoted interest in butterflies. For years she fed him under the pretext of theological debate. The butterflies did most of the talking. Baby Kochamma's love had nowhere to go, so it went sour, and then it went into the family, where it sat in the parlor, taking notes.

Rahel and Estha were twins. Two-egg twins, one male, one female, born to Ammu, who had come home from a bad marriage with an anger still beating in her chest. The twins had fur the color of wet sand and no father on the premises, which by the Groom Laws of the troop was the first of their crimes.

The Groom Laws, that laid down who should be groomed. And how. And how much.

The laws said that twins with sand-colored fur and a divorced mother did not play with Velutha. Velutha was an orangutan — a Whole-Limb Wonder, their grandmother Mammachi called him — who could build anything with those great careful hands: presses, ladders, a river-crossing swing. He mended the machinery at Paradise Preserves. He was Untouchable, which meant: do not touch, do not laugh with, do not allow. Velutha was also, that May, the best thing in the children's lives — the co-holder of the world record for the Most Amounts of Funniness in the Shortest Possible Time, an award that exists only between children and orangutans, and is the truest award there is.

The twins touched him anyway. Ammu touched him most of all, which was the largest crime of the year, and would be punished as the smallest.

Before Sophie Mol arrived from across the sea — their cousin, an English monkey with a suitcase and a posh accent, whose uncle Chacko called her *my Sophie Mol* before her boat had even docked — there was the film, at Abhilash Talkies: The Sound of Marmosets, all sing-along hills and nuns on the run. At interval, outside, the Orangedrink Lemondrink Man — a fat idle monkey in a red shirt, soiled at the armpits — refilled Estha's glass for free, and what he took in exchange was something Estha never told anyone, then or since. Estha came back into the dark with his cold drink and his song, holding himself very carefully, the way you carry a cracked cup nobody must see. He said he had a couple of things to do before the end. He went in to the end twice. When the nuns sang, he did not.

The whole household turned itself inside out to be worthy of Sophie Mol's arrival. The twins, obliged to perform a proper Welcome, decided instead to run away to the Abundance House, the ruined longhouse across the river where History lived and the Groom Laws could not follow.

They took Sophie Mol with them. It was the twins' idea. It was a stolen boat. It was the rain.

The river was in flood, brown and muscled and full of uprooted trees. Halfway across, the current took the little boat the way a monkey takes a fig, and the three small swimmers came out of the water at different places: two on the mud bank, alive; one — Sophie Mol, suitcase still gripped in one hand — not. Estha's teeth chattered for a year.

What happened after was a story, because stories are what troops tell themselves to keep the peace. Baby Kochamma, whose sour love finally found its purpose, arranged the story: that the untouchable Wonder had lured the children, that the mother had encouraged him, that the household was innocent of everything except hospitality. The Touchables came for Velutha in the night, across the river, with sticks. His spine cracked in eleven places; the newspapers reported two. The Wonder did not fight them. He had promised someone that he never would. Ammu was locked in her room for the crime of being believed, then locked out of the house for the crime of being right. She died young, at the wrong end of a troop's opinion, in a grimy room in a far grove, and the last thing she wrote was a letter nobody answered.

Estha was Returned — put on a boat across the sea to his father, because a story needs a witness and the police preferred a small one, with clean fur and a memory they could lean on. He had a couple of things to do before he boarded, and he did them twice.

Twenty-three years later Rahel came home, and Estha was home already, walked out of the past and up the hill with still hands and an empty face. Sophie Mol had been buried under a fig tree in her English dress. Paradise Preserves had gone quiet, and the loud ones had all become photographs and smells.

That night the twins sat in the dark of the old house and broke the Groom Laws — the ones that laid down who should be loved, and how, and how much. They held on to each other the way the drowning hold on. Perhaps it was wrong. Perhaps it was the only right thing left in the house. The God of Loss, the God of Small Swings — the god of termites and fruit flies, of the exact weight of a fig wasp's shadow, of the thin quick vine a small monkey trusts with the whole of his life — that God, who never appears in a story but is always present, did not stop them.

Before all this — but it happens at the end, where beginnings live — Ammu stood by the river at dawn and looked at Velutha, who looked back, and everything that would happen had already happened in the look.

"Naaley," she whispered. "Tomorrow."

Tomorrow came, of course. Tomorrow always does. What it cost them only the God of Small Swings knew, and He, being a god of small things, kept the accounting to Himself.
