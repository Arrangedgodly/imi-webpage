# The Book of Trees

*A parody of "The Book of Three"*

In the banyan of Caer Dallben, in the middle of the grove called Prydain, there lived an orangutan so old that moss grew on his shoulders. His name was Dallben, and he kept two things: The Book of Trees, which told of everything that had ever happened and some things that hadn't yet, and a pig named Hen Wen, who was — though she'd never admit it — oracular.

Taran, Assistant Banana-Keeper, wanted neither books nor prophecies. He wanted glory.

"I'd rather shape my own destiny," he announced, peeling his hundredth banana of the morning, "than peel another bushel!"

"You are shaping it," said Coll, the stout old warrior-turned-farmer. "Destiny starts with chores. Finish the bushel."

That afternoon the sky blackened with ravens — servants of the Horned Howler, the skull-masked terror whose shrieking heralds brought Arawn's war to the grove — and Hen Wen shrieked one of her rare, terrible prophecies and bolted into the forest. Taran chased her, tripped over a root, and met the tallest silverback he'd ever seen: Prince Gwydion, sword at his hip, carrying himself like a tree that had learned manners.

They were ambushed that night. Gwydion was knocked senseless, Taran was bonked for good measure, and when he woke it was inside a spiral burial mound, face to face with a chattering young spider-monkey princess holding a glowing golden ball.

"I'm Eilonwy," she said. "This is my bauble. It lights things. Also, I'm leaving through this tunnel. You may carry the sword I found."

"I don't think you should take things from burial mounds," said Taran.

"I don't think you should *lecture* a princess in a burial mound," said Eilonwy — and it turned out the sword she'd idly picked up was Dyrnwyn, the blade of legend, whose edge flickered with white flame when drawn by a monkey of true worth, and flickered not at all for Taran, which they agreed not to discuss.

In the corridor above, a tall enchantress named Achren smiled at Taran the way a spider offers a seat. "Stay," she said. "Gwydion is lost, and Annuvin has openings for brave young monkeys. I can make you a prince." Eilonwy, who was immune to glamour and most manners, whacked the ceiling with her bauble, and in the dark and the din they ran.

They escaped the mound — Eilonwy negotiating with the dead about lighting, Taran carrying things, which he would later realize was the theme — and fell in with Gurgi, a shaggy, gap-toothed howler-lad with a wicker satchel, who offered them "crunchings and munchings" every forty paces, and with Fflewddur Flam, a bard vervet whose harp-strings snapped whenever he fibbed.

"Thousands attend my concerts!" *(twang)* "Dozens, then. It was a very exclusive fig."

In the high hills they found Medwyn's valley, hidden from everything with a beak or a grudge, where wounded animals went to mend — eagles and serpents and one very embarrassed wolf drinking from the same spring. Taran wanted to stay forever, safe at the world's warm taproot.

"You have a path of your own," said Medwyn, a shepherd gorilla old as weather. "It goes through the thorns. That's how you tell it from the others."

Before he left, Taran splinted the wing of a gwythaint fledgling — one of the Horned Howler's own carrion birds, all talon and sulk. "A mistake, a great mistake, oh yes, yes," Gurgi warned. It was not the last mistake to prove otherwise.

Their road ran next into the hill of the Fair Folk, and an audience with King Eiddileg, a fretful marmoset monarch who complained about surface monkeys for an hour while feeding them continuously, then charged them for the meal at rates compounded since the reign of somebody unpronounceable. There, in the royal orchard — safe, smug, and mid-snack — was Hen Wen. Doli the dwarf capuchin, whose dearest wish was to master invisibility, was assigned as guide, and spent the journey achieving it at moments of maximum uselessness.

"I'm invisible," Doli announced, from somewhere.

"That's wonderful," said Eilonwy. "Could you be visible near the cliff, and invisible only when we'd prefer to be surprised?"

On the last leg of the road a Cauldron-Born patrol found them in the dark. Taran took a sword-cut across the shoulder and would have been carried off to Annuvin, except that a battered, mud-colored shape rose out of the reeds and taught the patrol the difference between dead and determined. It was Gwydion — alive, furious, and approximately thirty percent bandage.

By the Marshes of Morva, where they were otherwise hopelessly lost, they found the three ancient sakis of the bogs, Orddu, Orwen, and Orgoch, who mended Gwydion's ribs and Taran's shoulder with bog-myrtle and bad advice, and traded them a piece of wisdom for a piece of Taran's pride (a poor exchange rate for Taran, but there it was):

"The Horned Howler cannot be felled by iron," said Orddu, knitting.

"Nor by fury," said Orwen, knitting.

"He dies when his true name is spoken," said Orgoch, "which is why he never mentions it."

The battle came anyway. Cauldron-Born warriors swarmed out of Annuvin — the dead who smelled of turned earth and nothing else — and the Horned Howler himself rode the storm at their head, antlered skull gleaming, shrieking so loud that fruit fell unripe from the trees.

"I have fought in a hundred battles!" cried Fflewddur, joining the shield wall. *(twang)* "Skirmishes," he corrected, ducking.

Taran stood his ground at Caer Dathyl with a kitchen knife and a plan that mainly consisted of "not running." Beside him, Gurgi — who was afraid of everything — stood on the wall with a stick, guarding the doorway where Taran stood, crunching and munching very loudly to keep his courage fed. And that is when Gwydion returned, silver-furred and terrible, and stood beneath the monster's mask. Beside Taran, Coll of the farm arrived with a hoe and dropped an entire rank of Cauldron-Born with it. "A spade is just a sword that pays rent," he said, embarrassed.

And out of the shrieking sky came one gwythaint — splinted wing, bad attitude — to tear at the Howler's antlers and drag the skull-mask sideways.

"You have hidden it well," said Gwydion to the monster, over the storm. "But I found it, in the deep places of the Book of Trees. You have one name, and it has never been *Horned*."

He whispered it.

The Howler burst like an overripe jackfruit. (The name was — no. Some things stay in the marsh. Suffice to say, it was a very small, very silly name, and he had been teased about it as a young fiend.)

The Cauldron-Born crumbled, Hen Wen came home in triumph, and at Caer Dallben, Dallben let Taran look into the Book of Trees. The battle was there, freshly inked. The next page was blank.

"I found no crown," Taran admitted. "I mostly got kidnapped and carried things."

"You found your friends, kept your word, and stood between evil and a banana grove," said Dallben. "The Book of Trees is only beginnings, boy. It says nothing of endings, because endings belong to monkeys who do the chores."

Taran went back to the bushel.

But — crunching and munching — Gurgi stayed to help.
