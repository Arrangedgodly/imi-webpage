# Malibaboon Rising

*A parody of "Malibu Rising"*

It was the last weekend of August, 1984, and the whole coast knew what that meant: the Rivas were having their party.

Everyone in Malibaboon knew the Rivas. Malibaboon was a small town wearing a big city's bikini, a colony of the beautiful strung along twenty-one miles of sand, and the Rivas were its royalty.

The house sat above the point at the end of a private road, a low white shrine of redwood and glass that their father had bought in 1962 for eleven thousand dollars, back when he was the biggest singing baboon in America. He was mostly gone by 1965. The house stayed. Houses are better at staying than fathers are.

There were four of them now. Nina, twenty-six, the eldest, the famous surfer with the face that sold the swimsuits — she had put her husband through tennis stardom the way you put a coin through a machine, and this spring the machine had spat him back with a pop star on his arm. Jay, twenty-three, all shoulders and salt, the best young surfer nobody sponsored, because Jay surfed angry and everyone could tell. Hud, twenty-two, the photographer, the calm one, whose dark fur set him a shade apart from his siblings in a way everyone had quietly done the math on. And Kit, nineteen, the baby, the knife-eyed one, who worked the counter at the taco stand with Nina and said less per day than the parrot in the window.

They had one rule, inherited like the house: on the last Saturday in August, everyone is welcome. Whatever you did at the party, you did not do it to the family. And no matter what happened, the Rivas cleaned up their own mess in the morning.

By noon, Nina was picking limes on the point and telling herself the thing she told herself every year.

"You don't have to do this," said Kit, from the porch rail.

"Yes, I do."

"Why?"

Because her mother, June, had thrown parties in this same kitchen for a man who never came, and every year Nina swore she'd cancel it, and every year she lit the tiki torches instead, and every year it felt like carrying her mother's tray.

"Because," said Nina, "I'm the eldest."

"That's not a reason," said Kit. "That's a roster position."

---

**By 7:00 p.m.,** the party had made landfall. Two hundred baboons on the bluff, the biggest troop in the county: bandits in beach wraps, two Mick Riva tribute acts arguing over a tambourine. Mick's old records spun on the stereo — "Silver Nana," "Moon Over Malibaboo" — and the whole crowd sang along to a father's voice while drinking his children's liquor.

**By 9:00 p.m.,** Jay was on his fourth beer and his first mistake. He'd seen Ashley arrive — Ashley, his girlfriend of one year, surfer, sun-streaked — and he'd seen her scan the party the way you scan a beach for your towel, and find it, and it was not Jay. It was Hud, by the bonfire, and Hud looked back at her with a whole year of his own in his eyes.

"Tell me I'm wrong," said Jay, appearing between them like weather.

"You're not wrong," said Hud, who never lied; it was the calm that made it worse. "We've been together since June. I was going to tell you tonight."

"You wanted to tell me *at a party*?"

"I wanted to tell you *somewhere you couldn't leave*," said Hud. "Like a brother."

Jay looked at Ashley. He looked at his brother. Two hundred baboons partied behind him and the ocean did what the ocean does, which is whatever it wants.

"The house rules," Jay said finally, and his voice came out sandpaper. "Whatever we do, we don't do it to the family. So I'm going to walk down to the point, and I'm going to be back for the dawn patrol, and you two are going to be the ones who tell Nina, because I'm done delivering news."

**By 10:30 p.m.,** Kit was on the stairs doing tequila with Casey from the taco stand — Casey, with a laugh like a wave cracking, who had spent the summer being Kit's "best friend" in a tone that fooled no one at the counter. Casey said, "If I kiss you and it's weird, I get Sundays off." Kit said, "If it's weird, I'm keeping Sundays." It was not weird.

**By 11:00 p.m.,** Nina found the letter in the mailbox, the one she'd been dodging since spring, forwarded twice — a note on hotel stationery in the big looping paw of Mick Riva himself: *Kiddo. I hear you're having the party. I'd like to come. I'd like to finally meet my kids. — Dad.*

She stood with it in her fist until Hud took it from her and read it, and then the two of them stood at the window, and Jay came up from the point, and the Rivas convened in the kitchen over the tray, in the smell of lime and the ghost of their mother — June, who had waited at that window so many years that she'd worn a groove in herself doing it, who drank, and whose heart returned the favor and quit in 1979.

"He's here," said Nina quietly. "He's at the party. He came anyway. He came in through the crowd like a normal guest."

**By 11:30 p.m.,** Mick Riva stood in his own former living room, sixty and lacquered, holding court by the fireplace with a story about the Mamas and the Papas, and three of his children watched him from the kitchen door.

"Second verse," said Nina, and walked in.

The room noticed. The room always noticed Nina.

"Hi, Dad." She let the word sit like a splinter. "You're in my house."

"It's my house, technically," said Mick, with the smile that had sold forty million records.

"You signed it over in 1975 for tax reasons. So no. You're a guest, and guests get one drink." She took the glass out of his famous paw. "You have four kids in this room. You've met two."

"Kiddo—"

"Don't 'kiddo' me. We got by. Mom didn't, but we did. And the only thing you ever gave us that was worth anything is this house and this party, and tonight both of them are full of two hundred monkeys who love us — love *us*, not the voice, not the mane — and you're not going to stand here with your tambourine stories and take that too. You can watch the sunrise from the cliff. Public property. You're good at leaving before morning anyway. Consider it a reunion."

**By 1:00 a.m.,** the party had thinned to the brave and the broken. And it was 1:04 when the lamp went over in the study — a guest, a coat, a frayed cord, the old redwood dry as a drum — and the fire went up the curtain like it had been waiting twenty years for the invitation.

**By 1:20 a.m.,** the bluff was orange. Two hundred baboons stood on the sand watching the Riva house burn, and the four Rivas stood at the rail, holding one another's shoulders, doing the family's arithmetic: everyone out, the dog out, the photographs out, the guitars out, everything else belonging to the fire.

Nina watched the roof her mother had swept go up. She waited to grieve. What arrived instead was lighter.

"Shouldn't we do something?" said Casey.

"It's wood," said Kit. "It was always just wood."

Jay put his arm around Hud. "You and Ashley," he said. "You tell Nina yet?"

"We told Nina," said Hud. "Nina says the family's got a habit of falling in love at the worst possible moment."

"The family's got a habit of *surviving* the worst possible moment," said Nina. "Samefur, different coat."

Mick Riva came up the beach with a bucket, of all things, an aging king with a bucket, and none of his children turned him away and none of them spoke to him, and at some point he understood that this was the conversation, all of it, and he set the bucket down in the wet sand and walked north up the point, and that was the last any of them ever saw him, except on the radio.

---

At dawn the house was a black smile against the hillside, and the Riva siblings did what Rivas do in the morning: they cleaned up, the beach picked to the last bottle cap. Then Jay said, "Dawn patrol," because it was Sunday, and the four of them — and Casey, and Ashley, because the troop had widened by two — ran down to the water with boards under their arms, past the crowd of gawkers, into the surf that belonged to no Riva and no bank.

Malibaboon is a place, not a promise. It takes what it likes — houses, fathers, afternoons — and leaves what it likes, which is the light, the waves, and whoever paddles out anyway.

Nina sat up on her board past the break, the wreckage of the whole summer somewhere behind her on the bluff, and felt the swatch of it lift off her fur.

"You okay?" Kit paddled alongside.

"I'm good," said Nina, and meant it for the first time in a year. "I'm free, actually."

She caught the next wave all the way in, standing tall on water that had no memory of mortgages, and the troop in the foam hollered like baboons, because that part they were born knowing how to do.
