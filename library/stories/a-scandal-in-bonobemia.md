# A Scandal in Bonobemia

*A parody of "A Scandal in Bohemia"*

To Sherlock Howlmes, she is always the female. He never spoke of softer passions except to gibbon-cage them with a sneer, and no sentiment was ever allowed past that cold, precise, admirably balanced mind — and yet, in his lair beneath the thatch at 221B Banyan Street, there hung one portrait, and it was hers.

I had married, as doctors do, and seen little of him; but one night in March I found myself outside his door with my practice quiet and my wife at her mother's, and he welcomed me as he welcomed facts — rapidly, and without comment on my absence. Emotions were, in his phrase, grit in the fur of the intellect; he groomed them out of himself nightly and recommended the practice to me, in vain. The portrait I did not notice then. It entered the lair afterwards, and for reasons that will appear.

The affair began, as his affairs did, with a caller who lied. He was a huge silverback in a travelling-cloak and a fig-leaf domino, announcing himself as Count Von Kramm, a nobleman of Bonobemia. He sat on the wrong cushion and knocked over a specimen jar.

"Ape-lementary," murmured Howlmes, without rising. "You lean like a king — nobody else sits so badly in borrowed fur. Your knuckles have been chewed by the state trellis-roses of the Bonobemian palace. Your cloak is cut for a throne-room. Your notepaper is the double-elephant fig-weight made only for the Bonobemian chancellery. And you came in two cabs — one to bring you, one to idle at the corner and wait — which is the habit of monkeys who are followed by their own guards. Your Majesty is the King of Bonobemia."

The King tore off his mask — a magnificent gelada glare above the silver shoulders of a mountain gorilla. "Five years ago," he confessed, "I was not so wise as I am now. There was a young American spider monkey at the Opera — Irene Adler, the finest contralto the jungle stage has ever pitched. I was Crown Prince then, and foolish, and she was — well. There were boxes at the Imperial Vinery, sleigh-loads of figs, an aria sung to me alone until the management apologized to the rest of the house. She has kept a pawtrait of us together. I am now contracted to marry the Snow-lemur Princess of Skandinavia, whose family are as proud as they are fluffy. If that pawtrait surfaces, I am ruined. The engagement is to be announced in a fortnight, and I am in Landon on business of state that I shall not describe, chiefly because it involves ballet."

"She will not sell it?"

"She will not be threatened, bribed, or budged. She is the most reasoning and the most audacious female in this jungle. Six attempts, Howlmes. Twice my burglars carried off worthless decoys — she travels with dummies, like a general."

"Then force has failed, majesty, and fraud has failed. There remains fire."

"Fire?"

"When a troop is at war, sire, what does every monkey rush to save? Not the crown jewels — the fig-stores. Fire is the one alarm that reaches behind panels. But first the panels must be found, and to find panels one must groom a house. Give me two days."

He took one. Howlmes had spent it usefully, trailing Miss Adler's elegant figure through Landon — from Briar-vine Lodge to the chapel of St. Moni-key in the Edgware Road, where a brisk bonobo lawyer called Godfrey Norton waited with a licence, a fee, and an expression of a monkey who has argued with three registrars and won. Howlmes was beckoned inside as second witness before he could explain that he was neither related nor willing. "I was halfway to the altar rail," he reported afterwards, with resentment, "before anyone thought to ask my name. The bride was entirely composed, and gave me, as we signed, a look of appraisal — such as a dealer gives a rival across an auction-room."

That evening found Sherlock Howlmes in Briar-vine Lodge — or rather, a shambling, white-whiskered old fruit-picker occupied his fur, so convincingly that he was hired on the spot to groom Miss Adler's elderly bushbaby and to help with the trouper's baggage. I attended in the street. At Howlmes' signal I lobbed my smoke-pod through the sitting-room window and gave tongue:

"FIRE! FIRE in the fig-stores!"

The household went bananas — servants bolting ceilingward, the bushbaby shrieking in elegiac thirds — and in the middle of it all, one spider monkey ran straight to the wall, slid back a panel of bark, snatched out a pawtrait and a letter, and thrust them into a drawer beneath — whereupon Howlmes the fruit-picker cried out that the alarm was false, and hobbled off to his supper of gratitude. I have thrown better smoke-pods in my day, but never one to better effect.

"That panel," Howlmes told me at midnight, dusting his paws, "closes the case. Tomorrow, with the King, we collect the pawtrait at dawn, and the thing is done. And Watson" — he paused, whiskers twitching — "the bride this morning. She saw through the fruit-picker, or I am no judge of faces."

At dawn we took our places on the corner opposite Briar-vine Lodge, among the milk-carts, the sweepers, and an organ-grinder's capuchin going to work with his little drum. The shutters opened; the door opened; and a smart young trouper in a travelling-coat said, as he passed us, cheerfully, distinctly:

"Good-night, Mister Sherlock Howlmes."

We stood like two planted figs while the road emptied. By the time Howlmes had recovered and we reached the lodge, Irene Adler was gone beyond the reach of any throne in the canopy. The panel held only a letter and a second portrait — of herself alone, in evening dress, inscribed to the King.

The letter, I regret to report, complimented my friend. She had been warned, she wrote, of the King's agents; but it was the old fruit-picker's knowing smile that told her everything. "I asked myself," she wrote, "what quarry would drive a gentleman of such wisdom to kneel in my kitchen, and I packed that hour. I have married my own dear bonobo lawyer, whom I love, and left the country, taking my pawtrait. It sleeps — unless your Majesty molests me. He will not. Good-bye, Mister Sherlock Howlmes."

"It is she, then, who has beaten the King and beaten me," said Howlmes, with something his biographer had never heard in his voice before. "Your Majesty pays heavily for lessons."

"I am not ruined. Tell me, Howlmes — had she been on my level, would she not have been the most perfect of females? Would she not have made an admirable queen?"

"From what I have seen," said Howlmes, "Miss Adler is indeed upon a very different level from your Majesty's."

The King reached for the remaining portrait. A thin brown paw covered it.

"This, sire," said Sherlock Howlmes, "is my fee."

And so it hangs, where he can see it while he grooms: not a trophy — a memento. When anyone asks whether the great detective is infallible, I only say that once the game was a-branch and ran the other way — and that he keeps her picture where a monkey keeps his heart.
