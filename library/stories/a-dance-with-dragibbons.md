# A Dance with Dragibbons

*A parody of "A Dance with Dragons"*

Daenerys Treetopgaryen, the First of Her Name, Queen of Meerenut, Breaker of Chains, Mother of Dragibbons, and — her least favorite title — Knower of Absolutely Nothing About Ruling, sat atop her great stone pyramid and tried to govern.

It was not going well.

The city she had liberated from the slavers was fighting her with paper cuts. The Sons of the Hairy, masked killers in the night, were leaving bodies at her gates. The great families were offering her peace on the condition she un-liberate nearly everybody — and reopen the fighting pits, which every Meereenese noble swore were a proud cultural institution, the way a knife swears it is a fork. And in the cellar of the pyramid, in a darkness that clanked, her three children were growing into the problem all mothers of dragibbons eventually face.

Drogon-fur, the black-and-crimson one, had taken to hunting on his own. He had a growing appetite and no respect for livestock boundaries, and after he crisped a child's goats and then, some whispered, worse, Daenerys did what she had sworn she never would. She chained Rhaegal and Viserion in the dark, and wept doing it. Drogon escaped into the wild, unfed and unchained, which is a phrase meaning *this will absolutely come back later.*

"It is such a fine thing, to be a mother," said her counselor, Sir Barristan, the loyal old silverback. "But a dragibbon has three heads, and only one of them ever listens."

She married, or nearly: Hizdahr zo Figlaq, a noble of the great families, who courted on instructions from the peace treaty and smiled in nine directions at once. Daenerys looked at him the way one looks at medicine, and said yes to stop the killing, and the killing did not stop. She took a lover too, Darrio Naharis, a sellsword with a trident and a daily haircut, and enjoyed him the way one enjoys dessert during a siege. She was seventeen, ruling a city that burned her name in effigy twice a week, and the chronicles still call her the lucky one. The night before the pits opened she signed Hizdahr's peace on the dotted fig-leaf, and the corpses kept appearing at her gates, which taught her the treaty lesson every ruler learns twice: paper does not fear knives.

Meanwhile — a continent away, in a box — Tyrion Lannifur was sailing to her. He had murdered his father and his lover, been packed into a wine cask by a friendly fat man, and passed the voyage drunk, miserable, and horizontally. On the way he met Sir Jorah Bearmant, a burly exile knight who had been banished from Daenerys's side twice and hoped to win his way back with the most famous dwarf in the world as a gift — as though queens collected them. They were shipwrecked, captured, sold, and passed from hand to hand across the Red Waste in the company of a dwarf jousting marmoset named Penny, until Tyrion, having hit the absolute floor of the family fortunes, did the one thing left: he talked. He turned a sellsword captain's head with arithmetic and jokes — and signed on with the Second Sons, which in the parlance of the companies is pronounced *the Second Bananas*, the monkey who arrives after the plot and before the glory.

At the other end of the world, on the Thorn, Jon Mocha had become Lord Commander of the Night's Watch, at roughly the age most monkeys are still losing their milk teeth. He made decisions nobody liked, because they were the right ones. He let the wildlings through the Thorn to save them from the Frostbiters, and half the Watch never forgave him for refusing to let the troop's ancient enemies freeze in style. When one lordling refused an order one time too many, Jon said, "Edd, fetch me a block," which is, in the North, a complete sentence.

"Kill the boy," the ancient, blind Maester Ape-mon told him gently, "and let the ape be born."

At Hardgrove, the harbor tree-village of the wildlings, the Frostbiters came in the night — a massacre of falling bodies and blue eyes. They were not an army but a demotion: every ape they killed stood up again on their side, the worst recruiting policy ever devised, and unbeatable unless somebody upstairs did the arithmetic. Jon Mocha barely out with a boatload of survivors and a bad feeling that stayed with him, and everyone else, forever.

Far to the south, a foolish young prince named Quentyn Martello sneaked into Meerenut to woo the queen, found her already promised, and decided the way to a dragibbon queen's heart was obviously to steal and tame one of her chained dragibbons himself, at night, with no plan.

He was, in the language of the chronicles, "rejected." In the language of the dragibbons, he was flambeed. Quentyn Martello thus achieved the one thing every minor character dreams of: the most memorable exit in the book.

The danger in Meerenut came to a head at the great games in the fighting pits. Daenerys sat in the royal box, resigned, watching. Drogon-fur dropped out of the sky like a black boulder with opinions, scenting blood and cattle, and began to eat the arena.

Daenerys did the one thing no one else in the world could do. She walked toward him.

The whole pit held its breath. The dragibbon's breath came out in smoke. She looked her child in the eye, took his scaly face in her two small hands, and said the family word:

"Banacarys."

Then she climbed onto his back, and Drogon-fur spread his wings and rose over Meerenut — over her lovers and her enemies and her unsolved paperwork — and flew, and did not come back, and left the city she'd freed to fight over her absence. Sir Barristan, the last honest silverback in Essos-fig, drew his sword and went to settle the government; the chronicles of that night are usually titled, in translation, "Well, Someone Had To."

And far north, in the snow at the base of the Thorn, the Night's Watch gathered around their young Lord Commander, and one by one, in the dark, they drew their knives. Olly, the boy he'd saved. Bowen Marsh, weeping as he did it. Each one muttering, as the blades went in, the oldest excuse in the Watch:

"For the Watch."

Jon Mocha fell forward into the snow, and the book ended there, and a million monkeys threw it across the treehouse, which is the traditional review for a cliffhanger.

The dragibbon danced away over the red waste. The snow fell on the dead and the living alike. And somewhere between them, a boy who knew nothing had one last thought, which was not a question at all:

*The troop deserves better than this.*

It usually does. It rarely gets it. Ask the two monkeys who might fix it: in Kong's Landing that very night, Kevan Lannifur, regent and the last competent adult in the capital, sat down to finish the kingdom's paperwork, took a dart in the chest, and looked up to find Varus the eunuch standing over him with a crossbow and an apology.

"For the realm," said Varus, who had decided the realm worked better frightened, "you were doing entirely too well." And the little birds came — who were, in fairness, marmosets, and did not coo.

And that, cold little monkeys, is the dance.
