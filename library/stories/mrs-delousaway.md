# Mrs Delousaway

*A parody of "Mrs Dalloway"*

Mrs Delousaway said she would pick the figs herself.

The June morning swung open for her like the fold of a great green leaf, and Clarissa Delousaway stood at the top of her fig tree in the middle of the troop with the shiver of a diver about to plunge. How she loved it: the canopy, the dawn chorus, the gibbons warming up in the next tree. Big Beng, the old lead gibbon, howled eleven across the branches in two silver notes, and the notes went out in banana-gold circles and dissolved in the air. What a lark! What a plunge! She had a perpetual sense, of a morning, of being out and about and invisible, unseen, unknown — of the troop poised on its boughs and herself suspended in the middle of it all, utterly alone, and loving it.

She had the grooming party that evening — she had invented it, in a sense, the way she had invented half the troop's pleasures — and the Prime Marmoset was coming. She moved through her own figs quick as a monkey through water, thinking, as she always thought in June, of Peter Bonash, who had loved her in the old branches and who was arriving back today from the Outer Wild, wildly in love, as usual, with somebody new.

On the way through the troop she met Hugh Whitbread — a glossy, pink-faced gibbon of the first families, carrying a basket of figs to the palace for his mother, as he had carried it for thirty years. Hugh was respectable, which in Clarissa's private dictionary was the cruellest word in the canopy. It was at Lady Bruton's lunch table, that very day, that the fates of young monkeys were to be settled between the soup and the fruit by three elders and a napkin; Hugh would preside over the napkin.

Over the park a dirigible beetle droned, writing on the sky in smoke — TOFFO, TOFFO — and the whole troop stood staring up with open mouths, marvelling at a jungle clever enough to advertise in heaven and too modest to say what for. In the park, too, an old she-langur had been singing the same two notes by the underground roots for a hundred years; the troop said her notes were older than the fig trees; nobody had ever once looked at her.

Peter Bonash turned up at ten, flicking his tail-knife open, shut, open.

"Still playing with your tail-knife?" said Clarissa.

"Still cadging figs?" said Peter, and they quarreled tenderly for twenty minutes about a kiss that had never happened and one that had. For there had been Sally Squeaton, in the old summers — Sally with her figs and her scandalous opinions — and one evening by the fountain, Sally had stopped, picked a leaf, and kissed her on the lips. The whole canopy might have gathered round and said: *That is what we meant.* The most exquisite moment of her whole life, and Peter had interrupted it, which was Peter all over. He had interrupted it then and he interrupted it now, wanting to know was she happy, did Richard — and she shooed him down the tree before he could finish, and stood a long while by the rail afterward, being fifty-two, and having admitted at last to being a monkey who feels things.

Meanwhile, in another part of the park, a young vervet sat on a bench with his small marmoset wife, watching the leaves fall.

"They are signaling to me," said Septimus Warren Smith. His fur had never sat right since the Coconut War; his friend Evans had died beside him, and Septimus had felt nothing, and now he felt everything. Leaves were alive. Trees must not be shaken. The world was in a fig, and the fig was being passed from paw to paw, and he alone could not taste it.

"He is saying things," Lucrezia told the green shade, in her small foreign voice. "Doctor Holmes says there is nothing the matter with him." Holmes — that smooth baboon, who had cured as many monkeys with his cheerfulness as the fever had taken. Then Sir William Broadpaw, the great vet of Harley Branch, had pronounced his diagnosis: proportion. Rest in a darkened nest. No figs, no noise, no news, no other monkeys. Sir William could restore a monkey to the troop; he could not restore the troop to a monkey, which was the part Septimus had been trying to explain.

"They are coming at five with the net," whispered Septimus, and Lucrezia sat sewing a little grooming hat and did not answer.

At five o'clock, when the keepers came, Septimus went to the window. "I'll give it you!" he cried, to the troop, to proportion, to the whole apparatus of the darkened nest — and flung himself out into the ferns, and lay still, and the ferns closed over him as if he had never been.

That same afternoon Richard Delousaway, coming home from Lady Bruton's lunch full of pigeon pie and resolutions, stopped at the finest stall in the market and carried home an armful of roses. He meant, walking up the tree, to say it at last — "I love you." He said, "Here are the roses," which in the Delousaway dialect is the same sentence; Clarissa understood it perfectly and kissed him; and he felt obscurely ashamed of his happiness, and went off to a committee. Such are the victories of the troop.

At the party that evening — it was going beautifully, more fun than a barrel of macaques; the Prime Marmoset had been gracious; the young were swinging from the chandelier of vines; even poor Ellie Henderson, invited once a decade and always remembering it, was having almost a good time — a whisper reached Clarissa at the top of the tree: a young monkey had killed himself in the middle of the troop.

She withdrew into the little hollow room, and sat alone among the coats. Somehow she felt very like him — the young monkey who had flung it all away. She had failed him somehow, all of them had, the whole troop, with its parties and its grooming and its darkened nests. Death was defiance, she thought. Somewhere a young vervet had leapt, and here she was, Clarissa, sixty years in this one tree.

"Fear no more," she found herself humming, "fear no more the groom of the noon. Fear no more." Old Big Beng struck the half hour, silver on silver, and the circles dissolved in the air. Across the gap, in the opposite tree, the old langur paused at her shutters of leaves, looked out once at the darkening canopy, and closed them. There was an embrace? No — there was dignity in it, in both of them.

And Sally Squeaton arrived — gray, handsome, married these twenty years to a rich mangabey, mother of five enormous sons — Sally, of all monkeys; and the first thing she did was whisper to Peter, "Has she changed?" and the second was to say to Clarissa, plainly, the thing she had come to say twenty years late and meant entirely. Some things, it appeared, the canopy had not gathered round and said after all. It had merely waited.

Clarissa rose. She must go back. They must be assembled; she was the hostess; it was her fig.

She came out to the top of the tree, holding the best fig in the canopy, and the party surged up to meet her, and Peter was there, half in love, entirely hers. What is this terror? he thought. What is this ecstasy? He could not have said. For there she was — Clarissa Delousaway — holding the troop together with both paws, and the one perfect fig.
