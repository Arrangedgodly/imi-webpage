# A Wizard of Earthtree

*A parody of "A Wizard of Earthsea"*

Earthtree was a world of branches. Islands of green rolled to every horizon, and monkeys crossed the gaps on leafships that caught the wind between the great trunks. On the towering tree called Gont, in the little grove of Ten Palms, a boy was born who would be trouble and greatness in equal measure. His name was Ged, though only a few ever knew it, because a name is a dangerous thing to hand out. Everyone else called him Sparrowhawk.

He learned that early. His aunt, the village witch, taught him to call the goats by their true names; the goats came, and never quite forgave him. Later he learned the name of the falcon that stooped over the grove, rode the wind with it for one giddy hour, and came back changed. Names were not labels. They were doors.

When the Karg raiders came — a screaming troop of baboons in war paint — young Ged climbed the canopy and wove a mist from the leaves' own names, hiding his whole village until the raiders passed, scratching their heads. That night the old mage Ogion found him. Ogion was famous across Earthtree as the Silent Howler: a howler monkey who never howled.

"Come with me," said Ogion. "I will teach you. To hear, one must be silent."

Ged was silent for nearly a year, which for a teenage macaque was a kind of heroism. But silence did not suit him. "I don't want to listen," he complained. "I want to *do.* I want to be a cham-chimp-ion!"

So Ogion, sighing the sigh of the very patient, sent him to the school on Roak, the Great Banyan, where the Masters taught wind, weather, illusion, and Names. At the gate, the Doorkeeper asked the price of entry, and the price was Ged's true name, whispered; no monkey enters Roak nameless. There the Masters taught him the balance of things: the Changer showed him shapes, the Windkey taught weather, the Herbal taught healing, and all of them kept circling one warning — that to change a leaf changes the wind it rides, and a wizard's power is mostly knowing what not to touch. He found a friend there — Vetch, a plain, kind colobus who liked him anyway — and an enemy, a sleek older langur named Jasper, who needled him at every meal.

"Show us a trick, goat-herd," Jasper smirked. "Or are your talents all talk?"

"I'll summon the dead if I like!" snapped Ged — and that was the trouble with monkey pride: it always over-delivered. On the hill above the banyan he cast back the veil of the centuries to raise the spirit of the Lady Elfarrah, loveliest figure of the old tales. The lights came; the shape came; and inside it, like a maggot in a fig, came something else, cold as the space between stars. It leapt at Ged's face and left him torn and burning before the Archmage drove it off, spending his own life to do it.

When Ged healed, one cheek carried a scar, and the shadow was loose in the world, wearing his shape. He graduated with highest honors and a hole where his peace of mind had been, took a post among the Ninety Boughs, and did small good deeds while hating himself nightly. An otak adopted him there — Hoeg, a fist-sized tree-cat with the personality of a hairbrush, who bit everyone but Ged — which is the only kind of love a fleeing wizard can rely on.

Trouble followed him anyway. At Pendor, the great boa Yevaud coiled, thick as a strangler fig, with nine sons, demanding tribute of the Ninety Isles. Ged sailed out alone.

"None of them can lie," he said to himself, "in the Old Speech. And neither can I."

He spoke the serpent's true name, and Yevaud's tongue froze mid-hiss. "By your name I bind you: sleep, Yevaud, and never hunt these groves." The boa's children fled into the deep green, and Ged let the last one go with a warning: bargains in the Old Speech are binding, and so are threats. Even dragons keep them. Mostly. The fine print is written in teeth. The villagers of the Ninety Isles hung garlands on his door, and for one week Ged felt almost forgiven.

But the shadow found him wherever he was. In Osskil's snow a husk named Skiorrh came loping after him — an empty baboon skin with a familiar scar-shaped cold inside it — and in the tower of the Stone of Terrenowt a voice like buried gravel offered him all the power in the world if he would only pick it up. "It sounds like Jasper," Ged told it, and ran on. At the world's rim the husk caught him, and only the last art he had saved him: he became the falcon whose name he had learned as a boy, and flew until wings and will gave out together.

He woke on Gont, in Ogion's hut, human and half-wild. Hoeg bit his ear, once, gently, to say *you're an idiot* and also *welcome back.*

"To hear, one must be silent," Ogion said. "You have run from this thing, so it has chosen your road. Stop running. Turn. Seek what seeks you, and follow where it leads — even over the rim of the world."

Vetch would not hear of him going alone, which is the definition of a friend. In Vetch's home grove on Iffish, his little sister Yarrow fed the travelers figs and asked no questions about Ged's scars, which is the greatest kindness one monkey can do another.

"You cannot run any farther," Yarrow said softly. "So what will you do?"

"Turn," said Ged.

Out past the last island, over open sky with no branch between them and the dark, the shadow came on at last, wearing his own scarred face. On the last morning, Vetch bound his own wrist to Ged's with a cord. "Whatever finds you finds me," he said, in the tone of a monkey declining to argue. Over the rim of the world, where the sea stands still, they walked out onto the wet sand together, into the dry dark, following a shadow that walked ahead of them — and slowed — and turned.

Ged turned — not with a sword, for the shadow had no flesh; not with a spell, for the shadow had no name he knew — until, over a still pool at the world's rim, he finally understood what Ogion had been not-saying for years. The shadow had worn his shape because it *was* his shadow, cast the day his pride first flared. And a shadow can only ever be called by one name.

"Whence do you come?" Ged cried into the dark.

"I come from yourself," the shadow answered — and Ged, laughing and weeping, reached out and called it: "Ged."

The shadow spoke the name too. And the two were one.

"To light a candle is to cast a shadow," Ogion had told him once. Ged finally understood: you cannot kill your shadow. You can only become whole.

He went home to Gont swinging, unafraid of the dark places, the healed wizard of Earthtree — no longer the greatest, no longer the loudest, but whole, which in the Old Speech is nearly the same word.
