# The House on Mango Tree

*A parody of "The House on Mango Street"*

**Before That**

We didn't always live on Mango Tree. Before that we lived on Loomis on the third floor of a rented oak, and before that I can't remember. The monkey on the second branch said the pipes were his, and we said they were ours, and then we were climbing down with our bags and our babies, looking for the next tree.

Then we came to Mango Tree. It is a small tree on a small street, a tired mango with a sagging branch and a wound in the trunk where a car hit it, and a door somebody painted the color of a sunset nobody stayed for. The one thing it is, is ours. I am Esperanza Cordero. Mama says the tree was a bargain. Papa says never mind the wound. My little sister Nenny says the mangoes are not even real mangoes.

At school they gave me a badge with my address on it, and one day a Sister pointed from the top of her own tall, tall maple, all the way across the city, at our little branch.

"You live *there*?"

There. The way she said it made the tree feel smaller. I looked at where she pointed. I didn't want to stare, but I had to, the way you have to look at your own paw.

I knew then I had to have a tree. A real tree. One I could point to and feel like pointing. Not a rental branch. A whole tree, all of it mine, with my nest woven tight, and a porch of leaves for my own swinging, out in the quiet of my own sky.

**My Name**

In English, my name means hope. In our troop it means too many letters. It means sadness, it means waiting, it means a monkey sitting by the window.

It was my great-grandmother's name. She was a wild monkey of a female, they say, who would not settle, who laughed at the wrong hours — until my great-grandfather threw his lasso of charm around her neck and carried her off to sit by a window with her tail curled under her like a good female, and she looked out her whole life, the way some of us do, at the passing of the troop.

I have decided not to grow up tame. Not like the others who lay their necks along a branch and wait. I would like to baptize myself under a new name, a name more like the real me, the one nobody sees. But I did not choose my name, and still I inherit her, the wild one, waiting.

**Boys and Girls**

Carlos and Kiki are my brothers. They are each other's best friends. They keep to their side of the crown and we keep to ours, and between the two sides runs a border, made of nothing, stronger than rope.

Nenny is my sister, and I am responsible for her, but she is too small to be my friend. Someday I will have a best friend all my own. One who will understand my jokes without my having to explain them. Until then I am a red balloon, a balloon tied to an anchor.

**Alicia Who Sees Lice**

Alicia's mother died, and so Alicia inherited her mother's rolling pin and her father's whole argument. She studies all night, riding the monkey-train with her books, because Alicia doesn't want to spend her whole life peeling fruit in somebody's kitchen for a troop of monkeys who think a female's place is the lowest branch.

Her father says the lice she sees in the morning aren't real. Alicia sees them anyway, two of them, by the sink where the peels pile up. She is young and tired and scared, and she studies anyway.

**Mamacita**

Mamacita is the great fat mama of the third-floor fig, who will not come down. Her man works two jobs and brings her things — a blender, a radio, a curtain — and she cries behind it. "No speak English," she says, and means, *no speak, English, none*, and so she stays up in the fig with the radio speaking to her in the old tongue, thirty-one flavors of homesick, fat as a pear and far from her island.

Her baby boy, the one in the tiny fur coat, learned English off the television. He sings the jingle for a soft drink. *Want some, have some, want some more?* He sings it in English, and Mamacita, who has not come down the fig in a year, hears her own baby speaking a language that does not love her back, and asks whose fur is this, whose fur is this.

**Rafaela**

Rafaela leans out her window on Tuesdays while her young husband is at his games, because on Tuesdays he locks the door and takes the key. She is young, she is lovely, her fur is like a peeled lychee, and she stays inside, growing older.

Down the block, on the corner, the coconut-and-papaya cart goes by, sweet and cold for ten cents, and Rafaela, who would like to swing down and buy some, who is young enough to want sweet things, throws her crumpled coins to the kids on the sidewalk and asks us to fetch her one, please, one sweet cold cup, and we do, and we charge her a nickel for the errand, because on Mango Tree even the youngest monkeys know what a locked door is worth.

**Sally**

Sally is the girl with eyes like Egypt and nylons the color of smoke. She combs her fur with a broken comb and says she is in love. Sally's father says she is too beautiful to be trusted, and her mother says she is a shame, and Sally laughs at nothing with her eyes shut.

She showed me the marvelous things in her little case — the red red lipstick, the little painted sticks — and I wanted to be her friend the way you want to be the one holding the last banana. Then she married a young sales clerk in another state, far from Mango Tree, in a house he lets her clean and keep. She says she is in love. She says it is better this way. She sits at her window because she is not allowed to go out, and she looks at all the things that belong to the free.

I think she is trying to escape, the way Mamacita's baby sings.

**Four Skinny Trees**

Four skinny elms with skinny necks and pointy elbows grow on our street, out of the concrete, nobody remembers planting. They are the only ones who understand me. I am the only one who understands them.

Four who do not belong here but are here anyway. Four whose only reason is to be and to breathe. Four who reach down with their tough old roots and refuse, refuse, refuse. Their strength is secret. They send ferocious roots under the ground and up through the sidewalk cracks, and when there is nothing left to look at, they grab the sky and keep it.

When I am too sad and too skinny to keep keeping, when all I want is to run off with the young monkeys who swing for the far trees, then it is I look at trees. Four who grew without permission. Four who stay up anyway and do not apologize. When one of us leaves the street, and one of us will, the others will keep standing there, keeping our sky.

**The Three Sisters**

There were three of them, old sisters with hands like dried figs, who read the leaves and the wind, and they took mine at Nenny's christening party and turned it over.

"Esperanza. You go to school, yes? Good. That is good."

"Someday," one said, "you will go far."

"Someday you will have a sleeping loft of your own, and you will swing your own swing."

"You will be pretty, and there will be a story to your coat. But do not forget," said the middle one, pressing my paw like a leaf into a book. "You will always be Mango Tree. You can't erase what you know. You can't forget who you are."

"When you leave," said the last one, "and you must leave, you must come back. For the ones who cannot out."

**Mango Says Goodbye Sometimes**

I like to tell stories. I tell them to Nenny, to the trees, to myself in the dark. I put it all down on paper so the next day I can remember it, the way Mama remembers the prices of things and Papa remembers the names of the dead.

And then one day I gathered my books and my papers into a bag, and I swung down off Mango Tree and went away, the way the three sisters said, the way the four elms held my sky until I was out of sight.

They do not know I have gone away to come back.

For the ones I left behind. For the ones who cannot out.

I write it all down: Mango Tree, the small tired tree, the green mangoes that never ripen, the wound in the trunk, the sunset-colored door. The house I belong to but do not belong to.

One day I will have a tree of my own. But Mango Tree — sad, small, stubborn, sending its ferocious little roots through the concrete of the city that did not plant it — Mango Tree will keep standing there.
