# Lessons in Chimpistry

*A parody of "Lessons in Chemistry"*

"Children, set the table. Your mother needs a moment to herself."

Elizabeth Zott said this to the cameras at the top of *Supper at Six*, the highest-rated cooking program in the jungle, and every monkey watching assumed she was addressing the young ones at home. She was. She was also addressing her daughter, Mad; her marmoset, Six-Thirty; and the studio crew, who had long since learned to obey.

Twelve years earlier she had been a chemist — a chimpist — at the Hastings Research Bough, the only female chimpanzee in a canopy of males who treated her lab coat like a costume. Her dissertation director, Dr. Meyers, grew old and celebrated on the strength of her work on how life first sparked in the primordial fig-soup. Her reward was demotion: they had her calibrating test tubes, on the theory that a female chimp near real chemistry might frighten the equipment.

The Bough's proudest tradition was the annual awards dinner, at which Dr. Meyers took the canopy prize for the fig-soup work and thanked, in his remarks, "my team, my mentors, and the patience of science." Elizabeth was in the team, somewhere, between the mentors and the patience. That night she went home and ran his key experiment upside down, correctly, to see whether it noticed whose paws were on it. It did not. The equipment, it turned out, was never the nervous one.

It was there she met Calvin Evans — the brilliant, blunt, orphaned chemist who ran the fourth floor and won every canopy sprint he entered. He found her working at his bench at midnight.

"You're in my space," Calvin said.

"It was empty," said Elizabeth. "Like your argument."

By the end of the month they were sharing the bench. By the end of the year, everything else. Calvin never once asked her to be less than she was, which in that jungle made him one of a kind. He proposed in his own way, with a rowing machine and a speech about oxygen debt, which Elizabeth considered the most romantic sentence ever constructed by a male. They adopted Six-Thirty, a marmoset found — at six-thirty, naturally — with an uncanny gift for predicting thunder and the outcomes of experiments Calvin had not yet run.

Six-Thirty, left alone with the household's newspapers, taught himself to read, which the household discovered when he began leaving sticky notes on the dangerous items: NO (on the stove), NO (on the solvent), NOT YET (on Elizabeth's breakfast). He had a working vocabulary of thirty words and one opinion, which was that the world contained too many unlabelled dangers and that he was short-listed to fix it.

Calvin died the way the quick die: suddenly. A storm cracked the sky, Six-Thirty bolted for the tall cedars, and Calvin went after him across the flooded gap. The branch was slick. The rest is arithmetic that never balances, no matter how many times you check it.

Hastings let her go before the funeral figs had browned. An unwed female chimp, visibly expecting, was declared "a disruption to the science." The night Mad was born, Elizabeth was nearly alone in a ward of monkeys who disapproved of her, so she delivered the baby herself, mostly, with a chemistry manual propped open at chapter nine. She named the girl Madeline — Mad, for short — so that she would never waste time being reasonable.

She took work demonstrating cookware at the Grocers' Exposition, where she explained, to a steadily growing crowd, why sugars caramelize — the Maillard reaction, the behavior of proteins under heat, the exact point at which a banana stops being fruit and becomes dessert.

"Chemistry is change," she told the crowd. "Your pan is a laboratory. Your apron is a lab coat with delusions."

A producer named Walter watched three hundred monkeys take notes on a flambé and hired her on the spot. Walter proposed marriage to Elizabeth exactly twice over the years. She declined twice and accepted the television program, which he always understood to be the greater commitment. *Supper at Six* was born: half cooking program, half chemistry lecture, wholly unauthorized.

The network wanted banana bonbons for your big strong silverback. She taught stoichiometry.

"Note the ratio," she said, flambéing plantains into a blue crown of flame. "Two parts sugar, one part butter, no parts apology. Cooking is chimpistry, and chimpistry is change. Sisters, question everything — question me. Courage is the root of change, and change is what we are chemically designed to do."

The troop's females began running experiments at their own stoves. Ratings climbed. Sponsors threatened to leave. She ignored them, which was, by then, her signature.

"Sponsors," she told Walter, "are a control group that failed."

The board objected to her lab coat on air. She wore two. When they demanded she smile more, she smiled exactly once per episode, at the moment the soufflé rose, and the jungle waited for it every week like a sunrise.

Desperate, the network hired a co-host: Chef Bruno Brawn, a huge glossy chimp with a trademarked wink who called cooking "magic" and digestion "mystery." On their first shared episode he waved his paws over a sauce and said the secret ingredient was love. Elizabeth took the same sauce, listed its compounds on camera, measured its pH with a strip, and said the secret ingredient was citric acid, and Bruno's fondue broke on live television while the switchboard lit up with females asking where to buy strips. Bruno left the network, converted to chemistry, and credits her still, through gritted teeth.

Mad, meanwhile, got into trouble in school exactly once. The teacher explained that the Great Silverback had made every monkey in one afternoon, complete with figs. Mad raised her paw and said the fossil record showed monkeys arriving by increments over millions of years, and that figs had come first, which in Mad's opinion was the plot twist. The teacher sent a note home. Elizabeth sent back a reading list. The teacher became a viewer.

One evening, after the cameras went dark, Mad sat on a stool licking caramel from a spoon and asked, "Mama, will I be a chimpist when I grow up?"

"You already are," Elizabeth said. "Everything that cooks, curdles, ferments, or falls in love is doing chimpistry, Mad. The only question is whether you do it on purpose."

The neighbors across the way disapproved of her, of Mad, of the marmoset, and of the compost, in that order; when their gas line leaked at two in the morning, it was Six-Thirty who sat on their chest and shrieked NO until they woke, which made the disapproval complicated. Elizabeth's only stated rule about Mad, delivered to the school, the neighbors, and once, memorably, to a sponsor, was: "My daughter is not to be given advice about her appearance. Ever. If she asks you the time, tell her the time."

Six-Thirty, on the windowsill, thumped his tail twice — his signal for thunder. It was, of course, already raining.

The last thing Elizabeth did that season was not on camera. She climbed the stairs above the studio to a small bright room of her own — benches, burners, beets, bananas — and pinned a sign above the door where only she and Mad and a marmoset could read it:

ELIZABETH ZOTT, CHIMPIST. NO APOLOGIES. NO SUBSTITUTIONS.

Then she set the table herself, because supper was at six, and chemistry waits for no monkey.
