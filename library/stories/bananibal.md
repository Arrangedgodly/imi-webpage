# Bananibal

*A parody of "Hannibal"*

Dr. Bananibal Lecter — bonobo, psychiatrist, gourmand, fugitive — had been free for seven years when he wrote his second letter to Special Agent Clarice Spiderling. The first had helped end a case. This one ended with a flourish of copperplate: *P.S. — do the lambs still scream, Clarice, or is it only the bananas?*

Clarice had made agent, then made history, then made a mistake at the fish market, which is where this story finds her. The raid went wrong the way raids do: a crate of contraband parrots burst overhead like confetti, a macaw felon went for her eyes, and Clarice threw a bushel of plantains instead of her weapon. The Bureau called it "excessive fruit." Clarice called it Tuesday.

In Florence — where the fugitive was living as "Dr. Fell," curator of the Capybara Library, beloved of librarians and waiters alike — he had spent his exile being impossible to catch and unbearable to out-cultivate. He gave one lecture a season, by candlelight, on the capuchin Dante. "Consider the avaricious and the prodigal," he told a packed hall of Florentine monkeys, "doomed to roll weights at one another for eternity, each convinced the other started it. Dante understood: consistency of appetite is the only honesty. The wrathful chew the sullen; the gluttonous are rained on by their own weather. And in the ninth circle, the treacherous — but no." He had smiled at them then, a curator's smile. "Some spoilers are fresher than others."

He built, in those years, the memory treehouse that replaced every home he had burned behind him: a palace of branches where every banana he had ever loved hung ripening in its own room, tended nightly, visited with tapering candles. His favorites he kept slightly out of reach, on purpose. Anticipation, he says, is the only honest seasoning. And he had a second palace, inside his skull, colder: a corridor for every year of his life, a room for every person who had fed him or failed to, and Clarice's room he kept stocked and aired, which frightened him not at all, which is the frightening part.

Inspector Rinaldo Pazzi, of the ancient and unlucky Pazzi, recognized him from the reward poster while he was accepting a fig from the doctor's own hand.

"Your ancestor," said Dr. Fell pleasantly, when Pazzi came sniffing with questions, "was hanged from a window of the Palazzo Vecchio on a feast day, before all of Florence, for conspiracy. A public monkey. It is the family gift, being seen at the worst possible moment." Pazzi laughed as though this were charming, because three million banana dollars was listening. The reward poster was Mason Verger's.

Mason Verger, the mandrill meat magnate, was offering the three million alive, because Mason was owed. Once, under chemical suggestion in the doctor's office, he had peeled off his own face-paint and fed it to his father's boars — leaving him a plum-colored smear of himself, breathing through a tube, rich as a rotten fig. He kept forty boars and one grudge, and fed the boars better, because the grudge he was saving.

Pazzi climbed the library stacks at midnight with a net and a plan. He woke at dawn hanging upside-down from a high branch of the Palazzo Vecchio by one ankle — alive, neatly wrapped, with a DO NOT DISTURB tag tied to his fur — while below him, on schedule, all of Florence gathered for the feast day. Dr. Lecter did not believe in killing messengers. He believed in re-gifting them. Pazzi was led away by his own constables, to his own window, to his own family occasion, and retired from the case, the city, and the force, in that order.

Clarice received her letter at Quantico and smelled it before she opened it: vetiver, old paper, banana. *Dear Clarice, they have taken your badge for throwing fruit. There is hope for you yet. I have read your file — I read everything now, it is one of the few pleasures left that is non-caloric — and I notice the Bureau has you down for 'impulsiveness.' They say it with their chins. Clarice, when you threw the plantains you were not impulsive. You were precise, in a cause the Bureau does not have a form for. Do not let them file your best moments under their worst words.*

The letter also contained, on a separate card, a correction of the Tuscan misattribution in her college thesis. She had got a B-plus on it. She had always wondered why.

Meanwhile, in Florence, Pazzi met Mason's men in a shuttered café — two capybaras with cropped ears and a lawyer who did not touch his espresso — and the lawyer slid across a photograph, a schedule of the doctor's habits, and a syringe of banana-cream sedative, and said, "Alive, Inspector. If he arrives gray, you are not paid."

"You do not need to tell me who he is," said Pazzi.

"On the contrary," said the lawyer, "that is exactly what we are telling you."

Mason's men took the doctor at last — drugged banana-cream, a classic — and shipped him to the boar farm, where Mason waited in his chair like a plum in a bow-tie.

"Doctor," said Mason, thickly, sweetly, through his tube. "Do you know what the boars did with my face? They ate it with such *enthusiasm*. Tonight they dine on yours."

The boars were brought out, forty strong, grunting, hopeful. Dr. Lecter spoke to them quietly for some time before the pens opened. Nobody knows what he said. But boars, unlike mandrills, respond to honesty, and it is on record that when the floodlights came up, the boars had not eaten Mason Verger at all. They had climbed into his chair around him and made him their own — not dinner; worse: an audience. They keep him for his stories. He tells them nightly, say the farm-hands, to a full pen, and the boars grunt at all the right places.

Justice Department counselor Paul Krendler — a baboon of unshakable self-regard — had sold Clarice to Mason and been paid in advance, so Dr. Lecter collected him too, and hosted a small farewell dinner at a house in the country. Clarice, having tracked the whole rotten caravan by its paperwork and its perfume, arrived to find the table set with candles, and Krendler seated at it, mellow as mango mush, being served.

"Krendler's best part," said Dr. Lecter, lifting the lid on a sautéed dish of young coconut, butter, and a whisper of rum. "The sweet, tender part. I have always said so, haven't I, Paul?"

"Delicious," said Krendler, agreeably, spooning up another mouthful.

"He means the coconut," Dr. Lecter assured Clarice. "Quid pro coco."

There was a hunt afterwards, and a helicopter, and a fall into a river full of rosehips. And there were, in later years, only rumors: two figures at the opera in Buenos Aires, a spider monkey and a bonobo sharing a box; a rare-banana importer in Rio with a cellar of vintages; a couple, wherever they were glimpsed, who tipped in exact, perfect fruit.

The Bureau file stays open. The dinner invitations do not.

And once a year, a card finds Clarice wherever she is hiding from the fame she never wanted. Copperplate. Vetiver. Banana, faintly, underneath. Always the same closing line, and she has stopped pretending it doesn't make her smile:

*I must run, my dear — I'm having an old friend for dinner.*
