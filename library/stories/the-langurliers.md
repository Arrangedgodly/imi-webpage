# The Langurliers

*A parody of "The Langoliers"*

Night Glider flight 29 left Jungle Patch for Beanstalk with ninety-four monkeys aboard and arrived with eleven.

Captain Brian, riding deadhead in coach, woke to a wrongness before he could name it: rows of empty seats, little heaps of glasses and tail-bows and half-peeled bananas, and no monkeys. Not asleep monkeys. *No* monkeys.

He ran forward; the cockpit was empty too, so he landed the bird himself — with the help of Albert, a skinny kid with a ukulele and steady paws — at an airfield in Mangoor, through air the color of weak tea.

There were ten others. There was Dinah, a small monkey with bandaged eyes, whose ears more than compensated. There was Nick, a British officer type who folded his beret into his lap before he'd fold under anything. There was Don Gaffney, plaid and skeptical. There was Laurel, a teacher, who kept looking at the empty seats the way you look at a class that's stopped listening. There was Bethany, a teenager mourning one earring and its owner, both left behind in seat 14A. There was Rudy, who had taken the news of the vanished world as clearance to eat everything in the galley. And there was Craig Toomey, a banker with a sweat problem and a tie, who kept checking his watch against a world that no longer checked back.

"Excuse me," Toomey said, stepping off the plane into the terminal. "EXCUSE ME. I have an appointment in Beanstalk at nine-thirty. Where is everybody? This is outrageous. I will be lodging a complaint."

"With whom?" Nick asked mildly, and Toomey hated him for the accuracy of it.

"Everything is wrong," Dinah whispered, ears turning like dishes. "It's like the inside of an empty drawer."

Mangoor was stale. The air had no smell. The soda came out flat and the bananas, sweet-looking, perfect, tasted like wet leaves. Rudy spat out a honey-roasted peanut and wept a little, for the peanut and for himself. When Brian flipped a peanut at the terminal wall, the *tak* of its landing arrived a half-second late, like an echo that had missed its flight.

It was Bob Jenkins, a mystery author with rumpled fur, who cracked it first.

"We flew through a rip," he announced. "A rip in time. Everyone who was awake came with us. Everyone asleep — went on without us, left their fillings behind. And this..." He gestured at the whole dead world. "This is yesterday. We're standing in yesterday, friends. The used part. The peel."

"The peel," said Dinah, very quietly. Her ears had gone flat to her skull. "Something is coming to take the peel away."

"They chew," she said. "I hear them. Silver. So many."

Toomey laughed — a bark with no humor in it. "The Langurliers," he said. "My father told me. He said, *Craigy, if you shirk, the Langurliers will come for you. They peel the lazy places of the world, and they peel the lazy monkeys in it.*" His eyes had gone shiny and too wide. "I have an appointment," he said again, "in Beanstalk," and nobody liked the way he said it, the way a wheel likes a rut.

He came apart for good at the news that the phones were dead. He grabbed a butter knife from the galley and Dinah by the collar, shouting about schedules and the wasting of his morning, and it was Albert — Albert, of the ukulele and the steady paws — who crossed the aisle and flattened him with a lunch tray. Dinah got a scratched ear and a grudge. Toomey got loose, bolted out onto the tarmac, and stood there laughing at the horizon.

They saw them at the false dawn: the horizon moving. Coming with the sound of a hundred thousand monkeys eating celery — a vast silver tide of langurs, each the size of a pony, all muzzle and molar, their jaws whirling like beehives. And where the tide rolled, the *world peeled* — the grass curling up in long green ribbons, the runway lifting, the earth unzipping in strips, until the MANGOOR sign spiraled away like the rind of some enormous fruit.

Toomey stood in their path with his briefcase, shouting his appointment into the chewing roar. The runway peeled beneath his feet and he rode the ribbon up, up, flapping like a leaf in a gale, still scheduling, over the edge of the used world and gone.

"Fuel truck!" Brian bellowed. "NOW, if you want to see tomorrow!"

They hand-pumped the tanks while Bob kept watch and Albert, hero of the age, stood on a luggage cart playing his ukulele at the oncoming tide — because Dinah, ears wide, had discovered the Langurliers *hated* the sound and paused, puzzled, at every chord. "It's working!" Bethany shouted, and then, in a smaller voice, "Is music supposed to be a weapon?" "Everything's a weapon in the right paws," said Nick, who got the engines turning with Gaffney and Brian. The tarmac peeled behind the wheels. They raced a whole world peeling itself, climbed, and Brian turned to the cabin with the strangest request in aviation: "Nap time, everyone. Belt in and go to sleep. Trust me — you want to be asleep when we hit the rip."

"You're sure about the napping?" Bethany asked, buckling in. "It feels like a trick."

"The rip doesn't take sleeping monkeys," Brian said. "It never did. It took the awake ones and left the rest flying on to Beanstalk without us. Coming back, we do the polite thing and go by asleep."

"And who flies it?"

"The autopilot," Brian said, "and my very best vocabulary."

Gaffney volunteered to stay awake and wave them through, and was voted down on the grounds that being missing in a time rip was not a two-rhesus job. Laurel held Bethany's paw and told her about a field trip her class had taken, a story with no monsters in it at all, and one by one they dropped off — Toomey's stopped watch ticking on the empty seat where he'd sat, going nowhere, at nine-thirty forever.

They slept. There was a bump of gray sleepiness—

—and came out over a Beanstalk with lights in it, smells in it, echoes arriving on time. The sleeping ninety-four sat dreaming their ordinary monkey dreams, as if they'd never left. Rudy checked the galley: the peanuts were glorious again. He cried again too, but differently.

They landed in a world just before morning — empty, but *new*. That was the difference, Bob said, writing it in his notebook: not used, not stale. Tomorrow, still in its wrapping. Colors too bright, smells too close, the coffee-stand buns tasting like the idea of cinnamon, first draft, brilliant.

"Where's everyone?" Bethany asked.

"Not gone," Dinah said, head tilted, listening far ahead of the sunrise. "Not yet arrived. Wait for it."

They waited on the concourse, eleven monkeys and a ukulele, and at the far edge of the sky the aurora unrolled like a tablecloth. Then the world caught up with itself: a WHOOMPH of arriving sound, doors chiming, escalators deciding to live, cinnamon settling down into ordinary deliciousness, and far off, the first traffic of a day that was only just starting to be used.

Through the terminal glass, they watched their own doubles — the awake ones, the vanished ones — boarding flight 29 for Jungle Patch.

"Right on time," Dinah said softly.

"To the used part," said Bob Jenkins, raising a coffee, "and the monkeys who made it interesting."

"To tomorrow," said Laurel, "while it's still fresh."

Albert strummed a chord. Nothing anywhere paused, or gnawed, or minded — and that, they agreed, was the nicest sound of all.
