# As I Lay Delousing

*A parody of "As I Lay Dying"*

**Cash**

I make the litter on the bevel, seven poles of green wood, pegged and fitted, so it will not rack in the river crossings. She wanted to go back to her natal troop in Jefferpalm to be picked clean, proper, by her own kin before she's put under the roots. It's the picking that's the point, Pa says. Any ape can be buried.

**Vardaman**

When we got back to the nest and the litter was ready and she was laid out on it, my mother was lying in it, and the lice were already leaving her, one by one, going down the poles. Then Cash ate a banana that night, and it was raw inside, and I couldn't finish mine. My mother is a bunch.

**Darl**

Pa sits on the porch-bough all day, arms too short for the work he won't do anyway, saying, "I done my best, I never was a grasping ape." He has a theory that if he sweats he will die, and he is saving his sweat for something.

**Cora**

I brought her cakes, every kind I could carry, and did she eat them? Not one. That family. Addie Bundren was always a closed fist of a female, and look what it got her: carried forty miles of jungle in July, with the whole county holding its nose. I said to my husband Tull, I said, a family that would do that to a mother's fur has got a judgment coming, and he said nothing, because he helped tote the litter over the first ridge, and he is a better ape than I am a judge.

**Anse**

The doctor said I ought to chew. I have no teeth, chewed out by a hard world, a poor old ape who has done without. There is a new set in Jefferpalm, porcelain incisors, white as the moon, and if a man's own wife can't be carried forty miles of jungle in return for a set of teeth, then I don't know what a marriage is.

**Dewey Dell**

I need to get to the druggist in Jefferpalm. It is a female trouble and I will not speak of it, only I need a tonic, and Moseley the mandrill at the first town will surely sell me one without telling anybody, being a respectable druggist and me being a respectable female, and it being only a tonic.

**Moseley the mandrill**

A young she-ape comes in my shop wanting a tonic, won't say what for, won't say what for at all, and when I tell her to get on home to her mother, she says, "It's not my mother. It's me. My mother never had a tonic in her life." It's all going to the dogs, this jungle. I told her to go home. I didn't sell it.

**Darl**

We tried to buy a new handle for the litter pole in Mottson, but before we got to the store a storeman came out and asked us kindly, in the way apes ask when they are holding their breath, to move along. The smell had gotten to the far side of town before we did. Nine apes came out of the drugstore to watch us go. Vardaman waved at them. The lice had left her two towns back; even the lice had standards.

**Vardaman**

The town apes held their noses. Cash said it was the heat. It is not the heat. My mother is a bunch.

**Jewel**

Get the litter off my tapir's back. I'll carry her alone. All of you can ride the vines and talk, and Addie can ride Whitie, and I'll carry her alone.

**Whitfield the parson**

I rode to Anse's nest with the confession in my mouth. Eight miles I practiced it: Parson Anse, I said, the boy Jewel is mine, mine and Addie's, and I have sinned against your teeth — against your trust. I got down from my mount at the porch-bough with the whole of it ready. And Anse looked up at me and said, "You have had a hard ride, parson. A hard world." And I said the world was hard indeed, and I ate a fig, and I rode home with the confession still in my mouth like a seed I could not crack. He never missed it. He never misses anything that isn't figs.

**Addie**

I could just remember how my father used to say, the reason for living was to get ready to stay groomed a long time. And I have been ready since before the children came. Anse was just a word to me, the way words are: I knew that the word "love" was never once the doing of it, and that grooming never fits the fur it's laid on, and that the only fur I ever touched that answered back was Jewel's, who is not Anse's, and that is a secret the lice have known all along and told nobody, being better apes than the parson. When I lay dying I was already picking the world off me, one ape at a time, and it was clean work, and I did not mind it.

**Darl**

The river is up. We cross anyway, on the submerged log-lines, the litter on all our shoulders at once, and the flood takes the litter at the middle of the channel and throws Cash against the rocks and breaks his leg twice more than it was broke, and he holds to the litter with the leg hanging and won't holler, saying, "It's the bevel. It didn't rack."

**Dr. Peabody**

They set the leg themselves, in river clay, like a bunch of mandrills casting a fish-trap, and by the time I got to it the clay had set better than my splints would have. The boy is the only one of that family with two arms and no theory, and they broke him twice for it. And Anse stood by hoping I didn't expect payment in anything but gratitude, and he hoped it around a mouthful of my figs.

**Vardaman**

Cash's leg is set in cement of river clay. My mother is a bunch.

**Darl**

We lie overnight in the Gillespies' fig grove and the whole canopy smells of her now and the buzzards sit in rows above the litter like elders at a sermon, patient as elders, and I look up at the figs and the buzzards and I think: why not fire? The fire would clean what the lice have quit on. So I take the lantern. It is a kindness, and it is insane, and I know both at once the way I know things.

**Vardaman**

Darl set the grove on fire. The Gillespies' figs are burning. Darl is sitting on the litter and he is laughing.

**Darl**

Yes yes yes yes yes. They held my arms and put me on the wagon for the sanctuary of the funny apes, and I laughed because it was better than speaking, and Jewel called me a son of a — and I wanted to say: you cannot say it, because she is not, because she is ours, because she is a bunch.

**Dewey Dell**

The druggist at Jefferpalm turned out to be a scoffer, so I went to the back of a drugstore where a young clerk named MacGibbon sells what he ought not. He said the tonic was ten dollars, and that the dose must be taken with total faith, and that faith was extra. It was a box of dried fig-seeds labeled DO NOT OPEN ON THE ROAD. When I got to the road I opened it. I am still opening it, in a way. But there is a new lady on the porch-bough of our own nest now, and I have stopped asking the jungle for things.

**Cash**

They took Darl away howling in a wagon, and it wasn't the howling that done it, because plenty howl. It's like it aint so much what a ape does, it's the way the majority of the folks is looking at him when he does it. Darl burned a grove, and the folks looked at him one way; Pa traded him for a new set of teeth, and the folks looked at Pa another. I have thought about it on my leg here, and I aint sure yet which way the folks was looking.

**Cash**

It's fixed now. The lice left her at the county line; they knew before we did. We got her under the roots of her natal fig with her own troop's hands on her fur, one last pick, proper, like she wanted.

**Anse**

I done my best. I never was a grasping ape. I got my new teeth — feel them, look at them, white as anything in the jungle — and when you all come up on the porch-bough, I want you to meet somebody.

"Meet Mrs. Bundren," Pa says, grinning with his brand-new teeth, and we do. She has brought her own gramophone, and it is already playing.
