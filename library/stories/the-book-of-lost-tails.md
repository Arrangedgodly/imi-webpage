# The Book of Lost Tails

*A parody of "The Book of Lost Tales"*

The storm blew Oriel the young snow macaque off her troop's raft, and the sea carried her, spluttering, onto an island where the trees grew taller than mountains and the evening air hummed like a struck chord.

She had sailed with her troop's rafts since she could swim — they were drifters by trade, her people, following fig-seasons from island to island — and she had lost her grandmother the winter before, and her appetite for drifting not long after. She had shoved off alone on the last raft with a sail made of stitched leaves, seeking, though she could not have said what. The storm, at least, had ended the not-knowing.

A gatekeeper found her on the shingle: a mouse lemur no bigger than Oriel's fist, lantern on a chain.

"You're shipwrecked," said the mouse lemur. "Good. The wrecked ones listen best. I am Littleheart, warder of the door. Will you come up to the Canopy of Lost Play?"

"The what?"

"Mind the third rung. It's older than sorrow."

Up they climbed, ladder within ladder, into a tree-city of hanging lamps, where children of every troop played games nobody remembered teaching them, and a hall of living branches held old gibbons who remembered for a living. At its heart sat Rumil the Rememberer, silver-furred, with eyes that had watched the watching.

"A wanderer," said Rumil. "Sit, child. Eat. Then ask what all shipwrecked monkeys ask."

Oriel ate three figs — grief is hungry work — then asked it. "Why does my heart feel like something ended before I was born?"

Rumil smiled like a river bending. "Because it did. Let me tell you of the beginning — and of the tails we lost."

And Oriel listened, night after night, while Littleheart trimmed the lamps and the children of the Canopy crept in and out like moths with bedtimes.

*First, learn how the jungle was sung. Before trees, before troops, before the first fig, there was the First Hooter, who gave each of the Elder Ones a theme. To She of the Ripe Harvest, the slow green music of growing. To He of the Deep Water, the long low rolling of rivers. To the Toolmaker, the tap-and-clink of problems being solved. "Hoot together," said the First Hooter, "and all that is shall grow from the sound." And they hooted — gibbons, macaques, titis, langurs — and out of the great chord rose the mountains, the rivers, the first fig tree, and the canopy.*

*But Melkong the Great Ape, proudest of the mighty, wove a discord into the music — a sour note of envy, for he wished to hoot alone. Where his note fell, leaves browned, branches twisted, and the first shadows crept under the trees.*

"Is that where bad things come from?" asked Oriel. "A sour note?"

"From a prideful one," said Rumil. "Most sour notes are the same note."

*Then he told of the Two Vines — Telperch the silver, whose blossoms gave the moon its glow, and Laurelimb the golden, whose fruit became the sun. Their light was so beautiful the singers made three treasures to hold it: three golden bananas, the Simianils, which never browned, and never would.*

"Never *browned*?" said Oriel, deeply moved, as only a monkey can be.

"Never browned," said Rumil solemnly. "You grasp now why there was a war."

*For Melkong coveted them, came in the shape of a shadow with a gorilla's shoulders, slew the Two Vines with a blight, stole the golden bananas, and fled north to his fortress of thorn — Angbranch, the pit of knots — where the world's light mourns still.*

"The Two Vines are dead?" whispered Oriel.

"The Vines sleep," said Rumil. "The Elder Ones fall like great trees — and great trees reseed, child, though the floor stays dark a long while. Indeed, hear what came after: the singers took the last blossom of the silver vine and the last fruit of the golden, and set them sailing in the sky, in ships of woven air, one by night and one by day. The Moon is the silver blossom. The Sun — the Sun is a banana."

There was a pause of some length.

"The Sun," said Oriel, "is a banana."

"Which is why," said Rumil, "everything that has ever lived wants it. Now the tales of the North, and the sorrow of them."

*In the wars of the Simianils, elves and — that is to say, monkeys and apes and all the speaking folk fought Melkong's thorn-legions, and the telling of those wars is long, and many of them Oriel would hear in later nights: the oath-sworn brothers who would pay any price for the bananas save the honest one; the hidden kingdom that lasted a hundred years of figs and fell in an afternoon; the beloved king who went mad on the same note Melkong had gone mad on, which is how the story always goes. Each Simianil was lost in its turn — to fire, to sea, to earth — as though the treasures themselves were trying to get home.*

"At last Melkong himself was taken," said Rumil, "in the great battle, when the singers chained him and shut him behind the Door of the Deep Den. But not before the damage that concerns this island — and every island."

Then came the tale Oriel had half known all her life.

*Hear the saddest verse. In the days of discord, many great apes followed Melkong, for he promised them thrones — no climbing, no troop, no equals. And when the war ended and Melkong was chained, the First Hooter passed a sentence terrible and gentle:*

*"You who followed the prideful one shall keep his silhouette. Go tailless, you and your children, so every monkey who sees you remembers what pride costs — and so that you, every time you look behind you, remember what a tail is for."*

*And that is why gorillas, chimpanzees, orangutans and bonobos have no tails today: not because they were lesser, but because their grandsires hooted a solo. It is not a shame. It is a memory that walks.*

Oriel sat very still.

"My grandmother had no tail," she said. "She'd laugh when I asked. 'We're not missing tails, little one. We're saving them for later,' she'd say."

Rumil's old eyes shone. "And she was righter than she knew. The last verse says: when every lost tail is remembered — when all the tales are told true — the Vines will reseed, light will return to Angbranch, and there will come a second and greater hooting, wherein every voice that ever sang, even the sour ones, find the note they were given at the first. No one will ever hoot alone. Not even Melkong."

"Then someone has to *write them down*," said Oriel, standing so fast her tail curled. "Every tale you remember, Rumil. Every night. That's why the wrecked ones listen best — we've lost the raft, we're not attached to anything, we can carry it all. That's the whole point of us!"

Rumil looked at Littleheart, and Littleheart at him, and both old creatures smiled at the young one.

"We have waited a long monkey's age for a wanderer to say that," said Rumil. "Many have heard the tales. None has ever offered to keep them. Hearing is a feast; keeping is a harvest."

He handed her a blank book of fig-bark pages, bound in liana, heavy as a promise — and on the first page, in bark-juice ink, he wrote the old title of the hall, which had waited long enough: *The Book of Lost Tails.*

So Oriel stayed, and wrote. She wrote the First Hooter and the sour note; she wrote the Two Vines and the banana sun; she wrote the wars, and the sad verse, and her grandmother's saying beside the sad verse, where it belonged. Children of the Canopy came to watch the ink dry, and Rumil corrected her spelling of everything except grief. And on clear nights, when the Moon-blossom and the Sun-banana crossed paths above the tower, Littleheart would find her still writing, tail curled around the lamp, and put out one more lantern anyway, against the dark.

**So the book is open in the Canopy of Lost Play still, and no one has yet said "the end." And whenever a young monkey writes down a story instead of letting it rot, somewhere far off a tail remembers it has somewhere to grow.**
