# The Kongfusion

*A parody of "The Confusion"*

There are two ways to tell this story, and the century refused to choose, so it told both at once, tangling them like two vines on one tree. The monkeys who lived it called the result the Kongfusion. It involved gold, silver, love, hanging, and nearly every harbor on Earth. Begin with the vine that stinks worst: the galley.

---

**The vine of Jack.**

Half-Cocked Jack Macaque, King of the Vagabonds, woke chained to a bench.

This requires a word of explanation, since Jack's fans (he had fans; being a legend is a disease with symptoms) believed him dead, sold to the Grand Turk's navy by the physician who was supposed to be curing his French Itch. Instead he was on the galley *Porcupine*, hip to hip with the worst crew ever assembled: nine monkeys from nine nations, each a master of a vanished trade. Moseh the mint master, a Sephardic macaque who could read the character of a coin by touch in the dark. Vrej the Armenian, merchant prince of a family that had been swindled by every crown in Asia. Dappa, a bonobo philosopher who spoke eleven languages and had been sold for using the wrong one. Gabriel Goto, a Japanese swordmaster of unbreakable courtesy and breakable everything else. Jeronimo the Genoese, called the Insane, for reasons that stopped being funny the moment you watched him work. Nyazi, Surendranath, Yevgeny, and the rest — a cabal, in the old sense: a squad of monkeys whose talents, pooled, were worth more than most navies.

They called themselves the Cabal. They rowed, and they plotted, and they waited.

"Each of us," Moseh said one night, oars slapping, "was ruined by a different wing of the same machine. Courts, mints, banks, and ships — it is all one machine, amigos, and it runs on money that never sleeps."

"Then we shall steal the machine's blood," said Gabriel Goto, who spoke like that all the time.

When the *Porcupine* put in at a pestilent harbor and half the crew died of the local welcome, the Cabal took the ship. It was not a battle so much as an administrative correction. By dawn they were pirates — liberated galley slaves flying no flag, sworn to pool everything they took for ten years and then scatter it so deep the machine could never reassemble itself.

And they took, from a wreck and a traitor's vault in the islands, the hoard: a tonnage of ancient gold, pale and heavy, stamped in a script nobody could read — the Gold of Solomon, looted and relooted for two thousand years, every coin older than every nation that claimed it.

"Divide it nine ways," said Jack, "and hide each ninth at a different corner of the world. Money that cannot be found cannot be spent. Money that cannot be spent cannot rule."

Dappa, the philosopher, looked at him with something like respect. "Half-Cocked Jack, that is the first plan you have ever had. It may be the first plan anyone has ever had."

So the Cabal scattered. And Jack — because he was Jack, and a plan in his hands is a banana in a fist — took his ninth on a tour of the entire planet. Cairo, where he hid gold in a cistern and out-argued a mullah. The Mogul's court at Delhi, where the Emperor's orangutans paid him to blow things up and he hid gold in the throne room's foundations. Japan, where Gabriel Goto came home at last and Jack left gold in a shrine guarded by a dragon, or possibly a very committed monitor lizard. Mexico, where he hid gold in a silver mine as if hiding the sin inside the crime. Canton, Manila, the long Pacific swell. Jack Macaque circled the Earth with a fortune in his hold, and the Earth circled him right back, because everywhere he went, the machine's agents — courtiers, mint-men, the shameless physician who had sold him — bought his warrants and his scent.

Which is how, at last, standing on English soil for the first time in a decade, hero of three oceans, richer than most kings and filthier than all of them, Jack Macaque was arrested by his own brother and clapped in the Tower of London.

---

**The vine of Eliza.**

Duchess Eliza of Qwghlm — once a slave on a battlefield, now the most dangerous banker in Europe — sat in her salon in Paris, where she ran a private society of ministers, mathematicians, and mistresses called the Apeto, and moved more money before breakfast than the King of France moved all fiscal year.

Her weapon was the Kongfusion itself: the tangle of gold and silver, of coins clipped and minted, of promises traded as things. Eliza had understood, before nearly anyone, that money is a story monkeys tell each other, and she had appointed herself editor.

"You wish to fund a war," she told a minister, pouring his tea, "but you have no gold. So. You will issue paper — promises, stamped, numbered — and the paper will be *as good as gold* because I, the Duchess, will say so, and because everyone will see everyone else believing it. The war will be fought on confidence. It always is; I am merely making it legible."

"And if they stop believing?" said the minister.

"Then, monsieur, you will discover what the whole century is discovering," said Eliza. "That gold is a rumor with a price."

She had built banks, crashed rivals, made and unmade a king's finances — and she had done it all while keeping, in a locked drawer, a bundle of letters she never answered and never burned: Jack's. She had his son, too, raised quietly in the countryside: Jean-Jacques, a small laughing macaque with his father's gift for being in exactly the wrong century. She had never told Jack. To tell him would be to hand the machine a hostage; worse, it would be to admit that the finest strategic mind in Europe had, exactly once, been outmaneuvered — by her own heart.

Now the war of the Spanish Succession was grinding Europe into debt, the mints of every nation were sweating silver like a fever, and Eliza's Apeto was quietly assembling the greatest plan of her life: to gather the world's scattered monies into one system, one net, so tangled and interlocked that no crown could ever again melt it down for cannon. What she needed, at the center, was a mass of *old* money. Quiet money. Gold nobody could trace to an owner.

She needed Solomon's gold. All nine ninths.

---

**The knot.**

In the Tower of London, Jack Macaque was visited by a veiled lady with gray eyes, and the warders were paid to see nothing, which was their favorite thing to see.

"You're supposed to hang Thursday," Eliza observed.

"I've been supposed to hang since 1683," said Jack. "I've made a career of disappointing professionals."

"The Cabal's gold is surfacing. The machine knows. Every ninth you hid is being dug up, bought, or burned, and the Apeto cannot build on gold that is being hunted. I can get you out, Jack. There is a boat, and a tide, and a bribed chaplain whose cassock you would not believe fits you. In exchange, the gold comes to the plan. All of it."

"And what do I get?"

Eliza looked at him for a long time. "Out," she said finally. "Which is the only thing you have ever wanted."

He went Wednesday, in the chaplain's cassock, off the wherry at dawn, and only at the rope's end — the boat's rope, this time — did she tell him about Jean-Jacques, in one flat sentence, the way you give a man water in the desert, without ceremony, because there was no time.

Jack Macaque stood in the bow, staring back at the Tower, at London, at the whole gray machine of the world, holding the knowledge the way you hold a full glass in a rowboat.

"Money is a story," he said at last. "You told me that once, in Amsterdam, about grain."

"I remember."

"Then he's the only true thing I ever minted," said Jack, "and I'll not sell him cheap by staying." And he laughed — that famous, infuriating laugh — because laughing was how the King of the Vagabonds signed his name.

The boat took him to Holland, to the Cabal regathering, to gold reborn as the seed-corn of the world's first great bank — the machine's blood, this time in friendly veins. Eliza went back to Paris, to the Apeto, to the long game. The vines touched, sprang apart, and kept growing.

That is the Kongfusion: not gold, and not silver, and not love either, but all three braided — the century unable to tell them apart, and the two monkeys who understood them best choosing, in the end, separate branches of the same tree.

Somewhere in a countinghouse, a clerk dipped a pen.

Somewhere on the North Sea, a fugitive laughed.

The ledger balanced. It always does. That's the terrifying part.
