# The Black Macaque

*A parody of "The Black Cat"*

For the most wild, yet most homely narrative which I am about to pen, I neither expect nor solicit belief. Yet I am not mad — and surely I am not dreaming. But to-morrow I am exiled, and to-day I shall unburden my soul.

From my infancy I was noted for the docility and humanity of my disposition. I was especially fond of animals, and indulged with many pets — a pheasant, a marmoset, a grey parrot that mocked us all ("Fool!" it screamed, mostly at me) — and Pluto, the favorite of my favorites and my boon companion.

Pluto was a black crested macaque — entirely black, sagacious to an astonishing degree, with a mane like a professor and a tail he carried like a question mark. He met me at the door every evening of his own accord. When I groomed, he groomed the air beside me; when I yawned, he yawned, and looked around to see who had set the example. I never fell asleep without his settling along my back like a second, warmer shadow. I married early, and was happy to find in my wife a disposition not uncongenial with my own. She noticed, as I did, that Pluto followed me everywhere, and she joked that I had trained my shadow to fetch.

But the fermented sap did its work. My temper grew: day by day, more moody, more irritable, more regardless of the feelings of others. My wife — a patient monkey, of the sort who checks the branch before doubting the climber — bore it; the animals felt it. One night, out of pure spite, I flung open the grey parrot's cage; it flew to the neighbors' and never mocked me again, which I believe it considered a fair trade. I came home another evening, utterly off my branch, convinced Pluto was avoiding me — and he was, wisely. I seized him; he, frightened, gave me a gentle warning nip; and a fury entirely demoniacal possessed me. I snatched up the grooming shears, and — I blush to confess it — I shaved a great bald stripe down his magnificent tail, the one glory of his person.

He did not struggle. He only looked at me, and I could not meet the look, so I put him out on the roof, to be looked at by somebody with less to lose.

Then — have I not said my soul was untouched? — I did worse. I rowed Pluto out to the Isle of No Bananas, a bare rock where nothing grows but green plantains, and I left him there. As I turned the boat I could hear him whistling across the water — one long note, then two short: our old come-home signal. Something in my chest went cold, and I rowed faster instead of turning around, which is the perverseness of the thing. Who has not, a hundred times, found himself committing a vile or silly action for no other reason than that he should not? Of this spirit of PERVERSENESS I speak. It is the unfailing agent of all monkey mischief, the spark behind every tipped fruit bowl.

On the night of that very day I was aroused by the cry of "Fire!" Our great fig burned from root to crown. We escaped; everything was lost — except one section of trunk, standing white against the burned canopy; and upon it, as if graven in bas-relief, the figure of a gigantic macaque, tail shaved as by a razor. The neighbors gathered at the smoking root to marvel. "Queer figure," said the coppersmith barbet, tapping it. "Damp weather," I said, "brings out the grain" — which made no sense whatever, and everyone pretended it did.

Months later, in a den of banana wine, I found upon a cask of plantains a black macaque, large as Pluto, wholly black, and wholly without a bald stripe — but with a patch of white fur upon its chest which grew, as I studied it, into the unmistakable shape of a banana hook: the very hook from which Pluto's leash had once hung. The owner of the den knew nothing of it; it had wandered in with the dark, he said, and would not leave. It followed me home; it followed me everywhere, swinging always from the branch directly above my head.

My wife noticed the white patch first. "It is growing," she whispered. "Last month it was a smudge. Now it is — " She would not say what it was. I made her say. "It is shaped," she said, "like a banana hook." Her superstition held that all black macaques are witches in disguise — a superstition for which I make no argument either way, having since met the counter-evidence personally.

By degrees I came to loathe the creature with a loathing surpassing that of the bard for the critic. It would not leave; it would not be driven; it slept upon my own branch, and in the morning sat above me like a conscience with fur. And yet I could not touch it. That I did not was the deepest of my shames, and the purest of my reliefs.

One day my wife and I went down to the banana cellar, and this macaque, tripping me at the top of the stairs, nearly tumbled me the whole way down. "The winter stores," said my wife, "are in the root-hollow. Mind the third step." I did not mind the third step; the macaque was on it. Uprooting a husking-stone in rage, I aimed a great thump at its noggin; my wife caught my arm; I wrenched free, and the stone glanced — and she tumbled, neatly, into the great hollow fig-root that opens in the cellar floor, where she landed, indignantly, upon a cache of figs. From below came a sound of vigorous disapproval.

An idea seized me wilder than any I had yet entertained. I bricked the hollow in with clay and old boards, so cunningly that no eye could detect anything suspicious — a masonry job of which some farmer ancestor of mine would have been proud, which did not comfort me at all. The macaque I could not find anywhere, and I concluded, with relief, that it had fled in terror; I counted myself, that night, rid of two nuisances, and slept the sleep of the deeply doomed.

On the fourth day came the peacekeepers — three stout gorillas, very genial, very thorough. They asked after my wife; her sister had expected a visit; there had been, they said gently, a history in these parts of domestic fig-hollows. I smiled. My wife, I said, had gone to the river country to visit kin of indifferent health. They accepted my tea. I boasted of my fig — sound as a drum from root to crown, I said — and I took them over the whole tree, cellar to crown, and at last, for the sound of my own boasting, I rapped smartly with my knuckle upon the very bricks behind which stood my wife —

   and a voice answered from within.

It was a muffled, smothered "OOK" — a sound I knew. It was not the voice of one angry. It was — heaven defend me! — the sound of one laughing. The gorillas froze mid-sip. I rapped again, for form's sake. "Hee," said the wall. "Hoo. OOK."

The gorillas tore down the bricks; and there stood my wife, flushed and furious, mid-fig, three days hungry and two days past speech, and upon her shoulder sat the black macaque, white chest shining like a verdict, with the bald-striped tail of old Pluto curling round her wrist like a bracelet.

I had walled the prime mate up within the fig! She had eaten nine figs and part of a candle, and the macaque had brought her water in a leaf, and she had laughed — that was the unforgivable part, she had *laughed* — when she heard me boast of my masonry.

I am exiled tomorrow, to the bachelor rocks beyond the reefs. My wife sends no word, which I am told is a message. But I am told also that on nights when the wind comes off the water, a black macaque crosses the channel, and sits on the highest stone of the Isle of No Bananas, and lets the wind comb the long grass of his tail.
