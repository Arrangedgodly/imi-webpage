# Breakfast of Chimpions

*A parody of "Breakfast of Champions"*

This is a tale of a meeting of two lonesome, skinny, fairly old monkeys on a planet that hung, like a bruised banana, from the westernmost branch of the Milky Way.

Listen:

The planet was Midland Canopy. The monkeys were Dwayne Howler, a chimpanzee who sold automobiles, and Kilgore Troop, a chimpanzee nobody had ever heard of. I made them both out of spare parts. I am not sorry. Ba-boom.

Dwayne had oodles of bad bananas in him. Bad bananas, the doctors said, were a disease of the soul. Nobody blamed Dwayne for anything he did. Everybody blamed the bad bananas. And so on.

Dwayne owned the Howler Fangbird dealership, a share of the new Holiday Tree motor lodge, and the biggest Banana-Bowl franchise in the county. On the interstate above his lot, a billboard said: EAT MORE 'NANAS. NATURE'S MOST PERFECT FOOD. Farther down the road, another sign said: IF YOU'RE SO SMART, WHY AREN'T YOU RICH?

Dwayne was rich. He was also sick. His wife Celia had passed away some years before — she had swallowed Dr. Primate's Patented Drain cleaner, which was for drains, and the label said so. His son Bunny played piano in the cocktail lounge of the Holiday Tree, and Dwayne suspected — correctly — that the bad bananas were winning.

Consider Dwayne's empire from the parking lot, where a monkey named Wayne Hoobler stood all day, taking dimes from other monkeys for the privilege of leaving their automobiles where they were. Hoobler was fresh out of the county zoo, where he had done two years for taking dimes from other monkeys without providing a parking space. He worshipped the dealership as other monkeys worshipped the moon. He had memorized the sticker prices. He spoke of Dwayne Howler as though Dwayne were weather.

Kilgore Troop, meanwhile, lived in a basement in Ilium Grove and wrote science fiction that nobody read. His stories appeared only in magazines with names like Banana-Split Pulp. In one story, a machine peeled people like fruit and found, under the rind, nothing at all. In another, the smart monkeys of Earth left the planet forever because the atmosphere had grown tired of them. In a third, a monkey discovered that his memories were being written by somebody else as fast as he could live them, and filed a complaint, and the complaint was read aloud by the author, who thought it was funny. Troop believed the universe was a big dumb ape that had swallowed a clock. He was almost right.

Into this story limped Eliot Rosesap, a rich old orangutan who loved Troop's stories. He read them in the veterans' hospital, where he was recovering from being the only monkey in his family to survive the Sugar Wars, and they had saved his life, or at least his afternoons. Rosesap had sent Troop an invitation to the First Midland Canopy Festival of the Arts, and a bus ticket besides. Troop rode most of the way, because the bus driver threw him off for hooting. He walked the rest, through the ChemTar flats, where the river ran colors that had no names, past the new parts of town where the trees were all the same tree, stamped out of one mold. Troop had never seen a shopping jungle before. "So this is what the universe did with all the spare parts," he said.

The Festival put Troop on a panel called "Can a Monkey With a Typewriter Write Shakespeare?" Troop stood up and said, "I am the monkey. This is the typewriter. Where's my Shakespeare?" Nobody laughed. There is no laughter in Midland Canopy.

"I have good news and bad news," Troop told the audience, which was mostly other panelists. "The good news is that you are alive. The bad news is that it doesn't matter, because everything you do was decided before your grandparents met. You are made of extras." A philosophy professor called this pessimism. Troop said it was just inventory. And so on.

In the cocktail lounge of the Holiday Tree that night, Dwayne Howler sat drinking banana daiquiris and waiting, without knowing it, for the bad bananas to finish him. His sweetheart Francine, who ran his dealership and forgave him things no other monkey would, begged him to see a doctor. His son played "Moonlight Serenade" badly on the piano, beautifully. Wayne Hoobler stared at Dwayne through the glass and thought: there goes a free monkey, if ever I saw one. What Hoobler meant was: there goes a monkey who is fed. Hoobler had not been fed, reliably, in some time.

Then Kilgore Troop came in, because Rosesap had told him Dwayne Howler was the happiest monkey in Midland Canopy, and Troop wanted to see one, the way you might want to see a duck-billed platypus.

Troop's suitcase fell open, and one paperback skidded across the floor to Dwayne's feet. It was Troop's rarest novel, Now It Can Be Told, and it said this:

*To the reader: You are the only monkey in the jungle with free will. Everyone else — your mate, your troop, the bartender, the author — is a puppet of the Creator, going through the motions. There. Now you know. Hoot if you must.*

Dwayne read it twice. His face did something new.

"Excuse me," he said politely, "but is this true?"

"It is fiction," said Troop, who had been waiting thirty years for a fan and recognized the moment.

"Is it true?" said Dwayne, standing up, and the bad bananas took him.

He went, as the saying goes, completely bananas. He wrecked the lounge, drank out of the finger bowls, chased Francine over the piano while Bunny kept playing, dumped a bowl of banana chips over Bunny's head, and set fire to the bill for his own daiquiris, which was at least thrifty. He marched into the parking lot and invited the automobiles, one by one, to do their worst. The automobiles declined. And then he seized Kilgore Troop's tail in his teeth and bit the tip off it — just to see whether Troop was real.

Troop was real. Troop hooted.

The police came, eleven of them, and it took eight. The doctors came, and shook their heads over the bad bananas, and wrote Dwayne's diagnosis on a chart: bananas, bad, complete. Wayne Hoobler, meanwhile, did something nobody noticed until later: he put out the fire in the flowerbed with his hat, and kept the hat.

In the hospital afterward, Dwayne apologized to everyone, one by one, and paid for everything, and meant it. "I thought I was the only live monkey in a world of stuffed ones," he said. "That is a lonely way to live." Francine held his hand. Bunny played the visiting hours. Hoobler got the night job at the dealership, feeding the guard baboons, and pronounced himself, at last, close to the knobs of the world.

That night, in the parking lot of the Holiday Tree, the Creator came to Kilgore Troop. The Creator looked like a middle-aged monkey with glasses, sitting at a typewriter that was balanced, absurdly, on a shopping cart.

"You're the one who wrote me?" said Troop, tail bandaged.

"I'm one of an infinite number of monkeys with typewriters," said the Creator. "I'm the one who got the job. I made you suffer so the story would be interesting. I'm sorry. Bad bananas are my fault, too. I was trying to make the bananas interesting."

"Was Dwayne real?"

"He was as real as you are," said the Creator, "and you're real because I say so. That's the whole system. It isn't much, but it holds."

He said he was setting Troop free now, that Troop could go anywhere he liked, and Troop hobbled a lap around the cart to test the claim.

"One more thing," said the Creator, gathering up his pages. "Notice when you are happy, and murmur, now and then: 'If this isn't a nice banana, what is?'"

Troop considered his life: the basement, the pulps, the bitten tail, the fan who had eaten a lounge. It was, on balance, the only life he had, and the Creator was offering to make it longer.

"Make me young!" Troop cried at last, hopping up and down in the pine needles, and his voice carried all the way back to Ilium Grove. "Make me young! Make me young!"

Here the Creator, who was tired of doodles, doodled one last banana — this one — and closed his cover.

Ba-boom.
