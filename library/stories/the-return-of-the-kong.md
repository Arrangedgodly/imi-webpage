# The Return of the Kong

*A parody of "The Return of the King"*

The war of the Vine came at last to the White City of the South: Minas Tirith, seven circles of nest and hall in the living fig-cliff, down to its last good morning. Pip the bonobbit — carried there by the Whiteback after the affair of the seeing-stone — looked into the Palmantír once more, on purpose, to draw the Eye's stare away from the two small figures crawling east. After that, no more pretending. On the mountain-tops, one by one, the old beacons flared: Gondor calls for aid.

"And Roan will answer," said old King Théoden, and six thousand zebra-riders turned south.

But the city's hope was a rangy gorilla everyone called Strider. His true name was Kong, heir of the forgotten line of kings, and his hour came by the worst road available: the Paths of the Dread, haunted by oath-breaker ghosts who had sworn to fight for the last king and simply not turned up. Kong walked in, and the dead crowded the dark like cold smoke.

"Oathbreakers!" his voice filled the tunnel. "Your oath is owed! Follow me to the river, and then — rest."

They followed — ghosts keep bargains better than monkeys, once you get their attention.

Pelennut Fields became the battle of the age: cat-beasts the size of buses, and above all the Nine — the Nightgûl — with their captain the Witch-king, a crown on nothing. Théoden fell there, zebra and all; and over the fallen king stood his shield-bearer, who should not have been there: his niece Éowyn, disguised as a trooper, with a furious bonobbit (Pip) at her knee.

"No living *male* ape may hinder me," said the Witch-king, in a voice like a drain.

"I am no male ape," said Éowyn, pulling off her helm, and her braid fell like a war-banner. "You look upon a daughter of Roan — and her little friend." Pip stabbed him beneath his cloak; Éowyn swung; and the crown rolled away empty — the best way for a crown to be.

The fallen king, not yet gone, felt his niece kneel. "I go to my fathers, in whose mighty company I shall not now feel ashamed," said Théoden. "Grief will come after, little one. Let it."

Behind the walls, meanwhile, the Steward Denthor, ruler of the city and father of Faramandrill, had lost hope with terrible ceremony. He had looked into his own seeing-stone too long, and the stone had shown him exactly what the Enemy wished. When Faramandrill was carried back from the causeway, cold and senseless under orc-arrows, Denthor declared the whole line failed, the whole city lost, and ordered a pyre — for his son and himself, in the House of the Stewards, complete with the ancestral torch.

"He is not dead!" cried Pip, and ran for help, and found it in the Whiteback, who broke the door and carried Faramandrill out of the fire. Denthor, holding his torch like a verdict, looked at the ruin of his certainty and stepped into the flame alone, holding the palantír of the White City up to his chest — which the chronicles call pride, and the pyre called kindling.

Then up the river came the enemy's black ships, crewed by ghosts and one very large gorilla. Up the quays leapt Kong, a mountain with knuckles, and the foe broke like surf before a whale. The hands of a king, says the proverb, are the hands of a healer; a Kong's proved excellent at that too, and the wards went quiet that night — even the ward of Éowyn, whose wound was not in her arm but in her plan to die gloriously. In the Houses of Healing she lay face to the wall until Faramandrill, in the next bed, said gently that he had also tried despair and found it did not keep. "I wanted to die in battle, like my uncle," she said. "I did," said Faramandrill, "in a manner of speaking. The manner is called *surviving it.*" By the time the city was rebuilt, they had argued each other into hope — the standard treatment.

But wars are not always won where they are fought. The captains of the West did the arithmetic: they could not defeat the Enemy, but they could *distract* him — march on his own black gate with seven thousand apes, so hopelessly bold that Saurilla would smell the trick and look everywhere except the one place the trick actually was. Before the gate came its lieutenant, the Mouth of Saurilla, a helmeted herald with no face and excellent diction, holding up a small grey shirt of woven silver-vine: "The spy's. Shall I name his fate?"

"We came to name yours," said Kong, and the herald recoiled — for a king of the old blood had never answered him and lived to be announced. The Whiteback stepped forward and took the little shirt with both paws, as one takes a note from a kidnapper. "A lie," he said, "with a true prop. It ends today." The gates opened, and out poured the last and worst.

Two small figures crawled the ash-plain of the dark country: Fruitdo and Sam, starved, in orc-rags, the One Vine hanging from Fruitdo's neck like an anvil that whispered. "I can't carry it for you," Sam said, "but I can carry you" — and did, up Mount Bloom to the crack where the Vine was forged and alone could end.

At the last ledge, fire below, Sam behind, Fruitdo held the ring over the abyss — and the Vine, patient for three thousand years, spent its last card.

"I will not destroy it," said Fruitdo, in a voice not entirely his own. "The Vine is mine." He put it on and vanished; far away the Eye of Saurilla screamed and swung — too late. For out of the dark came Golly, wild with loss and glee; he bit, caught his preciousss, and danced on the rim:

"Precious, precious, precious!"

And the ledge gave way.

The Vine went into the fire, and Golly with it, and Mount Bloom came apart in thunder; the tower folded, the Nine fell like burnt moths, the Mouth was struck dumb mid-sentence, and the shadow passed like a bad dream at breakfast. At the black gate the whole dark army dropped like cut strings. Great hornbills — "The hornbills are coming!" — bore two small burnt apes off the mountain; and on the rock Fruitdo said: "I'm glad you're with me, Sam, here at the tail-end of all things."

"I'm glad you're with me too, sir," said Sam.

There was a coronation — Kong, the returned king, wed that midsummer to Arboreen of the ripening groves — but the moment everyone tells still is this: the new king turned to the crowd, and of the two little gardeners in the front row said: "They have climbed farther than any in the troop. **You bow to no one.**" And the whole host of the West — kings, captains, ghosts on furlough, one insufferable wizard — knelt to the bonobbites.

The Brier was scoured and replanted, box by garden-box, with Sam's Galadriel-soil doing the work of ten ministries; and in autumn a grey ship came to the Bough Haven, to carry the wounded ring-bearer to the Evergreen Lands.

"I tried to save the Brier," said Fruitdo at the rail, "and it has been saved — but not for me. We're none of us the same, Sam."

The ship passed into the West; and Samwise the Sturdy — mayor, gardener, bravest of small apes — walked home in twilight to his warm nest and waiting family, hung up his coat, and said:

"Well, I'm back."
