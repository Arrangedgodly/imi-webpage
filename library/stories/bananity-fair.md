# Bananity Fair

*A parody of "Vanity Fair" — a story without a silverback*

While the present century was in its teens, Miss Bananakerton's Academy for Young Primates sat at the edge of the Little Banyan Strip, ready for the departure of its graduates — and Becky Sharpteeth was first down the ladder.

Becky was small, sandy, and orphaned. Her father had been a failed fruit-juggler and her mother a French dancer of the low vines, and the Bananakerton sisters had taken her in half as pupil, half as servant. Now she was leaving forever, riding in the treetop carriage with Amelia Seedley, who was rich, gentle, and cried about everything.

As the carriage rolled off the platform, Becky made her last gesture to the Academy. She took the parting gift — Dr. Simian's great *Dictionary of Grooming Etiquette* — and flung it into the shrubbery below.

"So long, Bananakerton's!" she cried, waving her whole arm. "I'm gone, and glad of it. Three volumes of how a young female may sit on a bough without offending an elder — well, I can sit on a bough without a dictionary, and I mean to sit on the best one in the jungle."

Amelia was shocked. Amelia was always shocked on somebody else's account. "Rebecca, do you not care?"

"Not I," said Becky, examining her nails. "I think I could be a good monkey if I had five thousand bananas a year."

At the Seedleys' great nest, Becky set about charming everyone, especially Jos Seedley, Amelia's brother, a fat bronzed orangutan who had collected rare fruits in the East and would talk of it for six hours. Becky laughed at his jokes until he blushed through his face-pads; she ate a chilli fig at his urging and let him believe she had enjoyed it, which is the truest proof of love the jungle knows. Jos got as far as "Miss Rebecca, there is a certain grove, that is, a matter —" and then his nerve failed at the fig punch, and the family broke up the campaign before it could be won. Becky filed him under Later.

Then war came to the canopy — the great Territorial Clash at Waterloo Bough, where all the young males of fashion went off in polish to wrestle the Old Grove troops. George Osborne, a vain pink-faced macaque who had married Amelia's heart and her family's bananas at once, strutted in his new epaulettes of beaten bark. At the great ball on the eve of the battle he danced with Becky twice, wrote half a note asking her to run away with him, thought better of it, and tucked it into his sash, where Fate found it later and kept it, folded, for eleven years.

Rawdon Crawler, a great good-natured baboon lieutenant, had secretly married Becky — to the lasting fury of his aunt, the rich ancient Miss Crawler, who had meant to leave him her whole grove. Becky dashed to the aunt's sickbed with broth and flattery, and the old ape rallied gloriously whenever fresh visitors arrived and sank back into garrulous health between them. When she finally died she left every banana to the pious elder brother, and Becky, drying her eyes on somebody else's handkerchief, pronounced the funeral figs excellent.

"Gracious, Rawdon," said Becky, "you've cost me a plantation. You must go wrestle somebody until you're a general."

At Waterloo Bough the young males wrestled all day in the smoke of the fig-fires. George Osborne went out preening and did not come back. Amelia was a widow within the year, with a small son, Georgy, and every banana of her fortune in old Mr. Osborne's fist — nothing for it but to sit in a small nest and be faithful, which she did beautifully, for eleven years, with the help of Captain Dobbyn — a patient, knock-kneed gorilla who had loved her since before the war and had never once been taken seriously, which suited Amelia's grief very well.

Becky, meanwhile, was climbing. Rawdon made colonel; little Rawdon made an heir; and Becky's salon in the Great Banyan became the wittiest bough in the jungle, where dukes and novelists came to hear her sing the low-vine songs and to watch her lose at cards to the Margrave of Steyne on a schedule as regular as fruit-fall. Little Rawdon was not allowed upstairs when the Margrave was up them. He learned to sit on the stair and tell which of his mother's laughs meant come up and which meant stay down, and there are worse educations, though not many.

Old Sir Pitt Crawler died; Rawdon became baronet of Queen's Crawler Groves; and if the bailiffs were always somewhere below, no one at the party mentioned them.

Then the Margrave gave Becky a diamond-and-jade grooming comb, and Rawdon, sprung from the debtors' bough by his brother, walked home unannounced and found the Margrave comfortably situated in his own parlor-nest, being handed figs by his own wife.

"Well," said Rawdon to his boy, "your mother's cleverer than I am. Cleverer than the whole jungle. But done I am, my son, and done with it."

He handed Becky the comb and swung off to Macao, where he became the best-liked baboon at every gaming-bough on the coast and died, in the end, of a fever he had been dodging since the war, forgiving everybody in his last letter. His uncle and Lady Jane took little Rawdon in — Lady Jane was kind the way bread is plain — and the boy grew up straight, which is what comes of being fed without an audience.

Becky fell, in the way of the jungle, softly and slowly, through shabbier and shabbier nests — singing in third-rate groves for monkeys who half remembered her, until even the Margrave's set would not know her. And Amelia sat on faithfully. She kept George's portrait turned to the light that best showed the epaulettes; she kept his old gloves; she kept, above all, the wound, and polished it daily. At last even Dobbyn's patience came off its hinges.

"You worship a macaque who was not worth your little finger," he said. "Eleven years, Amelia. I have carried your grief like luggage, and you have never once asked whose back it was breaking. God love you — I am done waiting." He left for the Madras groves the next week, and Amelia cried into the portrait, which said nothing helpful, being a portrait.

Old Mr. Osborne took Georgy, as he had always meant to — the boy had his father's strut and his mother's eyes — and Amelia consented, because Georgy would have bananas, and the nest was quiet and faithful again, and unbearable. It was in that year that Becky reappeared, shabby and amusing, at a country station in the rain, and was rescued by a polite boy with a familiar strut. Georgy brought her home to Amelia's because he did not know what a guest was, only that she sang.

And it was Becky, in the end, who settled Amelia. One evening she talked softly of Waterloo Bough, of a certain officer who had been false to his wife before the fig-smoke cleared — and she put into Amelia's hand a half-written note, folded eleven years deep. George's own paw: *Dear Becky, come away with me —*

"He was false to me," said Amelia. The idol did not shatter; it simply set itself down, after all that time, like a tired mother.

"He was a male," said Becky. "I never blamed him and I never praised him. I want to see you married to that gorilla before I die, Emmy."

So Amelia married Dobbyn — the Madras gorilla having come home at once, at the double, gray with hope — and Becky wept at the wedding, sincerely and skillfully.

She went off after that to Pumpernickel Bough, where society was smaller and memory shorter, and where Jos Seedley, enormous and lonely and still telling the fruit-collecting story, took her in and paid her bills. It was noticed that Jos insured his life for a remarkable sum and grew quieter every month, as if listening for something. Becky came home at last with a carriage of her own, a seat among the pious, and no one at all to answer to, which was her idea of heaven.

At Queen's Crawler Groves, when all the funerals were finally done, there was an auction of the effects, and the monkeys of three counties came to bid. Item forty-one: one grooming comb, jade and diamond, provenance disputed. Item forty-two: three volumes, *Dictionary of Grooming Etiquette*, barely thumbed, inscribed to a young female who sat on boughs. Item forty-three: one rocking-horse, much ridden by a boy on a stair. Item forty-four: one fig punch bowl, capacity heroic. Item forty-five: one half note, unsigned — lot withdrawn at the widow's request. The bidders left feeling they had seen a show, which, of course, they had.

Ah, bananitas bananarum! Which of us is happy in this world? Which of us has his desire — or, having it, is satisfied? Come, children, let us shut the canopy on the troop, for our play is played out.
