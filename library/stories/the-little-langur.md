# The Little Langur

*A parody of "The Little Stranger"*

I first came to Hundred Bough Hall in 1919, a chimp of ten with my nose pressed to the rope barrier, watching the gentry swing through the fête. My mother had been nursery girl there before her back gave out. When the other village monkeys pulled a plaster acorn from the decaying orangerie and pressed it on me — a trophy from the big house, a joke — I held it all the way home like a lump of the moon. I have it still. That is the sort of monkey I am. I expect it accounts for everything.

Thirty years later I was Dr. Faraday, physician, and the Hall sent for me at last — to treat not the family but the dog, who had bitten the new maid at the annual fête, when the crowds pressed and the band struck up. I remember thinking, as my little car went up the ruined drive, that the house looked like an old langur gone thin: grand bones, moulted fur, too much sky behind the ears.

The Ayreses were silvery langurs, the last three of a troop that had run the county for two hundred years. Mrs. Ayres received me with the courtesy of a vanished age. Roderick, her son, kept his glove on his burned paw and his eyes on the middle distance; he had flown in the war and come down badly, and now ran an estate that was eating him alive. Caroline, the daughter, was tall, bony, kind, and joked the way people joke who have stopped expecting to be happy — I liked her at once, and went on liking her, which is my whole problem, as you shall see.

The dog that bit the maid had bitten him out of character, everyone said. Betty the maid was sulky, and said the house had *hustled* her. I gave her a shilling for the fright and thought no more of it.

I should have thought more of it. I am a scientist, after all. We are the worst ones for it.

It began, as these things do, with the bells. Hundred Bough Hall had an old system of nursery bells, disconnected since Victoria, a board of ivory tags in the pantry: *Day Nursery, Night Nursery, Schoolroom*. One night in March they all rang at once, long and merry, like a troop of monkeys let into a campanile. The wire was found snapped in three places. The footman went to the dogs; Roderick went grey.

"The house is doing it," he told me, gripping his burned paw. "You'll say nerves, Doctor. My mother says nerves. But something in this house *pulls* at me. It took my strength when the debts came. It sits with me while I do the accounts and it drinks me like tea. And lately it's stopped bothering to hide it. Last night it pressed so hard on my chest I couldn't —" He stopped. He was a brave monkey, and it cost him something to stop.

In April the schoolroom bell rang again and again, always between two and three in the morning, and when Caroline went up at last with a lamp, the door — locked since the war — stood open, and there was a smell of camphor and warm milk and bananas ripening. The schoolroom had not held bananas since 1912.

It was Mrs. Ayres who finally told me, in the greenhouse, among her orchids, with her chin up.

"There was another child," she said. "Before Roderick. Before Caroline. My first — Suki. A little langur, six years old, born with — with a hindrance. She couldn't speak. She couldn't grip the bough, so we kept her to the nursery floor. She was never *right*, Doctor, though I loved her as the sun. She caught the diphtheria in 1912 and died in that schoolroom, quite alone, while I was downstairs lying to guests about her existence. We shut the door on all of it. We Ayreses are famous for shutting doors."

"They sometimes open," I said, idiot doctor, thinking myself profound.

In May, Roderick set fire to his own room — fighting, he swore to the last, something that had hold of his paw — and was carried off to the private asylum at Leamhurst, and the county said *poor devil* and the bank said *about time*. I drove Mrs. Ayres to see him once. He was gentler there, and fatter, and did not ask after the Hall, which she said was the kindest thing he could have done.

After that the house grew bolder. Bangings in the night. Cold patches on the nursery stair. We held a séance — I, man of stethoscope, presiding like a fool over the parlourmaid's table, while the medium, a jaded old patas monkey, went through her range of cracks and groans, and then stopped, quite suddenly, and put her hand to her own cheek as if struck by something small, and would not continue. "There is a child," she said on the doorstep, packing her bag. "But Doctor — it isn't doing this for attention. It's doing it as *work*. Somebody's set it a task. It grips the house like a trainee grippers a bough." She looked at me longer than I liked. "Tell the family to look to their own."

Mrs. Ayres did not take my advice. In October they found her in the old nursery passage at the top of the house, in her nightclothes, her face turned to the little door with its worn low sill, wearing an expression I can only call *summoned* — and she was gone. Her heart, I wrote on the certificate, having no column for the rest.

Caroline and I, two dull sensible monkeys in the wreckage, drew together in the way of the last figs on a tree. We spoke of marriage — circled it, carefully, each afraid to be the first to look silly. In the end it went wrong over nothing: a message not passed on, a coat not sent for, my pride, her pride, that terrible politeness langurs have. I left for a fortnight's conference, and we wrote each other stiff little letters, and when I came back she had put the Hall up for sale and taken to walking the top corridor at night, "because," she told the cook, "someone ought to keep the little one company, and it may as well be someone who can't sleep either."

I meant to go to her that last night. I got as far as the drive and turned round, telling myself it was too late to call. The truth — and I have had years in which to turn this over like a suspicious banana — is that something in me did not wish to share her with the house, and something else in me was doing the sharing already, without my leave.

She went over the landing balustrade at some hour before dawn. The inquest said misadventure; sleepwalking; grief. The cook said the nursery bell rang all that night, soft and quick, like a monkey gathering. I said nothing at all. I identified her, signed the papers, and drove home, and was sick in my own hedge, and this is the first time I have set it down.

That was ten years ago. Hundred Bough Hall stands empty — the bank cannot sell it; nobody will stay a month. I pass it on my rounds. Last autumn I let myself in with the old key I never gave back, and walked up through the cold rooms to the nursery passage, and sat on the worn sill with my coat about me, listening.

The house was quiet. I remember being faintly disappointed — and then, in the black glass of the window, seeing the doorway behind me reflected, and in the doorway a small grey shape, standing patiently as a child kept from its mother's party. I did not turn round. Turning round is for monkeys who still believe what they see.

I raised my hand instead — and the little shape in the glass raised *its* hand, and it was not small at all, and it was wearing my face.

I have thought, since, of that plaster acorn in my drawer, broken off and pocketed in 1919 by a poor chimp who wanted a piece of the big house so badly. I have thought of grips, and of what longs to grip, and of a child that was given a task by someone who never knew he'd set it. But I am a scientist. I only write down what happened.

I went out, locking the door softly, the way you leave a nursery.

And behind me, patient as ever, the little stranger came.
