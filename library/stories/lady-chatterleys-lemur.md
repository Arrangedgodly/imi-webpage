# Lady Chatterley's Lemur

*A parody of "Lady Chatterley's Lover"*

Ours is essentially a tragic age, so we refuse to take it tragically — especially those of us who live on in the old jungle estates, where ivy climbs the walls and banana warehouses creep up the valley.

Constance Chatterley — Lady Chatterley to the troop — was a healthy, good-tempered young capuchin raised with the new ideas: that a female monkey might think for herself, chatter for herself, and pick her own fruit. She had married Sir Clifford Chatterley, a howler monkey of ancient family, just before the Great Jungle War; and in Flanders a bursting vine dropped him from the canopy, and his hind legs never gripped a branch again. They gave him the Barkby estate and a wheeled branch-chair; Lady Chatterley gave him her devotion.

Between them sat the fact that was never spoken: Clifford would have no heirs, and his wife, still in her second decade, was a beautiful banana hung forever out of reach.

"It is the life of the mind that counts," Clifford declared, taking to writing tales of the smart set — polished little stories of monkeys visiting one another's nests, full of irony and empty of jungle. The reviewers admired them, because he was a Chatterley, and because nothing delights the troop more than a fashionable ape who has been safely unlucky.

For a season there was a playwright — a quick-eyed macaque named Michaelis, who came to stay, made Clifford laugh, and told Connie she was the one unexplored branch on the estate. The affair was brief, baffled, and instructive in the manner of cold figs: he wanted admiration, took a full portion, and left behind the knowledge that a female monkey may be perfectly faithful and perfectly starved at the same time.

Her father warned her to beware the creeping paralysis of the old jungle — and of her own heart; and indeed Connie was growing vague and thin, her fur dulling at the tips. Clifford, in genuine distress, suggested that if a child came he would raise it as his own — provided it was done "properly," meaning discreetly, with some half-bald baron. There is a kind of monkey who wants the fruit without ever climbing. On the subject generally he was perfectly modern: grooming, he said, was like the monsoon — dull, necessary, and best discussed by nobody.

The hill above the wood settled the question of the chair. Clifford's branch-chair, taxed with the slope, stuck, hissed, and began to slide; and the gamekeeper, coming out of the pheasant-run, set his shoulders under the whole machine — baron, wheels, and grievance — and lifted it level, silent as a fencepost.

"You are a distinct acquisition to the estate," Clifford admitted, wheeling himself away. But being carried so easily by a silent lemur with staring eyes did something to a howler's pride that he mentioned to nobody but Mrs. Bolton, and then for an hour.

Meanwhile — the jungle being wiser than the plans of howlers — Connie took to walking in the old wood at the edge of the estate, past the sheds where the great trees had fallen to the teak trade. And there she came upon the gamekeeper's hut, and the gamekeeper.

He was a ring-tailed lemur, strange and still as a shadow, with eyes that saw straight through fur. Mellors kept the pheasants, the paths, the traps, and his own counsel, speaking thick jungle pidgin when he spoke at all.

"Tha'rt lookin' at th' birds," he said, not turning round. "They'n not for walkin' in on."

Married once and cruelly unlucky in it, he had taken the hut to be alone with his animals and his silence — a monkey gone wild to save his soul. And Connie, starving without knowing quite what for, kept coming back to the wood.

The first time he was merely rude. The second, indifferent. But there is no armor against a friendless female in a spring wood. One afternoon, in the rain, he held the door for her, their eyes met, and the whole jungle shifted on its branch.

He groomed her — properly, tenderly, the way one monkey grooms another when it means it — parting the fur at her shoulder, finding where she had been carrying her sadness. Connie discovered she had a body the way she had an estate: in her keeping all along, and never once visited. They shared the first ripe banana of his patch. They built a nest of ferns against the rain, and she laughed — laughed — for the first time in years.

That early summer she came upon him with the pheasant chicks — a soft, whistling handful of them, running through his fingers and back to the coop. She stood at the hut door watching: a lemur with eyes like moons, being climbed on by birds.

"Tha sees," he said, holding out a fistful of chick, "they'n no fear of me. Fear's a thing tha teaches."

The chicks cheeped. Connie, who had spent five years being carefully taught, went home very thoughtful.

Meanwhile at Barkby, Clifford had acquired a nurse — Mrs. Bolton, a fussy macaque who wore his dependence like new spring fur — and grew plump on her care. She had buried a husband of her own and found in waiting on the baron a slow, comfortable revenge on fate: she fed him, scolded him, praised him, and let him be a baby, which no howler can be in public. Between them the treehouse ran so smoothly that neither of them needed Lady Chatterley much — which was the whole of the arrangement, if either had looked. It was Mrs. Bolton who now heard about the mines and the talk of the valley; Clifford grew genuinely attached to her, as a howler will to any echo.

When Connie's sister Hilda arrived to inspect — a gibbon of iron who announced that no sister of hers would be found lying about the keeper's cottage — Connie had already decided. She was with child: a small, astonishing fact she carried like a ripe fig. She told Clifford from abroad, by letter, and he received it as a proprietor might — better a stranger's cub than no cub, provided the troop observed no irregularity. The troop, of course, observed everything except what mattered.

"I shall never believe you don't care for me," Clifford told her, when Venice was mentioned. "But do go."

She went as far as the wood. There the keeper told her his news: his wife had returned to plague him, the village had begun to talk, and he would give up the keep and wait for the divorces that let them marry on level ground.

"Tha'lt want me to write?" he said. "I can write, when it's worth it."

"Write," said Connie.

When the divorces were mentioned at Barkby, Clifford retired into his howler's dignity. A Chatterley of Barkby did not sell the wood to be laughed at, and did not grant a freedom to be laughed at either; he would not release her, and would merely outlive the question — which is the oldest of the howlers' estates.

The letter came weeks later, in the keeper's slow hand, ending with words the gentle reader will recognize, however far they have been swinging-armed from home:

"Tha'lt forget me a bit, an' I shan't forget thee. If monkeys could go gentle, an' womonkeys could trust 'em, we'd build such a jungle. John Thomas — that is, Tail-o — says farewell to Lady Jane, but not for long. An' what th' 'ell: put a bit of heart in it, an' we'll all come ripe together."

Which is more than can be said for most letters from the colonies, and indeed for most colonies.
