# Why Didn't They Ask Gibbons?

*A parody of "Why Didn't They Ask Evans?"*

Bobby Jones — fourth son of the vicar of Marchnut, golfer of stubborn hopelessness, and a young chimpanzee of no ambitions whatsoever — had climbed down the sea cliff to look for a lost ball when he found, instead, a dying monkey.

The stranger lay on the rocks below the ninth hole, crumpled as a dropped scarf. His eyes opened.

"Why didn't they ask Gibbons?" he said, quite clearly, and died.

In his pocket was a photograph of a beautiful she-monkey, and nothing else. A stranger in a linen suit — a silky white sifaka with the best manners Bobby had ever been failed by — volunteered to sit with the body while Bobby ran for the doctor. His name, he said, was Roger Bassington-Screech, and Bobby liked him instantly, which should have been warning enough.

At the inquest, a large comfortable female named Mrs. Cayman identified the dead monkey as her brother Alex Pritchard. Bobby, watching the newspapers, was troubled. The woman in Bobby's photograph and the woman who now wept for her brother were not the same animal. And when he compared the photo with the one printed in the press, the truth was worse: they were not even the same *photograph*. Somebody had swapped it, and the only monkey who could have swapped it was the charming sifaka who had sat alone with the body and its pockets.

Bobby told all this to his oldest friend, Lady Frances "Frankie" Derwent — a young bonobo heiress with a mind like a well-thrown coconut — and then two things happened quickly. First, Bobby was offered a mysterious splendid job in Buenos Aires and, refusing it, drank half a bottle of banana beer that nearly killed him: morphia, said the doctor. Second, Frankie decided they were going to solve a murder, since the police had declined to, and neither of them had anything better to do that summer.

"You realize," said Bobby, "that somebody tried to poison you."

"Poison *you*, darling. I've merely been threatened with inheritance." Frankie, who could arrange anything, arranged the rest.

The dead man, they worked out, was no Pritchard but Alan Nutcarstairs — a traveller, a hunter, once the trusted agent of the millionaire gorilla John Sago, who had lately died believing himself cancer-ridden and left seven hundred thousand banana bonds by will to a beautiful widow named Mrs. Templeton. The will had been witnessed at the deathbed by the Templetons' cook and gardener. Nutcarstairs had smelled something and gone sniffing — and Nutcarstairs was now dead at the foot of Marchnut cliff, and his last words were about Gibbons, and nobody named Gibbons would come forward.

The trail led to Merroway Court, where the Bassington-Screeches lived: poor Henry, the elder brother, sinking into addiction to poppy-sap; his wife Sylvia; and Roger, the silky sifaka, charming as ever. Nearby stood the Grange, a rest-canopy run by Dr. Nicholson and his sad young wife Moira — and Moira, Frankie discovered with a thump of the heart, was the woman in the original photograph. Nutcarstairs had carried her picture to his death.

"I am so frightened," Moira told Frankie, at the fete, in a voice like rain on leaves. "My husband wants me dead. You are the only monkey I dare tell."

Frankie believed her for a day and a half, which was a day and a half longer than Frankie usually believed anybody. Meanwhile, to get inside Merroway Court, she had staged a car accident at its gates with the help of their friend Badger — a stammering young baboon with a striped muzzle and a garage, hopelessly in love with Frankie since a garden party in 1931 — and lay in state in the best bedroom, being invalided at strategically.

It was Roger who found the trap closing first. He was too quick, was Roger — he bundled Bobby off the Grange grounds in a van, and lured Frankie to a lonely cottage as "Dr. Nicholson," and tied them both in the attic with the shutters nailed; and the two of them sat in the dark conducting the first entire honest conversation of their friendship.

"If it's the Sago will," said Bobby, "then the deathbed will was forged. And a forged deathbed will needs witnesses who never actually saw Sago. A cook and a *gardener*."

"Servants hired a month before," said Frankie. "Oh, Bobby. *That's* what he was asking. When Nutcarstairs lay dying, he wasn't raving. He'd worked it out and he was telling us the test that would break the case open. The lawyers asked the cook and the gardener to witness a dying millionaire. But there was a parlourmaid in that house — a real servant, who knew Sago's face on sight. Why didn't they ask *her*? Because she'd have looked at that deathbed and said, 'That is not Mr. Sago.'"

They whiled away the hours of captivity putting the rest of it together, on the theory that a monkey facing a possible dawn should not waste it on silence. The Grange, Bobby had found while snooping, kept a private dispensary and a locked west wing and a ledger with two sets of figures — poppy-sap enough in it, at clinical prices, to float half the fashionable addiction in the county. And it had been Roger, they now saw, who had done the floating: the Grange supplied the want, and the want supplied the money, and the money — seven hundred thousand banana bonds of it, if the Sago will stood — was meant to supply the escape. Henry Bassington-Screech, poor nodding Henry, had not been a partner in any of it. He had been the *bank*, milked by his own brother, and it surprised neither Bobby nor Frankie in the attic that night to learn, a week later, that Henry had been found dead in the wood with a gun beside him and a note in a hand that wrote a very steady imitation of his shaking one.

The attic door opened. It was Badger, stammering horribly, with a crowbar — he had come about the car, he said, and nobody, he said, had p-p-paid him, and he had heard v-voices. Badger got them out. Roger Bassington-Screech, as it turned out, had already gone, unhurried, like silk off a table.

The parlourmaid's name, when they traced her, was Gladys Gibbons — and she had married, and she was, at that very moment, the housekeeper at Bobby's own vicarage, baking scones two rooms from where this whole business had begun, a monkey so unremarkable that a murderer had planned a seven-hundred-thousand-banana fraud and never once thought about her. "That poor gentleman," said Gladys, when they asked. "Mr. Sago, away up in town? I knew his face well; he used to notice my fur. The monkey in that bed wasn't him, and I did say so at the time, and they gave me notice for it."

Moira Nicholson — who was Mrs. Templeton, who was Roger's partner in everything — arrived at Frankie's cottage next day with a look of wounded loveliness and a thermos of coffee. Frankie, who had by now catalogued the entire species of wounded loveliness, held the cups while the police held Moira; and in Moira's little beaded bag was enough poison to finish the household. She did not weep. She said, in a flat small voice that was the first honest thing anyone had heard from the whole enterprise: "He said we should be rich. He never said we should be *careful*. I always told Roger — I always said, the quiet ones, the ones with the scones — you must never, never overlook the ones with the scones."

Roger wrote from the Southern Canopy, some months later, a letter in beautiful silk prose, confessing to the murders of John Sago, of Alan Nutcarstairs, and of his own brother Henry, "whose habit I fed as a garden is watered." It was, Frankie said, the only sincere thing he ever composed.

They were married in the autumn, Bobby and Frankie — Badger gave the bride away, having been promised the whole wedding's motoring, and giving one another away being, Frankie said, the entire basis of their acquaintance.

"Best kind of marriage," said Badger loyally at the reception, "b-b-built on a corpse."

"On a *question*, Badger," said Frankie. "The corpse was only the punctuation. And do you know, the strange thing is this: the whole seven-hundred-thousand-banana plot, three murders, an attic, a faked motor accident and my entire acting career — all of it turned on one quiet monkey with a baking tin. Why didn't they ask Gibbons?"

"Because," said Bobby, who had thought about it on many cliffs since, "clever criminals plan for the monkeys who matter. And then the jungle, which is wiser than they are, always — *always* — seats the truth next to the scones."
