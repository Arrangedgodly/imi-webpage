# Scratch-22

*A parody of "Catch-22" by Joseph Heller*

It was love at first sight. The first time Yossarian the howler monkey saw the chaplain — a shy, velvet-gray loris with enormous apologetic eyes — he fell madly in love with him.

Yossarian was in the troop hospital on Pianosa, off the shoulder of the jungle, with a pain in his liver that was not jaundice. If it turned to jaundice, they could treat it. If it did not, they could not. So he lay in bed, free of flying missions, not dying, which suited him fine. To pass the time he censored letters, blacking out whole paragraphs as a public service and signing the worst ones "Washington Gibbon," until two C.I.D. apes came hunting Washington Gibbon, found each other instead, and arrested each other.

"The Ocelots are trying to snatch me," Yossarian explained to the chaplain. "Everyone is trying to snatch me."

"Why should they snatch you?" asked the chaplain, bewildered.

"Because I'm pelting them with overripe bananas! They're animals! And I'm the only one with the sense to be scared about it."

There was a dead flyer in the ward, too — the Flier in White, wrapped from crown to toe in fresh bandages. He never moved; unwrapped at last, he proved to be nothing at all: the bandages had been wound neatly around one very carefully peeled banana.

The war was going beautifully. Colonel Cathcart, a burly gibbon who wanted to be a general, kept raising the number of missions — forty, fifty, sixty, seventy — hoping each might get his fur pictured in the Dispatch. He kept a ledger of his life with two columns, dangers and feathers, and fifty-five missions was a feather while sixty was a danger, and nobody was ever told which number was current until after the briefing.

"Who's trying to kill you?" Clevinger demanded, when Yossarian said so.

"Every ape in the jungle but me. Look at Cathcart — he keeps raising the missions."

"Nobody's trying to kill *you*," said Clevinger.

"Then why do they keep shooting at me?"

"They're shooting at *everyone*."

"And what difference does that make?"

Clevinger, who had a lawbook for every occasion, vanished into a cloud over the ridge and was never found. Lieutenant Scheisskopf, who had won the war for himself by wiring his flyers together in threes for parade turns, was promoted above his own astonishment. Major Major Major Major — born a Major, named Major, made Major by a clerical ape with a sense of humor — could be seen in his office only when he was out, and was in whenever any ape wished to see him, and so spent the war alone and punctual.

Yossarian went to Doc Daneeka, the marmoset medic, who sat in the shade wrapped in his own tail like a scarf.

"Doc," said Yossarian, "the mites are driving me crazy. That's a scratch, isn't it? A flyer with mites is grounded."

"Sure it's a scratch," Doc Daneeka said. "But first the flyer has to report the mites to me. And any flyer who reports his own mites is clearly not scratching hard enough to have real mites. So he's sane, and he flies."

"That's some scratch," Yossarian observed.

"It's the best scratch there is," Doc Daneeka agreed. "It specifies that a concern for your own fur in the face of real and immediate dangers is the process of a rational monkey."

Doc Daneeka was later declared dead, having been logged — against his protests — aboard a flight that ditched at sea. He sat very much alive in the sun while paperwork declared otherwise; his wife took the insurance, wept sincerely, and moved to a grove where the officers were nicer, writing to him once, fondly. "I'm dead," Doc told anyone who would listen. "Can't any of you apes see I'm dead?"

Milo Minderswing, the spider monkey mess officer, was making everyone rich. "Everybody has a share!" he cried. Milo had cornered the banana market from Pianosa to the Mango Isles; he bought the crop green, sold it riper, and sold the peels back to the growers as fertilizer. When he had cornered the cotton crop too, and could not unload a boll of it, he served it to the mess chocolate-covered — "high in cellulose and morale."

"Milo," Yossarian howled, when the raiders came one dawn and hit their own grove with jackfruit, "you're bombing your own squadron!"

"It's strictly business," Milo said. "The Ocelots contracted me for the north groves. Then I contracted the Ocelots for the anti-jackfruit screens. Everybody has a share. What's good for the troop is good for Milo, and what's good for Milo is good for the troop."

Nately — a warm young chimp of excellent family, in love with a tired outlying she-ape who slept and slept and woke only to reject him — was killed on a milk run. His she-ape, hearing the news, grieved violently, held her fur-wrapped heart, and thereafter followed Yossarian through the camp with a fruit knife, springing from doorways. Even grief, in Pianosa, could be reassigned.

But it was Snowden who ended the war for Yossarian — Snowden, the tiny tail-gunner, hit over Avignon. Yossarian glided down and knelt in the wreck of leaves with trembling paws.

"I'm cold," Snowden whimpered. "I'm cold."

"There, there," said Yossarian. "There, there." He bound the visible scratch on Snowden's leg — the wrong one. Then he lifted the little bark flak-jacket, and Snowden's secret spilled out: beneath the fur he was small and bald and cold to the roots — a very young monkey, no more, who wanted his troop.

"I'm cold," Snowden whimpered. "I'm cold."

"There, there," Yossarian said, because that was all there was to say. And Snowden's secret was his to carry thereafter, like a stone under his ribs: a monkey was mostly fur and warmth, and warmth was something you got from your troop.

So Yossarian stopped flying. He walked about the camp backward, so no one could say he was running away, and refused his missions cheerfully. The chaplain, meanwhile, was himself arrested for interrogation — accused of intercepting Major Major's letters as Washington Gibbon, and of claiming, suspiciously, to be the chaplain. Cathcart offered Yossarian a deal: a ticket home, if only he would praise the troop, tell everyone what a chimp-ion the Colonel was, and how he loved flying.

And Yossarian, remembering Snowden, looked at the gibbon's plume, and the offer, and the launch towers — and turned it down flat. He went instead to the hospital, to sit out the war among the quietly ill — and from a doorway sprang Nately's whore, fruit-knife flashing, howling her widow's howl, and Yossarian ran for his life down the corridor with the knife whistling past his ears.

That night the chaplain came running through the tents, robes flying.

"Yossarian! News! Orr is safe! You remember Orr, who crashed into the lagoon every third flight? He was practicing! He paddled a raft of durian husks all the way to the Mango Isles, where the 'nanas hang like moons! Yossarian — he made it! Leap, I tell you! Leap!"

Yossarian sat up. The whole crazy canopy of the war swung overhead, and beyond it, yellow and possible, hung the Mango Isles.

"Well," said Yossarian, "if a silly little monkey like Orr can do it —"

He ran through the camp with the whole troop howling at his heels, up the tallest kapok to the highest launch tower, past Cathcart's roars, past Milo's contracts, past the C.I.D. apes arresting each other in the dark.

"Yossarian!" they howled below.

And Yossarian leapt.
