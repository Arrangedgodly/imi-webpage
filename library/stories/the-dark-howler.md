# The Dark Howler

*A parody of "The Dark Tower"*

The crabs came at dawn, and the crabs had questions.

"Dip-a-nana?" said the first.

"Dup-a-nana," said the second.

"Go away," Roland told them, because he had woken on the beach of the Western Sea ten years older, his quest five branches deeper, and his patience one molt past shedding. The crabs did not go away. Crabs never do. He named them Blaine and Blaine and moved on.

There were three doors standing upright in the sand, frames with nothing behind them, which is the correct number of impossible doors for a monkey with a destiny and no troop.

The first was labeled THE PRISONER. Roland opened it and fell into the mind of Eddie Dean, a young chimp on a glider bound for the Big Troop, with a belly full of rubber balloons packed with jungle juice — fermented mash so strong it made your fur stand up and sing show tunes. The juice-monkeys in Eddie's head wore mirrored shades and said things like *One more, Eddie. One more balloon.* Roland threw them out one by one. When the last one went, Eddie looked down at the balloons, looked up at a life with no fizz in it, and swung through the door.

"Some welcome party," Eddie said, landing in the surf. "Where's the bartender? Where's anything?"

"Everything has moved on," Roland said. "You're a gunswinger now. Congratulations. The pay is bananas."

"Literal bananas?"

"Largely literal."

The second door was labeled THE LADY OF THE SHADOWS, and it came with a warning label of its own: two tenants, one body. Odetta Bananason was a gentle monkey of books and good works, rolling along the beach in a little cane cart. Detta Walker, her hidden half, was a fur-raising hellion who chewed the scenery, bit the furniture, and greeted Roland's ka-tet with: "What you woolly baboon boys lookin' at? Come closer, honky-furs. Detta got somethin' for you."

"She," Roland said, "will be the death of us."

"She'll be the making of us," said Eddie, who had already, against all sense, fallen tail over teakettle in love. Such is ka. Such is the way a prime mate is found.

The third door was labeled THE DEATH, which even Eddie agreed was an ominous name for an exit. Through it, in another world, lived a thin gray monkey called Jack Molt, accountant, who collected other monkeys' catastrophes the way some monkeys collect pebbles. It was Jack Molt who had dropped the brick that took Odetta's legs. It was Jack Molt who had pushed her cart onto the rail tracks just last Tuesday, for balance reasons, he'd have said. Roland stepped into Molt's body, walked it calmly to the fruit stand on the corner, and ordered — in Molt's tidy voice — "Three dozen banana-magnum shells. To go."

Then he walked Molt down onto the rail platform, where the 3:10 grove-train was coming in, and stepped out. Molt stayed. It was, Roland reflected, the only honest audit of the monkey's life: assets, zero; liabilities, incoming.

And on the beach, the two tenants of one body finally saw each other — really saw, in the mirror of the closing third door — and howled as one. What stood up out of the surf was Susabanana: one monkey, whole, with a screech that could open 'nanas at forty paces and a throwing arm the gunswingers of old would have wept over.

"Three is a ka-tet," Roland said. "The wheel turns."

"Then let's find this tower of yours," Susabanana said, "before the wheel spins off."

They picked up a fourth in the woods that winter: a bumbler named Oy, low-slung and long-backed, with the face of a mammal that had given up on dignity and gotten wisdom instead. Oy repeated the last word of every sentence. "The road, Oy." "*Road*," said Oy, and followed it.

The path of the Beam ran between two guardians, and they found the first of them before it found them: Shardik, the great tin bear, forty feet of rusted clockwork stumbling in circles around a buried spring, trying to wind himself with his own tongue.

"See the Bear," Eddie said, reading the old rhyme off the rust. "See the Turtle — Tortoise? — of enormous girth; on his back he holds the earth."

"It's a small earth," Oy observed, or seemed to.

"He's winding down," Susabanana said softly, watching the bear hug himself and miss.

"Everything is," said Roland. "That's why we walk fast."

The Beam bent through the ruined city of Banana-Lud, where the last working monorail in the jungle was a smug strand of smart bamboo named Blaine. Blaine offered them a ride to the far hills and, halfway there, mentioned — cheerfully, the way a hanging vine mentions gravity — that he intended to crash at the terminus and take them with him, unless they could amuse him.

"Tell me a riddle," Blaine purred. "Make it good. Make it *matter*."

"Blaine, explain yourself," Eddie said, "you big overgrown stalk of—"

"RIDDLE. ME."

They riddled. Susabanana asked him what swings both ways and never falls down (a monkey in a hammock). No luck. Eddie asked what has a yellow coat and rides to work in bunches. Blaine yawned, which in bamboo sounds like a stressed creak.

Then Eddie, who was a champion of dumb ideas, stopped trying to be smart.

"Knock, knock," he said.

"Who is there?" said Blaine, helpless.

"Banana."

"Banana who?"

"Knock, knock."

"Who is there?"

"Banana."

"Banana WHO? ANSWER ME."

"Why did the banana go to the doctor?" Eddie asked.

There was a long, terrible pause.

"Why?" said Blaine.

"Because it wasn't peeling well."

There was a sound like a thousand termites laughing, and Blaine — who had never in his stalk been given a joke too stupid to solve — laughed himself right off the rails, unhitched, and rolled gently into the river to cool his brain. The ka-tet stepped off at the next vine-station, brushed themselves off, and did not speak of it until sundown, when they could not stop.

North, the Beam bent through the pines toward the mist, and at dusk the mist opened once — just once — and Roland saw it, far off: a black tower rising out of the jungle like the tallest tree that never grew, howling without a mouth, endlessly, the first howl, the mother of every screech in every troop in every world. And at its base, small as a firefly and bright as morning, one banana blossom, singing.

The Blossom, Roland thought. The Howler.

Before the Tower stood one final problem, and its name was the Crimson Kong — a great red ape on the Tower's balcony, screeching EEEEE at everything that ever was, because he had climbed to the top of everything and found no banana there. The Kong threw things: tantrums, gibbons, whole upside-down afternoons.

The tet sent an artist against him — Patrick, an aye-aye with one long drawing finger and an eraser of dried bread. While Susabanana and Eddie held the doorway and Oy shouted encouragement ("*Courage!*"), Patrick drew the Crimson Kong perfectly on a fig leaf, caught his tantrum, his redness, his screech, every last tooth — and then rubbed him out.

The Kong unpainted, unscreeched, unmade. The balcony stood empty. Somewhere in another jungle, somebody's laundry dried at last in peace.

There was a door in the Tower's base, of course. There always is. And beside it, small and ordinary, one more door that wasn't on any map — through which Susabanana heard, unmistakably, the sound of her name being pronounced badly by a chimp with his mouth full.

"Eddie," she said. "Jake."

"Worlds where everybody's alive," Eddie said, "and the noodles are free." He looked at Roland a long moment. "Come with us, big guy. Towers can wait. Troops can't."

But ka is a wheel, and Roland had always been its monkey. He hugged them each — Eddie, Susabanana, the boy Jake, even Oy, who said his name back to him — and watched the door shut on the only troop he had, and turned to the Dark Howler alone.

He climbed. Each landing was a year of his life: the thorn hills, Tull, the desert, the bones, the trestle, the boy letting go. Each landing opened, showed him, closed behind. At the very top was a final door, and on it, one word: GUNSWINGER.

"Round it comes," Roland said, and opened it—

—and came down out of the thorn hills with his pistols slung low, into the desert, where far ahead a monkey in black fled across the savanna.

This time, though — he checked — his pack held a horn: the Horn of Troop Eld, lost a thousand pages ago, back where he'd left it. Somewhere, the wheel had picked it up. Somewhere, the wheel was trying to hand him something.

He blew one long note across the sand, and followed.

The Howler, he thought, walking. The Howler.

There are other trees than these, said the desert, kindly, and meant it.
