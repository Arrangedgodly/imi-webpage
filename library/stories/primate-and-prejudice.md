# Primate and Prejudice

*A parody of "Pride and Prejudice"*

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single monkey in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a mate.

However little known the feelings or views of such a monkey may be on his first entering a neighborhood, this truth is so well fixed in the minds of the surrounding troops, that he is considered the rightful property of some one of their daughters — or nieces, or grand-nieces, or in Mrs. Bonnet's case, all five of them at once.

"My dear Mr. Bonnet," said Mrs. Bonnet one morning, grooming her husband's shoulders with more than usual enthusiasm, "have you heard? Netherfield Canopy has been let at last! A young male of large fortune from the north — a silverback, they say, with ten thousand bananas a year!"

"Ten thousand bananas," said Mr. Bonnet, turning a page. "That is indeed a great many peels. I hope Mrs. Bonnet records the number."

"How can you be so tiresome! You have no compassion on my poor nerves! A silverback with ten thousand bananas a year, and five daughters. Do the arithmetic, Mr. Bonnet!"

"Madam, I have every faith in your ability to do it for me."

The silverback was Mr. Darcy of Pemberley — and his best friend, the amiable Mr. Bingtree, lost no time in falling for Jane, the eldest and prettiest Bonnet sister. It was at the Meryton assembly, in the lantern-lit fig grove, that the whole troop first laid eyes on Darcy: proud, haughty, refusing every dance, towering under the torches like a statue carved from smugness.

At last Mrs. Hurst called out, "Do dance, Darcy! The troop is small, but surely some mother's daughter will do!"

Darcy's eye fell upon Elizabeth Bonnet, the second daughter, whose bright eyes and muddy petticoat — she'd walked three miles through jungle mud to see her sick sister, who was nursing a cold at Netherfield, where Elizabeth then sat out the whole visit refusing to be impressed — he had already catalogued with disapproval.

"She is tolerable," he said, "but not handsome enough to tempt me. Her fur is positively unruly. I am in no humor to give consequence to middle-troop monkeys who arrive caked in mud."

Elizabeth heard him. Elizabeth laughed. Elizabeth told the story to every monkey in Meryton — and there the prejudice was planted, right alongside the pride.

What followed was a season of monkey business. Mr. Wickham, a charming scapegrace of a macaque with a tragic tale about Darcy cheating him of his inheritance, dangled most agreeably before Elizabeth. At the Netherfield ball she was partnered, by pure mischief, with Darcy himself.

"We are each of an unsocial, taciturn nature, Miss Bonnet," said he, "unwilling to speak unless we expect to say something that will amaze the whole room."

"That is a very dismal picture, sir. I have always seen that you have a liking for contradiction."

"I have faults enough, madam. My temper would perhaps be called resentful. My good opinion, once lost, is lost forever."

"I cannot laugh at it, sir. You may safely hold me a fool — most monkeys in this county already do."

Meanwhile, to the distress of every Bonnet, Mr. Bingtree abruptly quit Netherfield for London — spirited away, it was quietly arranged, by Darcy and his sisters, who thought Jane too low in the branch-archy and too mild in her manner. Jane was packed off to the city to be sad politely among her aunt's fur brushes.

And into the gap came Mr. Collins — a pompous, toadying baboon of a clergyman, forever praising his patroness Lady Catherine de Bough, the most insufferable old mandrill in England — who proposed to Elizabeth in a speech of excruciating length.

"In the first place," said Collins, "my patroness instructed me to marry. In the second place, I assure you the bough will be very comfortable. In the third—"

"Sir, I am perfectly serious in my refusal."

"TO BE SURE, Lady Catherine predicted young females might refuse at first—"

"Sir! I am not a female to be refuted by repetition. I am a bonobo of decided opinions. NO."

Elizabeth's dearest friend, Charlotte Lucas — plain, sensible, seven-and-twenty, and burdened with no romantic illusions whatsoever — promptly married Mr. Collins herself. "I am not romantic, you know," she said. "I never was. I ask only a comfortable nest and a bough of my own." Elizabeth was horrified; Charlotte was lucid. It was Charlotte, in fact, who would shortly do Elizabeth the greatest favor of her life — by having married the baboon whose patroness she was then obliged to visit.

Then came Rosings, where Lady Catherine descended upon the party to inspect Darcy's poor relations like a customs officer. "You play and sing, do you? You ought to practice more. Are any of your younger sisters OUT, Miss Bonnet? All five out at once! Very odd."

Then came Pemberley — where Elizabeth, expecting to hate the great estate, found instead ten thousand branches of the finest timber, a river winding beneath, and a housekeeper who said, "He is the best master that ever lived — the gentlest groomer, the kindest hand with the little ones." And there came Darcy himself, suddenly humble, suddenly graceful, asking after her family as though they were a pleasure rather than a plague — and presenting his shy little sister Georgiana, who had heard so much of Miss Bonnet that she'd assumed Elizabeth must have six heads and was relieved to find only the one, with the bright eyes.

"Miss Bonnet," Darcy said at last, pacing before her, "I have struggled in vain. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you — in spite of your family's antics, your mother's nerves, your uncle in trade, and my own insufferable pride."

"You might have spared us both the speech's middle section, sir."

"I could not. My character required it. I am, you will find, a very poor conversationalist and a worse flatterer."

"Flattery! You could not have made me the offer of your hand in any possible way that would have tempted me to accept it. You were the last monkey in the world whom I could ever be prevailed on to marry — you, who ruined the happiness of my sister Jane, and Mr. Wickham—"

"Wickham!" Darcy cried. "He is the son of my father's steward, the finest-looking macaque in the county, and the most thorough rogue in it. He squandered his living, gambled away three thousand bananas, and tried to elope with my fifteen-year-old sister for her thirty thousand more."

"...Ah."

"Ah, indeed."

The letter came. The truth came — of Bingtree's removal, of Wickham's lies, of Darcy's silent, unbearably correct reasons. And when the youngest Bonnet, the silly Lydia, ran off with Wickham to sin in the city — no wedding, no nest, no future, Mrs. Bonnet taking to her bed with a shriek that frightened the figs from the tree — it was Darcy who quietly found them, paid the scoundrel's debts, bought the wedding, and sealed every mouth. He told no one. His aunt found out anyway.

Lady Catherine descended on Longbourn like a thunderclap. "I have been used, Miss Bonnet, to have my queries replied to! You are reported to be engaged to my nephew. I demand you deny it!"

"I am not engaged to him, madam — and if I were, you could not prevent it."

"Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted? He is to marry MY daughter! Obstinacy! Self-will! Are you resolved to have him?"

"I am only resolved to act in that manner which will constitute my own happiness, without reference to you, or to any monkey so wholly unconnected with me."

"I take no leave of you. I send no compliments to your mother. You deserve no such attention. I am seriously displeased."

Her ladyship departed in high dudgeon — and promptly reported the whole exchange to Darcy, as a scandal. It was the best service she ever did him: it proved to him that Elizabeth's heart might be winnable after all.

"You mean," Elizabeth said to Jane, when all was known, "that he did it all for me? That the proudest silverback in Derbyshire went begging favors of monkeys he despised, to save a family he had every reason to mock?"

"Lizzy! You love him."

"No," said Elizabeth, smiling. "I merely regard him with the calm indifference of a monkey who has decided she will accept him at the very next opportunity."

And when that opportunity came — Bingtree returned to Netherfield and proposed to Jane within a fortnight, and then Darcy, walking Elizabeth out among the orchards: "If your feelings are still what they were last April, tell me so at once. My affections and wishes are unchanged. But one word from you will silence me on the subject forever." — Elizabeth gave him that one word, and it was not "no."

"In vain I have struggled," Darcy repeated later, grooming her hair with inordinate tenderness. "It will not do. You have bewitched me, body and soul, and I love — I love — I love you."

"And I you," said Elizabeth. "From the very beginning — after the part where you insulted my fur."

It was, all the troop agreed, a match most happily made. Grumpy Darcy and lively Lizzy — pride and prejudice, groomed into primate and preference.
