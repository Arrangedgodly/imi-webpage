# Monkey and Son

*A parody of "Dombey and Son"*

Dombey sat in the corner of the darkened office of the great house of Monkey and Son — Wholesale Banana Merchants — and thought, as the firm had thought for generations, of one thing only. There were branches in every port and runners on every vine; 'nanas flowed through the counting-house like a yellow tide. But to Dombey, a tall, hard, gray-backed drill of a monkey, commerce was never about fruit. It was about the transmission of the name.

His wife had just died, and had done her part: she had presented him, at long last, with a son.

"A son," said Dombey to the infant. "Monkey and Son. Those three words convey the one idea of the house. The world has been made for the son, and the son to be the tail's continuation of the tail. You will be called Paul."

There was also a daughter, Florence, six years old — which is to say an accident of no commercial significance. Nobody was cruel to Florence in so many words; she was simply skipped, like a blank page in a ledger. She loved her little brother with the whole of her stopped-up heart, and the small monkey, odd and pale and old behind his months, loved her back with the only love in that house that worked.

"He has a fine old head," said Dombey, studying Paul.

"He has a very old old head, papa," said Florence gently.

Paul grew slowly and strangely. He asked questions like a dowager. "Papa," he said one evening, gripping his father's hard hand, "what is money?"

"Money, Paul, is so many 'nanas. It is power, Paul. It can do anything in the world."

"Anything, papa?" Paul considered the fire. "It can't make me strong and well, papa. It can't bring Mamma back. What is money after all?" — and Dombey, who could have bought the canopy and could not answer that, frowned at the fire instead.

To be made strong, Paul was sent first to the acid care of Mrs. Pipchin, a shriveled old she-ape with a cold grey eye who kept a moldering house at Brighton-beach and a philosophy ("monkeys are all wicked, and bananas are all mine") — and then, at six years old, six, to the great hothouse of learning of Doctor Blimber, where little monkeys were taken green and forced ripe by Christmas. It was a large garden-glass of a school where the thermometer of accomplishments was kept permanently too high; Cornelia Blimber, pale classics-presser, wrote extracts; and Paul, planted far too early in the forcing-bed, was expected to know Ptolemy, the Punic Vines, and the eight parts of fruit by the holidays.

"How many branches has the 'nana trade?" asked Doctor Blimber.

"As many as will hold," said Paul, who spent recess lying on his back watching the glass roof, asking the big boy Toots what the waves were saying. Toots adored him hopelessly and punctually — Toots adored everyone hopelessly and punctually — and could only ever say of anything, "Thank you, it's of no consequence."

The forcing worked; it killed what it ripened. Paul came home for the holidays brown at the edges like a bruised 'nana, and one evening, lying against Florence's shoulder with her name in his mouth — dear Floy, listen to the sea — he heard what he had always heard, the old, old fashion of the oldest fashion of all, and went with it. The whole house of Monkey and Son was moved by six-year-old grief; the clerks in the office wept openly; and Dombey, who had lost his investment in eternity, told himself he had lost a son, which shows how entirely he had missed having one.

Florence was left with her love running down the empty corridors like a light nobody lit.

The firm needed capital and continuation, so Dombey took a second wife, the beautiful, proud, penniless Edith Granger, a langur of such cold magnificence that everyone called the wedding a state treaty. It was, in fact, a purchase; Edith knew it, and married to escape her sponging mother, the mummified Mrs. Skewton ("Cleopatra," doped in ribbons, who called her son-in-law "my dearest Dombey" until she dehydrated entirely). In that house love was not discussed. What was discussed was obedience.

Edith's chief tormentor — the firm's great manager, Carker — was a long, yellow, smiling baboon with forty white teeth and a heart filed down to a hook. Carker grinned through every room of that marriage like moonlight through bars, flattering Edith's pride and Dombey's, managing each into worse. He was the only creature Dombey trusted, which was the house's final error, the firm having always confused teeth with loyalty.

It ended at the lips of a scandal. Edith, stung past endurance, cold past fury, fled the house in the appearance of flight with Carker: publicly linked to him, privately loathing him, buying her freedom with her name. "I will be shame in his house," she told him, "since I was never honor in it. But do not mistake me, dog: you may have helped ruin me, but you will never touch me — for that, you may tell him so from my lips."

Carker, who after all had wanted to touch, found his grin at last unheeded. He hunted her abroad, from spa to spa, to a hotel in Dijon where she was already gone — and turned to find Dombey in the doorway behind him, white with the one passion the house had never traded in. Carker fled into the yard, across the lines, in the roar and the steam of the junction; and the Great Trunk Line, the new iron vine that never stops for smiles, went through Carker on the turn, and cut the forty teeth off at the root.

The house of Monkey and Son came down soon after — not with a crash, but with a ledger closing; the 'nana tide had been running out for years while Dombey watched his name in the glass. Down with it went the office, the branches, the runners, the plate, the portraits of Dombey and Dombey and Dombey. That night Dombey, ruined, alone, remembering he had once struck Florence when she tried to love him — struck her, his one undepreciated asset, across her upturned face — put on his coat and walked to the river to finish the account.

But swinging past the counting-house as it emptied, going along the old familiar branches, his ear caught something he had never once stopped to hear: a small voice calling "Grandpa!" — no. "Papa!" For Florence was there, in the humbled little nest of her own marriage, with a child on her floor; and the child was named Paul; and Florence, who had been struck and skipped and ledgered out, had left her door open to the house that cast her off, all these years, the way the sky leaves its door open to the weather.

"Papa," said Florence, taking his hands in hers as he stood unable, "come in. You are old, and have been troubled. Be at rest. I am unaltered. I never changed to you — I never shall. Come in, papa, and let me be everything to you that Floy was to little Paul, and he to her. Come home."

He was humbled then, or he was merciful — which is one and the same thing in the pelt of an old drill. Dombey lived on in that little house by the sea, a great gray broken monkey, gentler every year, learning the branch-trade from his daughter's husband; and it was Walter Gay who ran the reconstituted firm — Walter, the clerk's son, once believed drowned on the ship *Son and Heir*, who came home rich in the only coin the old house had never dealt in, and wrote a new sign over the door, so that everyone who passed could read, in fair letters: no longer Monkey and Son, but Monkey & Sons; and first among the sons, the daughter.
