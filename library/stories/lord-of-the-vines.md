# Lord of the Vines

*A parody of "Lord of the Flies"*

The plane carrying the evacuees went down in the ocean, and a barrel of young chimps washed ashore on a forgotten jungle island. First out of the surf were Ralph and a fat, asthmatic chimp wearing thick spectacles, whom everyone called Piggy, because monkeys are not subtle.

"There are no grown-ups," Piggy observed, wiping his glasses on a wet leaf. "Someone has to organize."

They found a great conch shell on the lagoon beach. Ralph blew into it, and chimps came tumbling out of the treeline from every direction. It became the rule: whoever holds the conch speaks at the assembly, and everyone else grooms their own fur and listens politely.

"We ought to have a chief," said Jack, leader of a choir of red-clad chimps who had arrived marching in two neat lines and were now stripping off their cassocks in the heat. "I'm chapter chorister and head boy. I can sing C-sharp."

There was a vote. Ralph won, because he had the conch, and in chimp politics that is three-quarters of everything. Jack sulked, and Ralph, trying to be fair, gave him the hunters.

The rules were simple. They kept a signal fire burning on the mountain so passing ships could rescue them. They built shelters. They picked fruit. For a while it was the best troop ever — a fire, fruit, and no grown-ups to say *eat your leaves first*. Though it was Ralph, Simon, and Piggy who actually built the shelters, hauling log after log while the others bathed and bowled fruit rinds, and only Piggy saw that the real work of the troop was going begging.

Then a little chimp with a birthmark came gibbering to the assembly about a "beastie" in the dark, and everything began to unravel. And that very week came the worst quarrel: a ship appeared on the horizon — and the fire was out, because Jack had raided the fire-tenders away for his first hunt, and came back smeared with blood and chanting, carrying a roasted pig on a spit.

"We killed the pig! We bashed her and we had a feast!"

"There was a ship!" Ralph screamed, near tears. "A ship! Out there! And the fire was out — do you all want to be rescued or don't you? Do you want to be wild apes swinging in the dark forever?"

Jack struck him, apologized sideways, and hated him thoroughly from that hour.

Then a twin — Samneric, the two of them one chimp in policy — went to tend the fire at night and came screaming down the mountain: they had seen the beast, wings and claws, sagging in the dark. (It was the rotting corpse of an airman, whose parachute had tangled in the branches and bobbed in the wind like a puppet. Nobody climbed up to check. Beasts are much better cliffhangers.) The troop bolted to the beach, and Jack seized his moment: "My hunters will protect you from the beast! Come to my feast. Come to my end of the beach."

His hunters painted their faces with red and white clay, and behind the masks they were freed from shame. And one evening, in the panic of a storm, the hunters were so busy reenacting a pig chase that they beat a small blur of fur crawling through the circle — and it was the birthmark chimp, and he did not get up.

"We never done it," Jack said afterward, pale under his fur. "It was an accident. It was the beast."

"There is no beast," Ralph shouted, gripping the conch. "There is no beast, and the fire went out, and nobody cared!"

Only Simon, the slight, spooky-eyed chimp who fainted in the sun and knew things, went alone into the jungle to look. He found the sow's head the hunters had left on a stick, garlanded with flies, grinning in the green gloom — and in his fever the head spoke to him.

"You knew, didn't you?" said the Lord of the Vines. "I'm the reason why it's no go? Fancy thinking the beast was something you could hunt and kill! You knew, didn't you? I'm part of you. Close, close, close. I'm the reason why it's no go. Have a little fun, silly little monkey."

Simon climbed the mountain and found what the beast truly was: the dead airman, tangled and terrible and nothing but cloth and bone. He came down at the storm's height to tell them the good news — the beast is harmless, the beast is you — but he stumbled out of the dark at the wrong moment into the middle of the feast, and the chant was already rising.

"Kill the beast! Cut his throat! Spill his fruit juice!"

The troop, screaming, fell upon him. Afterward, no one would say his name. The rain washed the beach clean, and out on the water, the parachute-man drifted gently out to sea, bowing and bowing.

The conch broke the next day. Jack's tribe raided the beach for fire, and when Piggy went to reclaim his spectacles — for they had stolen the glasses to make flame, the conch being useless for that — he stood on the cliff path below the fort, holding the shell high, and made his last speech about rules, and which was better: to have laws and agree, or to hunt and kill?

"Which is better — to be a pack of painted apes, or sensible like Ralph is?"

High above, little Roger — who had once thrown stones at a younger chimp, aiming always to miss because the old world's invisible law still held his arm — leaned with both hands on the lever, and the great boulder thundered down. It struck Piggy, and he fell forty feet onto a red rock, shell and spectacles and all.

"See? See? That's what you'll get!" Jack shrieked, and Ralph ran, hunted through the jungle like a pig — with, he discovered at his hiding place, a stick sharpened at both ends waiting for him, exactly the way they had left the sow.

The tribe set the whole island alight to smoke him out, which was madness, because the fire burned the fruit trees they needed — and also, in the way of things, made a smoke column visible for miles.

Ralph burst from the burning treeline onto the beach, ready to fall, teeth bared at the spears behind him — and stopped. A naval officer stood in the sand, white uniform immaculate, boat grounded behind him.

"We saw your smoke. What have you been doing? Having a war or something?"

Ralph tried to answer and could not. He wept then — for the end of innocence, for Piggy, for the chimp with the birthmark, for the wise friend called Simon who had died trying to tell them there was no beast but themselves.

The officer, embarrassed, turned to the horde of painted little chimps.

"I should have thought," he said, "that a pack of British apes would have put up a better show than that."

And the little chimps, filthy and sobbing and glad it was over, followed Ralph aboard the ship — a broken troop, going home from an island that still smelled of ash and old bananas.
