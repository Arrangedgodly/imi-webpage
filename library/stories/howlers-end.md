# Howler's End

*A parody of "Howards End"*

Only connect the bough and the tendril, the root and the crown, the chimp and the chimp scratching beside him — that, Margaret Scratchleg was coming to believe, was the whole of her religion. Her sister Helen said it was the whole of indigestion.

The trouble began at a lecture. The Scratchleg sisters — Margaret, Helen, and their brother Tibby, who was mostly tongue — attended a debate on fig distribution, where Helen, seized by sudden rapture, nodded at a handsome young stranger across the fronds. By the end of the evening she had swung home with his leaf-umbrella, he had swung home with hers, and the sisters, being scrupulous, dispatched their Aunt Julip to return it.

Aunt Julip was met at the bark-stair of Howler's End by Charles Wilcox-el, who had been told to expect a Miss Scratchleg and assumed she was Helen, come to stalk his brother Paul. Charles rebuffed Aunt Julip at the threshold before she could finish a sentence about umbrellas; and the two families were off on exactly the wrong branch.

The truth was simpler and worse: Helen had swung to Howler's End, fallen into an entanglement with Paul, and been extracted again — all so fast that the family pretended it had never happened. Which was how the Wilcox-els and the Scratchlegs came to know one another, and how Margaret came to love the old matriarch, Ruth Wilcox-el.

Ruth was a howler in the old style, silver-gray and vast, who lived in a fig tree of enormous age with a jaguar's tooth set in the bark. She never argued; she simply knew things, the way fig trees know rain. She would say "the bough is dripping" and it would be, an hour before the rain arrived. She took Margaret walking without plans and told her, in fragments, of a childhood meadow and a hay-dry vine gone now; and Margaret, who talked of connection, found herself out-known by a monkey who had never once theorized.

One autumn, walking with Margaret past the great fig, she said suddenly: "If I could tell you, you would not laugh. You are the one who understands. Howler's End is not a tree. It is me."

She died that winter. And pinned to her will, in her shaky hand, was a note: *To Margaret Scratchleg I leave my fig tree, Howler's End.*

The Wilcox-els suppressed the note at once. You could not leave a tree like that to a stranger. It was, they agreed, best for all, especially themselves; the note was destroyed; and Henry Wilcox-el could not look Margaret in the eye for a month. But guilt being a climbing plant, old Henry, the silverback, found himself drifting toward Margaret's bower, then courting her, then marrying her, while his children shrieked that he had gone bananas.

"You are not marrying her," said his son Charles, "you are being planted."

"I am sixty and she is thirty," said Henry, "and it is nobody's branch but mine."

That same season the Scratchlegs lost their own nest. The lease of Wickham Place fell in, and the jungle of red rust came forward and swallowed the old garden — a fact the Wilcox-els found interesting only as figures. "They tell me your glade went for ninety thousand bananas," said Henry at breakfast. "The jungle wants it. The jungle always wants what it flattens."

Meanwhile Helen had taken up the cause of Leonard Branch, a wretched low-canopy clerk with a chronic cough, who had once lost his leaf-umbrella at the Scratchlegs' and to whom Helen — always susceptible to causes — had then and there given all her bananas, ruining him in the eyes of his employer. It was charity of the sort that flattens what it feeds. Leonard's mate Jacky, a large loud she-ape from the outer groves, trailed after him shrieking about better days, and one terrible evening at Evie's wedding feast she recognized Henry Wilcox-el, hung on his neck, and called him a pet name from ten years past. Henry, it seemed, had been climbing in the outer groves himself once.

Margaret stood very still under the lantern-fruits. "You have had your own life with the Jackys of this jungle," she said at last. "I have never once blamed you. But you sit there judging Helen and Leonard, and judging, and judging. This is not going on." She walked out into the dark canopy, and it was some weeks before Henry climbed, humbled and graying, to her bough with nothing to offer but himself. Which, it turned out, was enough.

Then came the reckoning. Helen, it emerged, was to have a kit — Leonard's kit, of one desperate night's mistaken comfort, after a day of misused charity — and she wrote from the far groves asking only to say goodbye to Howler's End before she left England forever. Henry forbade the house. A Scratchleg in that condition, under his fig? Unpardonable.

"Then I am unpardonable too," said Margaret, "and I am done with being managed. I am going to Helen. Come with me or do not, Henry, but hear this: you owe that family a debt you have never once priced, and it is not payable in bananas."

Margaret and Tibby lured Helen to Howler's End under pretence of settling the umbrella business once and for all, and shut the great gate behind her; and Helen, furious, cornered in the very tree she was heir to, broke at last and wept on Margaret's shoulder — and slept that night, for the first time in months, in Ruth's old room, while the tree dripped over all three of them an hour before the rain arrived.

And Leonard Branch, who had learned everything too late and blamed nobody correctly, came wandering up from the outer groves that same wet evening, mud to the chin, to say he was sorry for everything. He never said it. Charles Wilcox-el, armored in righteousness, hearing whispers of the kit and the name of his father's honor in it, shook the very branch Leonard stood upon — shook it to frighten him — and a rotten bough, ripe for years, chose that instant to fall.

It struck Leonard's shoulders. He folded, gently, like a leaf, and lay still at the foot of the fig with the jaguar's tooth looking down on him.

Charles was transported to the Edinburgh Zoo for it. Henry aged a decade in a season; his bark-gray fur went gray indeed; he sat by his own fires asking whether it was raining, when it had rained all night. And Margaret, who inherited Howler's End after all — the Wilcox-els at last too tired to lie about a note everyone had somehow always known of — did a curious thing: she kept the tree, and left it in trust to Leonard's orphaned son, a small steady ape with a cough, to be raised there with her own nephews and nieces — the low canopy and the high canopy, the howlers and the scratchers, all in one tree.

Helen's kit was born in the great fig's fork in the spring, squalling like the house it owned. Henry, doddering gently in the sun, was allowed to hold it, and said it had Helen's lungs.

"Only connect," Margaret said to Helen, as the year's figs ripened and the great boughs filled with screaming, laughing, grooming cousins. "It is not an order, liebling. It is only advice. But look what grows when anybody takes it."

The tree dripped, an hour before the rain arrived.
