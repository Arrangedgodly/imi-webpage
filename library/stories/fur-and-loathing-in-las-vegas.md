# Fur and Loathing in Las Vegas

*A parody of "Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas"*

We were somewhere around Barstow, on the edge of the desert, when the bananas began to take hold. I remember saying something like, "I feel a bit lightheaded; maybe you should drive..." And suddenly there was a terrible roar all around us and the sky was full of what looked like huge flying lemurs, all swooping and screeching and diving around the car, and a voice was screaming, "Holy mother of the jungle! What are these goddamn animals?"

Then it was quiet again. My attorney had taken his shirt off and was pouring beer on his chest to facilitate the tanning process. "What are you yelling about?" he muttered, staring up at the sun in wraparound Spanish sunglasses. "Never mind," I said. "It's your turn to drive." No point in mentioning the lemurs, I thought. The poor baboon will see them soon enough.

The trunk of the car looked like a mobile jungle pharmacy. We had two bags of overripe bananas, seventy-five pellets of mescaline, five dozen fermented figs, a salt shaker half full of dried papaya, a quart of toucan tequila, a case of Brazzaville beer, a pint of raw wasp honey, two dozen glowing amyls, and a whole coconut. Also plantains.

Not that we needed all that for the trip, but once you get locked into a serious fruit collection, the tendency is to push it as far as you can.

The only thing that really worried me was the fruit. There is nothing in the world more helpless and irresponsible and depraved than a monkey in the depths of a banana binge, and I knew we'd get into that rotten stuff pretty soon.

"What kind of story is this?" my attorney snarled. "The editors want a report on the Mint 400, the richest off-the-ground vine-swinging race in the desert. That's the assignment. And I need the fruit."

"As your attorney," he continued, "I advise you to eat the papaya and drive at top speed across the desert in a car full of fermenting figs, looking for the Jungle Dream. This is not a good town for psychedelic experimentation."

"Las Vegas is not a good town for anything," I said.

We checked into the hotel around dusk, and by the time the bellhop reached our room I was no longer entirely sure what a hotel was. The desk clerk was a lizard. I don't mean he seemed like a lizard; I mean the desk was staffed, as far as I could tell, by an actual iguana about nine feet high, wearing a visor, taking reservations in a slow grey voice. "He's fixing the races," I whispered. The attorney nodded gravely and tipped him a fermented fig.

The room had wall-to-wall carpet the color of a mashed papaya, and I spent some time lying on it, feeling the fibers groom my belly, while my attorney made a long-distance call to the district attorney. "Yes, we're here for the Mint 400," he said, "and as your attorney — wait, no — as a journalist I'd like to say that the D.A.'s office here has a fine reputation for corruption, and we intend to look into it personally." He hung up and announced we'd been invited to the District Attorney's narcotics conference, which was not for another four days, and which we were attending anyway, for material.

"You'd better take care of the maid," he added, gone suddenly serious. "I gave her the papaya salt. Told her it was baboon aphrodisiac. She's locked herself in the closet."

This is the problem, I reflected, flinging the closet door open for the terrified chamber-chimp, with letting an attorney handle the client relations. As your attorney I advise myself: no more of the fruit for him until we've covered the race.

The Mint 400 itself was the largest off-the-ground vine-swinging race in the desert, which meant two hundred monkeys on hang-gliders and zip-harnesses careening over four hundred miles of scrub, and by the time it started we were in no condition to watch it. The press tent was full of sportswriting orangutans drinking Margarita Mix, and the race itself was invisible — a distant haze of dust and feathers somewhere out past the first ridge. We reported, in my notes for the day, that the winner was Number 12, "a burly rhesus with excellent vines." I have never learned who actually won.

The Circus-Circus, that night, loomed up like a mad gorilla's fever dream — trapezes, spotlights, screaming macaws, and ten thousand monkeys stuffing tokens into the machines.

"The Circus-Circus is what the whole jungle would be doing on Saturday night if the gorillas had won the war," I said. "This is the main cage of the American Dream."

"We're right in the middle of it," he said. "This is the trough."

Somewhere in the middle of it we found the mescaline, and the floor of the casino turned to soft rubber, and a lounge act came on — a chimp in a gold lamé tuxedo singing the old songs of the Fifties canopy — and my attorney began, catastrophically, to cry. "Listen to him," he sobbed. "That voice. Those were the years, man. Nobody knew it was the top of the wave." It was the papaya talking, but it didn't matter; half the table of retirees was crying too, and the singer swung right through "Banana River" while I hauled my attorney out by the ankle.

Then things got out of hand in the room. I remember my attorney in the bathtub, up to his chin in grapefruit juice, croaking that the figs had turned on him, that the room was filling with reptiles, and that if I came any closer he would shred me into coleslaw. There was a Doberman in the corner. (There was no Doberman.) I threw him the coconut and told him the reptiles were his clients and he should bill them. By dawn he was asleep in the tub, and I was in the revolving restaurant at the top of the tower, wearing one shoe and a bathrobe colonized by fruit flies, watching the desert turn the color of a ripe plantain.

Strange memories on this nervous night in Las Vegas. Has it been five years? Six? It seems like a lifetime — or at least a Main Era, the kind of peak that never comes again. The jungle of the late Sixties was a very special time and place to be a part of. Maybe it meant something; maybe not. But no explanation, no mix of words or music or memories, is going to touch that simple truth.

There was madness in any direction, at any hour. You could strike sparks anywhere. There was a fantastic universal sense that whatever we were doing was right, and that the troop was behind us. Not in any mean or martial sense; we didn't need that. Our energy would simply prevail. We had all the momentum; we were riding the crest of a high and beautiful vine.

So now, less than five years later, you can climb the tallest palm tree in Las Vegas and look west, and with the right kind of eyes you can almost see the high-tail mark — that place where the great troop of the Sixties finally swung down, and never swung back.

We drove back to Los Angeles for a while, but Vegas reeled us in again — the editors decided I should cover the District Attorney's narcotics conference after all, on the theory that a drug-riddled journalist at a convention of narcotics cops was exactly the journalism the moment demanded. I checked in with a press badge and my attorney checked in as "an expert on aberrant behavior." For three days we sat in a ballroom while D.A.s from all over the desert lectured on the menace of the fermented figs, and a narcotics expert from the N.P.A. explained to the assembled baboons that the trouble with pushers was "that you can't tell them from customers, because they look exactly like everybody else." Every eye in the room found us. "That's the curse of this business," my attorney muttered, applauding. "No profile."

We blew the conference off in the end. On the last morning the maid found the room — the papaya salt, the smashed grapefruit, the iguana droppings I have no explanation for — and we left a hundred-dollar bill under the phone, which was probably not enough, and pointed the car west.

"As your attorney," I told him, "I advise you to eat the papaya before it eats you."

"We should get out of this town," he said without opening his eyes. "No. Finish the assignment."

The assignment was finished. It had been finished for days. There was nothing in the desert for us and nothing in the city either, and no story, and no Jungle Dream. Just a monkey and his attorney in a big red shark of a car, hurrying through the brown desert with the fruit of the whole Sixties rotting sweetly in the trunk.

Buy the ticket, take the ride. And when the going gets weird, the weird go pro. And the pros, brother, go bananas.
