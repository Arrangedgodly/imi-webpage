# Romantic Chimpedy

*A parody of "Romantic Comedy"*

Sally Milz had been a sketch writer at The Night Howler for nine years, which meant she had a trophy for writing jokes at 4 a.m., a divorce to her credit, and a permanent scent of stale banana-coffee. She was thirty-nine, a chimp of dependable charm, and she knew exactly how the show worked, which is to say she knew exactly how the show worked for handsome people.

The proof was Danny Horst. Danny was a cast member, sweet, shaggy, and built like a laundromat — and this very week he'd gotten engaged to a gorgeous, warbling, entirely unfamous pop star. This happened constantly: a marginally attractive famous monkey snagged a stunning unfamous one, and the tabloids gasped like it was a miracle of nature. No one ever wrote sketches about the reverse. Nobody's monologue joked about a plain female writer dating a himbo celebrity, because a plain female writer dating a himbo celebrity was not a thing that occurred in the wild.

Sally pitched it anyway. "The Danny Horst Rule," she called it, and the room, which was mostly male and mostly groomed, laughed in the wrong places, and the sketch died in dress.

At the Wednesday table read, the writers' room confirmed it. Noah Brewster asked, of a rookie writer whose sketch was dying, "What's the *second* draft of this joke?" — a question no one had asked in the history of the table — and then helped the kid find it, live, in front of everybody. The head writer, a 300-year-old orangutan who had not been surprised since the Clinton administration, looked down the table at Sally with one raised eyebrow, as if to say: *this one's ours, if we're smart.*

That week, the host was Noah Brewster.

Everyone loved Noah Brewster. He'd been a pop star since he was sixteen — actual melodies, actual heartbreak, sold-out trees from MSG to Red Rocks — and he had a public history of dating supermodels and a private reputation, the staff said, for being *nice*, which in show business is a rumor you don't believe until it opens its own dressing-room door and offers you a snack.

Noah sang his monologue, played a struggling gorilla in a tuxedo, improvised a rhyming couplet about the cue cards, and hit every joke Sally wrote harder than it deserved. In the goodnight wall, he found her in the scrum.

"You wrote the church sketch," he said. "The one where the choir riots. I've watched it four times in my dressing room instead of learning my lines."

"It's a flaw in your professionalism," said Sally.

"It's a whole flaw in my life," said Noah, laughing — a real laugh, the uncalibrated kind — and he told her the sketch had done something he'd needed done, and she said something self-deprecating, and he said, "No, but — " and then the stage manager pulled him away to smile at cameras, and the week ended the way weeks end, like a wave collapsing back into the sea.

Sally flew home Monday telling herself the usual: he was a pop star; she was a writer with necklines from 2014; the Danny Horst Rule only ran one direction. The show went on. Nine more episodes. She wrote a lot of church riots.

---

Then, in September, an email arrived.

*From: Noah Brewster. Subject: The choir riot sketch.*

*Hi Sally. This is not a bit. I hope. I keep thinking about something you said at the after-party — about how comedy songs are just lies with a melody, and love songs are jokes nobody laughs at. I've been writing my new album and it's terrible and I think it's because it's all lies with a melody. Do you ever find that? Anyway. No response required. — Noah. P.S. Not a bit.*

Sally answered in eleven minutes, composed over two hours. She said that all her jokes were love songs with the tune filed off. She said his second album had been underrated. She said "not a bit" was the least bit move possible, and she respected it.

He wrote back. She wrote back. Autumn turned. The emails got long. He told her about the house in Kauai and the eleven rejected songs; she told him about the writers' room, the divorce, the mortgage, her mother's estate. He was funny *on the page*, which is the rarest talent in the animal kingdom, funnier than the posters suggested, funnier than he was allowed to be. By December she had a folder in her inbox called DON'T GET WEIRD and it contained 214 messages and she re-read them on the subway like a lunatic.

In January he wrote: *Come to Kauai. I'm serious. I've been serious since the goodnight wall. P.S. Not a bit.*

Sally said no. Sally's sister said, "You said WHAT?" Sally said the Danny Horst Rule. Her sister said, "You invented the rule to mock the rule. Go mock it in person."

She went in February.

---

Kauai in February: warm rain, wild papayas, and a pop star in rubber slippers who greeted her at the airport with a leis-and-pickup-truck energy that no magazine had ever captured. For three days it was stupidly, sickeningly perfect. He cooked badly. He played her the eleven terrible songs and one that wasn't terrible at all, that had begun as an email to her, that rhymed her name with *bespoke*, and she laughed so hard she frightened some geese. He called her "Milz" in front of his neighbor like she was a person he had known all his life, and the knot in Sally's chest pulled tight.

On the fourth night, over Thai takeout, the knot spoke instead of her.

"You should probably know I've done the math," said Sally. "And the math is the Danny Horst Rule. Famous marginally attractive monkeys get gorgeous monkeys. That's the ecosystem. And you're not marginal, you're a stadium act, and I'm — I'm behind the camera, by design, because that's where writers live, and I have a whole suit of armor made of jokes, and at some point you're going to be on a red carpet with a supermodel and I'm going to be a chapter you tell charitably."

There was a silence with rain in it.

"Wow," said Noah. "So the woman who wrote a sketch about how nobody questions the rule believes in the rule."

"It's not about belief, it's a pattern — "

"It's a pattern *they* made. Sally. I've written to you every day for eight months. I wrote you a bridge. I invited you across an ocean, which for me is like leaving the one tree I know, and you're telling me the tabloids get a veto." He set down his fork. "I'm not a bit. I have never once been a bit with you. You're the only person I don't perform for, and I did the math too, and the math says I'd rather bomb with you than kill with anybody else. That's the whole album. That's the whole album I couldn't write until you."

"You rhymed my name with bespoke," said Sally, tearing up, aggressive.

"It half-rhymed," said Noah. "I'm a pop star. We're licensed."

They did the dishes. They did a lot of other things you do in Kauai in February, none of them bits.

---

In April, The Night Howler announced that month's host: Noah Brewster, with musical guest Noah Brewster. The writers' room greeted the news with a standing ovation of sarcastic slow claps. Sally wrote a sketch called "The Sally Milz Rule" — about a plain, funny, deeply neurotic comedy writer who ends up with a pop star, and every single character who says "but that never happens" is struck by a falling coconut — and Noah performed it like a man with nothing to lose, which, being in love on live television, he was.

At the goodnight wall, he grabbed her paw and held it up like a title belt. The cameras caught it. The tabloids gasped like it was a miracle of nature.

"Married within the year," they wrote, and for once in the history of the Danny Horst Rule, the tabloids' math was right.
