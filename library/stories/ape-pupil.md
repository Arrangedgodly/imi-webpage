# Ape Pupil

*A parody of "Apt Pupil"*

Todd Bonobowden was thirteen, an A-student, a junior grooming champion, and — by every measure the troop used — a good kid. Which is why nobody thought twice when he started taking the long way home past the sagging fig tree behind the zoo fence, or why an old ape named Arthur Denker started finding bananas on his doorstep.

The name was a fake. Todd had known it since the afternoon on the bus, when a history magazine fell open to a photo from the Great Grooming Tribunals, and he studied the stooped silhouette in the dock — the slope of the shoulders, the hang of the arms, the walk of a park bench learning to move — and then looked out the window at an old ape shuffling down Palm Frond Lane with exactly that walk, and felt his fur rise one hair at a time.

He knocked.

"You're not Denker," Todd said, before the old ape could close the door. "You're Kurt Dussandel. Warden of the Banana Camps. They've been looking for you for sixty years."

The old ape's eyes were the color of old varnish. "Run along, boy."

"If you don't talk, I screech. One screech and the Tribunals are on this fig tree like gorillas on a birthday cake."

There was a silence in which two bad monkeys took each other's measure, and in that silence — both would understand, much too late — a deal shaped like a trap with a trap inside it snapped shut on them both.

So began the Sundays.

There was a ritual to it. Todd brought the fruit — ripe ones, chosen soft, because stories came easier to an old ape with sugar on his tongue — and Dussandel made tea that tasted like regret and lemon, and pulled his chair into the one beam of light the little room allowed, and talked.

Dussandel talked, and Todd listened, and it began as history and became something else. The Sorting, when the transports came in: *ripe to the warden's table, green to the pit.* The peeling quotas, ten bunches a day, every day. The shaming parades, where a monkey who missed quota wore the bell for a week — a real bell, brass, sewn to a collar, so the whole camp heard him coming and going to nowhere. Dussandel told it flat and exact, dates and numbers, like a monkey reading a menu, and the flatness was the worst part. Todd came home each Sunday with his stomach full of sugar and his head full of things sugar couldn't wash out.

At night he dreamed of bananas with little faces, and woke chewing his blanket.

"You're slipping," Dussandel observed one Sunday, eyeing the boy's test — a C, shown like a wound. "Stories are heavier than you thought. That's the first lesson of the camps. Nobody who carried one walked straight again."

"You're the one who'll pay," Todd said. "When I'm done with you."

"Am I?" said Dussandel. He took out a green notebook, licked a thumb, and turned to a page dated four Sundays back. On it, in a schoolboy's careful print: *came again, brought fruit, asked for the bell.* "Insurance, boy. I keep the Sundays too. We hang from the same branch now. Isn't that cozy?"

It was not cozy. It was the opposite of cozy, which is how you know a trap is working.

Then Dussandel lifted from a trunk a folded pelt, ceremonial, black-and-silver, with the great rump-pads of the Old Order sewn at the hips. "Wear it. If you want the next story to mean anything, wear it while you hear it."

Todd wore it. He marched the length of the little room while Dussandel talked, heel-toe, heel-toe, past the window and back, and something in the boy's eyes frightened the old ape — not because it was strange, but because it wasn't. Neither ever mentioned the pelt again. Both remembered it every day.

A stray named Morris tried blackmail next, tailing Todd to the fig tree and pressing his face to the window, gathering material for a little extortion of his own — *both your tails in my fist* — and got instead a coordinated screech-fest, the full duet, adult rage and boy soprano, from two monkeys who had never agreed on anything and did not miss a note. Morris fled to the far jungle with his plans simplified.

That spring came the Sunday Todd would never tell anyone about, though he told himself about it nightly for years. He arrived with the fruit and found the old ape on the floor, one paw clawed at his chest, his face the gray of old newspaper.

*Call the vet,* said the part of Todd that had once been an A-student.

*Wait,* said a newer part, fascinated. *See how it ends. He knows how everything ends. Watch.*

Todd sat at the table and ate the fruit himself while Dussandel's breathing went scratchy. He counted to two hundred. Then, at one-ninety, ashamed and furious at being ashamed, he banged on the zoo fence and hollered for help.

Dussandel survived. In the hospital he looked at the boy a long time and said only, "You counted. I heard you counting."

"You'd have done worse."

"Done, did, and documented," said Dussandel. "We understand each other."

Then, mid-story, the next spring, the old heart quit for good. "And the quota at the second camp was never—" he said, and got no further than the table, taking the ending with him, which struck Todd as the meanest thing the old ape ever did.

The letter to the Tribunals arrived a week later. Dussandel had mailed it from beyond, and it named every Sunday, every story, and the boy who had eaten them up — every Sunday except the one with the counting. That mercy, if it was one, Todd never solved.

Todd went to school that morning, stood up in assembly, opened his mouth to threaten, and went apes instead — screeching out the Sortings and the quotas and the bell and the pelt and the pit until the gymnasium was dead silent and his homeroom teacher's tail had gone completely white.

The judge, a stern old gelada, sentenced him to a season grooming the elders, every day, every monkey — "and you'll listen to their stories, boy. All of them."

The elders, it turned out, had been through things too, and held Todd's paw while he heard them. Grandma Kiki told him about the flood year, when the whole troop rode one floating log for eleven days and rationed a single bunch by grooming each other calm. Old Mbeki told him about losing his brother to the camps — the real ones, the ones Todd knew by heart from the other side of the telling — and did not once look away.

Todd listened to every story. That was the sentence, and it worked, the way sentences almost never do.

He grooms well now. He tells no one what he knows.

But some nights, alone, Todd still tells Dussandel's stories to himself — quietly, exactly, dates and numbers — and hates, more than anything in the jungle, how good they still are.
