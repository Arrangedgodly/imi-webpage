# Adventures of Knuckleberry Finn

*A parody of "Adventures of Huckleberry Finn"*

You don't know about me unless you have read a book by the name of The Adventures of Tom Sawmonkey, but that ain't no matter. That book was made by Mr. Mark Twain, and he told the truth, mostly. I'm Knuckleberry Finn, a mangy young chimp of the Mississippi banks, and this is how it went.

The Widow Douglas took me for her son and set out to civilize me, which means combing, forks, and sitting still, and Miss Watson, her sister, kept a gorilla named Jim and a spelling book aimed at me both. I couldn't stand it, and then Pap turned up — my hairy old father, with his grey fur and his greasy grievances — wanting my money. He kidnapped me and hauled me to a fig tree across the river, where he locked me in and went off trading pelts for fermented sap. When he took to thrashing me with a switch for learning to read, I judged it was time to strike out for freedom. One night he got to seeing snakes in his fur, and snakes on the walls, and a big dark angel in the corner, and I reckoned it was time to strike out for freedom.

So I sawed out of the trunk, smashed a rotten banana, smeared it around along with a fistful of my own shed fur, and dragged the mess to the riverbank. Then I pushed off. Pap would think I was dead, the town would hold a funeral, and I'd be as free as a bird in a banana tree. I lit for Jackfruit Island, and watched my own funeral through the reeds, which is the finest entertainment there is, and I don't recommend the price.

There, hiding in the brush, I found somebody's gorilla — old Jim, Miss Watson's gorilla, sitting with his big silver back against a log.

"Doan' hurt me!" he says when I stepped on a twig. "I's a runaway!"

Miss Watson had meant to sell him down the river to New Banana-leans for eight hundred dollars, so Jim had slipped off in the night. We shook on it — his paw swallowed mine whole — and teamed up. We found a raft of fig logs, and we agreed people couldn't get no freer than floating on a big river with the stars overhead and a bunch of 'nanas ripening in the sun.

It's lovely to live on a raft. You feel mighty free and easy and comfortable on a raft. We had a rattlesnake scare (my fault — I put a dead one in Jim's blanket as a joke, and his mate come, and Jim got bit, and hollered at me till I was sorry clear through), and a fog that separated us all night, and when I found Jim again he was setting there with his head down, and then he says, not angry, just hurt: "Trash is what people is dat puts dirt on de head er dey fren's." I done it, and I'd about died rather than own up, but I owned up, and it was fifteen minutes before I could work myself up to humble myself to a gorilla — but I done it, and I warn't ever sorry it.

When men come hunting runaways one night, I headed the canoe straight at them and said my pap was aboard with the family, all down with the mango-pox. They threw me a twenty and lit out from themselves, so to speak. Jim said I was de best fren he ever had. I reckoned I'd done wrong both ways, and let it go at that.

Then two soaking-wet capuchins come swimming out to us claiming to be a duke and the heir to the throne of France.

"Me, I'm the Dolphin," says the bald one, bowing. "The lost Dauphin of the House of Ban-bon."

They weren't no more royalty than I was, and I'm no royal at all. But it was monkeys together, strong. They put on a show of "The King's Bananapple" that fleeced the whole town, and then a follow-up called the Royal Nonesuch, advertised LADIES AND GORILLAS NOT ADMITTED, which consisted of the king crawling out on all fours painted like a rainbow. The town got sold the same joke three nights running, because nobody would admit they'd been took.

One night we drifted into a feud. The Gingerfurds and the Shepherdsonapes, two old troops of howlers, had hated each other so long nobody remembered why, though it started with an insult at a grooming party thirty years back. They went to the same tree on Sundays, toted their bananas on the same branches, and shot at each other the rest of the week. Young Sophie Gingerfurd run off with a Shepherdsonape, and the feud busted loose, and when it was over my friend Buck Gingerfurd was done feuding forever. I decided feuding was the dumbest thing a monkey ever invented, excepting nearly everything else monkeys invent.

Then came the worst scrape of all. The king and the dolphin heard of a dead ape named Peter Wilks and impersonated his English brothers for the inheritance, crying on cue. They had it near about pulled off when I couldn't stand it — on account of Mary Jane, the dead ape's niece, who was kind to me and had the best heart I ever see. I stole the gold from where the frauds hid it, and when I heard them coming I tucked the whole bag into the open coffin and slid out, and to this day I don't know exactly where that money went down the river to glory. Mary Jane promised to stand by me, and she done it.

The king got his spite up about being busted, and sold Jim. Sold him, for forty dollars, after Jim had scrubbed that little fraud's fur and fetched his breakfast every single morning. I sat down and thought about writing to Miss Watson, and about how everybody believes a runaway gorilla belongs in chains.

Then I thought of Jim — how he called me "honey" and stood my watch for me, and how glad he'd been to see me. And I says:

"All right, then, I'll go to the zoo."

And I never regretted it. I tracked Jim to the Phelps farm, where they took me for their nephew Tom, and wouldn't you know it, Tom Sawmonkey arrived the next day and let himself be took for his brother Sid, because a chance like that don't come twice. We stole Jim out of that shed Tom's way — which meant warning letters, and a rope ladder baked into a pie, and case-knives, and a moat, and Jim raising a pet snake and a rat to be prisoners' pets — because Tom said prisoners never escaped easy if it could be helped. It was fifteen days of the finest evasion in literature, and at the end of it Tom got shot in the leg, and Jim — who could have been free and clear in the dark — turned back for him, and got caught, which settled with me forever the question of whether gorillas are people.

And it turned out Miss Watson had died two months back and set Jim free in her will, and Tom had known it the whole time. "I wanted the adventure," he says, grinning. I could have thrown a coconut at him.

Aunt Polly arrived, everything shook out, and Jim got his forty dollars for being a patient prisoner and a good friend. Now they're all bothering me about being adopted and civilized — combing my fur, wearing a collar, eating 'nanas with a fork.

I can't stand it. I been there before.

I'm going to light out for the Territory ahead of the rest — the deep canopy, where nobody grooms you unless you ask — because I reckon I got to growl up the river of life one more time before I settle.
