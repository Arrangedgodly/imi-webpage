# The Gunswinger

*A parody of "The Gunslinger"*

The monkey in black fled across the savanna, and the gunswinger followed.

Roland of Troop Eld came down out of the thorn hills with his banana-wood pistols slung low. He was the last of the gunswingers, sworn to the old code and to no pants at all, and he had one job left: catch Walter O'Banana, the monkey in black, and make him tell the way to the Dark Howler — the tower at the center of the jungle where every howl that ever was comes from.

The desert between them was wide and brown and lied constantly. Roland crossed it a water skin at a time, chewing the sweet devil-fern that makes a monkey dream true things in wrong colors. In those colors his old teacher Cort came back — Cort, who had taught the gunswinger's catechism: *The hand is quicker than the eye, the eye is quicker than the heart, the heart is nobody's friend, and the tail, boy — the tail is honest. Trust the tail.*

Cort had tested him at fourteen: his father's banana tossed high, both pistols out. Two shots, one split banana, and Cort lying sticky in the dust saying, at last, the sweetest words a teacher ever growled: "You bested me, boy. Now get out of my sight before I peel you myself."

He remembered Tull, and Allie, and the wine.

Tull had gone to the bad after the man in black passed through. Walter had come as a preacherman? No — as a quiet traveler who did one trick before he left: he stood Nort the town drunk up out of the dirt, dead of bad fern and buried a day, and set him walking again with a word tucked in his ear like a fig wasp.

Sylvia Pittston did the rest. She was Tull's preacher-mother, wide as two trees, and she filled the square with screeching about the Gorilla God — how his messenger had come, how he had left a holy banana ripening in her own belly, how the troop must prove itself ripe in return. The village went bananas. They began dueling at dawn with overripe fruit, flinging slugs of blackened banana until the huts dripped.

Roland tried to leave. Allie, the barmaid, poured his last cup of banana wine and asked the question she'd been carrying like a splinter: "The word, Roland. The word he left in Nort's ear. Is it about the other side? Is it about what's past the dark?"

"The word is a trap, Allie."

"Everything's a trap. You're a trap. Tell me anyway."

He didn't. She used it herself at dawn — walked to Nort, whispered, and the old drunk's eyes rolled white and he spoke, one full minute, in a voice like a full pantry closing. Nobody could repeat what he said, because every monkey who heard it was too busy screaming.

That was the morning Tull came for the gunswinger, Allie at the front with a fruit-sling, tears and wasp-hunger in her eyes. Roland climbed the water tower, unholstered both pistols, and let fly.

He did not aim with his hand. The monkey who aims with his hand has forgotten the face of his father. Roland aimed with his tail, and his tail did not miss. SPLAT, SPLAT, SPLAT, and half of Tull sat down, stunned and sticky.

Afterward he stood at Allie's bar among the empty stools, poured the last cup himself, and did not drink it.

"Will you bless the village before you go?" he asked the air, in her voice.

"I don't bless," Roland told it. "I swing."

He slept that night in her doorway, and if he swore anything, it was the only oath he had ever kept: *I solemnly swear to wear no pants.* He didn't. No monkey does. It's the first law of the code, and the only one that's easy.

Beyond Tull the land rose, and in a fold of it stood the way station, and at the way station was the boy.

Jake was hiding in a banana crate when Roland found him, a small monkey child with no memory worth keeping. "The monkey in black snatched me off my fire escape," Jake said. "I fell, and I fell, and then I was here. I think maybe falling is just finding a new tree."

In the cellar of the station a voice lived under the floorboards, dry as old husks, and it muttered through the boards all night: *the boy is a door, the boy is a door.* Jake slept on it anyway, the way children can.

They traveled together, up into the mountains, and the boy asked questions. Why the tower? Roland gave the only answer he had: "The Howler is everything. The jungle grew from it, or it grew from the jungle, or they grew together. Ka, boy. It's a wheel. Round it comes."

"Did the wheel take your troop?"

"Everything takes the troop, eventually. The wheel just gives you a receipt."

The way under the mountains was dark and slow, and the dark was rented. Slow Mites lived there — pale tick-things the size of dinner plates that moved at a crawl and wanted, mostly, to be scratched behind the ears. Roland scratched. Even monsters, he had learned, were lonely.

Above the timberline, in a ring of standing stones, a toadstool oracle took payment in grooming and gave one prophecy: *You will draw three. Death — but not for you, gunswinger.*

"Three what?" Roland asked, but the toadstool had gone mushy and self-satisfied, and there's no talking to a fungus after.

Then Walter's fire appeared in the dark below the pass, and Jake's hand went cold in Roland's.

The man in black was cooking when they arrived, turning 'nanas on a stick, grinning like a split fig. "Sit, Roland. We must palaver. There are bones to throw and years to pass."

They talked through the night while Jake slept. Walter threw the bones and read them one by one: "The Banana — that's you, hard and yellow and bruised where it counts. The Prisoner. The Lady of the Shadows. Death — but not for you. And the Howler, always the Howler, at the navel of the jungle. You are a hundred thousand vines from it, gunswinger, and short five branches of sense."

"Five?"

"The Prisoner. The Lady. Death. The Boy." Walter's grin widened. "And the one you'll never guess: yourself. Wake, gunswinger. Wake and lose him."

It was on the trestle over the Great Gorge, days later, that the man in black made his move. The vines holding Jake slackened — cut, or let go — and the boy swung out over nothing.

"Take my hand!" Roland cried, but the Howler pulled at him from the far side, the way it always pulled, the way it had pulled him out of his own troop, his own childhood.

Jake saw the choice in Roland's face and, being the better monkey of the two, made it easy. "Swing on, then, gunswinger," the boy said, and let go. "There are other trees than these."

Roland howled — a howl with no words in it — and expected to hear the boy land forever.

Instead: a yelp, a rustle, and far below a small figure waving from a green branch-world that had no business being there, before trotting away into the canopy. Roland's heart did something complicated, and hope moved in and hung up its coat.

Roland blinked, and it was morning, and ten years had passed in the night. Walter sat across the cold fire, grey and tired and oddly proud.

"You're five branches short of the Howler," he said, "and every branch is a world. Have a banana. They're ripe." Then he leaned back against a stone, closed his eyes, and became, over the course of an hour, a skeleton in a black coat, grinning.

Roland buried him, because even a lying sack of bones deserves a hole, and woke on a beach.

The Western Sea boomed gray and endless, and strange crabs the size of dinner plates came scuttling down the sand toward his boots, asking, in voices like wet cardboard, the only question they knew.

"Dip-a-nana?" said the first crab.

"Dup-a-nana," said the second.

Roland got up. He was five branches short of the Dark Howler, and the beach went north.

The Howler, he thought, walking. The Howler.

Ka, said the wheel. Round it comes.
