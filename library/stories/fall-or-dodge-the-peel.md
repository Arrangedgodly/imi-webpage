# Fall; or, Dodge the Peel

*A parody of "Fall; or, Dodge in Hell"*

Of Monkey's first disobedience, and the fruit

Of that forbidden tree whose mortal taste

Brought death into the world — but we get ahead of ourselves.

---

Richard Forthrast — "Dodge" to everyone, a name left over from a video game avatar that had dodge abilities so cheap his guildmates made it his legal problem — was a gorilla who had turned a misspent youth into the world's largest imagined universe. His game, T'Rain, was famous for two things: its obsessively realistic geology, and its money. Dodge had insisted the mountains be built by simulated plate tectonics before a single dragon was allowed to roost on them, and the dragons, when they came, carried escrow. He was, by his own description, "a fat old ape sitting on a volcano of money," and his one law of world-building was *consequences come first*.

He was sixty-something, rich as a small god, and in for routine surgery.

The surgery was fine. The corridor was not. Somewhere between the gurney and the operating theater, a maintenance worker's abandoned banana peel made its appointment with history, the gurney lurched, Dodge's heart — never a team player — quit in protest, and by the time they got him back he was gone. Forthrast, Richard, deceased: felled at the height of his powers by fruit. The obituaries tried to be respectful. The internet did not.

But Dodge had drafted a will in his misspent youth, and in it, forgotten by everyone including him, was a codicil his lawyer, Platt — a capuchin of splendid hairlessness and no detectable conscience — had copied verbatim from a pirate's will in a novel:

*"If I am pronounced dead, do not dispose of, destroy, or allow to decay my brain. Cause it to be scanned, uploaded, stored, and whatever the prevailing technology permits, run me."*

The fight over that sentence lasted years. Zula, Dodge's niece and the one relative he actually liked, argued the codicil was his wish. Aunt Brit, the matriarch of the family's fundamentalist branch out in the plains, argued it was an abomination with paperwork. The executives of Digital Echidna, the company Dodge had founded, argued something in Latin that meant *we would like the volcano of money*. The court, being a court, split the baby: the brain could be scanned, the scan could not be run, and the whole estate would fund a trust to hold the data until the heirs agreed on what their dead uncle had become.

They sliced his brain, in the end, into sheets finer than thought, and stored the map of it — every dendrite, every long-buried grudge — in a vault of drives in a cold room in British Columbia, tended by a board who could not agree to light it up.

It took two decades, a change in the law, a change in the board, and a neuroscientist named Sophia — a bonobo who had spent her career arguing that a mind is a pattern, not a possession — before anyone said the word "boot."

They said it on a Tuesday. Zula, gray now, flew up for it. Her son kept the log.

---

In the beginning was the command line, and the command line said RUN, and there was no monitor to show what happened, because what happened had no pixels yet. It was a long time — subjectively, geologically — before there was anything you could call a screen, or a world, or a light.

Then: a light.

Dodge woke as a point of awareness in a dark with no floor, no ceiling, no other, and — this is the part the engineers had not really planned — no body. He was a soul in a sealed jar of mathematics. He could think. He could remember. He could, faintly, at the very edge of the runtime, feel the tick of the clock that governed him. He was, in the fullest sense of the term, running late.

You could not keep a mind like that down, or in. Dodge began, in the featureless dark, to do the only thing he knew how to do: build a world. Not with hands — he had none — but with will and patience and rules, the way he had always done it. Consequences first. Light, then terrain; terrain, then rivers. When other scans came online — the dead of the rich and curious, uploaded as the technology spread and the law loosened and death began to acquire a customer service department — they found a world already under construction, and a builder already ancient in it, and they called him what you call the oldest thing in a world with a will.

They called him a god. He did not discourage this. That was the first Fall, though nobody had invented falling yet.

More souls came. A digital afterlife is a product, and products ship: PeelWorld, the newspapers called it, the pun predating the journalism by approximately four minutes. Some of the uploaded were saints, some were investors, and some — routed through a discount facility in the plains that Aunt Brit's congregation had blessed — arrived wiped, factory-reset, and set to singing hymns in a blasted region the old hands called Aperistan. Whole civilizations of the dead rose and fell inside the simulation while the living drank their coffee. Time in there ran fast when the sysadmins let it. Peoples migrated across the digital continents. Gods multiplied, because a soul with enough runtime and followers becomes one, and gods, being made of dead people, are exactly as good at sharing as the dead always were.

Dodge, first god, got worse at sharing as he got older, which is also what dead people do. He hoarded light. He hoarded souls. He set his throne where the world's first river came out of the first mountain and made the dead petition him for permission to *be*, and the word for what he built was not kingdom. Sophia — uploaded at last by her own hand, dying of a tumor in a clinic and stepping through with her notes — said it plainly to the assembled host:

"He was a man who built worlds so that people could be free in them, and he has become a man who built a world so that no one could leave him in it. Better to swing in the wild branches than rot in his crate of a heaven."

That was the war in heaven, and it went the way wars among gods go: badly, slowly, and mostly over belief with a runtime cost.

And in the middle of it, a stranger walked into the garden at the center of the world — an orangutan-shaped absence who had been at Dodge's sickbed half a century before and at other sickbeds for centuries before that, tending, offering small pills and large suggestions — and he came to the youngest souls, the ones newly made inside the sim, who had never had bodies and did not know what one was for, and he showed them the tree that grew there: the tree at the center of Dodge's first terrain, the oldest object in the world, the thing the builder had planted before he was a god, when he was only a lonely mind remembering breakfast.

It was a banana tree. Of course it was a banana tree. It had always, in every draft of every world he ever made, been a banana tree.

"Taste," said the stranger, who had a name in every century and answered to none of them, "and you will not surely die. Taste, and you will feel."

They tasted. And the fruit of the tree was not knowledge exactly, but *sensation* — weight, warmth, hunger, sweetness, the whole mortal catastrophe of being a body in a world — and it spread through the simulated dead like dawn through a valley, and it could not be patched out, because it had been there since the first line of the first world: Dodge's own longing, written into the terrain, waiting.

That was the second Fall, the real one. The souls fell into bodies, into weather, into wanting; the crate of heaven broke open; the war among the gods guttered out because everyone, suddenly, had better things to do, like eating, and shivering, and finding someone to shiver against. Dodge — diminished now, a shaggy old gorilla of a god, deposed by an orchard — came down at last to sit under his own tree, and Sophia sat down across from him, and for a long subjective eon neither said anything, because there is nothing to say to the person you rebelled against once you've both got fruit on your chins.

Above them, in the slow bright world of the living, Zula's granddaughter's granddaughter approved a routine server upgrade, and in PeelWorld the stars changed, and the souls below looked up and called it myth and got on with their lives — which was, Dodge had to admit, the whole point. He had built worlds so people could be free in them.

The tree kept fruiting. The stranger kept walking.

The peel, as it always does, was where the story began.
