# Delouser Claiborne

*A parody of "Dolores Claiborne"*

You want the truth, Inspector Makaki? Truth's the one thing I got left the troop hasn't picked over like grackles at a picnic. Pull up a stump and take it down exactly like I tell it.

And turn that recorder back on. I see you turned it off. A monkey your age doesn't get to be embarrassed at his work.

You think I pushed Vera Donobanana into the Old Termite Sink. I know you do — your young chimp there with the notepad, he wrote P-U-S-H-E-D and underlined it twice while I was still saying good morning. But I didn't, Inspector, and that's the flat truth, and I'll get to what I *did* do, because it's a hair off the same branch.

Forty years I groomed that monkey. Delouser, they called me, because there wasn't a flea, tick, or tangled dread on Vera Donobanana's old silver back that I didn't hunt down like it owed me money. Forty years of her orders and her insults and her Christmas bonuses that were exactly one banana, gift-wrapped — and no, I never once quit, and you can ask why till your tail curls.

Here's why: a monkey who's hard on everybody is usually just hard on herself first. Vera's own private hard patch was her Joey, gone forty years down a dark well of his own, and I knew about wells. We were two old she-monkeys with matching holes in our stories. You don't quit a monkey with a hole like that. You hold the comb and you wait.

And it was Vera gave me the sentence I lived by, the morning I was crying into her bathwater about Joe Gorilla-George. "Delouser," she says, cold as the far side of a rock, "sometimes you have to be a high-riding monkey to get things done." Hard sayin'. True teachin'.

Joe. Ah, Joe Gorilla-George. Sweet on him at sixteen — the swing of him, the shoulders, the way he could peel a 'nana in one strip and hand you the whole peel like a rose. Then jungle juice got its claws in, anything with a fizz and a lie in it, and the Joe I married turned into a hoarder with a temper. Every banana my Selena and I earned grooming went into his stash, down his gullet. He cuffed us when we squawked. Not a crime in the troop back then, Inspector. Just a Tuesday.

I could've lived with all of it, may the troop forgive me. What I couldn't live with was the winter he started looking at Selena's future the way he looked at a 'nana he meant to peel. Her Zoo York money, her one way off that rockpile — seen it, wanted it. I found his plans scratched on a fig leaf, item by item, like a shopping list. That's the day I stopped crying and started planning. It never sat well on my conscience. It stood.

The eclipse. Sixteen years ago. Vera called me that very morning. "Delouser," she says, "are you watching the eclipse? Are you watching it?" And there was something in that voice, Inspector. A monkey who'd looked into her own dark well once, and knew about looking. We watched it together over her bath, two old she-monkeys, and neither of us said one word, and I went home and did what I'd planned.

Yes, sir. I took Joe up to the Old Termite Sink that afternoon. Told him I'd been keeping a secret 'nana stash there, ripe and mine. I brought the good mash. I got him good and slushed, and when the sky went dark at noon, I looked at that monkey on the rim, swaying, and I thought about Selena, and I thought about the next forty years.

I didn't push him, Inspector. You'll write down I pushed him. But what I did was this: when he tottered, my arms stayed right where they were — folded. There's a difference between pushing a monkey and just not catching him, and the troop knows it, and God and all the grackles know it — but nobody ever hung a monkey for keeping her arms folded.

Selena got to Zoo York. That's the whole of my apology and my defense, and you can date the eclipse and write it next to my name.

Now you'll ask about Selena, since you're thorough. She grew strange to me after, the way daughters do when they sniff a thing without knowing what it is. Went quiet, went far, and one visit she looked at me across my own kitchen and asked, "What happened to Pop, Ma? Really?" And I said, "An accident, same as the newspaper said," and she said, "Fine," in a voice that wasn't fine, and for three years we were two islands.

She telephoned last spring, Inspector. Out of a clear blue sky. Talked about her job proofreading at the Zoo York Bugle, and a young monkey she fancied, and then, right at the end, quiet as dust: "Ma, you did what you had to do, didn't you." Not a question. And I said, "High-riding, baby," and she laughed and cried at once, and that's more forgiveness than most monkeys get in this jungle.

Now. Vera. This morning. Yes, I brought her banana bread, and no, there wasn't a thing in it but cinnamon — though I noticed your chimp there wrote C-I-N-N-A-M-O-N with a question mark, which is slander against my baking. Vera was old past her last good sense, chasing what she called the dust baboons, and begging me, "Delouser, don't let me turn into one of the dust baboons."

And this morning, after the bread, she looked at me clear as a young monkey for one whole minute and said, "Delouser. I'm tired."

Then she crawled to the sink — and I want this typed in capitals, Inspector, because I'll swear it on Selena's own tail: she was crawlin' TOWARD it. Not away. A groomer doesn't let go of her monkey, so yes, she was holding my paw at the end, and no, I don't know which of us let go first, and that's the God's honest answer.

You'll want the will too, since everybody always wants the will. Vera left her pebbles to the retirement wing at the zoo — every last one, which cracked the old bat up, she told me so while she was doing it. To me she left her bath chair and one line in her own writing: FOR DELOUSER, WHO CAUGHT EVERYTHING. So you can put away your money motive, son. All I ever got out of Vera Donobanana was the last forty years of her company, and I'd have done it for free except she'd have died of spite first.

So write it down: accident, both times. An accident is just a fall nobody helped with, and I've told you exactly how much help there was.

I was a mother, Inspector. I did what I had to do. And if you're sitting there waiting for Delouser Claiborne to say she's sorry, you just settle in, son, because you'll grow old and grey-furred in that seat first.

High-riding monkey. That's the whole of it. Hard sayin'. True teachin'.

Now take your recorder and go. It's getting dark, and I want to watch the sky a while. Nothing up there tonight but a smudge of a moon, hanging on — but I've seen one sky go dark at noon, Inspector, and I know what a monkey can do with her arms folded.

Turn the light off behind you.
