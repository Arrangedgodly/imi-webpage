# Chimpelina

*A parody of "Thumbelina"*

Once upon a time, in a jungle far away, there lived a kind but lonely she-monkey who wanted a baby more than anything — even a teeny tiny one. "Even one no bigger than my thumb would do," she sighed.

The jungle witch — an old loris with enormous eyes and a leaky cauldron — heard her wish and handed her a banana seed. "Plant it, water it, and wait. And don't nag it. Seeds hate that."

She planted it in a flowerpot by the window, and up came a slender green stalk with one splendid orchid on top. When the petals opened, there inside sat a tiny she-chimp, no taller than a thumb and no wider than a banana.

"So I shall call you Chimpelina," said her mother, who was delighted beyond words.

Chimpelina's cradle was a polished cocoa pod, her mattress a rose petal, her blanket a hibiscus. By day she paddled in a saucer of rainwater and swung back and forth on a thread spun by a friendly spider, who told her the neighborhood gossip, which, being spider gossip, was mostly about who had eaten whom. It was a lovely life.

But one night, while everyone slept, a great warty old toad hopped in through the window.

"A perfect little wife for my son Wartley!" croaked the toad, and she snatched the pod — Chimpelina and all — and hopped away to the river, setting the cradle on a lily pad. "There! No hurry, my dear. Toad weddings are very relaxed affairs. The music alone takes a week."

Chimpelina woke far from home and wept all morning, until the little fish below heard her, and decided among themselves that the toad was insufferable, and nibbled through the lily stem. Away the leaf floated, downstream and downstream, past tall trees and tall grasses, past a family of water buffalo who wished her a pleasant trip, until a shining scarab beetle spied her and carried her up into the tallest tree in the jungle.

"This is the finest little monkey alive!" declared the beetle — until the other beetles came to call, and jeered: "She has no shell! She has no antennae! She cannot even fly! What a very flat little face!" And the beetle, ashamed of his new friend, set her down on a leaf and abandoned her, which the leaf, at least, thought was very rude.

So Chimpelina lived alone through the long wet season, sipping dew, telling herself stories about the sun, and growing very good at being patient. Her favorite story was the one where the sun came back, and when she told it to the rain, the rain eventually stopped to hear the ending.

When the cold winds came she was half frozen, and would surely have become a very small, very polite icicle, had a kindly old field mouse not taken her into her snug burrow beneath the roots.

"You may stay for the winter," said the mouse, "if you groom my fur and tell me stories. Real ones, mind. None of your sunny nonsense."

But the sun was Chimpelina's favorite story, and she told it every evening, and the mouse grumbled every evening that it was the silliest story she'd ever heard, and also, would the sun be coming tomorrow, and where exactly.

"And mind you make a good match, dearie," the mouse added, smoothing her whiskers. "My neighbor, Old Mole the Orangutan, is rich as a fruit hoard. He's coming to supper — wear your petals."

Old Mole the Orangutan was enormous, orange, and tremendously pleased with himself. He wore a waistcoat with real pockets and told everyone about them.

"Charming girl," he rumbled, looking at Chimpelina the way one looks at a comfortable chair. "She shall marry me when the wet season ends. My den is deep underground, you know. Snug as a termite mound. No weather at all."

"No weather at all!" cried Chimpelina when he had gone. "No sun! No canopy! No sky! Dear mouse, I cannot marry him — I should die of gloom underground."

"Nonsense," said the mouse. "Gloom is very warm in the winter." But that night the mouse relit her candle a little nearer the door, where she could see more sky, in case any part of the story checked out.

Now all that winter, Chimpelina had been secretly nursing a swallow with a hurt wing. She had found it in the grain loft, going tweep-tweep in a small, brave voice, and had fed it seeds and splinted the wing with a splinter of matchstick, and told it the story of the sun until it could hold its head up on its own. In spring the swallow beat its strong mended wings and said:

"You healed me, little one. Now let me heal you. Winter is over — fly away with me, far from Old Mole and his snug, sunless den."

Chimpelina looked once at the mouse's burrow, and once at the dark hole of the mole's great den, where a wedding of quiet gloom was already being measured for furniture.

"Up," said Chimpelina, and tied her sash tight.

She climbed onto the swallow's back, and off they flew — over hills and high mountains, over forests like green seas, and the wind sang and the swallow sang and Chimpelina, for the first time in her whole small life, was exactly the right size for her adventure.

They came at last to the warm country, and there, in a grove of white blossoming trees, Chimpelina rubbed her eyes: in every open flower sat a tiny monkey no bigger than she, riding the breeze on petal-gliders, flashing like sparks in the sun. The swallow circled the grove once, dipped a wing to it, and turned north again, to keep a promise about a grain loft — which is the sort of friend swallows are.

Then came the crown prince of the marmosets, and the moment he saw her, he went completely ape.

"Who are you, sweet little thing?" he asked, recovering his dignity after two full flips.

"I am Chimpelina," she said softly — and then, bold as a ripe mango: "and I am very tired of toads and beetles and moles."

"Then stay," said the prince, offering her his crown of dewdrops. "Be my princess, and glide with us from blossom to blossom."

Chimpelina said yes — of course she said yes. The flower-monkeys gave her a gossamer cape of petals so she could drift on every warm wind, and a new name as bright as her new life: Chimpelina of the Flowers. And every evening the whole blooming grove gathered to hear her tell the story of the sun, which, in the warm country, everyone had met and everybody loved, and which never once failed to get a hand.

She sent the old field mouse a farewell bouquet with a note reading "THE SUN IS REAL," which the mouse kept pressed under a root, and looked at, secretly, every time it rained. She even forgave Old Mole his gloom, sending him a lantern for the wedding he'd have to hold anyway — he married a comfortable mushroom, and they were both very well satisfied.

As for the toad — she sits by the river still, telling the fish what a splendid daughter-in-law she almost had. And the fish, being fish, say nothing at all.
