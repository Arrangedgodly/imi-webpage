# The Trouble with Fleas

*A parody of "The Trouble with Peace"*

His father died as kings usually died in the Union of the Middle Canopy — in bed, suddenly, with several monkeys standing around looking helpful — and King Orso the First inherited the crown between one hangover and the next. He was young, clever, and desperately, tragically *nice* — and every monkey at court agreed this was going to be a problem. He had inherited a kingdom of shiny towers and rotting branches, and decided the answer was to be reasonable with absolutely everyone, which in politics is like being polite to an avalanche.

"I'll make peace in the North," he declared. "Peace with Stour Nightfall. How hard can it be? Everybody prefers peace."

His councillors exchanged the look of monkeys who have read the ending.

The trouble with peace, as the Northlands said, is that it gives the fleas time to breed. And in the Union that season, the fleas were breeding magnificently.

In the capital, Adua the Golden Bough, Savine dan Glokta — the king's half-sister, a langur of immaculate fur and ambition — owned six banana mills, three foundries, and a personality she kept in a safe. Her workers, crammed twelve to a roost in the mill districts, were grey with soot and alive with fleas, because in the rush to industrialize the jungle, nobody had budgeted for the monkeys actually doing the work.

"There's an itch starting, madam," her foreman warned, nodding at the floors below. "Down in the districts. They're calling it the Change."

"Change is good," said Savine. "I sell it."

"Not this change. They're grooming *each other* now. They've stopped grooming *up*."

Savine had also, that season, acquired a husband: Leo dan Brock, the hero of the North, married in a ceremony of flags and accountancy — she got his name and his legend, he got her money and her contempt, and both got somebody to sit next to at functions. It was, they agreed in the one honest conversation of the engagement, an arrangement. Arrangements, the fleas could have told them, are also politics.

Out in the Northlands, the itch had already become a war. Stour Nightfall — champion of the northern baboons, a slab of muscle and spite with a trophy-standard of his enemies' tails — had bent half the North to his banner. The Union's answer was Leo dan Brock, a young lion-maned macaque so handsome that recruitment posters drew themselves, so brave that caution had never once caught him.

Leo did what glorious young fools do: he challenged Stour to single combat in the circle of stones, to settle the war with one duel.

The duel lasted four minutes. Leo came off that field a different monkey — maimed, scarred, and famous, and the North only lets you keep one of those comfortably. Stour stood over him, axe on shoulder, smiled his crooked smile, and let him live, which was the truly unforgivable part.

"You have to be realistic," Stour told the Northern clans, mounting his heel on Leo's banner. "The Union's king wants peace. I'm the only monkey up here worth being afraid of, and I say the price of my peace is everything they have."

And that was when Rikke went to work.

Rikke was the daughter of the old Dogman, a scrawny young monkey of the hills who had inherited the Long Stare — the family gift of seeing what's coming, usually at the worst possible moment, usually with a nosebleed. The visions came like weather: Stour's standard burning. A dead monkey's teeth. Her father's voice: *a vision's not for knowing, lass. It's for choosing.*

So Rikke — whom the whole North assumed was a feckless seer with leaky eyes — sent Stour an offer of peace talks: a parley at the ford, no weapons, no tricks, just talk. Stour, who was many things, all of them loud, came to accept the North's surrender in person, because tyrants cannot resist an audience.

"You want peace," said Rikke, sitting across the ford from him, eyes already leaking red. "Here's the trouble with it, though."

The hills stood up. So did the trees. So did every clan Stour had ever wronged, told by a leaky-eyed seer exactly where to stand and when. Stour Nightfall, most feared baboon in the North, was taken at a peace-talk, by the peace he'd sneered at, and the joke was so perfect the clans dined out on it for a generation. Rikke kept him in a cage and fed him personally, which was somehow more humiliating than the cage. The North, quietly, began asking her opinion first, which is how the North crowns its queens.

The Union, displaying its legendary wisdom, demanded the prisoner be sent south, and so Stour Nightfall came to Adua in chains — and King Orso, who could not help being kind to anything in a cage, went down to see him. It went badly, in the way conversations with avalanches go.

"You're the boy king," said Stour, through the bars, delighted. "The nice one. Do you know what the North calls a nice king? *Ready.*"

"They'll write songs about this," said Orso.

"They'll write songs about *me*," said Stour. "You'll be the verse where I get interesting."

Orso climbed back to his daylight feeling that peace had been staring at him the whole time with teeth in, and that he had fed it from his hand like a pet.

But down in Adua, nobody was laughing. In the Inquisition, a young examiner named Vick dan Glokta — the old torturer's adopted whelp, with his limp and none of his excuses — had gone into the mill districts as a spy and come back troubled, because she had joined the conspirators she was sent to betray, and could not entirely say which side she was on anymore. The conspirators called themselves Breakers. They had a prophet of sorts, the Weaver, who spoke in rags and paradoxes: *why should the many groom the few? Who built the towers, and who lives in the shade of them?* The Weaver's rhetoric itched worse than the fleas, because — everyone privately admitted — it scratched where the bite was.

The itch came to a head on the night of the Great Molt — the revolution nobody planned and everybody joined, when the flea-bitten workers rose all at once, shedding collars and old fur, burning counting-houses and looting roosts. Savine dan Glokta walked out of her own burning mansion past mobs who knew her name. Gunnar Broad, a huge old gorilla veteran who wanted nothing from the wars but a little house and a quiet mate, found himself in a doorway with his old axe and a mob before him, making the last honest choice of a violent life. Vick ran through the smoke between both armies, taking minutes. And Leo dan Brock, maimed hero with a grudge and a famous face, did the arithmetic of a lifetime in one night — and lifted the mob's banner himself, Savine beside him, grim and gilded, the cleverest monkey in the city betting her whole fortune on the fleas.

King Orso, who wanted only to be loved, stood on his balcony, listened to his city howl, and understood at last that peace is not a treaty. Peace is what fleas call the pause before they bite. He left Adua before dawn with a handful of loyalists and the crown still on his head, which was, his chancellor observed, the traditional way to keep it there: one fleeing season per dynasty.

Everyone in the Union got what they'd wanted that year. And nobody, not one monkey, wanted what they got.
