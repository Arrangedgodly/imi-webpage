# Jane Hair

*A parody of "Jane Eyre"*

There was no possibility of taking a walk that day, on account of the rain, so Jane Hair sat in the window seat of Gateshead Hall, hidden behind the red curtain with a book about birds, until her cousin John Reed — a great blotting-paper of a baboon — found her and hurled the book at her head.

"Wicked, cruel ape!" Jane cried, and for the first time in her short, unhappy life she fought back. For that, her aunt Reed locked her in the red-room, where her uncle had died; Jane fainted clean away.

"I am not a bird; and no net ensnares me," she told herself when she woke. "I am a chimpanzee with independent fur."

She got her revenge before she left Gateshead — small, cold, delicious. "I am glad you are no relation of mine," she told Aunt Reed, trembling but standing firm. "I will never call you aunt again as long as I live. People think you a good ape, but you are bad, hard-hearted, and deceitful!"

Aunt Reed's jaw dropped a full inch of gorilla jaw, and Jane tasted triumph without alloy — a fruit, she later admitted, best eaten in small quantities.

Her deliverance came in the form of Mr. Brockleburnstar, a black pillar of a baboon who examined her before Lowood School. "Do you know where the wicked go after death?" he demanded.

"They go to the pit," said Jane.

"And should you like to fall into that pit and be burning there for ever?"

"I must keep in good health, and not die," said Jane — which he took for theology and she meant as plain sense.

She was sent away to Lowood, a charity institution for orphaned monkeys, where the ice-cold Mr. Brockleburnstar preached humility and served burnt porridge in portions calculated by a committee that dined on roast figs. He stood Jane on a stool before the whole school and proclaimed her a liar — "this girl is — a liar!" — and instructed the other pupils to shun her. Only two creatures declined. One was Miss Temple, the superintendent, a tall kindly gibbon who smuggled Jane seed-cake and an apology. The other was Helen Burns, a patient older chimp who bore every scolding like weather.

"Love your enemies; bless them that curse you," said Helen, when Jane planned to write a very stern letter indeed.

"If someone struck me with that branch, I'd break it over her head," said Jane.

"You mean well," said Helen mildly. "You would also be expelled by luncheon."

Then typhus ran through the damp rooms, and half the little monkeys sickened — and Helen, who had never been strong, lay dying in the quiet corner of the infirmary. Jane crept to her in the night, and Helen kissed her and said, "How comfortable I am. I am going to God. Do not grieve." Jane clung to her and fell asleep with her cheek against Helen's shoulder; and in the morning the teachers carried one small body gently out. When the scandal closed half the school, Jane stayed on — pupil, then teacher — until, at eighteen, she advertised for a governess's post and was hired at Thornfield Hall. Pupil: a little French monkey called Adèle, all curls and questions. Housekeeper: Mrs. Fairfax, a comfortable old gorilla who warned her of only one thing.

"The master is a little peculiar, dear. Just peculiar."

Jane's first meeting with the master was on the ice. She was walking in the dusk when a great ape on a dark horse came sliding past, slipped, and fell heavily to the ground.

"Give me your paw, then," the stranger grunted, and Jane helped him up — and only learned later that this was Mr. Edward Rochester himself, proud, sardonic, ugly, and interesting.

"Show me your mind," he demanded one evening by the fire. "Not your face, your *mind*."

"You have no right to command it, sir," said Jane, chin lifting, "any more than you have the right to command my fur."

"Hah!" said Rochester. "Most governesses show me their knitting. You show me your claws. Sit down."

The strangeness of Thornfield grew. A mad laugh in the attic. A fire in the master's bed one night, which Jane doused with every jug of water in the room. Then a house-party of grandees, led by Blanche Ingram, a beautiful society gorilla who hoped to be mistress of Thornfield. Rochester, disguised one evening as a gypsy ape with a shawl over his fur, read all their paws; he told Blanche precisely what she wished to hear, and saved Jane for last, and told her nothing at all, because he was too busy looking at her.

Jane, believing he would marry Blanche, wept where no one could see her — and Rochester called her back and asked her, properly this time, to marry him.

"Jane, you strange, almost unearthly thing — I have for the first time found what I can truly love. Will you be mine? Say yes, quickly."

"I will," said Jane, and meant it.

The night before the wedding, Jane woke to find a great shadow in her room — an ape-woman with wild, matted fur, who tore Jane's wedding veil in two, held it over her own face, and vanished. Jane told herself it was a dream. Half of Thornfield knew better.

On the wedding morning, as they stood at the altar, the church doors were flung open. Mason and a solicitor testified that Edward Rochester was already married — to a lunatic gorilla, Bertha Mason, whom he kept hidden in the attic, raving, setting fires, and biting guests.

Rochester led them all up the narrow stair and drew the bolt. There was Bertha, fur wild and matted, springing from figure to figure in the moonlit room, growling, and it took two keepers to hold her.

"That is my wife," said Rochester, low and terrible. "And this is what I would have made my prime mate. Compare us, and judge."

He begged Jane to stay with him as — well. Jane understood exactly what he meant, and stood straight.

"Do you think I am an automaton? A machine without feelings? You think that because I am small and plain and poor, I have no heart? I have as much heart as you — and as much fur! And if God had gifted me with beauty and wealth, I should have made it as hard for you to leave me as it is now for me to leave you!"

"I must respect myself," she said when he pleaded. "The more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained I am, the more I will keep the law appointed by my own troop." And she left him while he slept, with dawn coming, leaving one straight furrow of tears down her face and half her heart behind.

She fled into the wilderness with nothing — no bananas, no letters, no name — and nearly starved before the Rivers family took her in: St. John, a cold handsome chimp built entirely of granite and gospel; Diana and Mary, warm and witty. By impossible chance they were her own cousins, and when a dying uncle's fortune — left to Jane alone — made her rich, she split it four ways, because twenty thousand a year is ample for one ape, and company is worth more than coin.

St. John proposed that Jane marry him and carry the gospel to the far jungle of India — as his wife, though he did not love her.

"I scorn your idea of love," Jane told him, "and I scorn you when you offer it. I will go as your sister and fellow-laborer — but not as your wife."

"Jane, you are not forming a rational decision. You are forming a rash one."

"Then it is mine to form."

But before she could answer either way, one night in the forest the wind came through the trees, and she heard Rochester's voice on the wind: "Jane! Jane! Jane!" — and she answered, "I am coming," and waited, and heard nothing more.

She returned to Thornfield — and found it a blackened ruin. Bertha had fired the hall, climbed to the roof with the flames roaring under her, and fallen as Rochester watched from the lawn. He had gone in after her, and come out blind and maimed, one eye and one paw the poorer.

She found him at Ferndean, a lonely lodge in the woods — a broken ape sitting alone by a fire he could not see.

"Jane?" he said, disbelieving, when she came in without a sound. "It cannot be. You are only a dream — and a hungry one, for I have dreamed you every night for a year."

"I am here, sir. In the flesh — such fur as there is of it. I am independent now, and rich, and — if you will still have me — yours."

"Never mind your fortune. A blind ape can't count."

"Then let me count for both of us."

Reader, she married him. And when his sight partially returned — the first thing he saw was his firstborn son, cradled in Jane's arms — he wept like a great, tender, silly ape. From that day on they were each other's prime mate, equal as two monkeys sitting side by side on the very same branch — which, as every monkey knows, is the whole secret of happiness.
