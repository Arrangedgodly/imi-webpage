# Ookthello

*A parody of "Othello"*

Ook Ookthello was the mightiest silverback in all the Troop of Venezia-vine — a general with shoulders like tree trunks and a roar like the wet season. The whole troop loved him. Almost.

It began on a night of howling, when a lovesick spider-monkey named Roderigo sat at the edge of the vines, wailing about the gentle bonobo Desdemona.

"Tush, never tell me," said the capuchin Iago, dropping down beside him, pleased as a moldy fig. "You are upset that Desdemona has married the great Ook. I am upset that HE has passed ME over for promotion — again. I have served under him for years, following that enormous backside through every battle in the jungle, and what do I get? Second banana. He has made Cashew — that handsome, grinning, floppy young woolly monkey — my officer. I follow Ookthello to serve my turn upon him. I am not what I am."

"You are my only hope of winning Desdemona," sniffled Roderigo.

"And you," said Iago warmly, "are my purse. Thus do I ever make my fig-purse of my spider-monkey."

They began with Desdemona's father, old Brabantio, whom they waked at midnight with a chorus of hooting under his window: "Awake! What, ho, Brabantio! Thieves! Thieves! Look to your house, your daughter, and your fig stores! A great silver Ook is courting your daughter AWAY!"

But at the council, Ookthello rose and spoke quietly. "Most potent, grave, and reverend signiors: her father loved me, oft invited me to his branch, and I told him the story of my life — the moving accidents by flood and treetop, the sloths I wrestled, the long migrations. She loved me for the dangers I had passed, and I loved her that she did pity them. This only is the witchcraft I have used." And Desdemona said: "I am hitherto your daughter; but here's my husband. I do perceive here a divided duty."

"A divided duty," sighed Brabantio, "and the divider got there first. Take her, Ook. But mark me: she has deceived her father — hold that thought."

For Iago was a chimp off the old block, and twice as rotten. He had a plan, and the plan had a glove.

For when Ookthello courted Desdemona, he had sewed her a glove of banana-blossom silk stitched with little golden moons — charmed, said the old monkeys; there was magic in the web of it. She wore it everywhere.

Step one: Cashew must fall. On night watch, Iago sang drinking songs until the moon blushed — "And let me the banakin clink, clink; and let me the banakin clink!" — and poured cup after cup of fermented sap into young Cashew, who had one small weakness: he could not hold his sap. Cashew brawled with the guard monkey Montano over which of them had the finer haircut, and both ended in the mud, and Ookthello arrived, and Cashew's rank ended that night on the spot.

"Reputation, reputation, reputation!" wept Cashew the next morning. "O, I have LOST my reputation! I am nothing now!"

"Soft you," said Iago, patting him with fake pity. "Reputation is an idle and most false imposition, oft got without merit and lost without deserving. You have lost nothing at all — unless you repute yourself such a loser." And then, at his very gentlest: "The general's wife is the general's wife. Go to her; let her plead you back into his favor. She is of so free, so kind, so apt, so blessed a disposition, she holds it a vice in her goodness not to do more than she is requested. BESOUGHT her, nightly."

Which is how Desdemona came to plead Cashew's case at every hour — and how the whole plot rolled itself out like a vine, right into Iago's waiting paws.

For step two, Iago begged a certain glove from his own wife Emilia, Desdemona's best friend, a sharp-eyed marmoset, who picked it up where Desdemona dropped it, not knowing what it was for. And Iago dropped it into Cashew's nest.

The rest was gossip, deployed like artillery.

"My lord," said Iago, sidling up. "Beware of jealousy! It is the green-eyed jaguar, which doth mock the meat it feeds on. Not that I think Cashew would ever — I saw nothing — I say no more."

"You have said nothing," said Ookthello slowly, "and yet I have heard a great deal. Give me the ocular proof."

"O monstrous world! Does my wife give him secret grooming? And has a certain glove, dotted with golden moons, been seen in a certain young monkey's NEST?"

Ookthello rose to his full height, and the vines shook. "O, now, forever, farewell Ookthello's occupation's gone! Farewell the tranquil mind! Farewell the big troop, the pride, pomp, and circumstance of glorious grooming!" Which was Ookthello for: *I am about to have a very bad afternoon.*

"Excellent wretch!" he added, gazing after Desdemona as she passed. "Perdition catch my soul, but I do love thee — and when I love thee not, chaos is come again."

"Chaos is on schedule, my lord," said Iago, softly, to himself.

That afternoon Ookthello asked Desdemona for the glove. She searched her nest, her grooming pouch, her branches. Nothing.

"It is lost, my lord. But it will be found."

"Fetch it," said Ookthello, like distant thunder.

"It is not lost, but what if it were?" said Desdemona gently, and tried to plead for poor Cashew besides, which was the worst possible moment for it.

"The glove!" roared the great Ook, and stormed off, where Iago found him writhing in mental agony.

"My lord, I told you what I thought. Lie with her? Lie ON her? What?" said Iago, washing his paws of the whole business, then clutching his fur and crying when Ookthello demanded more: "Demand me nothing! What you know, you know! From this time forth I never will speak word!" — the most villainous way anybody has ever said *made you think it.*

That evening Desdemona sat by the window-branch, singing of willows. "The poor monkey sat sighing by a sycamore tree; sing all a green willow. His unkindness may defeat my life, but never taint my love... I called my love false monkey; but write 'prime ape,' 'prime ape' o'er his brow." Emilia fussed with her fur and said the world was a silly place when a glove could outweigh a heart.

At dusk, Ookthello came.

"It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul," he said grimly. "Put out the light, and then — put out the light." He raised his great paw — and, family legend insists, at the last instant the big ape found he could not. The legend is correct: he put out the lantern instead, and pointed to the Lonely Fig islet. "Go. Never let me see your glove, your face, or your golden moons again."

And Desdemona went weeping into exile.

Iago, meanwhile, had mopped up loose ends: he sent Roderigo to ambush Cashew in the dark, promising him Desdemona. But Roderigo was a spider-monkey, ninety percent elbows, and Cashew was a woolly monkey, ninety percent bounce — the ambush ended with both of them tangled in a single vine, and the whole night's work hung from a branch like laundry, in plain view.

Which was exactly when Emilia burst into the council, tiny and furious.

"O, thou dull monkey! IT WAS I! I picked up the glove by the pool, my husband begged it from me — said it was for a magic trick — and dropped it, I saw him, in young Cashew's nest! And for a pawful of berries and a lie, you have banished the truest bonobo that ever climbed!"

The troop turned to Iago, who said the worst thing a villain can: "I told him what I thought. Demand me nothing: what you know, you know. From this time forth, I never will speak word."

"Then SPEAK never AGAIN," said the council, and demoted him to the night-nursery, where for a season he re-folded forty mandrill diapers nightly, in silence — a fate worse than tarantulas. Roderigo, released from the vine, took up macramé and found himself.

Cashew was restored; the great silverback himself could not be. A fast boat of figs was sent to the Lonely Fig islet, and Emilia herself rowed Desdemona home — but Ookthello was gone by then. He stood before the troop one last time, quiet for such an enormous monkey.

"Soft you; a word or two before I go. Speak of me as I am, nothing extenuate: one that loved not wisely, but too well; of one not easily jealous, but, being wrought, perplexed in the extreme; of one whose paw threw away a pearl worth more than all his tribe."

He looked toward the sea.

"Tell them this also — that the mightiest grip in the jungle is no match for the smallest lie, and that I, Ook Ookthello, went ape for love and lost my prime mate listening to a monkey with a grievance and a glove."

Then he swung off alone into the misty mountains — where, the old monkeys say, he still sits at sunset, holding one glove of banana-blossom silk, stitching little golden moons until the light gives out. And they say a bonobo crosses the water to visit him, every full moon, with a lantern — and that the trick is not to put the light out. Never put the light out.
