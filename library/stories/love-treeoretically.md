# Love, Treeoretically

*A parody of "Love, Theoretically"*

Elsie Hannaway had exactly one marketable skill, and it was not theoretical physics.

Well — it was. She had a doctorate in vine theory from Banana Tech, a dissertation on the topology of tangled canopy systems that three separate committees had called "brilliant" and no department had ever called "hire-able." Theoretical physics, it turned out, was a very small treehouse with a very long waiting branch, and Elsie — a small, round-eared capuchin with allergic shiners and a student loan the size of a banyan — was not in the treehouse. She was an adjunct at Boston Ape College, teaching four sections of "Physics for Poets" to undergraduates who believed gravity was a state of mind.

Which was why, three evenings a month, Elsie had a second job.

She was a professional fake girlfriend.

It paid two hundred dollars an evening, plus dinner. Through the app — Rent-a-Primate — Elsie had been the adoring date of accountants, one alpaca farmer, and a groom whose entire family believed he was incapable of love. She held paws at funerals. She laughed at uncles. She was, in the words of her five-star reviews, "warm, believable, and great with grandmothers."

Her favorite client, the one she'd been seeing for eight months, was Jack Smith.

Jack was a forestry professor at Banana Tech — canopy science, old-growth groves — an absurdly good-humored gibbon with forearms like branch connectors and no discernible interest in physics. He booked her for family events only, always at the same trattoria, always sitting with his back protectively toward the wall, as if grandmothers might attack.

"Ground rules," he said, that first night, sliding the breadbasket toward her with a waiter's ease. "My mother thinks you're called Hannah, you think my research is fascinating, and if my brother joins us, you're studying to be a dental hygienist."

"Why a dental hygienist?"

"Because he'll stop asking questions immediately. He's an intellectual snob. Also he's vaguely evil, but that's a physics problem."

"Evil," said Elsie, "is not a physics problem."

"It is if you do it peer-reviewedly," said Jack, and smiled, and Elsie — who had not been on the receiving end of a smile in some years — wrote in her notes app, *Problem: client too charming. Unprofessional.*

The brother joined them in March.

"Jonathan," said Jack. "My little brother. Endowed chair of High-Energy Smugness at Banana Tech."

Jonathan Smith-Tiller was a sleek, silver-fanged academic in a blazer that cost more than Elsie's semesterly wage. And Elsie knew him instantly, the way you know a smell: he was Reviewer Two. Reviewer Two, the anonymous peer reviewer who had spent the last four years massacring her papers — who had called her vine-theory program "frivolous," her prose "overconfident," and, once, devastatingly, her career "a promising start." Every grant rejection, every lost postdoc, every door closed with a polite click: Reviewer Two. She'd had nightmares about him in which he was a faceless bureaucrat with her dissertation in his teeth.

He had a face now. It was asking her about dental hygiene.

"Do you find," said Jonathan, "that flossing studies lack rigor?"

"Yes," said Elsie, with strange calm. "Peer review is a mess. Anonymous reviewers with no accountability, using the veil to settle personal scores? I'd say it's the most rigor-free institution in science."

"Mm," said Jonathan, delighted to have found a hobbyist. "Blind review protects candor."

"Blind review," said Elsie, "protects cowards with red pens."

Jack, watching, mouthed *hired* across the table.

---

She should have ended the contract that night. Instead — here was the unforgivable, unphysical part — she kept taking the jobs, and the jobs kept changing shape. Fake dinners became real walks. Walks became the arboretum at closing time, Jack pointing out shagbark hickories like a father showing off babies. Once, when her grading marathon left her in tears at 1 a.m., he biked across the city with soup, sat on her fire escape, and said nothing for a heroically forestry-shaped forty minutes.

"You know what I like about trees?" he said finally. "They look like they're competing, and the whole time they're networking underground, feeding each other through the roots. Science spent a century thinking forests were a competition. Then somebody dug."

"Are you flirting at me with mycorrhizae, Professor?"

"Is it working?"

Statistically, she thought. Statistically it was working. She said, "Your brother ended my career. Every paper I publish, he kills in review. And he doesn't even know it's me, and I can't prove it's him, and I've spent four years telling myself it doesn't matter while adjuncting for the salary of a popcorn vendor. You come from the treehouse, Jack. I live in the yard."

"So apply," said Jack. "The department has a search. Theoretical group. This fall."

"With Jonathan Smith-Tiller on the committee?"

"With me on the search committee too," said Jack. "Trees, Hannaway. Network from the roots."

---

The Banana Tech job talk was the worst and best hour of her life. Standing in the seminar treehouse before the Old Boys of High Energy — fifteen elderly mandrills who had run theoretical physics since the Sixties — Elsie explained, in forty minutes, that vine theory could map the entanglement of canopy systems the way string theory mapped particles: that the tangle was not noise, the tangle was the structure, and if you dug under the competition you would find the forest feeding itself.

Question one: "Isn't this a bit — decorative?"

Question two: "Who else has cited this?"

Question three, from the back, silver-fanged: "A follow-up. Reviewer concerns have historically noted overconfidence."

And Elsie Hannaway, who had swallowed four years of *frivolous*, stood up on her little branch in her one good blazer and went ape.

"Let's test the confidence hypothesis," she said. "I've got the review trail. Same idiom across six papers, same three phrases, same one-month turnaround that matches one reviewer's sabbatical calendar. Anonymous peer review is a beautiful theory with one unphysical assumption: that the reviewer has no mass. Well, reviewers have mass. They bend careers around them. And I have spent four years orbiting one particular gravity well and I'm done pretending it's my own failure of trajectory."

Silence. Then, slowly, from a very old mandrill in front: "I was on her dissertation committee. She's right about the tangle."

And Jack, forestry, at the back, said: "For the record, the department asked me to comment on the candidate's collaborative record. In canopy science we have a term for a system where one node intercepts every nutrient before the others can feed. We call it a parasite. I'd move the question."

Jonathan did not move the question. Jonathan went, over the following weeks, quietly crimson, recused himself from the search, and produced — by way of apology, four years late — a review of Elsie's latest paper that used the words "landmark" and "long overdue," which Elsie framed and hung above her desk like a trophy head.

She got the job.

She also, that same week, took the unforgivable, unphysical step of showing up at the trattoria without an invoice.

"Hannah," said Jack, rising, alarmed, delighted. "This isn't a family event."

"No," said Elsie. "I quit the app. I've decided to do something reckless with my data."

"Which is?"

"Four years, one client, five-star reviews, zero third dates," she said. "The evidence says I'm not pretending. The evidence says I started falling for you somewhere around the shagbark hickories, and the evidence has never once been wrong, even when I was."

Jack Smith, who had never once in eight months reached for her paw without a contract authorizing it, came around the table like a gibbon crossing a canopy — fast, sure, arm over arm.

"I have a disclosure," he said, close enough to count. "The dental hygienist cover was my brother's idea. The Hannah cover expired in my mother's mind around dinner two. She thinks we've been in love for eight months and that I'm slow."

"Are you? Slow?"

"In forestry terms, Hannaway," he said, taking her paw at last, "I'm old growth. I take a century. But I'm deep-rooted, I network, and I'm extremely hard to cut down."

They ordered dinner. It went on the joint account. Somewhere across the city, in the great glass treehouse of theoretical physics, the Old Boys filed out grumbling under their umbrellas, and one new professor walked home through the rain with a forestry gibbons' arm around her, theoretically, practically, and every other way there is.
