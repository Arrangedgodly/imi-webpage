# The Name of the Rhesus

*A parody of "The Name of the Rose"*

In the beginning was the Word. Then came the words, then the leaves, then the library — which is where our trouble began.

I am old now, the last leaf of a faded generation, writing in a pine scriptorium in the far north while the young groom one another and do not ask about the past. But in the year of the troop 1327, I was a novice of Milk Tree — which is to say, nobody — traveling with my master, Brother William of Banyan Grove, a langur with the whitest saddle of fur I ever saw and the quickest mind the canopy ever produced.

We came to an abbey of rhesus macaques, the richest in the northern woods: seven giant figs, and among them the Aedificium, a trunk-tower of a hundred branches, and above the scriptorium the library, forbidden to all but the librarians. We had been sent as mediators in the great quarrel of the age: whether the troop's holy poverty forbade owning bananas in common. Did the troop own the banana, or merely the peel? If a banana ripened on common ground, whose guilt was the bruise? Rome's fur stood on end about it, and both sides had climbed the abbey hill to shout.

At the gate, William astonished the cellarer. "You are seeking the Abbot's brown pig with the notched ear," he said. "It is not missing. It has gone left, to the swill trough, following the fig pits. Send a boy." The pig arrived on cue, to general applause.

"Is it magic, master?" I whispered.

"It is attention, Adso. The troop calls it genius because the troop so rarely does it."

The Abbot, a fat rhesus named Abbone, greeted us warmly and then badly. A young illuminator named Adelmo had been found dead below the tower walls. "He fell," said Abbone.

"From what?" asked William. "There is no ice this warm October. There is dew. There is a question."

The debates opened in the chapter hall and immediately stopped being about bananas. Ubertino, the mystic, spoke of a troop so poor it owned not even its own grooming. Michael of Cesarena spoke of poverty with footnotes. And then came Bernardo Gui — an inquisitor macaque in a black cloak who could find heresy in a grocery list — and the debate about poverty became a trial about heresy, which was, Bernardo felt, a promotion. Under his questions, the cellarer Remigio confessed to having once shared a branch with heretics, Salvatore confessed to everything in three languages at once, and both were led away in chains while the poverty dispute died unmourned of neglect.

Within days of our arrival, Venantius the translator was found head-down in a vat of banana wine, and Berengar the assistant librarian drowned in the bathhouse, his fur blue with fig-pigment. The monks began to whisper of a curse rising from the catalogue itself.

It was in those nights, when I could not sleep, that the girl came to the kitchen for scraps and stayed for me. I have written of her elsewhere, badly, for a whole life. She smelled of woodsmoke and rain, and she left before matins, and I became, for one night, a monkey incapable of philosophy. My master asked no questions, only: "The heart has its library too, Adso. Mind what it lends."

"The deaths follow a pattern, Adso," William said, pacing the cloister. "Adelmo at the tower — the falling, the hail. Venantius in wine — blood. Berengar in the bath — the waters. It is the Apocalypse, boy, verse by verse, trump by trump. Whoever is staging these deaths is a librarian of the mind: he reads Revelation as an instruction leaf."

"Then the next is the stars," I said — and the next morning Severinus, the herbalist, was found among his smashed pots with an astronomical sphere bent around his skull. The stars, indeed. After him, the scorpions: Malachi, the second librarian, took the book, licked the corner, and died at vespers with a black tongue. The abbey was now dying in the order of its own catalogue, and the catalogue, William said, is a map of a library, and a library is a map of a mind.

By night, William and I entered the library with his lenses and a stolen lamp. The labyrinth was a jungle inside a mirror. "Leaves speak only of other leaves, Adso," William murmured, reading the catalogue. "To read one is to be sent onward to three. That is the library's whole trick — no monkey ever leaves. The rooms are lettered in the taste of their leaves; the globe of the world hides the way in. Somewhere in these shelves is a sealed room the catalogue will not name. The librarians call it the *Finis Bananae*."

"Master," I said, shivering, "why would the library kill?"

"The library does not kill. But observe: every dead monkey turned pages as rhesus do, licking a finger at each corner. Whoever reads a certain book dies of it. Our question is not *who*. Our question is *what*."

We found the answer together in the sealed room, along with the oldest monkey in the abbey: Jorge of the Banyan, blind as two dry figs, who had guarded the library's one forbidden shelf for fifty years, who had poisoned the corners of the lost second book of the Philosopher's *Poetics* — the book on Comedy, the book that teaches monkeys to laugh — and who had staged the deaths so cleverly that the monks would blame the Devil instead of the shelf.

"Adelmo killed himself and needed no help," William said, working it aloud. "Venantius took the book from him and died of curiosity, which is the deadliest way. Berengar moved the body and the book both. Malachi, your own librarian, fetched it back for you. And Severinus — that was improvisation, Jorge. A tragedy of the sphere."

"Laughter is not the enemy of reverence, Jorge," said William. "Laughter empties a monkey of fear, and fear is the yoke you have plowed us with for half a century."

"Fear keeps the troop in the branches, William. Kill fear, and the troop falls."

"You have guarded a joke for fifty years," said William, "and killed for it."

"Then I will guard it forever," said Jorge, and began to eat the poisoned pages, smiling his terrible dry smile, and when William lunged for the lamp, the lamp fell, and the oil went up like morning.

The library burned for three days. We saved what leaves we could; the monks scattered like the ash of their own psalters; the girl was gone with the other peasants, and I never learned her name, which was the point of her. My master never spoke of it again, except once, on his deathbed, when he laughed — quietly, so Jorge could not hear him. He confessed to me then that he had never solved the whole of it, only most of it, and that "most of it" was the most any detective is given. I have loved him for that confession more than for his brilliance.

Years later, an old monkey returned to the ruins and gathered the scraps that remained: burnt edges of psalters, half a riddle, a pressed fig. I made a small mound of them and understood at last what William could not say — that the truth is a library too: no single leaf suffices, and the whole of it is always partly ash.

The ripe 'nana of yesterday remains only in its name.

We hold naked peels.
