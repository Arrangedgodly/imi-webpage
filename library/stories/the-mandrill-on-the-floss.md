# The Mandrill on the Floss

*A parody of "The Mill on the Floss"*

A wide plain, where the broadening Floss hurries on between green banks to the sea, and where, on the bank nearest the sea, stood Mr. Tulliver's mill — a great wooden contrivance that ground the jungle's banana-blossom into flour and kept the Tullivers comfortable.

Dorlcote Mill, it was called, and in it grew up two young mandrills: Tom, thirteen, and Maggie, two years younger and infinitely more trouble.

Maggie Tulliver was a small, black-furred, long-limbed thing with a muzzle just beginning to blush into the brilliant blue and scarlet of her kind, and a soul that blushed far ahead of it. She was always in trouble — for climbing too high, for reading too many books, for forgetting her fur ought to be smooth, for loving too hard and too fast.

"You're a naughty little girl," Tom told her, with the satisfaction of a brother who was seldom naughty and never caught.

"I don't mean to be naughty," Maggie would say, her eyes filling. "It's only that I want things so."

What she wanted most was Tom's love; what she got, most days, was Tom's approval, and only when earned. Once, to be striking, since she could not be pretty, she hacked her fur off with the kitchen shears, close to the skin — and learned from the aunts' horror, and Tom's, that a female monkey's pelt is troop property. She pushed her cousin Lucy into the mud for pitying her, and ran away to live among the gypsies, on the theory that a monkey too much for her own troop might be a queen among strangers. The gypsies, who had assumed the arrangement would run the other way, returned her before nightfall.

Maggie wanted Latin, and books, and the tallest kapok in the grove, and to be loved best — particularly by Tom. But Tom's love was a wage: he loved Maggie when she was good and withheld it when she was naughty, an arrangement that never once struck him as unfair.

Then came the great ruin. Mr. Tulliver, a hot-tempered old mandrill who could never let a lawyer have the last word, went to law with Wakem over the water rights to the Floss — which mill might grind whose bananas — and the law, as it usually does, ground Mr. Tulliver. The Dodson aunts convened like a parliament of owls. Aunt Glegg lent money at family rates and mentioned it at family volume; Aunt Pullet wept for the lace, which could not now be willed to anybody; Aunt Gritty wept for everybody. The mill was sold over Mr. Tulliver's head — to Wakem himself — and Tom left school at fifteen and went to grind figures in the counting-house of Guest & Co., where he grew gray-furred before his time and hoarded revenge instead of wages.

"Father," said Tom one night, laying the money on the table, "it is done. Every debt is paid."

The old mandrill wept, dried his eyes, took up his stick, and met Wakem on the road. He fell upon his enemy with all his failing strength; and then, in the very hour of his victory, fell dead — the sort of thing that happens to hot-tempered mandrills in tragedies.

Maggie, meanwhile, had made two friends. The first was Philip Wakem, the lawyer's gentle crook-backed son, a small colobus with a noble spirit and a voice like rain. As children they met in secret in the Red Deeps, a fern-hollow under the bank, where Philip taught her Latin and she taught him happiness, and they read novels beneath the fronds.

"Do you never wish you were strong?" Maggie asked him once.

"I wish it every hour," said Philip. "But I have learned what the strong never learn — to sit still and look at things. You fly at the world, Maggie, like a bird at a window."

"And you praise the glass," said Maggie. "I would rather be the bird."

It ended when Tom discovered the meetings and broke them — calling Philip by his father's name with a cruelty that made even himself flinch. "You will not see him again," said Tom, who had inherited the lawsuit along with the stick. Maggie obeyed, out of the old wage-love; she had a lifelong talent for obeying orders she despised.

The second was Stephen Guest — a handsome mandrill with the finest muzzle in three counties, engaged to Maggie's cousin Lucy, and in love with Maggie on sight. Lucy, who was good the way bread is good, suspected nothing. Stephen, who was charming the way weather is weather, did nothing about everything.

One golden afternoon they drifted down the Floss — Stephen rowing, Maggie's heart in a storm of its own — and by the time she understood the current, they were miles toward Mudport and an elopement. At the last, Maggie refused him, climbed the bank, and walked home through the night.

The jungle did not forgive her. To the jungles of those days, a girl who drifted down a river with her cousin's promised monkey might as well have drifted to the moon. Lucy's door was shut. Philip sent one letter, which forgave her, which was worse. Tom shut his too.

"You are a disgrace to our name," said Tom. "Go."

Maggie went out into the world to earn her bread among strange troops, with a heart so worn it no longer fluttered. She taught young monkeys their letters and her own private doctrine — that one must not wound others to please oneself — by which she got three meals a day and no converts whatever.

Only Bob Jakin stood by her — a ragged, cheerful macaque who had loved the Tullivers since boyhood, lived in a ditch with a family of hedgehogs, and considered Maggie the finest monkey ever whistled at. He offered her his life's savings, kept in an old boot; when she would not take them, he carried her trunk to the stage-coach on his own back, and tipped the driver a beetle — his second-best possession.

But the Floss was not done with the Tullivers. In one dark November the rain came down as though the sky had become the sea, and the Floss rose like a rising thought in an angry mind. Maggie, rowing back through the drowning fields, saw the old mill wheel buried and heard, above the roar of the water, her brother's voice in the mill-house tree.

Tom was in the old nursery kapok, and the water was climbing.

Something in Maggie that had never once failed him lifted the boat's head toward the mill. She reached the tree; Tom climbed down; and for one moment the two mandrills sat looking at each other, all the old quarrels small as lice in the great flood of the world.

"Magsy!" Tom said at last, using the name of their nursery. "You came for me."

"Then hold on," said Maggie, "and let us go down together."

The boat shot out upon the brown fury of the Floss — past the drowning garden, past the mill, bearing away the lawsuits and the wage-love — toward the deep, wide river. Then the boat drove full upon the trunk of a fallen fig tree, and the two little mandrills were left clinging to each other as the flood took them.

In their death they were not divided.

They lie in Dorlcote churchyard under one stone, and if you visit it when the mill-wheel drips, you may hear the jungle say what the stone says: that the brother and sister, who quarreled over everything else, kept the oldest promise of all — no monkey left behind.
