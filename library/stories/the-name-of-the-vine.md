# The Name of the Vine

*A parody of "The Name of the Wind"*

It was night again. The Swingstone Inn lay silent, and it was a silence of three parts.

The first silence belonged to the rain, ticking on the big leaves over the roof. The second belonged to the regulars downstairs, who had run out of opinions. The third and strangest silence was the innkeeper's: a red-furred chimp called Kote, who polished a spotless table and listened for a knock.

The regulars were gossiping about Kvothe — Kvothe the Branchless, Kvothe the peel-less wonder, Kvothe Kingkiller — the way people talk about weather they were never caught in. Green flame, they said. Seen it myself, they hadn't.

And in the corner, a lemur scribe with quick eyes set down his pen and said, "I am called Chronicler. I've come to write a true story. Will you give me three days?"

The innkeeper's rag stopped moving.

When the doors were shut and the last drunk swung home, Kote the innkeeper stood in his own lamplight and said, quietly: "My name is Kvothe. You will have heard the rest."

And he told it.

---

"I was born in the Edema Rhesus," Kvothe said, "a traveling troop. We were actors and singers and liars of the harmless sort, and my father's gourd-lute had seven strings. When I was small, an arcanist called Ape-benthy came to us in a wagon hung with bottles and taught me sympathy — which is monkey see, monkey do, with energy — and he taught me the size of the world.

"And it was Ape-benthy who told me the thing that mattered. 'Secrets hide in the spaces between leaves, boy. Even a vine has a name. Listen long enough — seven words is often enough — and the jungle will tell you, and after that, it will do what you ask.'"

Kvothe stopped. He looked at his hands as though they belonged to a stranger he was fond of and angry with, both.

"The Chandriapes came on a night like this one. Seven of them, with fire the green of unripe bananas. When I found my troop, the fires were out and the wagons were silent. My father's strings were cut. That green flame is the truest thing about me, Chronicler. Everything since has been a story told to outrun it."

He told it anyway. Three years starving in Tarbean, where he survived the way rats survive sieges: on scraps, in walls, disliked. Then the Caniversity, where he talked his way in at fifteen with nothing but a lute, a temper, and one perfect fatherless glare. The admission trial: nine masters, nine questions. He answered three correctly, one with another question, and one by fainting in a way the admissions ledger described as "honest." Master Lorren set his tuition at three talents. Kvothe owned a lute, a temper, and half a button.

So he went to Devi, a bonobo gaelet with a smile like a closing trap, who lent him tuition at interest that made the Chandriapes look sentimental. And he went to the Cannery, the artificers' hall, where he learned to build cunning things and once detonated a jar of bone-tar that got him whipped and expelled for a day. Whipping didn't trouble him. Bloodloss and he had an arrangement.

"Master Hemme humiliated me before a lecture hall," Kvothe went on, "so I listened — truly listened — to the ancient creeper on the wall, and spoke its name. Every vine in the hall answered. Hemme left the room at speed, trouser-first, borne out by his own ankle-garden. They called it calling the Vine. Master Elowden's whole naming class, meanwhile, consisted of pointing at a leaf and saying nothing. For months. Then one afternoon I understood, and it was somehow better and worse than every other lesson.

"Ambose was another education. A rich young jackape with a noble father and a grudge sized like the family estate. He mocked my clothes, so I mocked his poetry, which has no witnesses surviving. Then he broke my lute." Kvothe's voice went very quiet. "So I called the wind, and the wind called Ambose a kite. He flew the length of Imre. It is the only time I have ever been sorry and proud in the same breath, which I'm told is the sound oaths make.

"And in the Undergrowth beneath the Caniversity there was Auri, a tiny owl-monkey girl who kept the dark halls the way others keep prayers, and who gave me, very seriously, a key, a candle, and one perfectly ripe banana, because she was always right about things like that. She named a door after me. I have been named after many things. That is the one I kept."

He paused again, and drank, and told the part he had never told anyone without changing the subject first.

"Near Trebanana, at the Mauten orchard, there was a wedding. Thirty guests, a fiddle, a bride in woven flowers. The Chandriapes came, because the family had dug up something old and sung the songs nobody sings. When I got there with Denna — she arrived like weather and left like it too — the orchard was ash and the fiddlers' strings were cut like a memory I was having. A surviving child drew them for me in the cold dirt: seven shadows. Denna wanted to know what we should tell the villagers.

"'The truth?' she said.

"'The truth needs a better witness list,' I told her, and I have hated myself for the joke ever since, because she believed it was wisdom. I let her believe it. That is the part I would cross out, Chronicler, if crossing out worked."

"There was no crossing out the draccus, either," he said. "A marsh dragon gone mad on fermented fig-resin, sweet as forty gardens, stamping toward the town and the festival bonfire. I poisoned the bonfire. I dropped the iron wheel. I saved Trebanana, and nobody in it ever knew a hairless young chimp had done anything but start a rumor about a large bird. I have stolen princesses back from sleeping burrow-kings. I burned a demon-wasp nest with the ten words every mother should know. And I spent a season among the Ademur of the deep canopy, who speak with their hands and fight with their faces — no, the reverse — Kvothe the grammarless, they called me, and they were right.

"Vine-shet taught me the Lethani, which is not a path but the walking, and not a destination but the direction. 'You fight like a kettle,' she told me. 'Boil quieter.' They do not show emotion in public, the Ademur, because the face is a weapon and you do not leave weapons lying about. I have never been more exhausted in my life, and that includes the fire.

"And still, with Denna, I never once managed to say the thing I meant. Not at the orchard, not at the singing in Imre, not the night she left with the patron nobody names. She was a song I could harmonize with and never find the key of. Every wise monkey knows at least one of those."

He looked up at last, and his eyes did the thing the stories left out: they were tired.

"I burn with a bright and terrible silence, Chronicler. My name is Kvothe. You will have heard the rest."

He said it the way you set down something heavy.

---

Upstairs, later, a young man called Bast — who was beautiful, wicked, and only technically a monkey — stood in the doorway. "Why did you send him the *sad* parts, Reshi? Why not the fights? He came for Kvothe the Legend!"

"Because legends don't limp, Bast," said Kote, "and I do. Three days. Write it true. Everyone tells a story about themselves inside their own head, Chronicler says. That story makes you what you are. Mine has a green light in it, and I have been polishing this table for years trying to see which of us it reflects."

He turned down the lamp. "There are three things all wise monkeys fear: the monsoon at full roar, a night with no moon, and the anger of a gentle ape."

In the Swingstone, the silence settled back into its three parts, thoroughly at home — and out in the dark, the rain moved along the vines like something patient, calling itself by its own name, waiting for someone to listen again.
