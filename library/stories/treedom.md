# Treedom

*A parody of "Freedom"*

The Berglinds were the smug pioneers of the re-gentrified orchard district — the young couple who had moved into the derelict corner grove when the old fruit company went bust, replanted the apple rows with their own hands, and then composted their neighbors' shame by being nicer, greener, and more annoyingly in love than anyone on Barrier Branch.

Walter Berglind was a handsome, serious young gorilla of rigid principle — the monkey who returned extra change, who rode his basket to work at the Cerulean Tamarin Trust, who grieved visibly for every acre of canopy. Patty, his wife, was a great laughing golden she-wolf of a monkey, six feet of charisma in any room, a former canopy-ball comet whose college games were still spoken of at the field house with the reverence of scripture. Together they produced Joey, the golden child so golden that the whole street watched him like a soap opera, and Jessica, the sharp-eyed kit who saw everything, and for eleven years the Berglinds were the standard the orchard measured happiness by.

The neighbors would have been gratified to know how much of it was performance.

Patty's famous sunniness was a job she worked at, because underneath it she was a depressive from a family of cold, elegant monkeys who had never once, in her entire childhood, come to a single canopy-ball game. Her mother had preferred her sisters, her father preferred silence, and Patty had responded at fifteen by falling into catastrophic, hopeless, operatic love with a brooding punk rocker named Richard Katz.

Richard was Walter's best friend and opposite: a lazy, brilliant, self-sabotaging lemur-songwriter with a needle-thin snout and songs like "Furless in Minneapolis," too cool to want anything, and therefore wanted by everyone. Walter loved Richard like a brother and lived in dread of him; Patty loved Richard like a fever and lived in denial of him; and Walter, good Walter, had loved Patty since the moment he'd carried her groceries home in the rain, and had married her in the astonished belief that his luck had finally outrun his principles.

For twenty years it held. Then Richard came to stay at the lake.

Nameless Pond was the Berglind retreat in the far north woods, a cabin on a blue muskeg lake, and Patty — alone that week, her marriage souring under the weight of Walter's long silences and longer campaigns — did the thing she had been not-doing since college. She and Richard spent two days like teenagers in the woodshed; and Richard, incapable of believing in his own happiness, fled back to his slum studio in the city, and Patty was left with a wound that never closed.

Her punishment on herself was patient and terrible: at fifty, after her life had quietly collapsed — Joey gone, Jessica gone, Walter entrenched in his nonprofit and his causes, Patty alone in the orchard house with her lobsters of depression — she sat down and wrote it all out, coldly, in a document she titled, in the passive grammar of her people, *Mistakes Were Chewed*. Then she mailed it to Walter and moved to Brooklyn to teach canopy-ball to rich kits.

"Mistakes Were Chewed" broke Walter the way frost breaks a branch: slowly, all winter, and then all at once.

Because Walter's own faith was already cracking. The Cerulean Tamarin Trust existed to save one tiny, gorgeous, doomed species of tamarin, bluer than a bruise, that nested only on one ridge in the Appalachicola hills. Walter, the last honest monkey in conservation, had cut the deal of his life with the devil — the Big Bunch Fruit Company, the gray giant of the banana world, whose tractors were already eating the hills. Big Bunch would fund the whole tamarin preserve, every acre, forever — in exchange for the right to strip the rest of the range: full canopy removal, whole mountains scalped of trees down to orange mud.

"Population is the problem, Patty!" he raged at his young assistant, at the rallies, at his own reflection. "Six hundred million monkeys and climbing! We are a blight on the canopy, an infestation with thumbs — the tamarin would be fine if it weren't for *us!* The deal saves the bird — the *bird*, Lalitha! — and if I have to shake hands with Big Bunch and let them scalp a few mountains, that is the price, and someone has to be adult enough to pay it!"

The rallies — the Free Space movement, whole drumming encampments of young monkeys sleeping in the Trust's hollow oaks — were Lalitha's work. Lalitha was twenty-six, a brilliant, warm, relentless young langur who believed in Walter the way Walter no longer believed in anything, who organized, who strategized, who stayed late; and when Patty's memoir arrived like a letter bomb, and the marriage formally detonated, it was Lalitha who held the pieces, and Walter, being only a monkey after all, loved her, and let himself be loved, and hated himself efficiently in the mornings.

The corruption at the heart of it — the scam Walter never saw because it was family — was Joey's. Joey Berglind, the golden boy, had responded to his parents' perfect love by moving out of the house at sixteen, into the neighboring Kerrigans', where the mother was wild and the food was terrible and nobody told him he was wonderful. There he married, absurdly young, Connie Kerrigan — the strange, devoted girl who had loved him since he was four, who was to Joey what shade is to sun. And Joey, to prove something, went into the parts business: truckloads of "Grade A" hardware for the government's park fleet, bought cheap from a shady supplier, invoiced at triple, and shipped out with Connie's help at midnight, while his conscience sat in the corner pricing itself.

Joey's moment came when he actually inspected a shipment — the bolts were brittle, the lug-nuts would shear, the stuff could kill a park monkey in a landslide curve — and he had the one clean chance of his life to take the loss and walk. He took it. That is the thing about the Berglinds, the orchive said afterward: even their sins eventually got beaten by their stubbornness. He ate the debt, unwound the deal, and came home to Connie with his tail between his legs and his soul intact, which is the only way anybody in that family ever arrived anywhere.

The end came fast, as ends do. The Free Space encampment was cleared by marshals; the Big Bunch deal went through and the first mountain came off like a scab; Lalitha, driving all night to file the injunction papers, fell asleep at a curve in the rain and was killed by a timber lorry coming down from the stripped ridge with the last of the old canopy on its back.

Walter went genuinely mad for a season — the loner of Nameless Pond, ranting at the loons, chunking rocks at canoeists, a big old gorilla unraveling — until Jessica and Joey extracted him, and time did what it does, and a diagnosis of the shaking sickness (his father's disease; his hands trembled on the cope saw) gave his anger somewhere to live.

And then, in the last pages, the Berglinds got their small miracle — the one the whole orchard had never believed in, real late-life treedom. Patty, sixty now, silver along the spine, wrote one more page of *Mistakes Were Chewed*, an addendum titled "Why I Still Love Him," and got in the truck, and drove north, and knocked at the cabin door under the pines.

Walter, hands shaking, made her tea. They talked until the loons came out. And she stayed — through the tremors, through the slow taming of the woods, through the harvesting of morels and the great argument about the compost, and one perfect afternoon, floating side by side on the cold blue lake, Walter looked at the two of their reflections, the silverbacks of the Berglinds, and confessed that he finally understood what the word had meant all along.

"I used to think treedom was the fences," he said. "You know? Range of motion. The acreage a monkey can roam before somebody stops him."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's somebody to be ridiculous in front of," he said, "for thirty years, without shame. That's the whole conservancy, Patty. Everything else is fundable."

Below the lake, a loon dove; above it, on the far ridge, a pair of cerulean tamarins — fifty-one strong now, and counting — flickered up through the green like two struck sparks, going about the ancient business of the future.
