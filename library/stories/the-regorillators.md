# The Regorillators

*A parody of "The Regulators"*

Summer on Palm Frond Lane was so hot the cicadas were on strike. It was the summer the vans came.

The first one rolled up the lane at nine-oh-four exactly: a red jeep with a mural of a gorilla in mirrored shades on the hood and, under the grille, in letters of flame, the motto WE PEEL FOR YOU.

Old Doc was on his porch, coffee in paw, and he had time to say, "Now that's not regulation paint," before a bandana'd gorilla in the passenger seat sat up and let fly.

SPLAT.

A slug of overripe banana took Old Doc square in the chest and knocked him, sticky and astounded, backward into his hammock — and the hedges moved. The hedge reached out a green arm, wrapped the old monkey like a burrito, and tucked him into the landscaping. *Bushwhacked,* the lane would learn to call it.

By nine-fifteen, three more jeeps had rolled in — blue, green, one painted like a banana split with flame decals — and Palm Frond Lane was a shooting gallery. SPLAT, SPLAT, SPLAT. Gary took one through the kitchen window and was bushed mid-sentence; his wife, Marielle, was bushed a minute later, mid-yell, into the hydrangeas. Next door, Cammie had set up the slip-and-slide for her grandkids at eight o'clock sharp; by nine-oh-five it was a tarp with opinions.

And reality itself had started to go cheap. The trees, if you touched them, were plywood with painted shadows. The sky had a painted sunset in it at nine in the morning, the clock tower said nine-oh-four forever, and the end-of-lane mountains had gone noticeably two-dimensional. The phones worked, but every number rang the same recording: an old jungle spirit, humming.

On the Wyler porch, nine-year-old Seth sat calm as a stone, his toy jeeps lined along the rail — red, blue, green, each an exact match for the vans in the street. His aunt Audrey crouched over him with a couch cushion for a shield.

"It's the show," she said. "Oh, Seth. It's *the show.*"

Seth had loved an ancient Banana Western called *The Regorillators* — gorilla lawmen riding through Tupelo Canyon, peeling the unworthy. Audrey knew it frame by frame, because she had watched four hundred episodes at Seth's side; that's what an aunt is. She also knew the boy had started watching it the spring his mother and father went down in the Tin Mine — the picnic at the rim, the blast nobody had filed a permit for — and that Seth had made his peace the way boys do: by inventing the Bananakops, a nicer troop, the week after the funeral. And something else had found him at that same rim: a cheeky old jungle spirit named Tuk, sealed in the shafts by a blast sixty years back, all elbows and appetite, who had looked at a lonely boy's head the way a cuckoo looks at somebody else's nest. Lonely boy, open door. Move right in. Tuk moved in right next door to the grief and played his shows at full volume.

*Tuk likes the show,* said a voice from Seth's mouth, in a rusty squeak. *Tuk likes the ending of things.*

There came a lull at nine-forty, as if the show had cut to commercial. The survivors gathered in Cammie's kitchen — sticky, terrified, and unarmed except for Johnny, who was armed with opinions. Down the lane the split-van's driver, a cartoon gorilla with a sheriff's jaw, hollered his catchphrase into the painted sky: "YEE-HAW! PEEL 'EM ALL AND LET THE SUN SORT IT OUT!"

"That's from season two," Audrey said. "The bad season."

"It's all bad, sweetheart," said Johnny. "It was bad in the fifties and it's bad now. Question is why a dead western is shooting at my street."

"Because of him," Steve said, and didn't have to point.

"Then we get him off the air," said Cynthia. "My mother canceled three shows with one letter. It can be done."

Tuk heard them, the way Tuk heard everything through the boy's ears, and the radio in Cammie's kitchen crackled to life on its own. *Tuk doesn't like talk-shows,* it squeaked. *Tuk likes the other kind.*

"You're a boring little snot is what you are," said Johnny Marinville — one book published, famously, clinging to the underside of the Selbys' porch — "Audrey! It's him or us! Change the channel!"

*Tuk could give you your own episode,* the squeak offered her, silken. *You'd be the star. Better hair. A catchphrase.*

"I don't do scripted," Audrey said, and threw a peach pit at the porch rail, where it burst like a small fragrant grenade.

There was a plan at ten: Johnny, Steve, and Cynthia would sprint the gap to the zookeeper's truck in the lull between vans. The sprint went beautifully for four seconds, at which point the green jeep rounded the flowerbed and SPLAT-SPLAT-SPLAT, and the truck, the mattress, and three monkeys ended up folded into the neighbor's topiary like a raffle prize.

"Plan B?" Cynthia asked from the shrubbery.

"Run better," Steve said.

Which left Audrey.

She had read to Seth since he was small. She knew the boy under the blankness, and she knew the difference between a hostage and a fan.

"Seth," she said, kneeling, slugs bursting on the railing overhead. "Look at my face. Not the show. My face. You've seen this episode a hundred times, and it's a *rerun*, and you hate reruns."

The painted sky flickered.

*Tuk likes reruns,* squeaked the voice, but weaker. Sulky. *Tuk—*

"You know what's better than the Regorillators?" Audrey pointed at the last jeep on the rail — the blue one. "The one you made up. The Bananakops. You told me they rescue everybody at nine-oh-four. It's nine-oh-four, Seth."

Seth's eyes came on like a small television. His paw moved, slow as syrup, and rolled the blue jeep an inch along the rail.

Out in the lane, the red jeep popped like a seed pod — and reinflated, repainted, with a cartoon banana badge on the door. The bandana'd gorillas looked at their bandanas — now sheriff's stars — looked at each other, shrugged with magnificent dignity, and drove off into the painted sunset, which had the grace to look embarrassed. The sky peeled away its paint like old wallpaper. Under it was the real morning — ten past ten, sun out, cicadas late back at work and grumbling about it.

Tuk came out of Seth's ear with a sound like a soap bubble losing an argument — one last squeak of *kill-joy* — and went off over the trees, looking, everyone agreed, for a ruder jungle.

The hedges burped. All across the lane, sticky, furious, alive monkeys came unwrapped out of the shrubbery — Old Doc first, indignant, already brushing off his coffee — and for a week afterward Palm Frond Lane smelled of banana bread and lesson learned. The clock tower, catching up on itself, rang ten past ten eleven times, just to be sure.

Cammie turned the slip-and-slide back on, and the grandkids had the lane's first and only water-skiing gorilla impression. Johnny Marinville began a second book, about a street that fought back, and told nobody it was mostly true. Steve and Cynthia dated cautiously, the way you re-enter a tree after a fall.

Seth never watched the old show again. Cartoons only, and only with Audrey.

At nine-oh-four the next morning, he rolled the blue jeep one more inch along the rail, and nobody anywhere got bushed.
