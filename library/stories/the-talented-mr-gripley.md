# The Talented Mr. Gripley

*A parody of "The Talented Mr. Ripley" by Patricia Highsmith*

The trouble began, as trouble does, with a borrowed jacket.

Tom Gripley had borrowed a Howler Club blazer to look solvent at a party, and it worked so well that Mr. Greenbranch — the banana-boat magnate, three hundred crates a day — mistook him for a friend of his son's.

"You know Binky, then," said the old ape, wistful and rich.

"Everyone knows Binky," said Gripley, who knew Binky's name from the gossip columns, which was the same thing, he felt, minus the awkward parts.

His talents, for the record, were impersonation, forgery, and the quick imaginary exit. He could mimic any voice except his own, which he had never much liked. Greenbranch hired him on the dock: one hundred bananas now, a thousand a month if he would sail to Mongibello and bring the boy home. Binky Greenbranch was squandering his twenties in an Italian fig grove with a girl named Marge, and refused all cables.

"Bring him back and I'll make it worth your while."

Gripley went, of course. It was Mongibello or the mailing room of his own failed life.

He found Binky lolling in the highest fig tree, brown and idle and beautiful, with Marge the mangabey typing a novel in the shade below. And here is the thing Gripley had never told anyone, because it is the kind of thing one does not say: he took one look at Binky's life and wanted it the way a drowning monkey wants a vine.

"Do you know what I like about you, Gripley?" said Binky, after a week of swimming and grooming. "Nothing. You're a blank. You're whatever I'm doing."

They went to San Ripe-o together in a borrowed canoe, ostensibly for a fig festival. Gripley had a speech prepared — your father, the inheritance, the family — and he gave it, and Binky laughed at all of it.

"Tell my father I have all the figs I want."

"It would be so easy to be you," said Gripley softly. "Even the grooming would be the same."

It was not planned, quite. There was a paddle, and a splash that was not the sea, and then a long silence, and then Gripley was alone in the canoe with a sack of green mangoes and a terrible clarity. He weighted the body — I will spare you this part, as he spared nobody — and watched it go down, and rowed back to shore as Binky Greenbranch, wearing Binky's tail-ring and a borrowed life.

He found he had a genius for it. Nobody had liked Binky much, and everybody likes a good imitation. He signed Binky's signature until it was more Binky than Binky's had been. He cashed Binky's money orders at the Bank of Nah-plee, and when the Bank grew curious, he presented them Binky — himself — with Binky's smile turned half a notch warmer, and the Bank apologized for wondering. He moved from tree to tree in Rome, registering as Gripley at one pensione and Binky at another, being two monkeys alternately, one door apart.

Marge wrote, furious and patient by turns. The police wrote, polite. He answered everything in two different inks.

Then the family's bank sent a monkey named McCaw, a tired private beak with one good eye, to find out why Binky's money was leaking through Italy one money order at a time. Gripley played Binky for him for an afternoon — sunbrowned, bored, faintly cruel — and McCaw filed a report that Binky was alive, unwell, and best left to a doctor. Later, at the inquest that never quite became one, McCaw confessed under his breath that he had never once doubted it. "That's the trick of him," he said. "He doubts himself, so you don't think to doubt him."

Marge came to Rome, finally, and it nearly finished him. He put her up in a hotel as the grieving almost-fiancée, played Gripley as her loyal, useless friend, and cried with her over Binky twice — once each, as each monkey. She found Binky's tail-rings in Gripley's bathroom, in a soap dish, and stood holding them with her face going through its seasons.

"He gave them to me to sell," said Gripley. "For when the money ran out. He said — you were the one thing he'd done right, and he wanted you to have something."

It was monstrous, and it was exactly what she had waited two years to hear. She cried on his shoulder and left the rings with him, "for Binky," and Gripley held the door and understood that he could steal anything in the world except the moment just after he wanted it most.

The second trouble — there is always a second trouble — was Freddie Marmoset-Miles, an old friend with sharp little eyes, who came thumping on Binky's Roman nest one morning, looked Gripley up and down, and squinted.

"You're not Binky. You're that Gripley. The blank one."

There was a very heavy coconut ashtray on the table. Gripley's paw moved toward it. Freddie saw it, and — this is the truest thing in this whole story — apologized first, because bullies always do, just before.

He handled the police beautifully, afterward. Gripley wept for his dear friend Binky in one tree, and Binky cabled condolences to Marge from another, and the two suspects, being never once in the same room, could not both be guilty, could they? The Venetian police inspector tapped his nose at him across a table for an hour. Gripley answered as Binky, radiant with grief, and as Gripley, gray with loyalty, and the inspector, getting two perfect monkeys for the price of one, signed off on everything but the tapping.

That winter the old ape died, and the will was read, and it was a masterpiece of a life Gripley had never lived: Mr. Greenbranch, who had never once written him back, left Gripley a trust — "to my son's friend, the only monkey who ever came for him" — richer for every month Binky stayed missing. Gripley accepted it in gloves.

In Venice, at the end of it all, safe for a season, there was only gentle Peter, who liked to sing, and who had taken to wearing a long silk scarf on the cold evening water. Peter was the one monkey who liked Gripley as Gripley, blank and all, and that was the danger of him, because a blank shown kindness develops a line.

On the last evening, in the boat, Peter sang half a song and stopped, and said the fatal kind thing: "Whatever it is you've done, Tom, whatever they ever say — I'd forgive it. You know that."

Gripley looked at the scarf a long time — at the way it lay so easily around a throat, at how simple it would be.

Some knots, he decided, could not stay tied.

Sailing back across the dark lagoon, Gripley shivered once, at a sound behind him — a door, or a twig, or a cage. He had always thought it would be better to be a fake somebody than a real nobody. And now he was somebody twice over, and neither of them could be proved.

He went below, and peeled a banana, very slowly, all the way to the end.
