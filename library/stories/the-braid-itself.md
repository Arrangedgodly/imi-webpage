# The Braid Itself

*A parody of "The Blade Itself"*

Say one thing about Logan Ninefingers, say he's a climber.

He was the last of his band, the last of the Named Ones. His king, Bethod, had turned on him; his friends were dead or worse; and now the Flatnoses had him treed in the highlands, snarling below with their stone axes, waiting for him to fall.

"When you fall, Bloody-Nine," their chief had promised, "we will make it last."

"You gotta be realistic," Logan told the storm, because he said that a lot, the way a monkey checks a rope. He jumped into the white water and let the river decide, because falling is just climbing with more honesty.

The old monkey waiting at the river's bottom, dry in the downpour, was not surprised to see him.

"Logan Ninefingers," said the stranger — a grey-bearded baboon in a plain hood, still as the eye of something enormous. "I've been looking for you."

"Logan," said Logan, hauling himself out. "The Ninefingers part's more of a request these days. Who are you, then?"

"I am Bayaz," said the old monkey, "First of the Mangos."

Logan waited. Far away, thunder grumbled, like something remembering an argument.

"First of the Mangos," Logan repeated. "Right. And what's that when it's at home?"

"A long time ago," said Bayaz, "your people and mine made the world. Then my order went quiet, and the world forgot us, and got worse. Now something is waking in the far North that once broke the world, and I find I am out of practice at being forgotten. I need a monkey who is hard to kill."

"Flattering."

"You're alive. That's the whole of the reference."

Behind Bayaz, at a respectful distance, stood Quai, his apprentice, a mild-mannered langur who agreed with everything Bayaz said, always, instantly, in a voice like a very well-rehearsed echo. Logan had known monkeys like that before. They were usually the last monkeys you saw before something bit you.

In Adua, the Union's golden capital of towers and grooming scandals, two monkeys were having worse mornings.

The first was Jezal dan Luthar, a young officer macaque of magnificent coat and negligible character, training for the Contest — the great tournament of the Ape-riont — under conditions of unbearable hardship, chiefly that his father wanted him to win and the drills started before noon.

"I am *meant* for better than effort," Jezal told his reflection, glossing his whiskers. "Winning is simply my fur, arriving on time."

His friend Collem West, a commoner with a commission and therefore a grudge with a uniform on it, had a sister named Ardee, who drank Jezal's wine, dismantled his self-regard, and beat him at stones in front of his friends, leaving Jezal appalled to discover that appalled was not the only thing he was. Meanwhile West himself, the most competent officer in the room, watched the lords' sons get promoted past him and practiced the special smile of a monkey who plans to be very correct about it later.

The second was Sand dan Glokta, an Inquisitor of His Majesty's Questioners — a gibbon who had once been the fastest climber in the Union, until two years in an enemy's cages. The racks of rope had ruined his arms and legs. Now he crept up stairs one at a time; the Inquisition had found his talents a use, because a crippled monkey knows exactly where the strong are tender.

His practicals were Severard, a chatty weasel-faced macaque in a hood, and Frost, an enormous albino gorilla who never spoke at all, and between the three of them they kept Adua's conspiracies trimmed like a hedge. Glokta's current case — tracing the money behind the attempts on the succession — kept curdling into bigger cases, the way milk dreams of cheese. And at the end of every session, his legs screaming on the stairs, his own reflection grinning at him from the polished steel of his instruments, came the same question.

"Why do I do this?" he asked the ceiling most mornings, hauling himself up the steps by the rail, one agony at a time. The ceiling never answered. He went in anyway.

Bayaz arrived in Adua with Logan, and the city bent around him like a branch in wind. His claim to be First of the Mangos got him precisely nowhere with the establishment — the Arch-Sealer closed councils in his face, and the Lord Chamberlain sent a note explaining that legends were seen strictly by appointment — except that the great tower of the House of the Maker, sealed for centuries, immune to axes, fire, and argument, unlocked itself at a word from his mouth and locked itself behind them. The tower's Seneschal, a monkey-shaped waiting-room of a creature, let them in with the air of one serving a very old, very patient bill. Monkeys who saw the tower open stopped laughing, usually forever.

Meanwhile, Bethod's envoys arrived from the North — hooded, uncouth, and one of them roughly the size of a doorway — to inform the closed council that the King of the Northmen no longer required the Union's opinion about anything, including its own existence. The council replied with a declaration of war, a funding bill, and a requisition for drums. West was given a post at the front, Ardee was left loose in the capital with Jezal's conscience, and Glokta was handed the northern questioners' files, which mostly bit back.

The Contest came. Jezal fought his way through the brackets on a rising wave of luck and vanity, met the whey-faced giant Gorst in the final — and discovered, with a feeling like a branch giving way, that the old man in the stands had quietly, without moving, *made him better*, and everyone now expected things of him. He won. He stood on the podium with his fur gleaming and had, for the first time in his life, the thought that perhaps he had not entirely earned it, which ruined the whole afternoon.

Bayaz gathered his little band in the Maker's shadow: Logan; Jezal, the ornament with a dent in it; and Ferro Maljinn, a scarred desert langur with a list of enemies written inside her eyelids, whom Bayaz had collected from a cell in the south over the bodies of several guards and all of her patience.

"There is something in the far North," said Bayaz. "It was stopped once, long ago, at the Edge of the World, and the tool that stopped it was left there. We are going to fetch it."

"How far?" asked Logan.

"Farther than any monkey has gone and returned," said Bayaz, cheerfully. "Pack warm."

And somewhere in the dark behind his eyes, something that had waited under the world for three thousand years felt the old baboon move, and smiled, and began, ever so slowly, to climb.

They say the blade itself incites to violence. In the Union, as any monkey's mother will tell you while she works the tangles from your fur: *the braid itself incites to grooming.* It starts with one monkey's back, and by the end everyone's involved, nobody's clean, and the whole troop has opinions about who owes who.

The world was like that, in those days. And getting worse.
