# The Grapeyard Book

*A parody of "The Graveyard Book"*

There was a shape in the darkness of the old fig-house, and it held a hunger.

The shape was a jaguar, and the jaguar's name was Jack. He was paid handsomely by a very old fraternity with a prophecy problem, and he did his work thoroughly, and without malice. He took the mother, and the father, and the sister, quiet as a shadow swallowing three smaller shadows. Then he climbed toward the nest where the baby slept.

But the baby was not asleep. It was a year old and newly everything, and had just discovered climbing. While the jaguar worked below, the baby climbed out of the nest-hole, and up the hill to the old vineyard no living monkey would enter after dark. It went in through the gap in the wall, and the fog closed behind it like a door.

The ghosts of the Grapeyard — the troop who had kept the old vineyard's presses a hundred years ago, and been buried among the rows, and stayed — gathered to look at the living child pulling himself toward them, warm and breathing and dribbling.

"The dead should not touch the living," said an ancient voice.

But a mist came through the vines, shaped like a mother monkey, and the mist said: "My son. Protect my son." And Mrs. Ohh-Ahh, a plump howler ghost, made up her mind the way mothers everywhere do: instantly, and forever.

"He needs looking after. I'll look after him."

"He's *living*," said the ghosts. "He doesn't belong here."

"He's *here*," said Mrs. Ohh-Ahh.

The dead debated it in their slow parliament, and the Lady on the Grey Path — the oldest power of all, riding nothing at all — gave the child the Freedom of the Grapeyard: to walk where the dead walked, to fade and slide and haunt, and to be protected.

The jaguar Jack came up the hill an hour behind him. He met the cold shoulders of the dead, and the eyes of Silas, the caretaker, hanging pale in the gatehouse eaves — neither one of the living nor one of the dead, a shadow in a traveling coat with wings folded like a cape. Silas had a quiet word with Jack about doors and exits, and Jack slunk away nagged by the feeling he had forgotten something important. The mother's mist faded, content, and Silas agreed to be the boy's guardian and bring him food, which the dead could not.

"He looks like nobody but himself," said Mrs. Ohh-Ahh.

"Then Nabody it is," said Silas. "Nabody Ohh-Ahh."

Nabody Owens — that is how the living wrote it, being terrible at ghost accents — grew up among the rows, learning his Freedom: a ghost macaque taught him to fade until he was grey as morning mist, and an emperor tamarin who died mid-performance taught him dream-walking.

And when Silas traveled, as Silas sometimes must, a substitute came: Miss Loopescu, a small hairy she-monkey with a wolfish grin, who fed him soup of beetles and bark and taught him the calls of the night in every tongue, including Ghoul.

"Silas never makes me eat beetles," Nabody sulked.

"Silas is not here," said Miss Loopescu. "Eat your beetles. And memorize the call for help in Ghoul. You will need it before your twelfth year, and you will not thank me when you do."

He was not grateful, and he did need it. Through the ghoul door in the old pressing-room, one night, came the ghouls of the Sour Cellar: the Duke of Down-River, the Empress of the Old Volcano, and the Honourable Archibald Fitz-Banana — plump, smug, smelling of woodsmoke and wrongness. They flattered him and lured him, as ghouls do, down into Ghûlheim, where the mist was the color of old wine and nobody living ever left.

Nabody called for help in Ghoul — a shrieking language he had sworn was useless — and through the mist came a great black she-wolf the size of a pony, the terror of ghouls. She carried him home in her jaws, and only among the safe vines did the wolf shrink back into a small hairy she-monkey with a wolfish grin.

"Hound of God," she said, straightening her coat, as if that explained everything. It more or less did. He never complained about the beetles again.

There was, in the founder's crypt under the oldest vine, a thing called the Sleer. Nabody found it at ten, with a living girl named Scarlett, a red-furred visitor delighted to find an imaginary friend with a vineyard full of dead aunts. The Sleer was grey and glowing, a coiling of serpents that might have been one or three, older than the vines and the walls, guarding the First Bunch of the First Vine, all gold, waiting — always waiting — for a master.

"The Sleer were here before the vines," it whispered. "The Sleer will be here after. We guard. We hunt. Would you be our master, live thing? Would you be kept?"

"Not today, thank you," said Nabody, with the courtesy of a boy raised by the dead.

Scarlett moved away, and the years swung past like vines in wind. And all that time the Order of the Jacks — hunters called Jack, jaguar and jackal alike — was remembering the job in the fig-house that had never been finished. For the Lady on the Grey Path had prophesied long ago: *the child who walks where the living may not, who is raised by the dead and kept by the night, will bring the Jacks to an end.* Every Jack knew the child had not died.

They came for him when he was nearly grown, four of them, in a week. Jack Kandar, sleek and silver. Jack Tar, who smelled of ships. Jack Nimble, quick as a lie. And their leader, come full circle: the jaguar Jack himself, older now, wearing a white coat and calling himself a historian while he pumped a visiting Scarlett for the location of "a certain old vineyard."

Scarlett, sixteen and flattered, told him everything. Then she came to the Grapeyard ahead of him, and found her old imaginary friend not imaginary at all: a lean young monkey with her grave face under living fur.

"You're *real*?" she said. "All this time you let me believe I'd made you up! Living or dead, Nabody — you can't be both. Which are you?"

"I'm me. Scarlett — the historian. What's his name?"

"Frost. Dr. Jack Frost."

And Nabody felt the whole cold shape of it click into place, like a Jack stepping out of a dark. "Scarlett, you have to run."

The Jacks came at moonrise, and found that a boy with the Freedom of the Grapeyard is a difficult thing to hunt in his own country. He faded into the mist and let Jack Kandar chase a rumor. He slid through the crypt walls and whispered to Jack Nimble in dead voices until quick-as-a-lie Jack Nimble ran out the gates and kept running. Jack Tar he led down through the pressing-room hatch, to a grey and glowing coil that had waited so very long for something to keep.

"The Sleer will keep him," it whispered contentedly. "The Sleer will keep him always."

The jaguar Jack was last. He cornered the boy among the oldest vines and said: "You have cost me a great deal, young man. I've been hunting you since before you could walk."

"And you've never once caught up," said Nabody. "Doesn't that tell you something about how this ends?"

Then he stood in the moonlight of the only home he had ever had and called for help in flawless Ghoul — and the vines moved, and the mist moved, and the dead rose in every row, and out of the crypt came something grey and coiling that had been promised enemies to keep.

The jaguar Jack was never found. The Order of the Jacks, its prophecy fulfilled, dissolved into the ordinary criminal world, where it remains thoroughly unremarkable.

Scarlett saw too much of that night to forgive it, and too much to keep it. "Alive or dead," she said, white-faced. "It isn't natural."

"It's only death," said Nabody, gently. "It isn't the end of the world."

Miss Loopescu walked her home, and when Scarlett reached the town she could not quite remember why she'd come.

Nabody grew, as boys do, even dead-raised ones — and as he grew, the Freedom of the Grapeyard loosened its grip, the way a fist opens into a hand. The dead grew harder to see; the rows grew emptier. That is the tragedy of bringing up a live boy in a place of the dead: it only works until it works.

When he was ready, Silas came to the gate with a passport, a wallet with money in it, and one piece of advice: "The jungle is big, Nabody. Bigger than the Grapeyard, bigger than the dead. Bigger than anything except what you make of it."

Nabody said goodbye to the Ohh-Ahhs, standing hand in misty hand among the rows, and could not speak, and did not need to. From the gate he looked back once: the trellises silver in the first light, and somewhere beneath them a grey and patient something whispering that it would keep him always, if he ever asked.

"I will see the whole jungle," said Nabody Ohh-Ahh, "and everything in it."

And he swung down the hill, out of the Grapeyard, into the world of the living, which is full of banana skins and wonders, and which, he had it on excellent authority, is the only place where anybody really grows up.
