# Glamorangutan

*A parody of "Glamorama"*

A camera crew was following Victor Whiskers around the penthouse floor of the Hotel Griffon on a Monday night in the middle of December, filming a trailer for a movie that did not exist, and Victor — twenty-five, an orangutan, a face that had sold fur conditioner on four continents — kept having to be reminded where his mark was.

"I don't do marks," Victor said, adjusting the cuffs of his Vanson leather jacket. "I *am* the mark."

"Chloé wants you," said the assistant director, bored. "Chloé bisou bisou."

Victor moved past the kliegs toward the window where Chloé stood smoking a clove, and below them, out over Manbanana, snow was falling on the club district, and the party — the opening of the club, Victor's club, which was not actually Victor's club but was definitely going to have Victor's name over the door until the financiers said otherwise — was starting without him on the floor below. There were models everywhere. There was a deejay spinning jungle. There was a shipment of Krug and a shipment of kumquats and a shipment of drama, and the whole room smelled of fur product and honeysuckle and menace.

"Where's Damien?" the crew asked. Damien was the money. Damien was late. The club was called Mannequin. The club was over budget. The club was, in the words of the narrator of the trailer the crew was cutting in real time, "the place where the beautiful people go to dream" — and the trailer kept zooming in on Victor's face while a voice-over said, over a track of cooing doves and ultrasound: *He ruled a world of beautiful people. He had everything a monkey could want. But in a world of beautiful people, the only thing you can't buy is the truth.*

"That's the tagline," the director said. "The better you look, the more you see."

"I love it," said Victor, who had not listened to any of it.

What Victor knew about the party: Chloé bisou bisou. What Victor did not know about the party: everything.

By two a.m. the club had been shut by the city (fire code, or drugs, or the mayor's daughter — rumors moved through the crowd like fruit flies), and a small, tanned, unremarkable monkey in an excellent coat was waiting for Victor in the Griffon's breakfast room, and this monkey's name was Palakon, and Palakon had a proposition, and the proposition was London.

"You know Jamie Fields," Palakon said. It wasn't a question. "Model. Actress. The face of the Tibi tangerine campaign. She disappeared in London in March. Her family is beside themselves. There's money in finding her, Victor. There's a documentary in it. There's a crew." He gestured at the crew, who were filming this. "You'd be perfect. You know everybody. The camera loves you."

"I don't do reality."

"It's not reality," Palakon said, smiling. "It's content."

And this was the thing about Victor Whiskers, the thing the tabloids never got and the crew always did: Victor could not tell reality from content either. He had been a face since he was seventeen; things happened around him in exchange for his being there; the only part of any room that was real to Victor was the part reflected in the black lens of a camera. So: London. First class. The crew followed him aboard, filming the trailer for the documentary about the search for Jamie Fields, and Victor ordered a Kir Royal and a side of banana and looked out the window at the clouds and said, to no one, "This is going to be so major," and the clouds did not disagree.

London: rain the color of a television. Victor found Jamie Fields in a members' bar off Brick Lane in forty minutes, which should have been the end of it, and would have been, except that Jamie was thinner now, and older around the eyes, and living in a big damp house with eleven other models and a charismatic monkey named Bobby Hughes, and the house had a film room and a bomb room — no, not a bomb room, Victor corrected himself, a *pantry* — and nobody in the house slept, and everybody in the house was beautiful, and by the third night Victor was one of them.

"They're not what you think," Jamie said, at four a.m., over a fruit plate. "It's a cell, Victor."

"A cell of what?"

"You know how the newspapers keep saying celebrities make the perfect terrorists? Nobody notices a beautiful monkey doing anything. The world watches the fur and misses the hands." She looked at him with terrible steadiness. "Bobby's been doing bombings all year. Patisseries. Award shows. The press calls it the Custard Campaign."

And they did do bombings, and Victor was there for them, or near them, or somehow always across the street with a coffee when it happened — the Whole Foods on Kensington High Street flooded to the ceiling with stiffly beating meringue; the Baftas, when the Best Supporting Actress was interrupted by a piano falling through the roof followed by four hundredweight of lemon curd; the cafe in the Fulham Road where Victoria Beckham's favorite florist was pied with such force and precision that the curd was later found in the letterboxes of three neighboring streets. Nobody died. Everybody was frosted. The city screamed about terrorism; the cell called it *performance*; and Victor — who had filmed a public-service spot against custard violence, or had he dreamed that — could not make anything stay in the same reality for two days running.

"Did we do the thing in Paris?" Victor asked Bobby Hughes, on the Eurostar, while the crew filmed them talking about it.

"We did the thing in Milan," Bobby said. He was forty, gorgeous, gray at the temples, the most convincing monkey Victor had ever met. "You did the thing in Paris alone, Victor. Don't you read your own press?"

"But the crew — the crew filmed the — where's the crew?" There was never a crew when he looked. There was always a crew when he looked away.

"Victor." Bobby took his paw, kindly. "What did you think the documentary was about?"

The trouble with names began in Paris, or in Milan, or on the boat. Chloé faxed, besou-ing, but the fax addressed him as *Victor Johnson*, which was his name, so that was all right; except the bills said *Victor Ward*; and the hotel in Milan billed a *Victor Kirk*; and by the time the cell moved operations to a yacht off Sardinia — a white hundred-and-forty-foot thing called the *Pathetic*, which Victor thought was a joke name and Bobby said was not — even Victor had to admit there were too many Victors, and none of them was in charge, and the handsome orangutan in the mirrors of the yacht's ten bathrooms was somebody Victor recognized less each day.

"Bobby," Victor said, the night the Sardinian lights went by the rail, one by one, like a ward being unplugged. "The bombs. Nobody gets hurt, right? It's curd. It's a statement about celebrity. Nobody gets — it's not *real*."

"Victor," Bobby Hughes said, pouring him something with an umbrella in it, "you of all monkeys should know by now. Everything you've ever been in was real. That's the cruelty of it. The fur is the hands."

Victor stood at the rail a long time after that, filming himself with a camera he may or may not have been holding, doing the trailer for a movie that did not exist: *He ruled a world of beautiful people. He had everything a monkey could want.* Below him the black water moved; behind him the party glowed, models swaying to a song about nothing; and somewhere over the water the last lights of the coast went out.

"Is this the part," Victor asked the camera, or the water, or Chloé, or the other Victor, "where I see?"

The better you look, the crew did not answer, because there was no crew, because there had never been a crew, because there had always been a crew — the more you see.

Bisou bisou.
