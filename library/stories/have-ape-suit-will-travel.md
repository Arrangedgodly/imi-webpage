# Have Ape Suit—Will Travel

*A parody of "Have Space Suit—Will Travel" by Robert A. Heinlein*

The ape suit arrived by freight canoe, crated in straw, smelling of another monkey.

Kip Chimpen had entered the Bubbly Banana Soap contest every day for a summer — "Bubbly Banana: the cleanest clean a chimp can be!" — thirty thousand entries in his own careful paw, which is to say his mother's paw, his father's paw, and one suspiciously literate spider monkey. Fifth prize: one used high-altitude ape suit, genuine fur-lined, no guarantee expressed or implied.

He named it Oscar. A monkey alone in a garage with a suit needs a friend, and Oscar fit the job.

Kip polished Oscar's gauges, cycled Oscar's airlocks, and told Oscar his plans — the High Canopy Academy, the far treetops, the top of the sky where the blue turns black. What he got, one warm night while testing Oscar's little radio, was a voice on a dead frequency, sweet as birdsong, and a light coming down through the fig leaves.

I want to be honest about the next part, because Kip never managed to lie about it: he was kidnapped by a flying saucer.

Aboard were two prisoners and one corpse-princess of trouble. The first was Pee-wee, a marmoset child hardly bigger than a banana, with red ribbons, the mouth of a diplomat, and the brains of a small university. The second was Mother Thang, a woolly grandmother the size of a kitten, whose voice was like a bell made of song. The third trouble was the Wormfaces — great gray caterpillar bullies from beyond the cold, who had never once in their soft damp lives shared a banana.

The saucer did not go home; it went to the Moon, to a warren under the gray fig-ash plains where the Wormfaces kept their filing system and their prisoners in the same cabinets, sorted by dampness. They took Oscar away. That was their mistake, and the second mistake was explaining, in detail, at Kip, that no monkey could cross the surface and live.

Once a day they questioned their prisoners, wetly. "How numerous are the monkeys of Earth?" a Wormface would drip, through a translator that sounded like a sponge having opinions. "What is the ripeness of your defenses?" Kip, who had nothing to lose and thirty thousand soap wrappers' worth of practice at paperwork, gave them figures off the top of his head: Earth defended by nine hundred thousand monkeys, a bubble machine, and a very large dog. The Wormfaces recorded it all with great satisfaction, filed the three of them under *Damp*, and turned out the lights, and that was the third mistake, because it gave Kip time to count the cabinets.

Kip stole Oscar back, and Pee-wee, and Mother Thang, and they walked out into the lunar night with one suit among three and a plan that consisted mostly of arithmetic: so many hours of air, so much horizon, so little sense. Mother Thang rode in the helmet, singing — a thread of sound that kept their nerves from snapping like dry vines — while Kip and Pee-wee took turns breathing and took turns being brave. When the air was down to fumes and Pee-wee's chatter was down to "I want my mother," delivered with the calm of a monkey who has already written the speech she will give about it, the sky filled with lights, and a net made of song settled over them, and Vegan paws pulled all three aboard. Mother Thang's people had followed the singing. The Wormfaces followed too, which is how the whole party came to be guests of the court, and exhibits, and defendants, all in one afternoon.

They took the whole party farther up, to the Vega Vista — a hollow jungle of light where the Three Great Jungles hold court.

Understand what that means. Three galaxies of people — the feathered, the flowing, the enormously polite — all gathered to decide one question: whether the Wormfaces should be unmade, and whether the Earth-monkeys, who could not stop squabbling over the ripest branch long enough to notice they had neighbors, were worth one more chance.

Kip Chimpen, seventeen, in a used ape suit that smelled of another monkey, was the exhibit.

The Wormfaces spoke through a wet translator and offered, in essence, their portfolio: they had eaten their neighbors, filed the results, and considered themselves the only true people in the universe, a claim their lawyer supported by producing no witnesses at all, having eaten them. The court gave Kip a translator and asked him questions no school had ever asked. What is a monkey for? What would your troop do with the sky? He answered the only way he knew, which was honestly, and when honesty ran out he said so, which counted more. Pee-wee testified too, standing on the rail so the light could find her ribbons, and told the Three Great Jungles exactly what the Wormfaces kept in their cabinets. Half the court had to have it translated twice, once into words and once into words they could bear. "She's eleven," said Kip, when it was over. "We know," said the feathered judge. "We voted on whether to arm her. It failed, for fear of the outcome."

Then came the test. A cliff of white stone, and beyond the edge a fall no monkey could survive, and the gentlest of the feathered judges saying:

"Jump, Kip."

The old Kip — the Kip of thirty thousand soap wrappers — would have asked for a rope. He looked down into the dark instead, and thought of his father, who had written him that very month: "A complaint is not a coconut, son. Nobody has ever been fed by one."

Kip jumped.

He fell about a fathom, into a warm slowness, like jumping into the idea of a net. The feathered judge folded her wings and looked at the assembly, and there was nothing further to argue, because a monkey who jumps is a monkey who might climb.

The Wormfaces are gone. The court said it with regret, and the sky said it with a light like a small new moon, and Kip, watching, felt exactly as big as he was — which is the only feeling worth traveling that far for. The Earth-monkeys got their chance, with a warning Kip was told to deliver and did deliver, to everybody, everywhere, for years, mostly to laughter, occasionally to a quiet monkey who wrote it down and looked up. Those, his father agreed, were the ones to count.

He got home with Pee-wee and Mother Thang — Mother Thang's people sang over him for a week, out of affection, and Pee-wee's mother fed him until his fur fit badly — and with Oscar, scorched, patched, and the best-dressed fur in two galaxies. The government men in gray-fur suits asked him what he had seen. Kip told them the truth, and they wrote down *weather balloon*, and one of them kept looking at Oscar's radiation badge, and looking at the stars, and did not write that down.

And when the High Canopy Academy finally measured Kip Chimpen for his own suit, tailor-made, no guarantee implied, he kept Oscar anyway, in the corner of his room, where a suit that has been to the Vega Vista earns the right to smell of another monkey forever.
