# The Underground Tailroad

*A parody of "The Underground Railroad"*

The first monkey to be brought to the Randall fig plantation was Cora's grandmother, Ajarry, who crossed the gray ocean in the bottom of a ship and learned in her first week that the world was a cage with a fruit label on it. Ajarry had three husbands and five children, and by the end only Cora's mother, Mabel, was left standing. Then, when Cora was ten or eleven, Mabel ran — alone, one night, into the swamp — and never came back. She was the only monkey in the plantation's history to make it out. She became a legend and a wound: the monkey who got away, and left her daughter behind.

Cora grew into that wound. By fifteen she was an outcast even among the field monkeys, the girl who had fought the big young buck Terrance's son when he tried to cage her, who lived on Hobbs' Island — the patch of scraggly ground where the unclaimable ones huddled — and who had learned the first law of the plantation: every monkey for herself, and no one's tail to hold but your own.

It was Caesar who changed the law. Caesar was a young chimp born free in the north and sold south again and again — bright-eyed,-talkative, with a theory of luck. He had a contact, he told Cora: a white-furred station agent named Fletcher, and a route north. He needed a partner with fury.

"I don't need anything," Cora said.

"You're the only one here who fights back," Caesar said. "I watched you. The rest of them have cages inside their heads. You never built one."

The night they ran, the swamp took them to the knees. Their friend Lovey came too, following like a kit after its mother — and when the catchers' torches found them in the dark, it was Lovey they took. And when the plantation's son cornered Cora against a cypress, laughing, calling her property with fur on, Cora picked up the river stone and brought it down. That made it true, what they said after: the Randall girl was a murderer now, worth a bounty, worth hunting to the ends of the canopy.

Fletcher hid them in a cellar beneath a barn floor, and fed them, and at midnight he led them down, down, past the roots, to a station platform cut into the red earth — where a train, an actual train, a hissing black-and-brown underground engine, waited on rails laid by monkeys nobody had ever thanked.

"I thought it was a figure of speech," Cora said. "The tailroad. Tail-road. A saying."

"Down here it's a real thing," said the conductor, an old macaque named Lumbly. "We figured if they're going to call it that, we might as well build it. Climb aboard, and see how quick it goes."

They rode north in the dark. Cora, who had never been off the Randall land, watched the earth walls strobe past and thought: it is real, it is under everything, under their boots and their fields, and they do not even know.

The first stop was a bright province in South Carolina — a city of brick rows where Caesar got work with a cooper and Cora, under the name Bessie, was given a clean dress, wages, a room of her own, and evening classes. It seemed too good. It was. The province's kindness was a zoo's kindness: doctors who admired Cora's bones and measured her skull for a museum, and a polite plan — explained at a charity supper while Cora served the nuts — to breed the fight out of monkeys like her, generation by generation, with a scalpel. She heard it through the door, and stood with the tray shaking in her hands, and understood that the cage had simply been enlarged and carpeted.

Then Ridgeway came. The famous catcher, Ridgetail — a heavy-chested olive baboon in an iron collar of trophies from every monkey he'd dragged back — walked into the brick rows with his boy Homer, a small silent kit in a bowler who dogged him like a shadow. Caesar ran for the rooftops and a hunter's stone brought him down in the street. Cora got to the cellar, and the train, by seconds.

North Carolina was worse. The province had solved its monkey problem by law: no captive could enter, and any found were displayed along the Freedom Trail in iron cages hung from the wayside trees, as a lesson. Cora spent months in one of those provinces, hidden by a dissolute, decent station agent named Martin Wells and his fierce wife Ethel — hidden in the garret over the carriage house, in a space so narrow she could see the whole street through one knothole, and the cages in the trees, and the families who picnicked under them.

"Why do you do it?" she whispered to Ethel one night, when the madness of the garret had made honest questions cheap. "You'll swing beside me."

"Because I was a girl who read missionary stories," Ethel said, "and I meant every one of them. That's all my goodness is, girl. Books that stayed stuck."

It was the servant girl, Fiona — terrified, bribed with a coin and a promise — who gave them up. The night riders came; Martin and Ethel were caged beside the trail before the week was out; and Cora woke in chains in the back of Ridgetail's wagon, going not south but west, because the catcher had business, and because — he said, turning his scarred face back at her — "I keep my trophies with me."

Ridgetail talked as they rolled. That was the thing about him: he had a philosophy. "The monkey who stays put, that's my philosophy. Movement, and the taming of it. The Tailroad wants you to think you're going somewhere. I'm the fact that tells you: you're property, going wherever I say."

Homer rode beside him, dandling his little bowler hat, loyal as a tick.

Ridgetail's business ended in Indiana, at Valentine farm — a free orchard of two hundred acres, founded by a fair-minded mandrill named John Valentine, who passed for what he wasn't and built a school. Runaways worked the scythes, kept their own plots, learned letters by lamplight. Cora had a hut there. Cora had a garden. A young monkey named Royal courted her with apples and patience, and showed her the schoolhouse, and for one season Cora let herself believe in ordinary days — even when the farm's council voted, trembling, over whether runaways like her had made the place a target.

The vote did not matter. Ridgetail had sold his map of the orchard, and the night riders came at lantern-out. Royal was shot in the rows, dying with his blood in Cora's hands, whispering *the tunnel, the tunnel* — and Cora, cornered at last by Ridgetail in the burning dark, did not fight the way they both expected.

She jumped him. She got her chain around that iron collar, and her legs around his chest, and she dragged the great baboon — thrashing, bellowing, amazed — backward through the barn, down through the hidden trapdoor, into the station under the farm, onto the rails themselves, where she hammered him against the ties until he was still. Then, bleeding, alone, not knowing where the engine came from or whether it would even come, Cora walked north up the underground line.

Three wagons overtook her at dawn, going west: a family of monkeys, free, with a nut-crop and a grandmother aboard. A young male named Ollie reined in and looked at her — the cuts, the chain, the set of her shoulders.

"Miss. It ain't the tailroad, but it's west, and it's free. You can ride if you don't mind babies hollering."

Cora looked north, where the rails ran out of her knowing, and then west, where the sun was coming up over somebody's tomorrow.

"West," she said, and climbed up.

They asked her story, as the wagons rolled, and she gave it to them the way she'd give it all her life — the island, the tailroad, the cages in the trees, the orchard, the dark — and if it were told falsely sometimes, and her tail stood in for a ribbon, then let it. The world had tried to write her story for her since the day she was born. It was hers now. She kept it.
