# Conversations with Fronds

*A parody of "Conversations with Friends"*

Bobbi and I performed spoken word at open-mic nights all over the jungle. That winter our set was called "The Grooming Lie," about how troops monetize intimacy. Bobbi was the better performer. I was the one who wrote everything, which meant I got to believe I was in control, a belief Bobbi found adorable. We had known each other since the academy, where we'd debated everything except our feelings, which were the only subject either of us took seriously.

"You're the terrifying ones," said a silverback with a camera around her neck, finding us afterward at the Hollow Fig. "Melissa. I write for Jungle Review. I'd like to profile you. Dinners, photographs, the whole ritual."

Bobbi liked her instantly. I said she seemed controlling. Bobbi said I thought everyone was controlling, and that this was itself a form of arrogance. I wrote that down, because it was a good line, and being quoted accurately is the most intimacy I reliably allow.

At Melissa's treehouse I met her husband, Nick. Nick was an actor; he had spent nine years playing a brooding veterinarian on *Canopy General*, the longest-running drama in the jungle, and now he was semi-famous in the particular way of former branch-opera stars, meaning elderly macaques sometimes asked him to look at their knees. He stood at the party like a handsome question nobody had asked. We talked by the window for two hours about the difficulty of being liked.

"You present as effortless," he said.

"I practice," I said.

I was practicing even then. I had answered an interview question that autumn with "I feel ambivalent about ambition, which is itself a form of ambition," and Bobbi had said it sounded rehearsed, and I had said it was, and she had said knowing that about yourself wasn't the same as being honest, and I had said it wasn't, and we had both gone quiet, each privately awarding ourselves the round.

We began writing to each other by leafmail. He wrote in full sentences, which I found erotic in a way I could not defend. *I liked watching you talk,* he wrote. *You seem very free.* I replied: *I'm not free. I'm just well edited.* I did not mention that I had started editing my life on purpose: my father's drinking, the way I sent him my fruit-money and told no one, including him, especially him. I don't consider that lying. I consider it curation.

In February, while Melissa was away photographing gorillas for a book about women who stare, I went to their treehouse, and Nick and I sat in the greenhouse among the ferns, and afterward he said: I feel like I've been very foolish. I said: yes. He said: do you mind? I said: no, I feel very efficient. Then we both laughed in a way that frightened me, because it was real.

At home I told the fern everything. The fern in my window had become my prime mate by then — a better correspondent than most monkeys, since it never interrupted and it photosynthesized my problems into something like calm. "Conversations with fronds," Bobbi used to call it, back when Bobbi still came over.

My father called twice that spring. The first call lasted four minutes and cost him three bananas' worth of courage. The second lasted nine, because he'd found out about the clinic — the clinic tells fathers nothing, but fathers find out everything — and he cried, and said he was proud of me, and asked if I was eating. I said yes. He said that was good, that was grand, that was all he wanted to know. I sat with the fern afterward and felt my whole theory of curation dissolve slightly at the edges, like a leaf left in the rain on purpose.

Then the pain started — a doubling-over pain in the middle of the fruit market, like a strangler fig of the insides. At the clinic the doctor used words like *chronic* and *manage* and *surgery, eventually*. I told no one, least of all Nick. I didn't want to be a body with a condition. I wanted to be a body with a choice.

It was around then that I ended it, in the bathroom at Melissa's, with the party going on below. "I'm not a person who does this," I told Nick, as if I were reading a review of somebody else's book.

"No," he said. "You're a person who does this and then writes it down."

That should have been the end. Instead the seasons rotated and brought us all back to the same branch: Bobbi had started spending her evenings at Melissa's, doing the crossword, being delighted at, and Melissa — who knew, I now understood, and had known longer than I'd suspected — poured me a glass of fig wine and said: "Nick and I damage each other in different currencies. I had my own affair, if it helps. It didn't. Nothing helps. Sit down, you look like a fern yourself."

And Nick had been cast in a real play at last, a proper one, with critics, and when we met again at the opening party he looked like a question somebody had finally asked. He kissed me in the cloakroom with total composure and no plan whatsoever, and we resumed, if you can call it that, since we had never really learned the first part.

It was Bobbi, in the end, who carried me home when the pain dropped me outside the market, and put me to bed. It was also Bobbi who, while I slept, read my leafmails — all of them, in order, like a novel with a plot she suddenly understood. She stayed another day anyway, which is how I knew our friendship was truly over.

"You think being cold is the same as being honest," she said, leaving.

"I think being honest is more efficient," I said, which proved her point.

The affair ended the way our poem ended: not with a scream, but with an ellipsis. Nick went back to being liked, and to the theater, where they were saying his name kindly. Spring came to the canopy the way it always does, without asking my permission.

Then one morning the strangler fig of my insides finally closed its grip, alone in my treehouse, and I learned the difference between being alone and being by myself. I called no one. I fainted with great efficiency into the fern, which, I want it noted, did not drop me. The doctors did their words — *surgery, eventually* becomes *surgery, now* — and I woke up angry at my own body for being a body, which is a very cold thing to be angry at, and the coldest part was how familiar it felt.

But I missed Bobbi, so one night I climbed to her branch and said everything unedited — the clinic, the fear, the fern, my father, all of it, no curation. She groomed my shoulders while I talked, which was her way of saying she forgave me, and also that she was starving, and also that we should get dumplings.

"Why didn't you tell me?" she kept asking, and I said: "Because then you'd have had to be nice to me," and she laughed like a drain and cried a little, and agreed to move in "purely for budgeting reasons," which fooled nobody, least of all the fern.

That night I sent one more leafmail, to Nick:

*Tell me it's not too late. Tell me you still want to come and get me.*

For an hour nothing. Then the door-flap rustled and he stood there with rain in his fur, holding his tail very formally, like a coat.

"Hello," he said.

The fern, I felt, approved.
