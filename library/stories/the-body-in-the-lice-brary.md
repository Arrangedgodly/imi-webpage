# The Body in the Lice-brary

*A parody of "The Body in the Library"*

The lice-brary at Gibbonstone Hall was the finest grooming bench in three counties — forty feet of polished branch, ranked velvet cushions, coconut-fiber combs worn smooth by five generations of Banantry rumps — and no strange female had ever once been found dead upon it. Until the Tuesday morning the housemaid lifted the curtain, saw a blonde langur sprawled across the best cushion with a length of vine knotted round her throat, and screamed so hard the peacocks down in Dingle Dell went bananas.

"Nothing whatever to do with us!" blustered Colonel Banantry, quivering like a fig leaf in a gale. "Never saw the female in my life! I don't even approve of grooming!"

"Which explains your coat, Arthur," said his wife. "But it has happened in our lice-brary, and somebody is clearly trying to make monkeys of us." And she sent for the one monkey she trusted most: Miss Maple of St. Mary Banada, a small silver-muzzled rhesus with china-blue eyes and half a century of village gossip filed neatly behind them.

Miss Maple examined the stranger before the police had even swung into the drive. Fur dyed platinum blonde and grown out dark at the roots. Claws painted hibiscus-red by an unsteady hand. Cheap glazed fig-seed bracelets. New satin dance-pumps, stiff as Sunday hymnals. And beside the cushion a beaded evening-bag, packed crooked, as if in a hurry.

"Dance hostess," she murmured. "Hotel work. Strangled elsewhere and carried in — how odd, how very odd. It all smells of the theatre, does it not? And nobody in this house has ever set paw on a stage. Unless somebody wished us to believe they had."

The theatre in question was the Majestic Banyan Hotel at Datemouth, where a dance-hostess called Ruby Vine had kicked her golden slippers under the bandstand nightly for the pleasure of Conway Jefferson — a rich old mandrill confined to a rolling couch since the fall that killed his mate and his son, and hopelessly enchanted by Ruby's gum-chewing charm. Ruby had gone up to dance at midnight and never come down. Her cousin Josie — a jaw like a coconut cracker and an ankle perpetually "sprained" at convenient moments — had raised the alarm, along with Mark Gaskell, Jefferson's widower son-in-law, a gorilla of handsome, wilted charm who told the constables twice, unprompted, how charming he was.

Superintendent Harpy of the County Constabulary did everything correctly, which was his misfortune. He brought two monkeys over from the Majestic Banyan to identify the corpse. Josie took one look, uttered a wail you could have strained fig-juice through, and fell across the cushions: "Ruby! My own cousin! My own little Ruby!" Mark Gaskell identified the body sadly, handsomely, and at length. Old Jefferson, wheeled in on his rolling couch, would not look at all. "I shall remember her dancing," he said. "Kindly wheel me out before you pull the sheet back." The identification, thus triple-sealed, went into the constabulary ledger, and Miss Maple, from the window-seat, filed it under the only heading she trusted: *theatre*.

It was Mrs. Banantry, ferociously hemming tea-cloths, who supplied the second thread. "The Reeves girl, Arthur — the langurs at Danevale. Their Pamela, fifteen, never came home from the Jungle Scout rally. Run off to the pictures, they fear. Stage-mad, the child. Her mother calls the stage the road to perdition, and says so twice daily with the marmalade."

"Stage-mad," repeated Miss Maple, and her china-blue eyes went as round as two full moons. "Does she dance?"

"Lawks, yes. Medals. She'd have gone anywhere for a screen test."

So Miss Maple paid a call at the Danevale school and found Pamela's form-mate, a twittery young galago sworn to silence for three fig-sweets. "She didn't run away," the galago whispered. "A gentleman was going to put her in the pictures. She was to meet somebody after the rally hooter, ever so secret, because of her mother and perdition. She practised her screen face the whole march-past."

Three days later a burnt-out car was found at the bottom of Slate Copse quarry, and Superintendent Harpy's tidy case fell out of the tree. The fire had been fed with fig-brandy; the number-vine had been torn off and dropped down a disused shaft; and what the flames had left of the body inside wore the brown knitted uniform of a Junior Jungle Scout. A camp badge, half-melted. A satchel-buckle. Nothing the Majestic Banyan's dance floor had ever seen.

And Conway Jefferson announced, with terrible mildness, that Ruby had been about to become his adopted daughter — and his heir.

"Ah," said Miss Maple, accepting a fig. "Now the bananas ripen." She turned to Harpy, who had gone grey to the ears. "Ask yourself, Superintendent, who loses when that will is signed. Mark Gaskell — and poor Mrs. Adelaide Jefferson, and her little Peter. It is never popular, asking after heirs. Kindly take me to your mortuary."

What she noted there she set out that evening in the lice-brary, before the whole perched and anxious troop, with two tables, four cushions, and a pot of tea for authority.

"I was reminded," she said apologetically, "of our greengrocer's daughter, who dressed as the squire's niece for the harvest pageant. Nobody was deceived, because the squire's niece holds her tail curled, and the greengrocer's daughter swings hers flat. Miss Vine was a dancer. She swung her tail flat. The body on this cushion had a curled tail. It was never Ruby Vine at all. It was a young Jungle Scout called Pamela Reeves, lured from a rally by a promised film test, drugged, and killed."

Uproar. Josie's famous sprained ankle slid to the floor, forgotten, and bore her whole weight.

"Observe the little points," Miss Maple went on, serene as sundown. "The claws are painted across the knuckle — the way no female paints herself, but the way one monkey paints another who cannot feel it. In the beaded bag was the varnish bottle itself, packed away wet, by somebody in a hurry with two bodies to dress. The pumps have never danced a step; a hotel hostess wears her shoes in by supper or kicks them off. The teeth have never met a dentist, which in hotel work is a miracle of economy. And the quarry fire was lit by somebody with the run of the Majestic Banyan garage, for the car is in their book. Ruby's own body burned in that car, dressed in Pamela's uniform, to be found, mourned, and filed as Pamela Reeves. Two dead girls, each carrying the other's name. The identification was not broken, Superintendent. It was *built* — seal by seal, wail by wail, by the hotel party."

"Mark Gaskell supplied the plan. Josie supplied the child. Josie — cousin, nurse, and jealous keeper of Ruby's golden slippers — met Pamela after the rally hooter and drove her to the hotel with a dose of fig-syrup in her belly. They dyed her, painted her, dressed her in Ruby's gown, and left her to be found a whole county away, so that Ruby Vine should be safely, famously dead. Then Gaskell took Ruby up the back stair, and the vine was on her before she could spit her gum. One car out of the hotel garage at midnight; one body in a quarry, one body on a cushion twenty miles off. The adoption dies with Ruby. The estate stays where the charm has always kept it."

"You'll never prove a word of it," snarled Gaskell, showing every tooth he owned.

"No," agreed Miss Maple, mild as milk. "But the constables are digging the quarry, the garage book is signed in your paw, and I have never yet been wrong about a pageant. It is the little grey fur follicles, you see. They keep everything, and they tell me things."

As the troop constables swung the pair away, wrists vine-bound, old Jefferson was wheeled to the door to watch them go, and was silent a long while. "I am an old mandrill," he said at last, "and I mistook a glittering leaf for gold. There will be a fund, Mrs. Banantry, in the child's memory. A dancing class, where any greengrocer's daughter may learn to hold her tail however she pleases." Adelaide Jefferson, who had quietly mothered the entire staff through the worst fortnight of their lives, pressed his paw; and young Peter, understanding nothing, whistled through a fig-leaf, which was the only suitable comment anyone made all day.

Mrs. Banantry let out a breath she had held for a week.

"Miss Maple. However did you see it?"

"My dear," said Miss Maple, taking up a comb, "wickedness is much the same in a big troop as in a small one. Somebody wanted the whole bunch, and was prepared to break the stem to get it. Now — do allow me to do your shoulders. You have been carrying a dead langur on them all week, and your fur is simply full of worry."
