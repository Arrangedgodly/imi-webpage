# Nineteen Apey-Four

*A parody of "Nineteen Eighty-Four" by George Orwell*

It was a bright cold day in April, and the canopy-screens were howling thirteen.

Winston Simian, his chin burrowed into his ruff to escape the vile wind, slipped through the glass doors of the Troop Housing Block, though not quickly enough to prevent a swirl of gritty dust from entering with him.

The hallway smelled of boiled cabbage. At one end a colored poster, too large for indoor display, was tacked to the bark wall. It depicted simply an enormous silver face: BIG BABOON IS WATCHING YOU, the caption beneath it ran, and the eyes in the face followed you up the stair.

In his tree-fork another poster dominated the far wall: the same silver face, with the words SCREECH IS SILENCE — SITTING IS SWINGING — GREEN IS RIPE.

Winston turned his back on the screen, the fur of his ruff rising. Somewhere near, the Thought-Pickers listened. It was said in the troop: "You had to live — did live, from habit that became instinct — in the assumption that every sound you made was overheard, and every gesture, except in darkness, was seen."

He took out his diary — a forbidden bark-scroll — and wrote: DOWN WITH BIG BABOON. Then, feeling oddly steady, he wrote to the future: "To a time when thought is free, when truth exists and what happened happened — from this ape, greetings."

It was not illegal (nothing was illegal, since there were no longer any laws), but it was punishable by the cage, and Winston, a smallish frail chimp of thirty-nine with a prematurely gray ruff, worked in the Ministry of Troop Truth. Every day he took the stale back-numbers of The Canopy Times and rewrote them, planed smooth and re-carved, so the harvest was always glorious and Big Baboon always right. "Who controls the past," ran the Party saying, "controls the future: who controls the present controls the past." Winston controlled the past daily, from nine to five, with a lunch break.

"It's a beautiful thing," Syme had said, working on the Eleventh Edition of the Chimpspeak Dictionary, "the peeling of words. By Apey-Four we shall have peeled away every useless word for rebellion and ripe. We shall make thoughtcrime impossible, because there will be no words left to think it with. The whole of Chimpspeak will be one word: 'nana.'" Syme was vaporized in the spring. He had seen too clearly and spoken too plainly, and apes who do that disappear from the records — become, in the official phrase, "unripe."

At the Two-Minute Hoot, the screen filled with the face of Goldpaw, the Enemy of the Troop — a bleating, sheep-faced ape with a mangy tail — and three hundred rumps rose in fury, fur bristling as one. Winston hooted with them and hated himself.

In the flat across the landing lived the Parsons family, whose cubs paraded in spy-costumes, demanding, "Ee's a traitor! Ee's a Goldpaw! We want to see the hanging!" — and poor Mrs. Parsons, their mother, aged thirty, with dust in the creases of her fur, would whisper, "They do get so noisy at bedtime." Later, Mr. Parsons was dragged off to the Ministry by his own cubs. "It was my little girl," he told Winston proudly, dazed, in the Ministry corridor. "She listened at the keyhole and heard me saying 'Down with Big Baboon' in my sleep. She's a fine one, she is. Brings her up proper."

The proles, at least, were left alone — the brown-furred masses, 85 percent of the troop, who were permitted songs and fermented sap and no politics at all. "If there is hope," Winston had written, "it lies with the proles." Down in their yards a great washerwoman sang as she pegged diapers, arms swinging, some anthem from before the revolution, and her voice carried over three districts. "They can be trusted with song," said the Party. In song, thought Winston, there was more freedom left than in all the truth-ministries of the canopy.

He dreamed, sometimes, of his mother, who had vanished in the great purges when he was small — his mother, gaunt and silent, holding out the last chocolate bar to his little sister; and he had seized it and run, weeping, greedier than grief. Some ape had always known this about him. Some ape had been watching.

That evening a dark-eyed young chimp from the Fiction Department stumbled against him and slipped something into his paw. At home he unrolled it: three words.

I LOVE YOU

Her name was Julia, and in a green clearing, where a bird sang steadily in the hush, she sat beside him and began, shockingly, to groom him.

"You know," she said, "the Troop doesn't allow this. Private grooming. The affection you give, you're supposed to give to Big Baboon."

"Then this," said Winston, "is a political act."

All through that soft spring they met — in the clearing, and above Mr. Charrington's junk-nest, where Winston bought a paperweight of amber sap with a tiny golden banana suspended in it, like a little world. "The future is real," he told Julia, "if we keep the past alive. Freedom is the freedom to say two plus two is four — and a green banana is green. Once that is granted, all else follows."

A silverback came to them at last — O'Brien, an ape of such presence that the leaves went still. "You are prepared to give your lives?" he asked. "To commit sabotage? To splash dye in the rival troop's fur?" And then: "We shall meet in the grove where there is no darkness." He gave them the Book — Goldpaw's Book, with its three slogans on the cover — and Winston read it in the room above the junk-nest, Julia asleep beside him, understanding at last that the war was not meant to be won, only to be continuous; that the Troop's hierarchy held its power not despite the misery but because of it. "The peel," the Book concluded, "is the thing. The ripe is the peel."

They met instead in the grove of the Thought-Pickers, with the amber paperweight on the table — and the voice came from the canopy-screen, an iron voice.

"YOU ARE THE DEAD," it said.

The paperweight struck the floor, and the little golden world burst into fragments. Mr. Charrington came in — no longer the bent old junk-keeper, but a young, alert ape of the Thought-Pickers, and the iron voice had been his all along.

In the Ministry of Grooming, O'Brien held up a banana. "How many bananas am I holding up, Winston?"

"Two," said Winston. "Two! However many the Troop says — two!"

"Sometimes they are five. Sometimes they are three. Sometimes they are all of them at once. Green is ripe, Winston, when the Troop says so. Where is the banana, Winston?"

"There is no banana!"

And at last they brought him to Cage 101, where the worst thing in the world waits.

"For you," said O'Brien gently, "it is the Peeler."

The whirring came nearer, hungry for the fur that had been his whole life, and Winston's last loyalty tore loose like bark from a lightning-struck tree.

"Do it to Julia!" he cried. "Peel her! Peel anyone!"

They let him go. He sat in the Kapok Café, sipping Victory Nectar, to the tune from the telescreen: Under the spreading fig I sit, I love Big Baboon. Somewhere at another table, Julia sat with the look of an ape who had been peeled and did not care for it; they had agreed, wordlessly, to meet again, and both had known, wordlessly, that it would come to this. A bulletin of glorious victory blared; the troop howled with joy; and a warm lump of love rose in Winston's chest, swelling until it filled him entirely.

Everything was ripe and yellow and right. He had won the victory over himself.

He loved Big Baboon.
