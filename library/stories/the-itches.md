# The Itches

*A parody of "The Witches"*

My grandmother was the finest storyteller in the troop — an old monkey with fur like chimney smoke, a missing thumb, and strong opinions about Itches.

"Itches?" I said. "You mean like the ones you scratch?"

"No," said Grandmama, lowering her voice. "Itches are the most dangerous creatures in the jungle. An Itch looks exactly like an ordinary monkey lady — that is its whole trick. But listen close, because they can be spotted. One: Itches wear gloves, to hide long grooming-claws. Two: they wear wigs, for their heads are bald as river stones. Three: they wear curled vine-belts, to hide the most terrible thing of all — an Itch has no tail. And four: to a child, they smell of rotten durian."

"How did you lose your thumb, Grandmama?"

"An Itch," she said, "took it. Fifty years ago. I got away with the rest."

I believed her — a good thing too, because a week later, in the banyan market, a monkey lady in little green gloves leaned toward me and smiled.

"Here, little monkey," she cooed. "A lovely ripe banana. Sweet as sunlight. I do so love little monkeys."

I stepped back. Gloves. Fur that never moved in the wind. A fat vine-belt — and behind it, where a tail should be, nothing. And then I smelled it, faint and foul, like fruit gone wrong.

She kept coming, still smiling, gliding on little wheels of patience. "Don't you want it, my sweet? It's a very fine banana. It's your favorite."

I ran screaming for Grandmama, and when the market monkeys turned, the Itch melted away into the crowd like vinegar into soup.

"An Itch!" cried Grandmama, when we got home. "You escaped one! But listen to me: an Itch never gives up. Never go near a monkey lady in gloves."

That winter I came down of a fever, and the doctor ordered rest and air and no damp fur, so Grandmama took us to the Treehouse Magnifique, the grandest hotel in the jungle, where the trays of complimentary banana taffy had lately appeared at every fruit stall for miles. Grandmama frowned at the taffy the way you frown at a hat that has been left somewhere on purpose.

I should explain about the hotel. It was full of monkey ladies. All of them in gloves, for the season. All of them with fur that sat still in the ceiling fans' breeze.

I was behind a carved screen in the ballroom, retrieving my dropped skipping-vine, when the monkey ladies arrived. The banner over the dais read: THE ANNUAL GENERAL MEETING OF THE ROYAL SOCIETY FOR THE PREVENTION OF CHEEKY CHIMPS.

The ladies took their seats. Then, all together, they pulled off their fur. Wigs, every one. Off came the gloves — claws. Off came the vine-belts — no tails, not a hair of one. A hundred bald, clawed, tailless Itches in ballroom dresses, and I pressed into the screen and made myself the size of a fig seed.

And at the front, on roller skates, rolled the leader: a small, fierce figure behind a velvet mask, whom the others called the Grand High Itch.

"Cheeky chimps!" she screeched. "Filthy, giggling chimps! But here — THE SOLUTION! FORMULA 86 DELAYED-ACTION MARMOSET-MAKER! One drop in every free taffy in the jungle! At half past three the next afternoon — POOF! — the little monkey becomes a marmoset! And who minds a marmoset? No one! But no house will stand a screeching, banana-thieving marmoset for a week — out it goes with the peel! And that, my Itches, is how we shall rid the jungle of every cheeky chimp alive!"

The Itches chanted and capered. The Grand High Itch rolled up and down the dais teaching them the smell. "A chimp child," she said, "smells to us of overripe banana. You will know them anywhere. Breathe deeply!"

A timid Itch at the back raised a claw. "Madam, if we turn them all into marmosets at half past three, what is to stop them telling their —"

POOF. Where the timid Itch had sat, there now sat a potted fern.

"You see?" said the Grand High Itch. "She went to pieces. Anyone else got a question?" Nobody ever had a question.

And then the wind changed.

"ROTTEN DURIAN!" screamed the Grand High Itch, nostrils flaring. "THERE IS A CHEEKY CHIMP IN THIS HALL!"

They dragged me out by my ears. "The whole bottle," said the Grand High Itch, grasping my jaw. "For eavesdropping, the whole bottle."

They poured Formula 86 down my throat, and it burned like frostbite in reverse — fur to fluff, tail to a banded ring, the whole room growing enormous — until I stood on the table no bigger than a squirrel, with a magnificent white mane: a marmoset in my own shoes.

I did not stay to be squashed. I ran — and here is the important part — I ran the other way from everyone's expectations: straight into the Grand High Itch's own room. I saw her arrive, I saw her peel off the velvet mask (underneath was mostly nose), and when she skated out to dinner, I dragged her suitcase of Formula 86 from under the bed, hid behind it, and spent the night in a green glove, which I thought was poetry.

At half past three the next day, a fat boy called Bruno Jenkins swaggered into the hotel garden to prove his taffy story. "I had five," he announced, "and they were fr —" POOF. He finished the sentence as a marmoset, in a very expensive jacket.

That evening, while the Itches dined, a small marmoset climbed the great tureen of green banana soup in the kitchen and emptied in an entire bottle. The chef nearly caught me; the chef caught a ladle to a marmoset's memory.

At supper the Itches came to dinner — all one hundred, wigs and all. And between the soup and the nuts, POOF, POOF, POOF, a hundred times over — marmosets among the teacups, ferns among the fingerbowls. The staff chased the lot out with brooms, and a marmoset that has been broomed out of a grand hotel never comes back.

Grandmama, of course, recognized me at once. "Is that you?" she said, and gathered me up. "Those are your eyes, and that is your grandmother's scowl. And this —" she picked up Bruno, who was sulking in a bread roll — "must be the Jenkins boy. Bruno, stop eating the centerpiece."

She took us home — Bruno too, once he stopped sulking — and nobody in the jungle could ever tell us from ordinary marmosets, except that we had secrets.

"How long do marmosets live, Grandmama?"

"Longer than you'd think," she said, "and it doesn't matter. Because a marmoset never has to grow up, does he? No school, no trousers, no speeches."

"No," I said, delighted. "Just swinging. And Grandmama — we still have the suitcase."

Her smoky smile came back, and she unrolled the map of the jungle, and her finger came down, thoughtfully, on the Grand High Itch's next known address.

And that is where we are tonight — one old monkey and two small marmosets, going tree to tree with a bottle of Formula 86, hunting Itches.
