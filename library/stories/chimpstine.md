# Chimpstine

*A parody of "Christine"*

Dennis Guilder told it this way, years after, when anyone was fool enough to ask.

Arnie Chimp was the class drip: fur uncombed, glasses mended with vine-sap, laugh tracked by nobody. In the fall of our junior year, walking home past Darnell's Do-It-Yourself Grooming & Repair Bay, he stopped like he'd been harpooned.

"Jeez, Dennis. Look at that."

Behind the fence, in the weeds, sat a car — an actual car, a rusted red 1958 Plantain Fury, long as a funeral, dead as the dodo. Rust had eaten it down to lace. Behind the wheel slumped the shape of an old monkey.

"Yo, Darnell," Dennis called. "Whose is the wreck?"

Darnell's eyes slid over us the way oil slides over water. "Roland LeBay's. He's inside it. Been inside it since July." He tapped the glass. The old monkey's head turned slowly, and even through fifty years of grime I could see the look: a monkey daring you to want what was his.

Later, Arnie said it was love at first sight. It wasn't love. It was worse: it was adoption.

He bought her for a banana peel and a promise, and christened her — he would never say why — Chimpstine. I met LeBay once more before he died, in the hospital wing of the county home, to try to talk some sense into the purchase. Sense had left the building. "She ain't for sale," LeBay rasped, gripping my wrist with a hand like a stick shift. "She's the only monkey — the only MONKEY —" and then something about the assembly line, and three bad years, and how his brother should have kept his opinions to himself. His brother found me in the hall. Old LeBay's brother was a dry stick of a patas, and he said one thing worth writing down: "Three of them owned that car before your friend. My brother lasted longest. He only fought her to a draw." Then he told me about the others, and I thanked him and didn't sleep much.

That was September. By Halloween, Arnie had moved her into a bay at Darnell's, working evenings, coming home with red rust in his fur like a fever breaking. His mother, Regina, campaigned against the car with the fury of a monkey who senses a rival, and lost, the way you always lose to a rival that comes with a radio. By Thanksgiving Arnie had taken to staying nights in the back seat at Darnell's, doors locked, "for security." Whose, nobody asked. She smelled of old red bananas and old red fur, and Arnie combed his fur down flat and smiled with his teeth shut, and from behind — I saw him once across the lot and felt my stomach drop — he had the slouch of a monkey twice his age, a monkey with a hip replacement and a grudge.

The night we finished her we went for a ride, top down, three across the seat. The radio played "Moon Banana," a song nobody had requested. The odometer, I swear on my troop, ran backward — unwinding its miles like a monkey picking a knot apart.

"Like it, Dennis?" Arnie said softly. "She can be bad. She can be real bad." And I laughed, because I didn't understand yet.

Darnell understood. He stood in his doorway watching her idle, arms crossed over his coveralls. "I sell parts, kid, not souls," he said, and went in. Business being business, he took Arnie's money anyway.

I tried once, that fall, at the bay door. It was late; Arnie was inside her with a work lamp, tuning something that didn't need tuning.

"It's a car, Arnie."

I heard him set the wrench down very carefully. "Don't you say that about her," he said, in a voice with gravel in it from a quarry I didn't know. "Don't you ever say that about her." And the radio, helpfully, played "Moon Banana," and I noticed for the first time that the song had no other side.

Understand: Buddy Rapton, a bully with a pompadour like a toilet brush, wrenched a wiper arm off Chimpstine in the school lot. Arnie went for him — I swear this — and the punch moved like a clutch engaging, low gear, no spin, all torque, a punch with a manual transmission. Buddy sat down in the gravel and reconsidered several things. But it was the Fury that closed the account. That night she rolled out on her own. The next morning Buddy's scooter was a red-painted pancake and Buddy himself was found up a lamppost by his tail, minus one eyebrow, deeply reformed. When Moochie Welch went at her with a tire iron, the parking-lot lights browned out, something red went by very fast, and Moochie spent a month in traction telling anyone who'd listen that a car had chased him the wrong way up a one-way vine-line. Nobody laughed where the car could hear.

The dents rose like bubbles in a milkshake and smoothed flat. The cracked windshield sealed like a wound. Chimpstine drank banana-gas and grudges, and grew fat on both.

Then Leigh Capote, a bright little capuchin Arnie actually liked, choked at the drive-in on a plantain chip that went down sideways — and I watched that car do nothing. Not a light, not a horn. The radio played on while Leigh's lips went dark, until a trucker two rows back thumped her spine and shot the chip into row F. Chimpstine's headlights, I want this on the record, were off the whole time, and when they came on they came on slowly, the way a smile does. Afterward Leigh said, quietly: "It wasn't Arnie in there. Whatever's in that car, it wanted me gone." She broke it off with Arnie at the ice-cream stand, and the Fury idled by the curb with her radio whispering, and Arnie — I hardly knew him — said, "You'll come back. She says everybody comes back."

We waited too long to do anything about it. Arnie's mother died in the spring, and Arnie drove the pass to the funeral with the radio on, and his aunt said he looked, at the graveside, like his own grandfather. The paper called the crash at the ravine bend "a freak stall." The only survivor of the two-car squash was the one that was only one car — barely scratched, radio playing, patiently ticking as she cooled, glass shining like a fresh coat of paint over a fresh coat of something else.

Leigh and I did it in November, at Darnell's, with the big banana-press compactor. We lured the Fury in slow — engine breathing, radio coaxing, Leigh talking to her in a low voice like a keeper with a bad animal — and I have to tell you she knew. She fought the gate. The gate won because I dropped it, and I am not ashamed to tell you I closed my eyes at the crunch, and not ashamed to tell you I heard, inside it, something like a word.

Under the yellow work-light, in the tangled red metal, something glinted. Like an eye.

We heard, years later, from a junkyard down in the banana republics: a kid had found a red fender out behind the fence, still warm in the morning, though the night had been cold.

And at night, they said, the radios around there played a song nobody requested.
