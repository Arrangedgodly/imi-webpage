# To the Lousehouse

*A parody of "To the Lighthouse"*

"Yes, of course, if it's fine tomorrow," said Mrs. Rhesusay. "But you'll have to be up with the lark, and up with the louse."

She was making the promise to her son James, six years old and certain of exactly one thing in an uncertain jungle: the Lousehouse. It stood across the bay on its own rock, a squat gray tower where the Keeper — an ancient orangutan with a lamp, a comb of ivory, and no other company — sat all night delousing the wind. His light went round and round the bay, and monkeys said that if you reached the Lousehouse, your fur would be clean forever, and every monkey who had ever groomed you would know it somehow, and be glad. It was a gift, that promise; she let the beam of it rest on James and warm him, and he would remember it all his life.

"May we go?" asked Cam. "Is it going to be fine?"

"It won't be fine," said Mr. Rhesusay, pacing the terrace with his green grooming-bag, inflicting accuracy upon his children. "There will be rain. You will be soaked to the underfur. The Keeper will be up his tower combing lice out of the lamp, and no son of mine will drown for the sake of a scratch."

Had there been a coconut to hand, or a fig knife, or any implement that would gash a hole in his father's dignity and let the pomposity out of him, James would have seized it. As there was nothing but a banana, he held the banana and hated him with it. The trouble was that Mr. Rhesusay needed sympathy too; he was forever demanding to be admired for being right about the weather, which is the least admired of the virtues.

On the lawn, Lily Boniscoe, a plain little colobin with a bark-canvas, walked with old Mr. Bankes, a courteous vervet who had known Mrs. Rhesusay thirty years and never once felt looked at straight. Lily's problem was the picture: there was the Lousehouse, there the rock, and there a branch with a shadow, and the whole would not come together. "I shall put the Keeper further in," she said, "and the tree further out," which solved nothing and comforted her completely.

That afternoon Mrs. Rhesusay sent the young ones to the beach — Minta and Paul with their nets, Nancy with the towels, Andrew with his bucket — and made them promise to be home for dinner. On the way back Minta lost her grandmother's brooch in the dunes, and Paul found it; and by the time they straggled in, wet, sandy, and late, the two were engaged. Mrs. Rhesusay accepted the credit quietly. It was, she felt, the least they owed her.

That evening Mrs. Rhesusay gave them the great fig stew, two days in the making, with the leaves laid in like rafters. Around her table sat the troop: her husband, who needed to be told he was admired; young Charles Bonobsley, a bony student of primatology — which was, in his view, the study of his own reflection — giggling his superior giggle; and Lily Boniscoe, gripping her bark-canvas under the table, where she had drawn a little branch with a shadow.

"Monkeys can't paint, monkeys can't write," said Bonobsley, helping himself to the best fig. "What's the use?"

Mrs. Rhesusay looked round the table and gave, and gave. Her husband was fed; Bonobsley was soothed; Lily was made to feel that her painting was no disgrace; James was promised the Lousehouse. And it seemed to her, folding her napkin of leaves, that of such moments the enduring thing is made — the stew, the quarrel, the candle, the little daily miracles; that this was enough; that this was, after all, the whole of it; and that it would remain.

"But it will be fine tomorrow," she told James, folding him into her arm. "I promise."

It was not fine. It did not become fine for ten years.

The wind unpicked the thatch, stitch by stitch. The figs went over, one by one, in the night. A snake came up the rain-pipe, and Mrs. Rhesusay died in her sleep, in the middle of a dream in which she was giving some monkey exactly what they needed. Prue's branch broke. Andrew went to the Great Canopy War and did not come back from it. The pot of fig stew stood on the dead fire, and the treehouse woke to the jungle. For years nobody came at all; then old Mrs. MacNab, a scrub-langur with a bucket and a lifelong grudge against dust, was sent in, and thumped the cushions, and let in the light, and chased the lizards out of the bedrooms, until the treehouse, if not exactly alive, at least remembered how a troop expected it to look.

Then, one spring, Mr. Rhesusay said: "It will be fine tomorrow. We go to the Lousehouse."

So James, seventeen, and Cam, sulking, took the outrigger with old Macacalister and his boy. They had been commanded, and they had come; and each, in the sour secrecy of the young, had privately resolved to resist — to say nothing, to give nothing. "Think of the delousing," Cam told herself, being ironic, and then could not remember why irony had ever seemed safer than hope. On the water the old arguments rose and fell like the swell. Someone had blundered. Mr. Rhesusay had blundered. No — the whole troop had blundered, for ten thousand years, in every tree since the first fig. Macacalister's boy pulled a mackerel out of the gray sea, and it thumped and flashed blood-dark in the bottom of the boat, and James steered, and his father sat in the bow reading, looking up now and then to say something accurate and unkind about the tide.

"Steer like that," said his father suddenly, gruffly, trying the words out, "and you'll make a pilot yet."

So that was it, then. James steered magnificently. Cam took the rope, and the pact of resistance broke up quietly in the swell, like a fig going soft in water. The waves boiled around the rock, the Keeper's comb-flash swept over them once, twice — and they landed. The Lousehouse was almost startling: a stark tower on a bare rock, with not one softening lie about it. There was the old orangutan with his comb, and they sat fur to fur in a silence that was not sulky at all, while the lice of ten bad years were picked away, one by one, and flicked to the fish.

On the lawn above the bay, Lily Boniscoe set up her canvas of stretched bark. Bonobsley was in the south now, saying monkeys can't paint, professionally. But there was the Lousehouse; there was the rock; there was the gray old Keeper bent to his combing. The problem was only where to put him — where to draw the line. Mr. Bankes stood beside her, politely professing to see a picture where he saw a bark.

"The Keeper was the difficulty," she told him. "He insists on being everywhere at once, like a monkey at a grooming party."

And Mrs. Rhesusay came back and stood by her — astonishingly beautiful, in her shawl, handing out the figs at dinner — and Lily thought: she made it all one; and if I can put her here, with one stroke, it will be one. Cam waved from the tower. *They have landed,* she thought, *and it is all right.* She drew her line down the middle of the bark, one stroke, and it was done.

"I have had my vision," said Lily Boniscoe. "And a thorough delousing."
