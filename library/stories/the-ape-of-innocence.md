# The Ape of Innocence

*A parody of "The Age of Innocence"*

In the high season, the old fig-aristocracy of the Great Central Grove went to the Orang-Opera in their Sunday fur, and opening night was as fixed a rite as the first ripe banana of the year. Two great authorities presided from the boxes: Sillerton Jackson, who knew every scandal in the canopy's history, and Larry Lefferts, who knew every tail-knot in it; between them they judged the season, and the season did as it was told.

Newland Brancher, a young gibbon of excellent family, sat in his family's box and looked down at May Welland — cream-and-gold, serene, of the Welland boughs — and congratulated himself: their betrothal was to be announced that very evening at the Buffort ball. Everything about May was exactly right, which is the highest compliment the old grove permits.

Everything about her cousin was exactly wrong. The Countess Ellen Olenska — back from the Continental menageries, fleeing a drill of a count with manners like wet bark — arrived late, in the wrong fur, at the wrong box, and was seen at the same supper as a fast macaque. Larry Lefferts examined her tail-knot with his glass and pronounced it tragic. The Wellands despaired, and Newland was deputed by the family to manage the countess's threatened divorce.

"A divorce," said old Mr. Leafblade of the family lawyers, "read aloud in the lowland courts. The grove would not survive the reading. Manage it, Newland."

It was the van der Luydens who settled the social question meanwhile — the oldest of the old figs, who received so seldom that a leaf from their tree outranked a proclamation from anyone else's. They gave a dinner of twelve for the Countess Olenska, at which the grove's snub was quietly repealed; Sillerton Jackson, who had pronounced her tragic, pronounced her received; and Larry Lefferts, studying her tail-knot at fifteen feet, revised his verdict to "original," which in Larry's language was a pardon.

He managed the rest. He sat opposite Ellen in her grandmother's dusty old hollow, among shabby branches she plainly loved, and explained duty, and form, and what the four hundred families of the canopy would and would not swallow — and wondered the whole time why she seemed the only real thing in the grove.

"It must be nice," said Ellen at last, giving in, "to belong to a family that is sure of everything. We had a grove like that, once. It fell."

Afterward, at the Old Fig Museum, among the glass cases of labeled antiquities, she stood before the most damaged exhibit in the collection and said: "I like this one. It's been somewhere." She turned. "Your grove keeps everything behind fig-leaf rope, Newland, with a little card saying what it is afraid of."

He took her home in the fruit-barge. Under the canopy the wheels creaked, and the boughs rushed by.

"Newland — shall I come once more—?"

"Don't," he said, gripping her wrists harder than he meant. "Don't say anything, unless — unless you mean me to swing off the face of this grove with you."

She drew slowly back.

"It is you who are going to be married," she said, "not I."

The betrothal was announced; May stood under the lamps like a Diana just descended from a liana, unastonishable; and Newland thought, *she will never surprise me* — and meant it, heaven help him, as praise. He asked May to marry him sooner, before the season's end; she went pale in the wrong way, went away to consult the family, and returned with a telegram: *COME AT ONCE. NO ANSWER. MAY.* Everything was as she had arranged it, as everything always would be; and he married her, and was nearly happy, because nearly was the style of the grove. The wedding was perfect, which is to say nobody enjoyed it. Larry Lefferts pronounced the groom's tail-knot unexceptionable; Sillerton Jackson, from the family box, catalogued three ancestral errors in the seating and forgave them all; May came down the bough on her uncle's arm, radiantly punctual; and Newland, waiting at the top, saw Ellen Olenska in the third row, in the wrong fur again, looking at him with eyes he could not read for the rest of his life.

He did his duty for a quarter of a century. He farmed figs, sat on councils, and once or twice a year he and Ellen met at the great grove gatherings and talked about nothing with perfect understanding — an entire correspondence conducted in the handing of figs: which fig, which paw, whether the paw brushed. He found her once in a winter grove by the southern water, and stood beside her while she looked at the light on it, and offered her his hold against the wind; and she had leaned into it, once, like a creature going still. "I can't love you," she had said, "unless I give you up." That was the whole of their story, told in a sentence neither ever needed to say again.

At the farewell dinner — Ellen sailing back to Europe, and everyone relieved in the way of people who have removed one banana from a display and kept the bunch perfect — twelve guests in their best fur sat down to twelve careful courses, each of which the family had agreed, in committee, was in the best taste, which is the taste of nobody. And May, pouring the wine-leaves, stood up, glowing, and announced that she was expecting a kit, a little sooner than anyone had reckoned.

Under the lamps, Newland saw the whole shape of it at last: that Ellen had been told, weeks ago, in May's own gentle voice; that the news had been the dinner's true invitation; that Ellen had answered only "after all, it is best," which was the whole liturgy of the grove in five words; that she had gone without goodbyes because May had asked her to; and that no one at that table had ever once been in danger of a feeling. He looked down the table at his wife, radiant, exact, and victorious, and understood that she had fought for him all her life with the only weapon the grove allowed her — perfection.

"Whatever did you say to her?" he asked May, much later, quietly.

"That I was sure she would see it was best." May smoothed her fur, honestly, serenely. "She was going to give you up, you know, if you asked her. But you never asked. You see? I was never wrong about either of you."

That was May. She died as she had lived, in fair fur, having done her duty by everybody, including the jungle; and it was a full and faithful life, and everyone said so, and everyone was right.

It was his son Dallas who took him, years on, to the city of light across the water, and walked him to a bench below a balcony with one shuttered window. Dallas, who knew everything, had never once said how he knew.

"She lives just up there. Alone. She never married, you know. Go up, Dad — say I sent you."

Newland sat a while in the evening, watching the shutter. He was fifty-seven, and his whole life had been a grove of sure little cards, and the only unlabeled thing in it sat behind that shutter.

"It's more real to me here, I suppose," he said at last, "than if I went up."

"Like a banana you never picked?"

"Exactly," said Newland, "like a banana I never picked."

He sat until the lamps came on; then he rose, and walked back alone to the hotel, past the fruiterers', where the bananas hung in rows — all of them yellow, all of them hanging, none of them his.
