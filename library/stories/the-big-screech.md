# The Big Screech

*A parody of "The Big Sleep"*

The greenhouse was as humid as a howler's armpit. General Sternwood, old silverback, sat wrapped in three blankets beside his wheelchair, being pickled alive by his own orchids and daring anyone to mention it.

"Whiskey," he rasped. "Marlowe. Sit down. Mind the sprinklers — they take this jungle seriously here."

Norris the butler, a colobus so discreet he barely existed, poured the whiskey and evaporated.

Philip Marlowe, chimpanzee, private inquiry agent, sat. I'd been hired over a matter of canned screeching: somebody had letters signed by the General's younger daughter, Carmen, promising gambling debts to a rare-book dealer named Geiger, and the General wanted it stopped.

The elder daughter, Vivian, lounged against a potting bench looking like a spider monkey who'd learned diamonds in a finishing school. "Daddy's being blackmailed," she said. "He calls it bad grooming. Do you screech much, Mr. Marlowe?"

"Only at my rates."

Carmen came in mid-screech — a capuchin with powder-blue fur polish and the attention span of a moth. "You're cute," she told me, blowing fig-scented bubbles, and fell over an orchid on purpose.

"You want to know why I care," the General said to me when the screeching died down. "Rusty Regan drank with me. Only monkey who ever told me off to my face and made me like it. He could sit in this wheelchair jungle and make it a grove. Find where he went, Marlowe. Not for the letters. For me."

Geiger's establishment fronted as a rare seed-catalog library. The clerk had frigid paws and a manner like a wet leaf: "First editions only. We do not stock anything so vulgar as a bestseller." Nobody had ever bought a catalog. It was a lending library, and what it lent was photographs.

I tailed Geiger, a langur with a goatee, to his bungalow in the bromeliads, and I was rolling a fig around my mouth in the brush when I heard the screech, then two bangs.

Inside: Geiger, dead; a camera on a tripod; and Carmen, doped to the ears on overripe sap, propped on a throne-like chair in the middle of a stage set. She'd been posed among banana peels with her paws in a termite mound — ruin in twelve prints, for a fig-only debutante of the oldest grooming aristocracy in the valley.

I carried Carmen home. When I came back, the body was gone, and the negatives were gone with it.

They turned up, partway, in the furthest place anyone hides anything: another crook. Joe Brody, a twitchy patas monkey who sold seed-catalog subscriptions and blackmail out of the same suitcase, had the negatives and the nerve to quote me a price in his own apartment, with a paw-axe under the newspaper. "I'm a businessman," said Brody. "Geiger was in photographs. I'm in correspondence."

"Then you'll love this letter," I said, and took the plates off him while he decided whether the axe was worth my rates.

He never got to decide. There was a knock, and the frigid-paws clerk walked in — Carol, Geiger's clerk, and considerably more than a clerk — and shot Brody three times with a small gun, for the monkey who had killed the only monkey he ever ironed his shirts for. Only he hadn't. "He thought Brody did Geiger," I told the constables. "He was wrong, but he was sincere, and around here that passes for a virtue."

Meantime the Sternwoods' young chauffeur, Owen, turned up floating in the koi pond, sleeping with the koi. I did the arithmetic nobody wanted done: Owen had loved Carmen from the driver's seat, had seen the stage set through Geiger's window, had put two bangs through the bromeliads and walked off with the camera's heart. He'd have made a fine blackmailer, and an even better witness. The pond got him first, and the coroner wrote "misadventure," which around this valley means "we're tired."

Eddie Mars, a howler who ran a nightclub called the Scream Room, kept appearing wherever the light was bad. I found Vivian at his faro table, losing like it was a hobby she practiced. "Rusty Regan," she said, before I could ask. "The Irish howler. Daddy's favorite. Gone four months. What of it?"

"Gone with Mars's wife, they say."

"They say." She lit a fig-leaf cigarette. "They also say the sprinklers are decorative."

"How much do you owe him?"

"More than my allowance. Daddy thinks I play for matchsticks." She smiled with every tooth she could spare.

Mars gave me his best courtesy and his gorillas folded me into a nutcracker of fists as a professional courtesy. "You fell down some stairs," the big one explained, anatomically. "How many?" I asked. "All of them." Harry Jones, a bushbaby informer the size of a fig, sold me Mona's hiding place for fifty bananas, and I watched through a window while Mars's enforcer, a mountain named Canino, fed him a fig. "From Mr. Mars," Canino said, "with sympathies." Harry ate it like a tired child. I have never wanted to be a bigger monkey than I was behind that glass.

I went after Canino anyway; he tied me to a chair at a fern farm to think about my regrets. It was Mona — eyes like still pools, the only honest face in the jungle — who stepped between us when he came back with his sap-rag. "I'm not sorry about the sap," she told me, untying one paw, "and I'm not sorry about this either." It was the most honest thing anybody said all week. And it was the branch I had sawed three-quarters through that Canino found with his foot when he chased my whistle into the dark. Some monkeys never look down. He never looked down again.

The last act happened in the General's own tree, and I staged it myself. I gave Carmen a camera and a loaded-looking pistol and told her to show me how she had entertained Rusty the night he disappeared.

She giggled. She posed. She screeched like a kettle and shot at me, and the hammer fell on air, because I had emptied it myself.

"There's your blackmailer, your runaway, your whole case," I told Vivian, while Carmen blew bubbles, bewildered as a wet hen. "Carmen tried that same act on Rusty — the pose, the pout, the pistol. He laughed and patted her head, so she planted him in the grove and forgot him by morning. She's killed twice. She'll kill again. Put her somewhere with soft branches and good locks — or don't, and come see me in five years for the family rate."

"And Eddie Mars?"

"His enforcer went on a nature retreat. His story about Mona and Rusty stays a story, or I tell it aloud, with voices."

Vivian lit a fig-leaf cigarette with a paw that didn't shake. "You're a screech, Marlowe."

On the way out I stood a minute under the General's window. Somewhere down the valley the night troop had started up — that endless, beautiful, indifferent racket, every monkey in the world hollering at the dark, none of it meaning anything, all of it going on forever.

It's a good jungle to sleep in, once you quit asking what it means. Old Sternwood has the right idea: sit among your orchids, sip your whiskey, let the screech roll over you. You're only sleeping the big screech anyway.
