# Alice's Adventures in Wonderlangur

*A parody of "Alice's Adventures in Wonderland"*

Alice the young langur was beginning to get terribly bored. She sat beside her sister on the riverbank, watching her sister read a book with no pictures and no bananas in it. "What is the use of a book," thought Alice, "without pictures or bananas?"

Just then a Bushbaby in a waistcoat went rushing past, checking a large pocket watch. "Oh dear! Oh dear! I shall be too late!"

Alice had never seen a bushbaby with a pocket watch, so she loped after him to a hollow tree — and down she went, tumbling slowly past cupboards, bookshelves, and jars of mango chutney, to land softly on a heap of leaves.

At the bottom was a tiny door, and beyond it the most beautiful banana grove she had ever seen. On a table sat a little bottle labelled DRINK ME. "Though it may be monkey business," said Alice, and drank. It tasted of banana-nog, and it shrank her to the size of a marmoset. Then she found a little cake labelled EAT ME, and ate it, and grew so tall her ears poked the canopy. "Curiouser and curiouser!" cried Alice, crying great big tears that made a pool the size of a lagoon, until she had to swim in her own salt water while a whole troop of soggy creatures splashed beside her.

"How do we get dry?" Alice asked a Slow Loris.

"The best way," announced the Loris, "is a Caucus-swing." So everyone swung in a great circle for half an hour, nobody won, and everyone got a banana as a prize, including Alice.

The Bushbaby rushed by again, muttering, "The Duchess! The Duchess!" — and when Alice tried to help, he looked straight through her and snapped, "Marianne! Fetch my gloves this instant!" — for to a bushbaby in a hurry, every langur is a servant named Marianne.

So Alice fetched the gloves herself, found another little bottle on the table, drank it to be tidy, and shot up so fast that her head went through the roof, one arm out the window and one foot up the chimney.

"Send Bill down the chimney!" cried the crowd, and lowered an iguana named Bill tail-first down the flue — where Alice, sneezing at the dust, sneezed him clean back out over the treetops.

The crowd pelted her with pebbles, which turned into little green cakes as they flew. She ate one, shrank to squirrel-monkey size, and trotted away while the crowd tried to decide whether to be angry with her or with Bill.

In the wood she sat on a mushroom exactly her own height, and on top of it sat a slow loris in a dressing gown, smoking a hookah of dried hibiscus, looking down his nose at her.

"Who are YOU?" said the Loris.

Which, Alice felt, was rather rude, coming from somebody dressed like a sofa. "I — I hardly know, sir, just at present. You see, I've been marmoset-sized, giant, and squirrel-sized before breakfast."

"Explain yourself," said the Loris.

"I can't explain myself, sir, because I'm not myself, you see."

"I don't see," said the Loris. "And what size do you want to be? One side will make you taller, and the other side will make you shorter." He meant the mushroom. "Keep your temper," he added, and slid backwards down his own mushroom and out of the story.

Alice broke off a piece of mushroom from one side, and a piece from the other, and with one bite and the other she adjusted herself all morning — chin-up, chin-down — until at last she walked at her proper height, tail at its proper curl. It is not every langur who can be measured in mushroom.

The dosing brought her to a small house where the pepper was so thick a cook was throwing pots at a Duchess. The Duchess was a wide uakari with a red face like a sunset, nursing a baby that sneezed and howled by turns, while up on the ceiling, for no reason anyone gave, floated a grin.

"Please mind what you're doing with that pepper," Alice wheezed.

"Here! You may nurse it, if you like," said the Duchess, flinging the baby at her. But the baby grunted in such a snorting, fig-scoffing way that Alice set it down at once — and it turned, before her eyes, into a fat little piglet, which trotted off looking smug.

"What an unpleasant child," said Alice. But as she left, she noticed the grin stayed a moment after everything else had gone — a monkey she felt she would like, when he was in a better mood.

She did not have long to wait. Around the next bend sat the grin's owner on a branch: a Capuchin, folded up and purring.

"Cheshire Capuchin," he said, by way of introduction. "How's the temper?"

"Kept, thank you," said Alice. "Though I'm cross with everyone. This is the silliest jungle I ever swung through."

"We're all mad here," said the Cheshire Capuchin. "I'm mad. You're mad."

"How do you know I'm mad?" said Alice.

"You must be," said the Capuchin, "or you wouldn't have come here." And he began to vanish — tail first, then body, then ears — until only his grin remained, hanging in the leaves like a crescent of ripe banana. "Try the tea party," said the grin, and was gone. A grin without a monkey, thought Alice, is a very rare sight indeed.

The tea party was under a fig tree: the Mad Hoolock, in a hat stacked with fruit, and the March Macaque, having tea with a Dozy Owl-Monkey asleep in the teapot.

"No room! No room!" they cried.

"There's PLENTY of room!" said Alice firmly, and sat down.

"Riddle time," said the Hoolock. "Why is a baboon like a banana bunch?"

Alice thought and thought. "I give up. Why?"

"I haven't the faintest idea," said the Hoolock. "It's always six o'clock here, you see. Always snack time, never dinner. We never have to wash up."

"Well, I think it's rude," said Alice, "wasting a perfectly good riddle," and off she stomped.

Then a trumpet sounded beyond the trees. "The Queen! Make way for the Queen of Bananas!" On came a procession to the croquet ground, led by the Queen of Bananas herself, a massive crowned macaque shouting "OFF WITH THEIR TAILS!" at everybody — with the King of Bananas trotting beside her, saying "Yes, dear," and "Only where necessary, my dear." The gardeners were anxiously painting the green bananas yellow, because the Queen had ordered a yellow harvest and somebody had planted green.

"Can you play croquet?" the Queen barked at Alice.

"Yes," said Alice, "but where are the mallets?"

The mallets were live toucans, who would not hold still, and the balls were rolled-up armadillos who unrolled and walked away, and the Queen screamed "OFF WITH HIS TAIL!" so often that by dinnertime there wasn't an unthreatened tail in the garden. The King quietly pardoned every one of them, following with a little notebook: PARDONED, PARDONED, PARDONED.

Then the trumpet sounded again. "Silence in court! The Knave of Nanas stands accused of stealing the Queen's banana cream tarts!"

The Knave, a long-limbed young macaque with jam on his chin, was marched to the dock. The King put on his judging wig, kept specially for judging, and read from a parchment scroll:

"The Queen of Bananas made some tarts, all on a summer day; the Knave of Nanas took those tarts, and slyly ran away!"

"I never!" said the Knave. "I only licked the cream off the top, which isn't even stealing the good part!"

The trial began, and it was the silliest she ever sat through. The first witness was the Mad Hoolock, who had come straight from tea and could talk of nothing but his hat. The second was a jar of jam, which said nothing but looked guilty. Then the Bushbaby burst in shouting "I'm late, I'm late," and testified so fast that nobody understood a word — including the King, who called it very valuable.

"And do you have a letter?" said the King. The Knave tremblingly produced one.

"Begin at the beginning," the King commanded, "and go on till you come to the end, then stop."

The letter was not written by the Knave, nor about the Knave, nor in any order whatsoever. "It doesn't prove anything," said Alice, who had got taller while reading it, which happens when one is interested. "It isn't even signed!"

"If you didn't sign it," said the King, "that's worse. It means you were up to something, or you'd have signed it proudly."

"Sentence first!" roared the Queen. "Verdict afterwards!"

"Stuff and nonsense!" said Alice, growing taller with every word until she towered over the whole court, ears brushing the canopy. "The idea of sentencing a poor Knave without any evidence! Why, you're nothing but a pack of playing apes!"

At this, the whole army of card-carrying apes flung themselves into the air and came raining down upon her — and Alice woke beneath the fig tree by the riverbank, covered in leaves, her sister gently grooming her head.

"What a long sleep you've had!" said her sister.

"I've had the curiousest dream," said Alice, picking a leaf out of her ear. "A whole jungle gone bananas — a Loris who wanted explaining, a Duchess with a pig, a court that couldn't count its verdicts — and I've had quite enough of Wonderlangur."

For now.
