# Romance of the Tree Kongdoms

*A parody of "Romance of the Three Kingdoms"*

The forest, long divided, must intertwine; long intertwined, it must divide. Thus it has ever been beneath the great canopy, where the branches of ten thousand trees knit together into one green country — and into one green argument.

In the final years of the Han-Fig dynasty, the court rotted from the crown outward. The eunuchs of the inner branches sold offices like fruit. And in the understory there arose the Yellow Peels: rebels who wore the skins of ripe bananas on their heads and promised a golden age, led by the three Zhang brothers, of whom the less said the better, since two of them were the same Zhang.

"Canopy is failing!" they screeched. "The peel has fallen! A new shoot must rise!"

Into this disorder walked a young kong of the imperial line: Liu Bough of the Fig, a monkey of modest means whose arms reached past his knees — a distinction the jungle regarded not as oddity but as destiny, for in the trees, reach is righteousness.

At a village well he found two strangers eyeing the same recruitment notice. One was a red-faced douc langur with a beard like a waterfall in sunlight, magnificent beyond all sense: Gibbon Yu. The other was a black-faced snub-nose with a voice like a rockslide and a temper like a wet baboon: Zhang Flea.

"I would save the canopy," said Liu Bough, simply, "but I have no troop."

"We have no leader," said Gibbon Yu, inclining his beautiful head.

"I have no patience for this conversation," announced Zhang Flea. "Come. There is a peach orchard behind the shrine."

And in that peach orchard — the trees heavy, the fruit absurdly perfect — the three swore the oath remembered ever after on every branch of the forest:

"We three, Liu Bough, Gibbon Yu, and Zhang Flea, ask not to have been born on the same branch, but pray our spirits will leave it on the same morning. May the Canopy above and the Roots below witness it. Brother, brother, and brother — till the last leaf falls."

They plunged into the wars against the Yellow Peels, and the forest learned their names. Gibbon Yu's green robe and nine-foot reach made him visible to whole armies, which was convenient, as he intended to be seen. Zhang Flea's spear cleared marketplaces. Liu Bough wept a great deal, magnificently, and recruited everybody who saw him do it, for in those days a lord who wept for the people was worth ten who merely taxed them.

But while the brothers fought rebels, a subtler creature climbed. In the north, in the service of the court, was a mandrill minister named Cao Claw: brilliant, ruthless, sarcastic, devoted to the forest only in the sense that a vine is devoted to a tree. When the old chancellor died, Cao Claw coaxed the young emperor into his own coils, then coiled tighter.

"The Minister is too generous," murmured the court.

"The Minister is the government," said Cao Claw, pleasantly, and practiced gorilla warfare with his standing armies — actual gorillas, drilled in the mountain style, the finest heavy infantry in the north.

The Han-Fig dynasty gasped, wobbled, and fell. Warlords took provinces like figs off a low branch. Cao Claw took the emperor, the north, and the weather. And Liu Bough, virtuous, weeping, and losing, wandered the forest for years with his sworn brothers, seeking what every displaced kong seeks: a strategist.

Word came of a recluse in the far south: Zhuge Liang, called the Sleeping Gibbon, who slept twenty hours a day and dreamed strategy, waking only to play the seven-string zither and to feed his crane. Liu Bough went to his grass hut. The Sleeping Gibbon was out. Liu Bough went again through the snow. Out again. On the third visit the sage was home — awake, it was said, only because he had calculated the exact hour of arrival.

"You have come three times," said Zhuge Liang, "which proves sincerity or arithmetic. Either will do."

He unrolled a map of the forest on the floor. "Cao Claw holds the north. The Sun clan holds the east and the great river. For you — the west, the mountain figs, the high branches. Three trees, one canopy, and no peace until the argument is settled. Also," he added, yawning, "you will want to borrow some arrows."

"Arrows?"

"Ten thousand. I will arrange it. And an east wind. I will arrange that too, though it will cost me years of my life, which I have budgeted."

The matter came to a head on the great river, where Cao Claw's northern fleet — gorillas in armor, a thousand ships lashed gunwale to gunwale because his troops were seasick — faced the allied eastern and western troops under the young lord Sun and the borrowed genius of the Sleeping Gibbon.

Cao Claw's mistake was timber. His armada had come south with the whole northern dry season in its hold: baled hay, oil, rope, optimism.

Zhuge Liang looked at the fleet, looked at the weather, and lay down for a strategic nap. He woke at dusk and issued three orders.

First: twenty boats of banana leaves, in the fog. The eastern troops rowed close to Cao Claw's anchored fleet, drumming and screeching. The northerners, unable to resist, fired every arrow in the north at the noise. The arrows stuck in the banana-leaf bales. Zhuge Liang sailed home before dawn with ten thousand arrows and thanked Cao Claw courteously, by name, across the water. Cao Claw's reply was not recorded, on account of the swearing.

Second: surrender. Several captains rowed over to Cao Claw pretending to defect, bringing fire ships as gifts.

Third: the east wind. It blew.

The fire ships went in with the wind behind them, into a fleet lashed together by an architect of genius, laden with hay, and it was not a battle so much as the largest lantern the world had ever lit. The river ran orange. The Yellow Peels of an earlier age could only have doffed their peels in respect. Afterward, historians named it for the drifting embers that painted the cliffs till morning: the Battle of the Red Peel.

Cao Claw escaped north with a handful of gorillas and all of his sarcasm. The forest settled into three great trees: Cao Claw's north, the Sun clan's east, and Liu Bough's mountain west, where he was finally crowned kong of Shu — a tree so high its figs were said to ripen among clouds.

But the forest, long divided, was not done dividing.

Gibbon Yu, guarding the eastern marches, grew proud as only a red-faced douc with an unbroken record can. The eastern lords pretended friendship and struck from behind. Cut off in the maze-vine country at Maicheng, wounded, alone at a crossroads of branches, Gibbon Yu was offered terms.

"I have sworn," he said, "to die on the same morning as my brothers. You will have to discuss it with them."

They did not have to discuss it with Zhang Flea, whose own groomers cut his throat in his sleep for being exactly himself. And Liu Bough, hearing, died of grief within the year in the Hollow Fig of the west — but not before whispering to Zhuge Liang: "The tree matters more than my fruit. Save it, if you can stay awake."

The Sleeping Gibbon stayed awake. For years he marched the northern marches against Cao Claw's heirs, his zither on his back, his fan of a hundred voluntarily shed macaque tails never still. He built ox-carts that walked. He wrote his plan in a document that outlived him. He died on campaign in autumn, on the plain of Wuzhang, mid-fan-stroke, and the northern armies did not dare attack the western camp for three days, because they could not believe he was gone until the smell of the zither strings faded.

And in the north, in the shade of Cao Claw's old tree, the Simia family quietly grew. First as ministers, then as regents, then as something with no other name. The sons and grandsons of the great warlords' warlords absorbed the east, then the west, the way a banyan absorbs a fence: no battle, just patience, root by root by root.

So it happened that the Three Kongdoms became one canopy again, and the Simia called it whole, and peace of a sort stood on the forest for a season.

But the branches kept growing. And any branch, given time, meets another branch.

The forest, long intertwined, must divide; long divided, it must intertwine. Ask instead what the old singer meant when he bent close at the campfire, over the figs and the wine, and gave it to you plainly:

It is not the canopy that is ruled, little listener — it is the reach. And there has never been a monkey born whose arms were longer than the trees.
