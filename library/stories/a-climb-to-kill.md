# A Climb to Kill

*A parody of "A Time to Kill"*

The two rogue baboons drank fermented sap boilermakers all afternoon in the woods outside town. They were hunting for fruit to steal and trouble to start, and they found little Tonya Leaf — four feet of gorilla girl hauling a sack of plantains home from her family's market stand. What they did to her out among the ferns, the zoo vet would only call "a bad business." She lived. She told her mama who did it, and her mama told her papa.

Carl Leaf Hailey had never made trouble in his life. He was a gorilla of the old sort — quiet, churchgoing, fixed vines at the mill — the kind of father who carried sleepy children on his back all the way up to the sleeping boughs. He sat with his little girl all night at the clinic, saying nothing. Then he borrowed something his cousin had brought back from the war: a vintage coconut cannon.

On arraignment morning, Carl Leaf hid in the broom closet of the Banyan County Courthouse, third floor, behind the mop. When the deputies walked the two baboons past the door, he stepped out and fired eleven coconuts at point-blank range, hitting both baboons, one deputy's knee, a water cooler, and Judge Lasso's portrait square in the mustache. The two rogues never climbed again, on account of the coconuts.

Sheriff Ozzie Walls — a chimp who ran Banyan County with a can of peanuts and total recall — gathered up the brass, the evidence, and the history of the world in about nine hours, mostly because the two baboons had told their whole crime to a cousin, and cousins talk. Thus began the biggest case in the county's history, taken by Jake Briga-branch — a young chimp attorney with a wife, a daughter, a mortgage on his treehouse, and an ego the size of a baobab.

"You've lost your mind," his wife Carla told him. "They'll convict him in an hour and you right along with him."

"Maybe," Jake said, gazing out at the moonlit canopy like a lawyer in an advertisement. "But somebody's got to stand up in that courtroom and say out loud why a father goes ape."

The Troop Advancement Association sent three famous silverbacks in a fleet sedan to offer Carl Leaf their best counsel, free of charge and full of cameras. Carl Leaf looked at their suits, then at Jake's frayed vest. "I want the one who knows my treehouse," he said. The silverbacks left in a huff. Jake stayed with a mortgage.

The whole jungle swung in to watch. The Rogue Brotherhood — baboons in bedsheets with eye-holes, burning little banyan crosses on Jake's lawn — came up from the swamp to sell souvenir sheets, and their Grand Snapdragon held a rally that was better attended than the county fair. The district attorney, Rufus Buckleigh, ran for reelection on the case. Judge Omar Lasso presided with a gavel and a permanent frown. Jake's sauced ex-partner, the orangutan Lucien Wilbanks, contributed strategy, chaos, and an expert witness of intermittent sobriety.

Then came Ellen Roarkoon, a law student gorilla with red-bottom reading glasses, a photocopier's reverence for precedent, and a cooler of sandwiches she issued like subpoenas. She had flunked intimidation twice and was proud of it. She sat through jury selection taking notes Jake would later steal shamelessly.

Selection itself took a week. Buckleigh struck every gibbon who had ever been a little girl's father; Jake struck anyone who had ever been hit by a coconut. They met in the middle with twelve faces that could jury-rig anything.

The Brotherhood escalated on schedule. A sniper put a dart through Jake's window and the water cooler. A midnight march ended in pushed monkeys and broken tailbones. In October somebody torched Jake's treehouse to the knot-hole; the insurance company called it "a claim with a fan base." Carla took little Hanna to her mother's grove in the high mountains.

"Win," was all she said, which in gorilla means goodbye, I love you, and come back.

At trial, the state's psychiatrist swore Carl Leaf knew exactly what he was doing. Lucien's miracle expert, the eminent Dr. Bassett — flown in to swear that Carl Leaf had been legally insane in every direction — was pulled over on day four driving Judge Lasso's own golf cart, three sheets and a par-5 from the courthouse. That left the defense with no witness, no insanity plea, and one closing argument, which is how Jake Briga-branch became either a great lawyer or a dead one.

Carl Leaf took the stand in his church vest, so big and gentle and sad that even the court reporter stopped typing.

"Mr. Briga-branch says I'm crazy," he rumbled. "I ain't crazy. I'm sorry about the coconuts. I'd be lying if I said I was sorry about the rest."

The jury was twelve gibbons who hadn't smiled in nine days. Outside, two mobs sang at each other while the National Guard ringed the courthouse in helmets. The Grand Snapdragon held a rally that only reporters attended, which was the plan, and was later convicted of tax fraud, which was not. The jury treehouse door stayed shut for five hours. Inside, at the end of it, a gibbon named Corinne finally stood up.

"Y'all close your eyes," she told the others. "Now picture her yours."

Then Jake stood up for closing, took off his glasses, and did not look at the jury at all.

"I want you to close your eyes," he said quietly. "I'm going to tell you a story, and you're going to picture it."

The courtroom went still as the top of a redwood at noon.

"Picture a little girl. Four feet tall. Fur like a fresh blackberry. She's walking home from the market with a sack of plantains, humming, kicking a pinecone along the trail. Now picture two big baboons, mean and drunk on bad sap, pulling that little girl off the trail and into the ferns."

A juror's hand went to her mouth. Jake's voice never rose.

"They climb on her and they hurt her and they laugh. She's screaming and nobody hears. She wants her mama. She wants her papa. Now hold that picture. Hold it."

He let the silence sit until it ached.

"Now imagine she's golden," Jake said. "Imagine she was born in the high canopy. Imagine her papa owns this courthouse. Imagine her grandpapa is the governor. Can you see her now?" His voice finally cracked, one clean split down the middle, like a banana. "Now imagine she's your little girl. What would you do?"

Nobody in that courtroom moved for a full minute. The forewoman wept into both sleeves. The jury came back at dusk with the verdict: not guilty, by reason of temporary troop-madness. The courtroom went off like a fig tree in a windstorm, and Wanda the bailiff took up a collection for Jake's burned treehouse that raised two branches and a roof by Christmas.

That evening Carl Leaf appeared on Jake's porch with a crate of plantains and stood there awkwardly, the way big gorillas do.

"You did good, Jake."

"We did good together. Now go home before Lucien starts singing." Jake paused. "And Carl Lee — next time, let the courts go ape first."

"Ain't gonna be a next time," said Carl Leaf, and swung off into the dusk with his crate, one relieved daddy at a time.
