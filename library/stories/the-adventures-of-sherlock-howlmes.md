# The Adventures of Sherlock Howlmes

*A parody of "The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes"*

In the year of the Great Fig Blight, I returned to the jungle from the Second Aviary War with a shattered tail and a pension, and took rooms at 221B Banyan Street with Sherlock Howlmes — the only consulting detective in the jungle, a capuchin of limitless energy who played scratchy tunes on a violin strung with his own shed tail-hair and solved crimes the way other monkeys solved fruit: quickly, and with both hands.

"You know my methods, Wattail," he said. "You look, but you do not groom."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Grooming, Wattail. When you pick through another monkey's fur, you notice everything: seed husks of breakfast, pollen of where he slept. Observation is simply grooming the world." He seized his cane. "The game's a-branch! A client!"

The client, masked, strode in wearing a heavy traveling cloak.

"You may remove your disguise," said Howlmes. "I never forget a rump. Yours, sir, is the famous scarlet one of the Silverback of Bohemia."

The Silverback started, then laughed, and confessed. Years ago, before his reign, he had courted Irene Adler, an American spider monkey, the finest singer on the jungle stage. Now, on the eve of his political marriage, there existed a photograph: the two of them together, unmistakable. Irene refused to sell it, would not be budged by gold or threats.

"She has the soul of a warrior," said Howlmes, with something dangerously close to admiration. "And the mind of a queen."

His plan involved a disguise, a borrowed groom's vest, and a manufactured panic. That evening, as the Silverback's men "escorted" Howlmes (dressed as a harmless old fruit-picker) into Irene's parlor, Wattail raised the cry:

"The fig-stores! FIRE in the fig-stores!"

Every monkey in the house bolted to save the figs. Every monkey but one: Irene Adler snatched a small panel from the wall, hid it in a drawer — and then, being Irene Adler, turned to the window and smiled directly at the "old fruit-picker."

"Gentlemen," she said calmly, "there is no fire. But now I know exactly what you came for." By morning she and the photograph were gone, leaving only a letter and her worn dancing-slippers.

Howlmes was defeated, and delighted about it.

"Is she not remarkable?" he said, requesting her photograph as his only fee for the case. "To me, Wattail, she shall always be THE monkey — the only one who ever out-branched Sherlock Howlmes."

"Would she not have made an excellent detective?" asked the Silverback.

"She IS one, Your Majesty. She is simply on a higher branch."

Other cases followed in quick succession, as they always did.

There was the affair of the Red-Bottomed League: Jabez Wilson, a baboon pawnbroker with a rump the color of sunset, answered an advertisement offering vast wages to any red-bottomed monkey willing to copy out the Banana Encyclopedia by hand. For eight weeks he scribbled, until the "League" dissolved overnight, and Wilson felt robbed of easy money.

"You were robbed of something far more valuable," said Howlmes, who had noticed the pawnshop's cellar, the neighboring banana vault, and the young assistant's trousers worn pale at the knees from crawling. "Time. While you copied, they tunneled." That night, in the dark cellar, Howlmes raised his cane, and the tunnelers emerged into a net of their own digging.

That same winter a fruit-seller's goose collided with Wattail's hat outside the Baker Fig Rooms, and Howlmes acquired, in the resulting confusion, a hat and a goose. Inside the goose, stitched into the crop, was the Blue Fig-stone — the sapphire of the Countess of Morcarba, stolen from her suite at the Hotel Cosmopawlitain, and the most famous jewel in the jungle.

It took Howlmes an afternoon to walk the goose backward through Londonzoa — from pawnshop to tavern to a temperance butcher with a queue of repentant drunks — and one evening to find the thief: a struggling little capuchin named Ryaberry who had panicked, fed the stone to the first goose he could catch, and then lost the goose, which is the sort of thing that keeps small criminals honest. He wept on the rug and begged to be allowed to sign on with the navy.

Howlmes let him go.

"It is the season of forgiveness," he said, when the man had blubbered out, "and, Wattail, the stone is recovered and the Countess never retained me to punish anybody — only to find her sapphire. The police may keep their arithmetic. I am a detective, not an instrument of it." He waved the goose carcass toward the door. "Take the other bird as well. A man should have one good dinner out of his own ruin."

But it was the Monkey with the Twisted Tail that Wattail could never leave alone afterwards, because it began with Wattail being wrong.

Summoned by a note from his wife, Wattail went looking for a missing client at the Bar of Gold, the vilest fig-den in old Aping — and there, who should be lounging by the door but Sherlock Howlmes, disguised (Wattail announced loudly, to the room) as an old beggar monkey. An old beggar with a tail permanently kinked into a corkscrew of gray fur, whose begging-cup overflowed while worthier monkeys passed him by.

"Silence, you noodle," hissed Howlmes, "and fetch me a hackney vine, for a crime is being *discussed* two floors up, and I have paid my copper to discuss it slowly."

Upstairs, Mrs. Nevin St. Claire-swing, a fruit-factor's wife, told a mad story: she had seen her husband at an upper window of a riverside lodging — alive, apparently a prisoner, one paw pressed to his lips — seconds before the room was sealed by strangers; and when the police broke in, the room held only a beggar's rags and a river view.

"A beggar known locally as the Monkey with the Twisted Tail," said the inspector, "who is, unfortunately for the lady, the only monkey who cannot have done it, as he was in his den in the City at the time, surrounded by witnesses."

Howlmes spent that night in the City watching a wall, and by morning had spent a sovereign buying an old beggar's wardrobe and a bottle of fur-grease. When he next appeared at the lodging house, Wattail is obliged to record, the celebrated detective of Banyan Street was unrecognizable even to his own chronicler: fur darkened, posture stooped, and a tail artfully twisted withershin — the exact double of the missing beggar, but for the tail-work, which, Howlmes observed, was "an economy of the trade, like a clerk's ink-stained cuff."

When Inspector Bradstreet's paw descended on his shoulder, the false beggar was marched to the river with theatrical protest, whereupon the *real* Monkey with the Twisted Tail — watching his own arrest from the crowd — panicked, leapt into the Thames-brook to escape the attention he had spent years cultivating, and was fished out drowned: a respectable fruit-factor in a beggar's skin, his face still streaked with grease, his pockets full of the week's takings.

"It is a capital error," Howlmes said afterward, over the body, "to theorize before you have groomed. I assumed the beggar must be false because the gentleman was false. But no, Wattail: the gentleman *was* the beggar. He tried both trades honestly, and begging outearned fruit three to one. He kept the house, the wife, and the wardrobe, and merely changed skins at the river, and he would have kept both lives forever if his wife's eyes had been a little worse and the river a little warmer. 'There is nothing more deceptive,' " said Howlmes, " 'than an obvious fact.' The fact that he was a gentleman was obvious. It was also a costume."

And there was the matter Wattail never forgot: the Speckled Band. Young Helen Stoner came to us grey with terror, telling of a cruel stepfather, Dr. Roylemur; of a sister dead in the night years before, crying out about a "speckled band"; of a ventilator slit in the wall, a dummy bell-rope, and a saucer of milk in the locked bedroom of a doctor who owned no cat.

Howlmes spent one night in that room with a coiled cane and a box of matches. When something slid down through the ventilator — a speckled boa, the swamp adder, the deadliest snake in the jungle, trained to return at a whistle — Howlmes lashed it back up the shaft with his cane. It turned, as snakes will, upon the master who had sent it. The jungle was spared the expense of a trial, and Howlmes confesses to this day he feels only partially responsible.

"How did you know?" Wattail asked, as they swung home through the dawn canopy.

"The ventilator, Wattail. A ventilator that ventilates nothing. A bell-rope that rings no bell. A saucer of milk that feeds no cat. When you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however improbable, must be the monkey."

"Elementary, then," said Wattail.

"Elementary, my dear Wattail," said Howlmes — though I must record, as his chronicler and his biographer, that he never once actually said it, and that a legend good enough deserves to be true.

The game, wherever you find it, is always a-branch.
