# A Peel View of Hills

*A parody of "A Pale View of Hills"*

From the window of my cottage in England I have what I have come to think of as a peel view of hills: in a certain evening light, the Downs across the valley turn a soft, sharp yellow, the yellow of a banana ripening on a sill, and I find I cannot look at them without a small sting behind the eyes, which at my age one does not investigate.

Niki, my younger daughter, is staying with me for a few days. She is twenty-five, writes fierce little pieces for the magazines in London, and disapproves of the countryside on principle — the quiet, she says, is a form of denial.

"You could come back to the city," she said last night. "There's nothing for you here but weather."

"I like the weather," I said.

"You say that about everything here. The garden. The sheep. It isn't natural, Mother."

Her father — my second husband, an Englishman, long dead now — used to say Niki came into the world arguing. She did. It is the one gift of mine she kept.

It is Keiko we do not discuss, of course. My elder daughter. Niki will circle the subject all week, expertly, the way her father circled anything that mattered. Keiko died four years ago, in a small room in a city in the north, and I will not describe how she was found. There are things a mother does not put into sentences, and I have found that sentences, once built, are terribly hard to demolish. They stand in you like the hut frames they left standing in Nana-saki after the war, too solid to remove and too ruined to use.

Nana-saki, then. I find that even now I speak of that year carefully, as though walking across tatami in the dark.

I was married then to my first husband, Jiro, a young professional langur with a good position and an appetite for advancement. I was expecting our first child — Keiko, though of course she had no name yet, only the kicks, which came mostly at night, like a polite visitor who knows the trains have stopped. Jiro's father, Ogata-San, a retired schoolteacher, was staying with us that summer, a gentle, worn-out old monkey who spent his days playing go against himself and losing on purpose.

"You are too hard on Jiro," he told me once, mildly. "A young monkey now must push. There is no branch left for the polite."

Perhaps. Or perhaps I simply found, in that steaming postwar summer, that I had married a position rather than a husband, and that the two of us, Jiro and I, were climbing the same tree from opposite sides, close, industrious, and never once meeting in the crown.

It was that summer I knew Sachiko.

She lived with her small daughter, Mariko, in a cottage down by the river — a sagging, rosy, impractical cottage with a dead tree beside it and, hanging from the largest branch, a rope swing that moved in the smallest breeze as though a light child had just left it. Sachiko was a widow from a good family fallen a long way, and she was — I have searched for the word across forty years — unembarrassed. That was it. Famine, ration lines, a bombed city, a dead husband, a feral daughter, and Sachiko discussed it all as though reporting on the weather in a country she had no plans to revisit.

"You find me shocking," she said once, laughing, watching me not answer. "Etsuko, you mustn't. The war took shame the way it took the roof tiles. There was simply too much to be ashamed of, and we had to ration that too."

Her daughter Mariko did not come when called. She was a thin, wild little thing who spent whole days by the river, or up in the dead tree — Sachiko's tree, with the swing — talking to something in the branches that no one else could see. A woman, Mariko said. A woman in the tree, watching her. The neighborhood had decided she was wrong in some unspecifiable way, the way neighborhoods do.

"Shouldn't someone look to her?" I asked one evening, as the light went violet over the water and Mariko stood, as she so often stood, at the very top of the dead tree, one arm out like a tightrope artist.

"Who?" said Sachiko, with her short laugh. "There's no one to look to anything, Etsuko. There never was. That is the great discovery of my thirties." Then she looked at her daughter in the tree for a while, and the laugh went out of her face the way tide goes off a flat. "She saw something, once. During the bombings, on the river. She's never said what, and I've never asked. That's the arrangement."

Sachiko's great project, that summer, was Frank. Frank was a big amiable American baboon attached to the occupation in some supply capacity, pink-faced, enormous, always cheerful, smelling of diesel and chewing gum, and he had promised — Sachiko assured me he had promised — to take her and Mariko to America. He had connections in the south, he said. He knew men who owed him. Everything was arrangement, he kept saying, everything was arrangement.

"Americans," I said carefully, "are said to make many arrangements."

"You disapprove," said Sachiko. "That's because you still believe in the tree you're climbing. You think if you go up correctly, branch by branch, there's a crown. There isn't a crown, Etsuko. There's only the climbing. I intend to climb a different tree, that's all. It's a stranger tree, and no one will catch me, but at least the view will be new." She was quiet a moment. "You could say I'm choosing to go. That would be kind. In another year it won't be choosing anything. It'll just be later."

Twice that summer I made excuses to Jiro and went down to the river cottage in the evening. The second time — I remember it with the awful clarity of a cicada stopping — Frank had not come. Sachiko was sitting very straight on the step, watching the road the way you watch a lit fuse, and Mariko was up in the dead tree in the dusk, talking to the woman who wasn't there.

"Mariko will not wish to go," I said, finally, because someone had to. "She will make it very hard."

"Mariko is a child. Children go where they're taken." Sachiko's voice was flat and final, and then it changed, and it was the change that frightened me, because there was something underneath it I recognized — something soft and tired and made entirely of mornings. "She'll thank me. One day, when she's grown, and the war is a story older than she is, and she can't even remember this place — she'll thank me."

I said nothing. We were quiet for a while. When I rose to go, she said, without looking at me: "You're thinking of the child you're carrying. You're thinking: I would never."

I did not answer her. It was growing dark. Up in the dead tree, the swing moved once, twice, in a wind that was not blowing on the river.

I have not thought about those evenings in years. Niki's visit has stirred it all up, the way her visits do — that is her gift; her father could do it to a room without moving from his chair.

What I remember most about Sachiko, in the end, is not her voice, or the cottage, or the violet evenings by the river. It is one small thing. The last time I saw them, Mariko was in front of the cottage, refusing, and Sachiko was crouched in front of her daughter, speaking quietly, patiently, the way you speak to a frightened animal at a crossing, and what she said — though I have wondered, over the years, whether I have not at some point exchanged her words for someone else's — what she said was this:

"If you don't like it over there, we can always come back."

There was, of course, no coming back, and she knew it, and Mariko knew it, and I, watching from the road with my hand on my belly, knew it too.

Yesterday morning Niki left. She stood by her little car in the drive and finally said: "You know it wasn't your fault. What happened to Keiko. She chose to come to this country, and she chose — she was grown. You mustn't keep doing that thing you do, with the window."

"What thing I do," I asked, "with the window?"

"Nothing," said Niki, and kissed my cheek too quickly, and drove off badly down the lane, as she does everything.

After she had gone I made tea and drank it standing at the window, and I watched the evening come over the valley, and I did not think about very much at all — not Jiro, not Ogata-San losing to himself by lamplight, not the cottage by the river, not Sachiko or Frank or the child up in the dead tree. The hills across the valley turned, briefly, their soft, sharp yellow, the color of ripening, and the sun went in behind them, and I have no wish now to blame that particular view of hills for anything.

The swing moves, sometimes, when there is no wind. That is all I know about trees, and it is all anyone knows.
