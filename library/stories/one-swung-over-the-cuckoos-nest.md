# One Swung Over the Cuckoo's Nest

*A parody of "One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest"*

They're out there. Keepers in white suits, up before dawn, hosing down the concrete while the mist machines pump fog into our enclosure. Me, I'm in the corner with the broom the aides hand me, sweeping the same patch of floor over and over. I'm the Chief. Big old orangutan, arms like ficus boughs, and I haven't said a word in years, because a monkey that can't hear and can't talk is invisible, and invisible is safe from the Combine — the great machine that runs the whole zoo, gears inside gears, keeping every monkey mild.

I used to be big for real, not just in the arms. My father was a chief too, Palm-that-Stands-Tallest, and the government apes came smiling with papers, and bought our river, and bought our trees, and then bought the rights to the buying, until my father made himself small enough to fit on a form and drank himself the rest of the way down. That's the Combine. It doesn't beat you. It processes you.

Then Mac swung in.

He'd busted loose from the Banana Plantation work-farm — sixty-eight days of hauling 'nanas, he said — transferred to our ward for evaluation, and the first thing he did was crack his knuckles and hoot at the glass. A brawny macaque, red-faced and grinning like a snapped branch. He took one look at Nanny Ratched, the Big Nurse, a gorilla the color of wet slate who never raised her voice because she never had to, and licked a finger and held it up.

"Betting a week," he announced, "before I have her going ape."

Every morning we had Grooming Circle, Nanny Ratched's favorite circus. She'd open a monkey's file and read his shame out loud until the whole troop picked at him instead of picking at each other — a regular pecking party with fur flying everywhere. When she cornered Harding, a fussy chimp with a chest like a defeated hen and hands too pretty to admit to, into stammering himself smaller, Mac had had enough.

"Why don't you all stop acting like a bunch of scared little marmosets?" he hollered. "She's one gorilla! What is she, fifty of you? Sixty? It's the pecking party, don't you get it? The flock gets scared and starts pecking the rabbit with the torn ear — and the whole time, the rabbit's asking for it, right? You know who never gets pecked? The wolf. And you know why? Because nobody ever heard of a rare wolf. This world belongs to the strong, mon."

He bet us he could lift the feeding-station control panel, the steel console that portioned our mash and locked the gates besides. He spat on his palms, wrapped both arms around it, and hauled until the veins stood out on his neck like jungle lianas. It didn't budge an inch. But he stood there grinning anyway.

"But I tried, though," he said. "Bananas and gravy, I tried as hard as I ever tried. At least I did that."

And damned if the fog didn't lift a little. Monkeys who hadn't laughed since the wet season were snickering into their mash. Sefelt and Fredrickson, the two epileptic capuchins who traded seizures the way other apes traded fruit — Sefelt's medicine made his gums bleed and Fredrickson took half of it and half the fits — were rolling on the floor. When Mac called a vote to watch the Banana Bowl on the ward television, and Nanny Ratched counted only six hands, the big galoot sat in front of the dark screen and called the play-by-play himself — "He's winding up, folks, it's a long fly ball to deep center field!" — until forty monkeys were roaring, and her slate face went nearly purple with the effort of staying slate.

The next week he ran the vote again, and this time twenty hands went up, and the Big Nurse said the meeting had already been adjourned, and voting was closed. Mac looked at her, looked at the glass front of the Nurses' Station, and put his fist through it like it was a wet leaf. Then, while the aides came running, he reached through and did it again with the other hand, calm as you please, saying he was just airing the place out.

When the fog machines pumped it too thick for me, I used to go deep and hide in it — time inside the fog doesn't run, it floats, and a monkey could drift clear of the whole Combine in there. But Mac had a way of reaching into the mist and grabbing my arm like he knew right where I'd be. "Chief," he'd say, "you ain't as big as they told you, and you sure ain't as small as you're pretending. Which is it going to be?" And the fog would thin around his voice, and I'd stay on the surface another day.

He got bolder. He bribed a keeper and smuggled a dozen Acutes out on a fishing trip beyond the fence, and those sad old monkeys came back with their fur full of sun. He found out I could see through his fog and started asking my opinion like it counted for something, which it hadn't since my father's river. Then he threw one last party, with two wild macaques, Candy and Sandy, climbing in over the wall, and juice that was rather more fermented than the label promised. By morning the ward looked like a barrel of monkeys that had actually been shaken, and there was Bibbet, the shy young chimp who stammered, sitting up in the nesting box with Candy, grinning like life had finally handed him a ripe one.

Nanny Ratched arrived with the fog. She fixed Bibbet with her little eyes. "What would your mother say, Billy?"

The grin dropped off him like a peeled rind. He crawled into the nesting box and pulled the leaves over his head, and nothing anybody said would bring him out. Mac went for her then — not to hurt her, I think, just to tear that calm mask off her face once and for all — and the white suits swarmed him under the fog.

They took him upstairs twice. The second time was for the shock machine, after he flattened an aide who was pushing around George, a fastidious old baboon who could not bear an unclean anything. They strapped Mac to the table and conducted him up.

"Anointest my head with conductant," he said to the ceiling, rolling his eyes like a camp-meeting preacher. "Do I get a crown of thorns?"

They cooked him and put him back, fogged to the eyebrows, and he came off the table swearing the machine was the monkey and the rest of us were the treatment. Monkeys on the ward started asking to be shocked just to hear what he'd say next.

But upstairs had worse than electricity in it. When he came back the last time, weeks later, he was flat-eyed and friendly as a pet marmoset, sitting in a corner with his hands folded.

I went to him and put my huge arms around him and held him a long time, and told him — first words I'd said out loud in years — that he'd made us big again, every last one of us, and that whatever they'd taken out of him up there, it wasn't his to carry anymore. He didn't say anything. He was already gone somewhere I couldn't follow.

But whatever it was in him, it had already swung away. It was in the rest of us now.

I put my huge arms around that control panel, remembering how he'd tried, and I lifted. This time it came up, roots and all. I heaved it through the feed-room window, glass and steel out into the dawn, and then I went out after it, over the fence line and into the trees, running toward the wild dark canopy the way Mac had talked about it — the geese going north, the logs rushing the river, everything big and moving and free.

One swung over the cuckoo's nest. And that's the truth, even if it didn't happen.
