# New Baboon

*A parody of "New Moon"*

It was Bella Swing's eighteenth birthday, and she had one wish: that nobody make a fuss. Naturally, the Culianas threw a party.

It was a lovely party, right up until the wrapping paper. Bella opened Alice's gift, ran her finger along a fig-leaf ribbon, and got a thorn-prick. One drop of blood. That's all it took.

Jasper — ninety years of sap-drinking discipline — turned toward her like a compass needle. Edward slammed his brother into a piano. The party ended with Bella wearing more bandages than bows, and Edward quieter than a dropped banana.

Days later, he walked her into the deep canopy and said the unsayable. "My family is leaving. This is the last time you'll ever see me. I don't want you."

"You don't mean that."

"Bella, I don't want you." His eyes were flat and cold as river stones. "It'll be as if I never existed."

And he was gone, and the jungle went with him. The color drained out of everything. Even the bananas tasted like nothing, which for a monkey is the truest sign of despair.

October.

November.

December.

The narrative itself gave up, one bare branch of a page at a time, because nothing happened to Bella Swing and nothing ever would again. She sat by her window and became a hole in the canopy.

Then her father, hoping to shake her loose, sent her to see an old friend in the lowland scrub — Jacob Brackle, easy-grinning, with a smile you could hang a hammock on. Jacob fixed up two dead vines and taught her to swing the long dangerous ones, the ones that dropped you into free-fall for one screaming second.

And in that one screaming second, she heard Edward's voice.

"Keep going, you idiot," it said, disapproving and beautiful.

She became a connoisseur of adrenaline: the taller the tree, the looser the vine, the louder the hallucination. Jacob grew up beside her, sprouted shoulders, and became the "new baboon" nature had sent to fill the vacuum. Nature abhors a vacuum, and stuffs it with baboon.

For a while, it almost worked. Jacob talked too much, grinned too easily, and treated her broken heart the way you treat a splinter — steady hands, no commentary, which is more than most doctors manage. He told her about the scrub, about his troop's legends, about his sisters; he called her "Bell" when she was being difficult, which was often, and waited out the bad afternoons the way the sea waits out weather. Charlie, watching from the porch with tactical silence, began leaving two baskets of figs out instead of one.

"Admit it," Jacob said one evening, tightening a splice in the second dead vine. "You're having almost half as much fun as you look like you're having."

"Close enough," said Bella — and heard it again, faint and cold, from somewhere just past the free-fall: *keep going, you idiot.* She gripped the vine harder.

In the spring, Bella went alone to the old meadow, to be miserable in the exact spot where Edward had glittered at her. She was not as alone as she believed. Laurent stepped out of the ferns — the polite one from the coconut-ball afternoon — gaunt now, and frank.

"James's mate is still hunting you," he said, almost kindly. "Victoria wants it slow, and I am under orders. Look at it this way: I'll be quicker than what she sends."

He was still explaining the mercy of it when the treeline stood up. Five enormous shapes — red-faced, blue-nosed, gold-maned, each the size of a small gorilla — hit Laurent like a landslide hitting a lizard, and what was left of Laurent was not enough to bury. The biggest one looked at Bella across the meadow with Jacob's eyes.

Which is how she finally got the truth. Jacob was a mandrill — or the mandrill was Jacob; the troop was divided on the grammar.

"We prefer shape-shifters," said the great beast, in Jacob's voice. "Or, you know, mandrills. Sam Ooley's whole troop carries it. We exist to protect the scrub from cold ones, and we're built for it — our running is so hot the vampire monkeys can't track it. And Bella — the Culianas didn't leave because Edward stopped caring. He left because of the birthday. He thinks he's the dangerous thing in your life. I'm telling you it's him."

"Also," said Emily, at the troop's kitchen, ladling muffin batter with scarred fingers while enormous wolfhounds-of-monkeys waited politely, "welcome to the troop, which eats more than the treaty allows. One of us imprinted on a grub once. We don't talk about it."

Then Alice Culiana came flying back into Fronds, leaves scattering, eyes wide. "You jumped off a gorge fig! I saw it! Rosalie told Edward you were—"

Bella's stomach dropped faster than any vine. Because Edward, misinformed, heartbroken, had gone to Italy — to Voleria, city of the Verveturi, the ancient royal coven of vampire monkeys — intending to stroll into the noon sun at the Festival of the Banished Monkey and sparkle himself to death in front of ten thousand witnesses. The Verveturi do not tolerate exposure. They enforce it with finality.

Bella and Alice ran the treetop highway like a commute. They reached Voleria at high noon, the plaza packed with banana-yellow cloaks, and there was Edward, striding toward the sun-drenched fountain, fingers unbuttoning his collar.

"Stop!" Bella slammed into him, arms around his cold chest, tumbling into the shadow of the fountain. "I'm alive! You cannot sparkle at these people!"

"Bella?" His voice broke. "You're — ow, you're crushing me, but you're here."

"That's what happens when you try to guilt-suicide-by-glitter, you idiot. I came halfway around the jungle. I'm alive. Ripe, even."

The Verveturi dragged them into the vaults anyway: Aro of the mind-reading paws, sour Cage-us, and poor bored Marmoset. Little Jane, tiniest and most terrifying, tried her stinging-nettle stare on Bella and found it didn't take, which offended her more than the intrusion had.

"The girl knows too much," said Aro pleasantly. "She joins us, or she dies. Those are the fun options."

"Then I'll change her myself," Edward said, with the enthusiasm of a monkey volunteering for a hornet's nest.

Back home, the matter was put to the whole family, one raised hand at a time, because the Culianas settled existential questions the way other troops settle pizza toppings. Alice: yes, the leaves were very clear. Jasper: yes, as a professional courtesy. Emmett: yes, and about time. Carlisle: yes, gently, whenever she chose. Rosalie voted no with a look of sincere condolence, as one who knows exactly what is being given away. Edward voted no twice, and was overruled by his own girlfriend.

"Graduation," Bella said, "then transformation. That's the deal."

"Then marry me first," Edward said, and knelt in the moonlit canopy with a ring carved of white fig-wood.

Bella stared at it in genuine horror. "Marriage," she said. "You people have watched ninety years of this jungle and you still believe in it. I'm seventeen. I would rather be bitten."

"I know," said Edward, radiant. "It's my favorite thing about you."

Above them, in the dark trees, a huge mandrill watched with hurting eyes — because every love triangle, in the end, is just three monkeys in one tree, each holding on with everything they've got.

"Jacob, wait—" Bella called.

Too late. The new baboon swung away into the night, and the jungle hummed with the sound of one heart breaking and another, cold as river stones, learning to beat again.
