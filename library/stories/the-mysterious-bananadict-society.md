# The Mysterious Bananadict Society

*A parody of "The Mysterious Benedict Society"*

The advertisement appeared one Tuesday in the Stone-Fruit Town Gazette, in bold print:

**ARE YOU A GIFTED YOUNG MONKEY LOOKING FOR SPECIAL OPPORTUNITIES?**

Reynie Macaque read it over his breakfast fig and felt, for the first time in his short serious life, that an advertisement was talking directly to him. Reynie was an orphan rhesus with a patient face and a mind that unsolved puzzles the way other monkeys unpeeled bananas. His tutor, the kind Miss Perumal, read it too, and raised one elegant langur eyebrow.

"Special opportunities," she said. "It's the *looking for* that worries me. Still — you could use a challenge."

So Reynie sat the test.

The testing house was tall, gray, and eccentric, and so was everyone in it. The written test was full of impossible questions, such as: "Are you brave? (True or False)," and "Which of these is not like the others, and why did it try?", and one entire page that simply said: TRY AGAIN. Reynie noticed the other candidates furiously copying, lying, and guessing, and noticed, also, that a wrong honest answer seemed to please the examiners more than a right stolen one. On True or False he wrote: *Both, depending on what's behind the door.* Then, in the waiting room mirror-maze, he noticed the mirrors were angled wrong on purpose, and stepped calmly through the wrong way round, arriving behind the examiners instead of in front of them.

He was not, it turned out, alone in oddness.

There was Sticky, a spectacled langur who had run away from parents who sold his brain to quiz shows, and who remembered everything he ever read and polished his glasses whenever he was afraid, which was nearly always. There was Kate Capuchin, a wiry acrobat with a steel bucket on a rope slung over her shoulder, containing, at various times: rope, a grappling hook, marbles, a saw, a mango (emergency food), and a sense of invincibility. "Rule one," said Kate, "never be without your bucket." And there was Constance — a cotton-top tamarin roughly the size of a large emotion, who communicated almost entirely in insults.

"You're all going to fail," Constance told them pleasantly. "Except me."

"You failed every question," said Sticky.

"Yes," said Constance, "correctly."

All four passed.

They were brought before Mr. Bananadict: a small whiskered bonobo in green plaid slippers, with a laugh like a kettle and a condition that when he felt any emotion too strongly — delight most of all — he fell fast asleep. He had already fallen asleep twice upon meeting them.

"He's delighted," explained Rhonda Kazembe, his brisk colobus assistant, thumping him gently awake. "Now, children: the truth. And the truth is a thing called the Emergency."

The Emergency was this: for a year, the whole jungle had been frightened out of its fur. The Jungle-Vision broadcasts, which played in every tree in every town, had begun whispering of catastrophe — rivers drying, figs failing, distant troops arming, plagues in the wind. There was no evidence for any of it. There was only the whisper, again and again, until even sensible monkeys darted glances at the shadows and told their children not to dream too big.

"The broadcast has one source," said Mr. Bananadict, sitting up, eyes suddenly sharp, "an island in the Old Mango River, and a school called the Institute for the Very Enlightened. Its headmaster is my brother, Mr. Drooptha Curtain. And the machine under that school — the Whisperer — can put thoughts softly into a mind, and quietly sweep memories out. If Curtain's plans ripen, no monkey in this jungle will be able to think a private thought again. I cannot infiltrate his school. I am, as you may have noticed, a whiskered narcoleptic bonobo, and he knows my face."

"You want us," said Reynie slowly, "to be his students."

"I want you to be his students, his prize students, and his undoing. You four are the oddest, cleverest little monkeys in the land. That is precisely why he will want you." He beamed at them, and fell asleep again.

Number Two, a red-haired uakari who ran on three hours of sleep and a crate of lemons, woke him, issued the children false names, and sent them down the Old Mango River by night.

The Institute was white, symmetrical, and wrong. The students marched to the dining hall in silence and hung on Mr. Curtain's every word, for Mr. Curtain — a bald-crowned baboon who rolled about in a motorized locomobile and could turn from grandfather to executioner between one sentence and the next — rewarded loyalty with rank, colored sashes, and extra dessert, and punished doubt with a trip to the Waiting Room, from which monkeys returned quiet and blank-eyed and strangely polite.

"I've mapped the tunnels under the gym," Sticky whispered one night, glasses fogging. "There's a machine down there. It hums. And every evening, students sit in chairs and put their heads inside helmets. They call it Improvement."

"They call it being Improved," said Kate grimly. "S.Q. says it makes everything easier."

S.Q. Pedalian was a huge, kindhearted gorilla lad, Curtain's adopted son, who adored his dreadful father and could not for the life of him understand why kindness kept leaking out of him. He sneaked the children extra rolls and asked them, wistfully, if they thought a monkey could be good at a bad thing, or bad at a good one.

Curtain, of course, discovered the four prodigies at once and gathered them close — too close. He was cleverer than his brother; even Reynie felt the pull of his attention like a warm current. But they held on to each other, and to the memory of the whisper, and on the night of the great Broadcast — the night Curtain meant to turn the Whisperer up and pour his calming, emptying voice into every sleeping skull in the jungle — they went down into the tunnels.

"Ten Apes on the stairs," Kate reported, upside down outside a vent. Ten Apes: Curtain's executives, perfectly dressed monkeys with briefcases full of tricks — smoke bombs, spring-loaded ladders, piano wire. They had once been ordinary businessmen; the Whisperer had swept them clean and hung them like suits in a closet. "Guarding the machine room. Also, a trapdoor. Also — I can get us past all of it. Bucket."

They got past all of it.

The Whisperer filled a cavern of its own — a throne of wires and helmets and glass, humming like a nest of dreaming wasps. And Mr. Curtain sat at its heart in his locomobile, and when the four children appeared before him, he did not rage. He smiled, because he had known for weeks, and had wanted them exactly there.

"Little geniuses," he said. "Do you know what loneliness is? It is a jungle with no other voices in it. I will fix loneliness forever. In one hour every monkey will be calm, and safe, and part of one great quiet thought. Mine."

"That's not a troop," said Reynie. "That's a cage."

"It's a family," said Curtain, softly. And there was something so hollow and hungry in the word that even Sticky stopped polishing his glasses.

The broadcast began. The helmets lit. Somewhere above them, the jungle's ten thousand Jungle-Vision vines carried the hum out into the night — and Constance, tiny furious Constance, marched to the foot of the machine, planted her feet, looked the Whisperer in its humming heart, and screamed.

Not with her lungs. With her mind.

It was a scream pitched exactly at the frequency of *NO* — every no Constance had ever issued, concentrated into one pure refusal. The Whisperer's hum climbed, wavered, cracked. Glass ran like water. Helmet after helmet spat sparks. The great machine, built to sweep minds clean, met the one mind in the jungle that could not be swept, and surrendered.

The Ten Apes on the stairs dropped their briefcases and stood blinking like monkeys waking from a long nap on a train. S.Q., at the door, took one look at his father's face — the grandfather mask and the executioner mask gone, leaving only a frightened bald baboon — and stepped, gently, out of his way. Curtain roared, slammed his locomobile to full, and rattled off through the tunnels toward the river, Mr. Bananadict (arriving, unhappily lucid) in pursuit.

They never caught him. But by morning the broadcast was silent, the helmets were junk, and all across the jungle, monkeys woke up grumpy, opinionated, and gloriously unimproved — which is the natural condition of monkeys, and the healthiest one.

Mr. Bananadict adopted all four of them. (He fell asleep for an hour and a half when the judge said "family.") The Society kept meeting in the tall gray house, because Rhonda Kazembe, arriving one evening with lemons and maps, observed that a mind like Curtain's does not rattle off a cliff merely because the locomobile did; somewhere out there the jungle still had an Emergency or two left in it.

"Then we'd better stay odd," said Kate, feet on the table, bucket within reach.

"Yes," said Constance. "No. Yes."

And Reynie, who had begun the whole affair with an advertisement and a fig, thought that for a bunch of peculiar little monkeys, they had found the one thing no Whisperer could ever sweep away.

Each other.
