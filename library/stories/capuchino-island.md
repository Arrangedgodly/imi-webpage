# Capuchino Island

*A parody of "Camino Island"*

The five masked monkeys came over the Firegrove Library wall at Primeton at three in the morning. They dropped down the elevator shaft on braided vines, stood in the dark of the special collections floor for eleven minutes while the youngest of them hyperventilated into his mask, and then went to work on the vault door — rated against gorillas, and defeated, after a fashion, by patience and a borrowed maintenance code. They came out with the only five bananuscripts of F. Scoot Fitzgibbon in captivity: *This Side of the Canopy*, *The Beautiful and Danged*, *Tender Is the Fig*, *The Last Top Banana*, and — crown of the collection — *The Great Gatsbanana*, in its original banana-leaf folder. The youngest monkey carried the crown, all the way up the shaft, with his eyes shut.

The job had been put together by a gorilla called Denny, who had planned it in prison, rehearsed it in his mother's garage, and paid his crew in promises, which are legal tender in his jungle. The plan said nothing about what to do next, which, Denny's crew agreed afterward, had been the plan's one flaw. You cannot sell the only five of anything. Every fence on three continents knew what they were the way every monkey knows a ripe fig: by the smell.

By breakfast the Feebs (formally the Federal Bureau of Investigation, but nobody had called them that in years) had the campus cordoned off and the librarians sedated. The insurers at Northeast Bananal Mutual had a quieter problem: manuscripts, unlike monkeys, are hard to fence.

The trail stayed cold for a year. Then it thawed, in the way of trails, on Capuchino Island — a sandy, coffee-scented spit of jungle where the capuchins have grown beans under the canopy for generations and gossip at the rate of three cups a morning. On its main street stood Bayleaf Books, staffed by Noe, who kept the rare room and spoke in whispers because the books slept, and Myra, who ran the register and the world; and behind the register reigned its owner: Bruce Cable, linen shirt, distinguished grey stubble, a monkey who sold first-smelling editions to rich tourists and first editions to richer ones, and who flirted with visiting novelists the way other monkeys breathe.

Into his orbit the insurers dropped Mercer Monn: thirty-ish, a novelist with one dead book, a live debt, and a buried past — in grad school she had lifted a signed first-smelling edition from her own department, and only the insurance lady knew it.

"Rent the cottage next to his store," the insurance lady said. "Write your novel. Keep your eyes open. Twenty-five thousand banana notes a month — and remember, you're just a writer who likes coffee."

On Capuchino Island, that was easy to believe. The whole island ran on coffee and gossip, generally in the same cup. Mercer wrote mornings, kayaked afternoons, drank cappuccinos with actual latte art (little foam monkeys, whiskers intact), and in the evenings drank wine with Bruce Cable, who talked about books the way other monkeys talk about football and meant every third word.

At his Thursday salons she watched him hold court for five visiting novelists and a poet asleep in the hammock, and once, when someone asked why first editions mattered, she got the full sermon. "It's the smell," Bruce said, closing his eyes. "1925 fig pulp, a hundred years slow in the making. You cannot counterfeit a smell. You can counterfeit provenance, boards, endpapers, the signature — the smell is the author's last honest opinion." Then he looked straight at Mercer and added, "Everything else on this island is counterfeit, of course. The charm included. I keep that for the weekends."

She also snooped, of course, in a tasteful, novelist-ish way, and reported on Tuesdays to a prepaid phone the insurance lady had sold her along with the cottage.

"Find anything?" the insurance lady asked, every week, in the same tone she probably used for dental appointments.

"He took me through the stock," Mercer reported once. "He handles a genuine page of *Gatsbanana* — just one loose leaf, a dealer's page — with cotton gloves and a lecture. He calls it 'the green light at the end of the vault.' He wants the whole thing, Elaine. He wants it the way a monkey wants a banana some other monkey is holding."

"That's motive, not evidence," said the insurance lady. "Buy him another bottle."

There was the locked room behind the shop, which Noe guarded like a reliquary. There was T-Bone, Bruce's capybara-sized factotum, who moved boxes by day and, on request, other monkeys by night. There was Bruce's odd trip to the mainland, unexplained, in February. There was a fence called Vin who surfaced, moved something heavy in a golf bag, and was pinced by the Feebs in a parking garage with a breath mint and no lawyer. And there was Denny, who had begun calling the island's only bookstore and asking for Bruce by name, in the tone of a monkey entitled to money.

"You know what I think?" Bruce said one dusk, handing her a mug. "I think the Fitzgibbon papers came through this island. I'd give a great deal to smell them — just smell them — before they vanish into some banker's vault forever."

"Would you," said Mercer, "buy them?"

Bruce sipped. "I'd negotiate," he said. "There's a difference, and it's worth a fortune to me. Nobody has ever proved I bought so much as a stolen napkin. Negotiation, on the other hand, is just conversation with insurance."

That, Mercer wrote in her notebook that night, was either an alibi or a confession, and she could not decide which, and — this was the professional problem — she had stopped wanting it to be a confession. She was three chapters from the end of her own book and four glasses past impartial.

The sting went off at Bruce's own party — the annual Fitzgibbon for Fig Growers gala, drinks on the deck, stars out, insurance investigators in the hedges. While Bruce poured, the Feebs opened the locked room, and there they were: five folders, banana-leaf and legend, worth more than the island. Behind them stood T-Bone with the golf bag, looking resigned, the look of a bodyguard who has worked for authors before.

The deal was quiet, because everybody's lawyer advised everybody that a trial would only raise the second-hand prices. Denny drew eleven years for his trouble; the vault at Firegrove got a second door, rated against gorillas with doctorates. Bruce gave up the papers, the golf bag, and every name he had ever heard — Vin, Denny, two couriers, and a lawyer in Antwerp he described as "a disgrace to the profession, and I mean that professionally" — and kept his shop and his sunsets. The insurers got their treasure back; the university got a tax write-off, which mollified it completely.

Mercer's cover did not survive the week — Bruce had known for some time, since he owned the cottage she was renting, and the walls of a bookseller remember everything.

"You wrote a novel under my nose," he said, "and I sold you the coffee you wrote it on. I'd call us even."

"Almost even," said Mercer. "You let me fall for the charm. The counterfeit charm. Weekends included."

"I never once lied to you," said Bruce. "I only let the island do it, and the island has tenure."

Her book came out the next year: *The Banana Republic*, a comedy about a heist, by an author who had, she insisted, made the whole thing up. Bruce stocked it at the front register, face-out, and sold it at full price.

"Because that," he told his customers, "is what friends do."
