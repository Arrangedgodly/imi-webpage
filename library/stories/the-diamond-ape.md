# The Diamond Ape

*A parody of "The Diamond Age"*

In the Leased Territories of New Atlanta Banyan, where the great Phyles jostled for the future like monkeys at one fig tree, there lived a small macaque girl named Nell, her brother Harv, and their mother's succession of boyfriends, none of whom stayed long and one of whom threw things.

Nell owned exactly one book, and Harv had stolen it for her, which was the single most important fact of her life.

It was called *The Young Monkey's Illustrated Primer*, and it was not a book the way other books are books. Its pages were living leaves; its pictures moved; and — this was the thing — it *read you back*. It knew when Nell was hungry, when she was lying, when she was scared of the dark landing outside their nest. It told her a story about a monkey named Princess Nell who lived in a castle among four great branches, held by a great gray Djinn of the Smoke, and every night the Primer left the Princess at the edge of some danger, and every morning the story waited for Nell — the real Nell, the small hungry one with split knuckles — to solve her own life before the Princess could solve hers.

Every Primer has a voice. Somewhere in the racting-houses of the Territories, a performer named Miranda — a bonobo with a warm, low voice and no children of her own — had taken the contract to voice one little thete girl's book, sight unseen, as fill-in work. By the second month she had refused all other roles. By the second year she had spent every coin she earned keeping the connection alive, and would have spent her fur. She had never met Nell. She was, in every way that counted, Nell's mother, and both of them knew it, one leaf at a time.

The Primer was supposed to exist exactly once. It had been designed that way.

John Hackwort, engineer of the Veekies — the neo-Victorian phyle of New Atlanta Banyan, gorillas in waistcoats, believers in standards — had designed it, for his own little daughter Fiona, and then, unable to afford a thing he had largely built, done a stupid, human, monkey thing: he had gone to Dr. Xi, the ancient golden snub-nosed sage of the Celestial Kingdom, to commission a secret, illegal copy. And Dr. Xi, who traded in favors the way rivers trade in water, had demanded his own price: the copy would go where Dr. Xi wished. It went, by ways nobody has ever fully traced, to a thief in the Leased Territories, who sold it for food money, and it ended by theft and accident in the hands of Nell.

Which is how Judge Fang came into the story.

Judge Fang was a mandrill, and if you have never stood before a mandrill magistrate in full facial color — the blue and scarlet of authority blazing across his muzzle like a legal notice — you cannot understand the entire Celestial Kingdom's respect for the phrase *the Judge looked at me*. He took the case of the stolen Primer less as theft than as omen: a book that could raise a child was abroad in the Territories, the Vines that fed the old order were creaking, and the Judge, who was old and honest, suspected he was watching an age end without being able to say so in a verdict.

For Engineer Hackwort, the reckoning was swifter. Caught between his phyle's fury and Dr. Xi's silken hooks, he accepted his punishment from the Veekies' tribunal — ten years' service and the humiliation of his house — and then, because Dr. Xi held the other half of his leash, a worse bargain underneath: the Primer's source had a flaw, a knot in its deepest logic, and Dr. Xi wished it found. To find it, Hackwort would have to go among the Hummers.

The Hummers were a cult of the deep forest who had discovered that if enough monkeys drummed on hollow trunks in perfect rhythm all night, every night, their separate minds began to share one beat — and that a thought could be carried in that beat, held for one mind at the far end. It was the slowest computer ever built, and it only ran one program at a time, and Hackwort, mourning his daughter Fiona from a distance of ten years and a thousand drums, gave them his flaw to carry. He drummed. The forest drummed. Seasons went by like weather. The Hummers' rhythm carried the Primer's knot down its endless wooden nerves, one beat at a time, searching for the loose strand — and Hackwort's fur went slowly gray at the temples, and then everywhere, and his fingertips hardened like bark. When at last he walked out of the forest, those who saw him said he glittered faintly in the lantern light, as though something crystalline had gotten into him. They called him after that, half in jest and later not in jest at all, the Diamond Ape — the monkey who had paid for a thought with ten years of himself.

By then, Nell was nearly grown, and the world was coming apart in the way worlds do, tidily at first.

The Coastal Republic, which fed its orphan girl-monkeys in state crèches and raised them on cheap pirate copies of the Primer — ten thousand marmosets, the littlest monkeys, with the littlest books, drilled by their leafy pages in locks, tactics, loyalty, and the story of Princess Nell — had begun, for economic reasons too dull for anything but catastrophe, to sell the crèches' contracts. The marmosets objected. The marmosets had been raised on a story in which the Princess fights for her sisters. Ten thousand of them marched on the Source of the Vine itself — the great taproot-tower through which all the Phyles' matter and fruit flowed, the Vine that made New Atlanta Banyan possible — and the Phyles sent soldiers, because that is what Phyles are *for*.

Nell went to the Source too. Not to march: to finish the story. Miranda's voice was waiting at the far end of the Primer's last leaf, as it had been all her life, and so was Harv, back from the bad years, and so — glittering, gray, patient beyond reason — was Engineer Hackwort with the answer to the knot, and Judge Fang's last loyal clerk with the dead mandrill's seal, because Judge Fang had died as he lived, asking the right question too late to be answered by anyone but a girl.

At the gates of the Source, the marmosets' ten thousand Primers all spoke at once, and they all spoke with Miranda's voice, because the cheap copies had copied her too, and when the little monkeys heard the real Princess Nell ask them to hold, they held. Not one marmoset was harmed, which no soldier on either side could afterward explain. The Vine was not destroyed. It was *released* — seeded, at the old scarred gorilla's hands, with the knot's answer: the Seed, the self-planting code that would grow matter as a tree grows fruit, no tower, no taproot, no permission — a future in which every grove fed itself.

The Veekies called it catastrophe. Judge Fang's clerk called it the Mandate of Heaven changing hands. Miranda called it the moment she finally, finally held her daughter.

Because that is what happened, in the middle of the crowd, while the soldiers lowered their weapons and the marmosets sat down in the road: a bonobo performer pushed through ten thousand small monkeys who all had her voice, and found the one who had her heart, and Nell — who had been raised by a book, and through the book by a stranger, and through the stranger by love — climbed into her arms as though into the branch she had been swinging for all her life.

Fiona Hackwort got her Primer at last, her father's apology bound in living leaves, on the day the Phyles' schools quietly began teaching what he had gone crystalline to learn.

And in the nursery-vines of the new groves, the old classification was already being rewritten by monkeys who could not remember the Vine, and it read like this: *the Age of Nuts, the Age of Figs, the Age of the Vine — and now, because one small stolen book was read kindly, the Age of the Seed.*
