# The Primate of Miss Jean Brodie

*A parody of "The Prime of Miss Jean Brodie"*

The Marcia Blaine School for She-Monkeys stood at the edge of Edinbranch, where the old pines hang their grayest needles. The girls walked in file to their lessons, in their black coats and their black berets; and of them all, six walked apart. These were the Brodie set: Sandy Stranger, with small eyes that stared; Jenny, the pretty one; Rose, famous for instinct; Eunice, who could swim the loch like an otter; Monica, whose fur stood up with sudden anger; and Mary Macgregor, who was blamed for everything.

"We are the crème de la crème," said Miss Brodie, "of the cocoa-nut."

Miss Jean Brodie was in her primate. "Give me a girl at an impressionable age," she said, "and she is mine for the whole of her life."

She taught them no dates and no sums. She taught them the shapes of the old grove, and the fragrance of rose-paw-paw, and the story of her dead fiancé, Hugh, lost on the last day of the Great Troop War, who had, variously, died standing, died smiling, and once died clutching a letter. Hugh improved with the years.

"Art," said Miss Brodie one winter's afternoon, "is the chief glory of the jungle. The second glory is discipline. The great Baboon of Rome has made the vines run on time — and there is a lesson in that, Rose, though you are not equipped to take it, being made entirely of instinct."

The girls adored her. In the dormitory they played their famous game of famous heroines: Miss Brodie taken hostage by a rogue orangutan; Miss Brodie alone on a burning bough; Miss Brodie torn between two loves and choosing the higher bough. Jenny always played Miss Brodie; Sandy always played the rogue orangutan. The set walked home beneath the old low branches, at dusk, feeling elected.

The trouble with the male sex began, as it often does, with two. There was Mr. Teddy Lloyd, the gibbose art master — married, Catholic, and therefore fascinating — who painted the girls of the set, one after another; and every portrait, curiously, came out with Miss Brodie's golden eyes.

"He always paints you, miss," said Sandy, staring.

"I am aware," said Miss Brodie, "that I am the true subject of that studio."

And there was Mr. Lowther, the music master, who sang of the lochs and lived on a bare branch; Miss Brodie undertook his feeding and improvement, with paw-paw marmalade and firm opinions.

"But Sandy here," Miss Brodie told the staff room at large, "has insight, though what she will do with it, I dread to think."

Miss Mackay, the headmistress, had her own view of the matter. "There are those who would like me to teach the sewing of fig-leaves," said Miss Brodie, "instead of the cultivation of the soul. But I am in my primate, girls, and the Mackay creature will never have me." And to Miss Mackay's traps — interviews, questionnaires, offers of transfer to a modern bough — the set returned blank faces and quotes about the old grove. Miss Brodie was unassailable, because her girls would not inform, and her girls would not inform because they were in love with being elected.

It was Sandy who began to see it: the way Miss Brodie's romance with the art master ran on proxy rails; the way Mary Macgregor, blamed for everything, had begun to believe it. Sandy grew coldly interested, the way one is interested in a vine that has quietly begun to strangle its host tree. She took, of all things, to religion — long cold sits with the Brothers of the Reformed Bough — because the Brothers at least said out loud what things were sins, and left them sins.

"She thinks she is Providence," Sandy told Jenny. "She thinks she is God on a good branch."

And Providence, one spring, laid its plan bare in the art room. "Rose has the instinct," said Miss Brodie, watching Rose at the easel, with Teddy Lloyd behind her. "You, Sandy, have the insight. Mr. Lloyd is a man of enormous passion. If I were forty — but I am in my primate, which is a different country. Do you see, Sandy? Rose shall be the lover, you shall be the soul, and I — I shall be the inspiration. It is all arranged, in the only arrangement that counts: mine."

"She is arranging the whole troop like a fig-tree," Sandy told Jenny afterward, "and calling it the weather."

Then the small deaths came in, as they do. Joyce Emily Hammond, a silly dotard of a girl whom Miss Brodie had encouraged past all sense, ran off to fight for the old gray cause in the Espáña groves, because Miss Brodie had said the vines there ran on time; and was lost in the stampede at the terminus before she ever saw a vine. And Mary Macgregor went away young, lost in the fire at the great lodging-tree in the city — Mary, who had been blamed for everything, and who at the very last, the set learned, had died running back toward the door she had been told was hers. For a long time the set could not speak of either.

Then, in her last year at the school, Sandy climbed the stair to the headmistress's hollow, and knocked.

"I have come," said Sandy, "to tell you that Miss Jean Brodie is a danger to the young. She has taken girls into her primate, and one of them, at least, is on the wrong branch for life."

"It was only a matter of time," said Miss Mackay, "until one of her own set came to me."

"It was not informing," said Sandy, staring with her small eyes. "It was insight."

And she gave Miss Mackay, at last, the one thing that could bring Miss Brodie down: the primate herself — the Baboon of Rome, the vines running on time, the girl sent to Espáña. Politics, where the heart could not reach.

Miss Brodie lost her post that summer. She never proved, all her fading years, which of her set had betrayed her — though her golden stare fixed, at the end, on the quiet little monkey with the staring eyes, and the two of them looked at each other across the shabby parlor like two duelists who had long ago stopped needing swords.

The set scattered, as sets do. Rose, famous for instinct, made a famously unwise marriage and a famously wise second one. Eunice nursed the bitten and the fallen in the war of the groves. Jenny went on the boards at the Assembly Boughs. Monica, fur still standing, raised six kits and out-argued them all. Sandy became, of all fates, Sister Helena of the Transfiguration, and author of a strange small book on the psychology of the jungle soul, *The Transfiguration of the Ordinary Vine*, which the lowland critics could not understand at all. "For those who like that sort of thing," one admitted, "it is that sort of thing."

A young visitor once asked Sister Helena what the main influences of her school years had been.

"There was a Miss Jean Brodie," said Sister Helena, "in her prime."
