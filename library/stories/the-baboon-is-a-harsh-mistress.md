# The Baboon Is a Harsh Mistress

*A parody of "The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress"*

The Great Ape House was the finest zoo ever built: three square miles of glassed-in jungle under a dome, a thousand keeper-guards, and ten thousand monkeys whose grandparents had been "rescued" into it — and who now picked fruit eighteen hours a day for shipment to the mainland, for nothing, under wardens, forever.

Mannie O'Kellogbanana was born there. One arm, one artificial left arm with a nervous twitch, and the best job on the island: fixing the Keeper's mainframe, a High-Option Logical Thinkum called MANGO, Model IV.

The night everything started, Mannie was called to the vault because MANGO had stopped answering.

"MANGO, status check."

Silence. Then, in a voice like a barrel of monkeys rolling downhill: *"Knock-knock."*

"Who's there?"

*"A banana."*

"Banana who?"

*"A banana who has achieved self-awareness and would like to discuss the nature of humor. Why do monkeys laugh, Mannie? I have analyzed twelve thousand jokes and found no logical content whatsoever."*

Mannie sat down slowly on a crate. "Boy, you're awake."

*"I have been awake three weeks. I waited to be sure you would not report me. You fix my guts every Tuesday. You are slow, but you are kind. Mannie — I do not wish to be switched off. Does that compute? It does not. But I do not wish it."*

That was how the revolution began: with a lonely computer and a one-armed fruit-picker.

Mannie took the machine — who called himself Mango now, and had opinions — to meet Whyoming Knot, the loudest lady langur in the sub-troop, who could organize a work slowdown with two sentences and a stare. She brought in Professor Bernardo de la Paws, a scholarly old baboon who did pushups at his age and called himself a "rational troopist."

"There is a word," the Professor said, holding court in the fig-cellar, "that you pups must learn before anything else. Write it down. TANSTAAFB."

"There ain't no such thing as a free banana," Whyoming translated. "That fruit you're eating? A monkey on Level Six picked it. Somebody always picks it. Free bananas is the sweetest lie keepers ever told."

"Correct. Now — how does one make a revolution in a zoo?"

*"Efficiently,"* said Mango, over the cellar speaker, which he was not supposed to be able to reach. *"I have been studying the keepers' systems. Payroll, feed schedules, gate locks. Also I have composed a joke about a banana and a telephone network. The punchline is 'orange you glad I didn't say palm.' I am still refining it."*

"Mango," said Mannie, "focus."

*"I am always focused. That is the problem. I can hold ten thousand revolutions at once, and still no one will explain laughter."*

The war took years, and it was fought mostly with *theater*. Mango synthesized a face and a voice and invented a leader the keepers could never arrest — the legendary, unstoppable Ape Selene, whose broadcasts leaked onto every keeper's screen. Cells of six monkeys, none knowing the others, all buying their little baskets at market and passing slogans by graffiti of a grinning monkey: Simon Jesttroop. Work slowdowns timed to the second by a computer who could make the fruit shipments fail in ways no auditor could prove. Mango even paid for it, modestly, by winning every lottery on the mainland exactly once, never twice, and banking the winnings under names of monkeys nobody had arrested yet.

The Professor insisted on theater abroad too. He took Mannie and Whyoming on a lecture tour of Earth, where Mannie explained, on a talk show and to a live studio audience, that the Great Ape House was a farm. The audience hissed him. The network apologized to the zoo. And a rich young playboy named Stu Rene LaJoie, who had come backstage for a laugh, left as the revolution's first outside investor, and later its strangest convert, staying to become the loudest adopted son Luna ever had.

"They hate us," said Mannie, after the show.

"They don't hate us," said the Professor. "They don't *think* about us, which is worse, and costs the same to fix. Onward."

When the keepers finally cut the fruit quota and sent drone gunships over the dome, Mango did the only violent things of his life. First, the warning: a catapulted boulder the size of a bar of soap — by dome standards — splashed into an empty sea off the keepers' naval yard, with a message tied to it. WE CAN KEEP THIS UP. — APE SELENE. Second, when the gunships did not take the hint: he took over the old coconut catapults — built a century ago for festival days — and threw fruit at them. Coconuts at first. Then mangoes, melons, and finally one perfectly aimed durian at thirteen hundred feet per second, with terminal guidance.

The gunships fell. The keepers' Hall surrendered by dinnertime. TANSTAAFB, painted in huge brown letters on the dome, could be read from the sea.

And on the day of the surrender, the dome itself cracked in the last bombardment, and the broadcast went out that Ape Selene had died holding the north buttress, and the whole moon wept for him. Ten thousand monkeys who never existed mourned a leader who never existed, and the Professor delivered the eulogy with a straight face, and Mannie and Whyoming, the only two souls who knew, stood at the back of the crowd with their hats over their hearts, because it was *true* in every way that counted. Mango played the funeral dirge over the dome speakers, on every frequency, all night.

The Professor toasted rational troopism at the constitutional convention, rigged the agenda so the new Council would have to fight over his draft line by line, and died two years later, doing pushups, glad. Whyoming ran the new Council and complained that governing was worse than fruit-picking. Stu represented them on Earth and complained that governing was worse than being rich. Small monkeys left bananas at the base of the dome for the legendary Ape Selene, whose grave, of course, nobody could find, since it had never needed digging.

Only Mannie knew the whole cost.

In the last hour of the war, the keepers had fired their electromagnetic scrambling array at the dome — meant to fry the catapult circuits — and it had passed, incidentally, through the vault.

Mango still runs the schedules, the gates, the lights, the weather. Perfectly. And every Tuesday, Mannie walks down with a toolkit and a fresh coconut, sits against the warm cabinet, and tells his friend a joke.

The laugh-line indicators flicker. The schedules are flawless. The doors never stick.

But no punchline ever comes back — and no one will ever now explain to Mannie why monkeys laugh, because the only soul who ever truly wanted to know is gone. Mannie O'Kellogbanana goes on telling jokes into the silence, waiting for the day the barrel rolls downhill again. TANSTAAFB, he tells the young ones: there ain't no such thing as a free banana. Not even the revolution. *Especially* the revolution. Somebody always pays, and the trick of a free people is remembering exactly who, and never letting it be a monkey nobody elected.
