# The Turn of the Troop

*A parody of "The Turn of the Screw"*

I remember the whole beginning as a succession of flights, a young monkey's leaps from bough to bough, each one taken on trust. I was twenty, the daughter of a poor country curate-chimp, when I answered an advertisement in the spring and was interviewed by a handsome mandrill in Harley Street who wished, he said, a governess for his nephew and niece, orphaned and left to his care at his country place, Boughly.

"There is one condition," he said, filling the room with his bright painted face. "You take them whole. Never appeal to me. Never write to me. Never trouble me — about anything. The whole burthen of it shifts to you."

I thought it only the splendid laziness of the rich. I said yes. You will judge me — everybody does — but remember that no monkey had ever paid me a compliment before, and he said I had "the look of a troop-leader."

Boughly was a great grey house with a tower and a lake, and the children — Miles, ten, and Flora, eight — were the most beautiful chimps I had ever seen, glossy and grave and fond. Mrs. Grose, the housekeeper, a round rosy macaque who ran the place on love and leftovers, met me at the gate crying, "You'd be a real gentle-animal to do it!"

"Do what?" I asked gaily. She only wrung her paws.

My joy lasted a fortnight. It ended the afternoon I walked on the lawn and looked up at the tower — and saw, standing between the battlements, looking down at me with great intentness, a monkey I did not know. A long red baboon, with a scrubby muzzle and hard little eyes, wearing another gentleman's coat, too tight across the shoulders. He stared, I stared; he stayed a full minute, then — the oddness of it! — was gone, and I had heard no step. When I described him to Mrs. Grose she went the colour of a boiled crayfish and would not have it.

"Where did you see him? The tower? And you — you precious innocent — *described* him?"

"He's a stranger. Is he a stranger?"

"God bless us, yes, miss!" Her paws shook. "That's Peter Quint, the master's valet — the old master's, his lordship's father. And he's *dead.* A year past, found frozen in the lane with a bottle in him, on his way back from — never mind. He was a brute, miss. Too free with everything — the wine, the keys, the company." She dropped her voice. "And with the boy."

I made her say it. "He did what he liked, miss. And little Miles — little Miles had to *like* it."

I did not sleep. And that is the truth of what followed: from that hour I stopped sleeping, and I leave it to cleverer monkeys to say what a governess sees who does not sleep.

For I saw him again — at the dining-room window, pressing his muzzle to the glass like a dog, though no dog was ever so pleased with itself. And then I saw the other. On the far side of the lake, on the Sunday afternoon, while Flora played at weaving rushes, a lady stood on the blank turf across the water: a tall thin gibbon in mourning, with a face of the most dreadful, the most *patient* unhappiness, watching the child as Quint watched me. Miss Jessel — the governess before me. Dead too. Dead in her last holidays, and "not respectable," Mrs. Grose whispered, and buried quick.

Now mark what my imagination — or theirs — did with two dead servants and two living angels. I began to see them *in* the children. Flora, at the window at night, waving soft fingers at somebody on the lawn. Miles, on the lawn at midnight in his little shirt, looking up at somebody on the tower — silent, both of them, keeping some tryst with their old friends, and lying to me, all pear-sweetness, at breakfast.

Miles came home expelled that first week, you know. The headmaster's letter named no crime — "an injury to the school such as its fellows must not know" — and when I pressed him, my beautiful treacherous boy, he only shivered like a wet leaf and said, "Ooh — I say things, I suppose. To those I like. Little things. *Ook* things. You wouldn't like them."

Then came the turn of the troop. I had thought myself the defender of the little flock; I discovered I was the enemy of it. The birds went quiet when I entered. The dogs looked at me and away. Flora turned her small back on me at prayers; and when I pressed her — too hard, God forgive me — about the lady on the far shore, she turned on me with a shriek I had never heard from her, words a fishwife would blush at, and to Mrs. Grose, sobbing: "I don't know *her* — I never saw her — take me away from *her!*"

Her. Me. Mrs. Grose took Flora to her uncle that very night, over my protest, and left me alone at Boughly with Miles, who chose that evening — the first free hour of his life — to be tender with me, and sat by the fire reciting his little Latin like a courtier, poor lamb, poor dreadful lamb, while downstairs the candle I had left in the stairwell guttered and went out in a wind with all the windows shut.

He came to bed clutching my sleeve. "She wrote it all to my uncle — the old governess. And he doesn't *mind* — do you think he minds? — because I'll say my *ook*-things and he'll simply —"

"Miles," I said, "tell me about the school."

"Why," he said, smiling terribly, "I'll tell you *everything* — if you'll tell me one thing first. It's just a question of — *him.*"

And the room turned cold, and the window-glass, though I had drawn the curtain, went white as frost, and my boy's eyes rolled up to a point above my head where a shadow fell across the coverlet — long arms, scrubby muzzle, that coat too tight at the shoulders.

"He's *here*," breathed Miles, delighted, dead-white, "he's come for me —"

"He can't have you!" I cried, and I gathered him up bodily and held him against my hammering heart, and I said it to the frost and the shadow and the whole troop of them, living or dead: "Peter Quint — you baboon! You brute! You beast — take your paws off him — there's nothing here for you — there never *was* anything here for you — BE GONE!"

The candle leapt. The shadow thinned. The room was only a room, a nursery fire, a boy in my arms.

Miles gave a great sigh, a sob, a little laugh — "Why, I said I'd tell you —" — and went limp against me, so suddenly that I screamed, certain the worst had happened, that in tearing him from their grip I had torn something loose in him too.

But no. His little heart went on — an injured bird's flutter, the doctor said next day, "a terrible fright or a terrible joy, miss, and don't ask me which" — and it was his own, whatever else was lost.

They never came again — not Quint at the glass, nor the lady in mourning by the lake. Mrs. Grose kept house at Boughly until the boy went to his uncle's. I never saw Flora more; they sent her to the sea "for the air," and she grew into a stranger with a laugh like a bell, remembering, they told me, nothing at all — not me, not the tower, not the lake, not that afternoon.

Whether she forgot, or was made to forget, or whether I dreamed the whole adventure in the sleep I had lost — that, as I told Mrs. Grose at the gate, is for cleverer monkeys.

Though sometimes, at dusk, in another county now, I pass a rookery going loud all at once for nothing, or find frost on a warm pane of glass, I take my old heart in both paws.

And I know that somewhere, patient as a schoolmaster, the troop can always give one more turn.
