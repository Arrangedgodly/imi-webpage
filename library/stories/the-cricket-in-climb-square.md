# The Cricket in Climb Square

*A parody of "The Cricket in Times Square"*

Chester Cricket never meant to leave Connecticut, and he certainly never meant to leave the jungle's edge at all. He lived in a willow stump under the big sky, and one evening, drawn by a smell too wonderful to ignore, he hopped into a picnic hamper to investigate a banana-bread sandwich. The lid closed. The hamper traveled. When Chester finally kicked his way free, he was not in the savanna anymore.

He was on a branch of the Great Banyan, and beneath him lay Climb Square.

It roared. It rang. Monkeys swung overhead in packs like flocks of birds; fruit-sellers cried "Figs! Sweet figs!"; news-runners hollered the day's gossip from the bulletin-vines; and the whole enormous tree shivered with traffic, tail to tail, troop to troop, from root to crown. Chester, a very small cricket in a very large banyan, sat still as a seed and felt his antennae droop.

That was where Mango Bellini found him — a young monkey, son of Papa and Mama Bellini, whose family ran the last papaya stand under Root Six.

"Chester," Mango said, naming him after the chimp who once bowled a mango across two counties, "you're a cricket. Would you like to live at the papaya stand?"

So Chester took up residence in a little woven cage, and Mango fed him fig leaves and defended him fiercely when Mama Bellini said the family could not afford a pet. "We can barely afford us," Mama said, and she was right. The stand's luck had gone bad; Papa joked that they had two customers a day, "and one of them is a beetle."

Chester met the neighbors that same night. First came Tuck, a tarsier with enormous eyes and a fast tongue, who lived in a knothole stuffed with treasure — shiny seeds, a bottle cap, a thimble, a marble — and talked like tomorrow might be canceled. Then came Harry, a jaguarundi who could have been a terror of the treetops if he had not loved music too much, and who came and went like a large dignified shadow.

"A cricket," said Harry, examining him. "You know, crickets are lucky."

"Hah!" said Tuck. "Lucky is a full knothole. But he's welcome anyway."

One dreadful night, Chester — hungry and confused — ate half of the Bellinis' golden fruit-token, the two-fig piece that was the family's whole fortune. Mama Bellini was not a monkey to be argued with when money was missing. "Either the token is repaid," she said, "or the cricket goes back to wherever banana-bread sandwiches come from."

Tuck, whose conscience was flexible but whose heart was not, emptied his knothole: seeds, thimble, marble, all. "It's an investment," he said. "In a cricket. In better luck."

Then came the fire. A jar of glow-beetles from Tuck's hoard tipped against the dry papaya husks, and the smoke went up the Root like a shout. Chester saw it first and sounded the alarm — but the stand was scorched, and Mama Bellini's patience scorched with it. "Out!" she cried. "A cricket that eats money and draws fire is no luck at all!"

Chester sat that night in his scorched cage, and because he was heartsick and homesick and the Jungle-Vision radio was playing the opera hour, he did the only thing he knew how to do: he chirped. He picked up the aria note for note — every trill, every long gold thread of it — and sent it out over Climb Square.

Mama Bellini stopped mid-sweep.

It was her mother's favorite aria, from the old country, from the trees on the far ridge where she had been a girl. She stood with the broom in her hands and tears in her whiskers, and when the last note faded she said, "Never again will we have music like this in Climb Square. Except," she said, "for now. Nightly. If the cricket is willing."

He was willing. And so it began.

The very next market day, Mango carried Chester down the fruit-row in his old cage, and an old pangolin shopkeeper named Pang Shin, who sold silks and seeds and cricket cages woven like little pagodas, stopped them with one raised claw.

"A musician," said Pang Shin, "should not travel economy." And he would take no money for the finest pagoda-cage in his shop, only the honor: his own grandfather, he said, had kept crickets for their songs in the old country, on the far ridge, back when music was worth more than luck.

At rush hour, monkeys by the hundreds gathered under Root Six, going still as stones while Chester played. He gave them opera and lullabies and the swing-tunes the troops danced to at festival, all of it crisper and sweeter than the Jungle-Vision ever managed. A quetzal critic called him "the finest musician the jungle has ever produced, and the smallest." Crowds collected. Tips rained. The Bellinis repaid the token, refitted the stand, and put up a sign: CHESTER CRICKET — CONCERTS NIGHTLY, EXCEPT WHEN IT RAINS, AND EVEN THEN HE MIGHT.

Tuck was in heaven. "Fame! Fortune! We'll franchise! Chester, baby, we'll get you a bigger cage — no, a theater!"

But Chester was not in heaven. Autumn was coming to the jungle's edge, far away, where his willow stump stood empty under the big cold stars. His chirps grew thinner. He ate less. He stopped taking bows. One night, in the middle of arias that made the bankers of the high branches weep into their silk, he laid down his wings and would not play the encore, and the Square went home puzzled and quiet.

He confessed it at last to Harry and Tuck on a rooftop of leaves, looking out at the lights of Climb Square: "I made a mistake coming here, and it turned out to be the best mistake of my life. But a cricket's season is short. If I don't go home now, I'll never see the stump again. You can love a place and still belong somewhere else. That's the trouble with jungles. They're bigger than one small song."

"Stay," said Tuck, and it was not greed, it was love. "We need you."

"Jungles don't need one more musician," said Harry gently. "But a willow stump might."

Mama Bellini, when she understood, did not fuss. She packed him a hamper of banana-bread sandwiches, "so the smell that brought him here can carry him home," and Papa shook his small hand with one careful finger.

Chester gave one last concert at rush hour, and the whole Square went quiet for it — the fruit-sellers, the news-runners, the bankers of the high branches, every monkey who had ever sneered at the papaya stand. When it ended, no one applauded for a long moment, because it felt like the end of something, and it was.

Then Harry carried the hamper to the edge of the canopy, Tuck pressed the bottle cap into it ("For the stump. Every stump needs a bottle cap"), and Chester Cricket rode out of Climb Square on the evening wind, chirping the first bars of his travels.

"Do you think we'll ever see him again?" asked Mango, who had come to say goodbye and mostly not cry.

"Sure we will," said Harry. "When the wind's right, we'll hear him. That's the thing about a cricket: even one, in the whole big jungle, carries."

And far below them Climb Square roared on, monkey business as usual — but a little quieter, that night, all the way up Root Six, as if the great tree itself were listening for a song going home.
