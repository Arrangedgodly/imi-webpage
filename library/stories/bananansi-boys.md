# Bananansi Boys

*A parody of "Anansi Boys"*

Fat Charlie Nancy was, on the whole, the least mythical monkey in London Zoo's primate house. He was a chunky capuchin with an office job at the Grahame Coconuts Agency, a tidy flat, and a fiancée called Rosie who thought he was sensible. He got his nickname the day his father, at a troop picnic, announced to two hundred witnesses: "Ladies and gentleapes! My son cannot climb! Watch — Fat Charlie! Tree! Go!" And Fat Charlie had gone, straight into the hydrangeas.

That was Dad all over. Bananansi Nancy: wife-collector, troublemaker, karaoke legend, and — though Fat Charlie only half knew it — some kind of god. Not the God of Bananas, he'd always insisted. The god who *won* bananas. Back at the beginning of things, Tiger had held all the stories and all the fruit. Bananansi had bet Tiger he could catch three impossible creatures — the hornet-queen, the fairy-mosquito, and Marmoset-Midget, smallest of the small. He'd caught them with a gourd of water, a smear of honey, and a really outrageous lie. All stories were monkey stories after that. And all the best bananas.

Grahame Coconuts, Fat Charlie's boss, had a smile like a waiting trap and a set of books that never balanced twice. "Numbers, Nancy," he liked to say, "are like bananas. They go soft if you leave them out." Fat Charlie filed things and did not listen. This would turn out to be most expensive not-listening of his life.

Now Dad was dead. He'd died on stage at the Palm Court Lounge, mid-song, falling off the piano into the drums with a grin on his face. The funeral was on a Caribbean island, where four ancient aunties in enormous hats fed Fat Charlie food he didn't want.

"Your daddy was a god, child," said Mrs. Higgler, slapping down a plate of plantains. "Which makes you half divine. You have a brother, you know."

"A brother?"

"Spider. Next time you need him, just tell a spider." She glared at him as if this were obvious. "Now eat."

There was also, at the funeral, a police constable called Daisy, who had come about a paperwork matter and stayed for the singing. Fat Charlie had taken her to dinner exactly once in London, an evening so catastrophically awkward that neither of them had ever quite stopped thinking about it.

Back in London, Fat Charlie told a spider in his bathtub, mostly as a joke. That night a monkey was asleep in his guest room: tall, elegant, wearing Fat Charlie's best suit far better than Fat Charlie ever had, with Dad's grin and Dad's eyes.

"Evening," said Spider. "Heard you were looking for me."

"You can't just — who are you?"

"Your brother. Chimp off the old block, hm?" He smiled, and the room warmed, and Fat Charlie found himself agreeing to let him stay one night.

It did not stay one night. Spider turned up at the office party and charmed Grahame Coconuts so thoroughly that the man's smile closed on its own tail. He charmed Rosie's terrifying mother, who asked Fat Charlie, wounded, why he'd never brought this one before. Then — unforgivably — he charmed Rosie, who thought Spider was Fat Charlie being suddenly wonderful, and kissed him.

"That is IT," said Fat Charlie. "OUT."

"You can't evict family. Only a god can throw me out, little brother, and you're not a god. You're barely a rainy Tuesday."

And because there was more of Dad in him than he knew, Fat Charlie went back to Mrs. Higgler, who marched him into her back garden and down through the roots of a banyan tree, into the Country-at-the-Beginning-of-the-World.

The road there was older than roads. Somewhere off it, in a cave, something striped paced and muttered in the dark — *"Nancy... Nancy..."* — and Fat Charlie walked faster, the way you do when you hear your name called by something with too many teeth in its voice.

And in a clearing of broken cages and old bones lived the Bird Woman — huge, feathered, hungry — harpy eagles eat monkeys, and she looked at Fat Charlie the way a fat monkey looks at a banana.

"You want your brother gone," she said. "I will help. Payment: one of your father's blood, freely given."

"Freely given — what, like a tooth?" said Fat Charlie, who had not read a single folktale in his life.

"Like blood."

He agreed. He should not have agreed.

That evening the birds came for Spider. Sparrows first, politely, holding the contract. Then the crows, who do the lifting. They carried him off in his best suit, still charming, and the Bird Woman took him feather by feather — his fire, his songs, his name — until London began to forget him, and Rosie dreamed of a room going slowly grey, and Fat Charlie, waking alone in the flat, discovered that he missed his brother so much it hurt like a rib.

He was arrested on a Tuesday. The books at Grahame Coconuts had finally been opened, a great deal of money was missing, and the only name on every page was NANCY. Grahame Coconuts himself had vanished with one suitcase and both smiles.

"Numbers," said Fat Charlie bitterly, from a cell in Lewisham, "are indeed like bananas."

It was Daisy who got him out. She had read the books properly, and the numbers only ever went soft on days when Fat Charlie was on holiday. "Also," she said, not looking at him, "I don't think you did it. And I am an excellent judge of dinner companions."

Meanwhile, in the Country-at-the-Beginning-of-the-World, an old rule had been broken: the birds had fed on god-blood that was not freely given. Something was loose that had waited ten thousand years for exactly that mistake. Tiger, mad, striped, ancient, holder of a very old grudge, came padding out of the Beginning-of-the-World, hunting Nancy blood, and passed through London like a rumor with claws.

The end of it happened in Kew Gardens. Rosie had come to return Spider's one remaining glove — the birds, at least, had posted it back — and Fat Charlie was trying to say something true to her, for once, when something roared past on four huge paws, and Rosie was in its path.

And Fat Charlie did the one thing nobody expected, least of all himself. He climbed. He went up the biggest cedar in Kew Gardens like a rocket with a panic attack, and from its branches he threw the first thing he found — a rock, a lie, a song, it was never quite clear afterward — straight into Tiger's face.

And Tiger stopped, because it was *that* song, the old one, Dad's song. And songs are things.

Then Spider came back. He walked out of the hollow of a yew tree, pale, tattered, half-forgotten and therefore very hard to hold onto — you cannot eat what you cannot remember — grinning his father's grin. And the brothers did, at last, the one thing the Nancy family had always done best: they performed. Spider played the crowd; Fat Charlie, chunky, sensible Fat Charlie, sang the low part in a voice like warm mud; and every bird and beast of the Beginning-of-the-World gathered to hear the story of how Tiger, mightiest of the great cats, was fooled by a monkey with a gourd of water. They told it with feeling. They told it twice. A god's most powerful work is not fire and it is not vengeance; it is making an enemy look silly for eternity.

The Bird Woman got her payment in the end, but not from Fat Charlie; she got it from Tiger, who had broken the oldest rule of the Beginning-of-the-World, in front of witnesses, to the accompaniment of his own theme song. There is a cage of old bones in that clearing now with something striped in it that will not sing along.

Rosie went off with Spider, who made her laugh like a barrel of monkeys, which Fat Charlie had never once managed.

And Fat Charlie stayed on the island, at the edge of the Beginning-of-the-World, half-divine, sensibly, with Daisy, who had transferred to the local constabulary on the grounds that London's paperwork had gone bad. He discovered he could not fall out of trees. And sometimes, of an evening, he would sing — just quietly, to the fruit — and the fruit, slowly, would ripen to listen.

"Daddy was a god, child," said Mrs. Higgler, watching from the veranda, "and you're the other half. Takes after his mother," she added, and would not be drawn further.
