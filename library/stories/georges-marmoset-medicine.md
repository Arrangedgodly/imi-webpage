# George's Marmoset Medicine

*A parody of "George's Marvellous Medicine"*

Grandma was grumbling at George again.

George was a whiskery little marmoset, no bigger than a mango with a tail attached. Grandma was an enormous elderly gorilla with a scowl like a storm cloud that had misplaced its sunshine. She grumbled from breakfast until bedtime, and she had grumbled so long that her grumbles had worn grooves, and she slid down the same ones daily.

"You gobble your figs like a gorilla," she snapped, which George thought was a bit rich, coming from a gorilla. "You grimace like a gibbon and you dawdle like a sloth with a sunburn. Sit up straight! And STOP monkeying about!"

"Yes, Grandma," sighed George.

"Your problem," said Grandma, jabbing her walking stick at him, "is that you've no backbone. In MY day, marmosets were hung from branches by their tails until they learned manners."

"That's not true," said George, "and also I'm the only marmoset you know."

"Don't answer back. Now listen carefully. At eleven o'clock I take my medicine. One spoonful of brown goo in a cup of water. Spill it, you grubby little nit-picker, and there will be trouble."

When Grandma had hauled herself off to her chair, George sat on the doorstep and thought harder than he had ever thought before. What Grandma needed, he decided, was not brown goo. What Grandma needed was a whole new medicine. A *marmoset* medicine. A medicine so marvellous, so fizzingly fantastic, it might shake the grumpiness clean out of her — and if it blew the roof off, well, that was just bonus ventilation.

So George fetched the biggest stew pot in the baobab and crept out into the jungle on his most serious monkey business yet.

In went a glug of spider-silk shampoo, for glossy fur. In went a scoop of termite talc and a smear of beetle-black boot polish, for extra shine. In went the squishy heart of an overripe durian ("Pongy," said George approvingly), a fistful of fig sap that had gone slightly wrong, a dash of chilli oil squeezed from a fire-fruit, and a wobbly spoonful of frogspawn scooped from a bromeliad cup. In went three squished stinkbugs — George apologised to each one politely — a single wasp's wing, and, for the final flourish, the dregs of Grandpa's grog.

"Stir, bubble, fizz and boil," chanted George, stirring round and round with a liana, "turn this grumpiness to joy!"

The pot went purple. It went glug-glug-GLUG and smelled like a fruit bat's armpit after a marathon. It was, George felt certain, exactly right.

At eleven o'clock sharp, he swapped the cup of brown goo for a cup of Marmoset Medicine and handed it over.

"What's this?" growled Grandma, sniffing it. "Smells like monkey business to me." But she glugged it down in one, because grumbling is thirsty work.

"It tastes of durian and—" she began.

Then smoke came out of her ears. Then her feet went BOOM through the floor. Then Grandma shot up through the roof of the baobab like a rocket with opinions — taller than the kapok, her head among the clouds and her scowl blown clean away.

"WELL I NEVER!" she boomed, patting her hair. "I CAN SEE THE WHOLE JUNGLE!"

Being forty feet tall agreed with Grandma enormously, mostly because now everyone had to listen to her. She rearranged the weather by pointing at it. She ordered a passing vulture to fetch her figs, and the vulture, no fool, fetched them. She called down to the neighbors over two hills, correcting their posture. When George asked how she felt, she considered a long moment and said, "SEVENTY PERCENT LESS GRUMPY," which everyone agreed was the warmest thing she'd said since the wet season of some earlier decade.

By teatime Dad had come home from the farm and gone thoroughly ape with excitement. "A GROWING MEDICINE!" he cried, dancing round the pot. "George, my boy, think of the harvest! Think of the herd!" They fed a spoonful to the pygmy marmoset, who grew to gorilla size and had to be gently dissuaded from crying. They fed one to the scrawny brown hen, who swelled to the size of an ostrich and laid eggs as big as watermelons. The grey warthog grew as round as a hippo. Dad carted one giant egg to market and came home with a new roof, a cart, and an offer of marriage, in that order.

"More!" said Dad. "We must make MORE!"

But George could not remember everything he had tipped into the pot. He'd thrown it all in by feel, which is the trouble with genius.

Batch Two (green, with red spots) gave Grandma a neck as long as a climbing liana, which she used to spy on the neighbors from inside the baobab. "THE HENDERSONS ARE EATING MY FIGS," she announced from a window, her head out the roof. "I NEVER LIKED THE HENDERSONS."

"Did it work?" cried Dad. "Is she cheerful?"

"SHE'S GRUMBLING ABOUT NEW PEOPLE," said George. "That's growth."

Batch Three (blue and cheerful about it) turned her fur bright pink and curly. "I look like a flamingo's bad hair day!" she roared — which, George noticed, was almost a joke, and they wrote the recipe down hopefully anyway.

Batch Four (brown again, by accident) simply made her sneeze in dialects. Batch Five, into which George had confidently added sloth shampoo, snake oil, and one extra 'nana for luck, frothed green and smelled hopeful, and nobody — NOBODY — could think why.

And that was the one Grandma grabbed, by mistake, thinking it was her Gripe Water, and glugged the lot.

And then she shrank.

Past gorilla. Past baboon. Past monkey. The whole family gasped. The giant hen peered under the furniture. Grandma dwindled smaller and smaller, waving her tiny arms and saying "NOW SEE HERE" in a voice like a squeaking hinge, until — PHEW — she stopped at exactly George's size, sitting in the middle of the floor, blinking, in a nightgown now the size of a handkerchief.

Everybody waited for the explosion.

"George," said Grandma, in a small new voice, "might I trouble you for a fig?"

Well. A polite gorilla! Nobody had ever seen such a thing. Dad sat down heavily on the watermelon egg.

From that day on, Grandma rode about on George's shoulder, holding his fur like a saddle, saying "please" and "thank you" and "fascinating" to the beetles. She tried to grumble at the Hendersons, discovered that from marmoset height she could no longer see them, and grumbled at a caterpillar instead until it apologized. She ran the farm from George's shoulder, and her advice, it turned out, was excellent — it had simply needed carrying by somebody gentle. In the evenings they groomed each other on the step, and Grandma admitted that being small was no trouble at all when your grandson was an absolute champ.

"Mind you," she said, one evening, her chin on his head, "I've not gone soft. You're still to sit up straight."

"Yes, Grandma," said George, sitting up straight.

"And STOP monkeying about."

"Grandma," said George, "you're a marmoset now too."

She thought about that for a long, dangerous moment. Then she snorted — a small, rusty, unpractised snort — and George, feeling bolder than any marmoset in literature, called it what it was: a giggle.

George, meanwhile, was already planning his next mixture: a medicine that makes grandmas *grin*. He reckoned it would need just one more 'nana.
