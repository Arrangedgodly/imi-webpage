# The Fault in Our Tarsiers

*A parody of "The Fault in Our Stars"*

Late in the dry season of my second year as a nearly-grown tarsier, my mother decided I was depressed, presumably because I rarely left the nest and had lately taken to gnawing the same fig leaf over and over while staring at nothing.

So she enrolled me in Support Group.

It met Wednesdays in the temple basement, in a room the yoga troop had hired on Tuesdays, which meant we gathered, wobbly and cannula'd and bandaged, beneath a banner reading THE LITERAL HEART OF JESUS. Our leader was Patrick, a well-meaning spider monkey who had survived a lump in a part of the anatomy he named every single week, and who opened each session by making us share our feelings in that bright doomed voice adults use around sick young primates. My only friend there was Isaac, a gibbon going blind in both eyes, whose girlfriend Monica had recently concluded, via text message, that she preferred her boyfriends sighted.

I am a tarsier. My species' eyes are each larger than its brain — literally, look it up — which means I see nearly everything in the dark and understand approximately none of it. I had a thyroid thing with a preference for my lungs; I hauled a little tank of canopy air behind me on a cart and wore the prongs in my nose like a permanent party favor.

At my very last Support Group ever, a new monkey looked at me. He did not glance; he looked, the way you look at the last fig on the tree.

That was Augustus Waters. A howler monkey my age with a prosthetic hind leg — he'd lost the real one to a bone thing — a crooked grin, and a cigarette dangling unlit from his lips.

"They don't kill you unless you light them," he said. "And I've never lit one. It's a metaphor, see: you put the killing thing right between your teeth, but you don't give it the power to do its killing."

"It's a metaphor," I said, "probably chosen to make you look cool at Support Group," but I admit my tail curled. He asked me over to his tree to watch a movie, and his parents, who collected encouragements printed on little leaves and hung them everywhere, fed us sandwiches cut diagonally, because, Augustus said, diagonal sandwiches taste better, which was also a metaphor. The movie was *V for Vervet*. I fell a little bit in like before the opening credits, and being me, I wrote it off as a side effect of the cannula. Before I left he said "Okay?" and I said "Okay," and he said, gravely, "Maybe 'Okay' will become our 'Always.'" Isaac and Monica had "Always." We had "Okay." Ours was better, because ours came with a question mark, which is to say, with a choice.

Then he read my favorite book, and that was the end of the beginning. *An Ape-rial Affliction*, by Peter Van Hooten, about a young monkey dying of the same rare something as me, ending mid-sentence — because that is how death ends you, mid-sentence — and I had spent years wanting to know what happened after the last page. Augustus wrote to the author. The author wrote back, eventually, from Amsterdam: I cannot tell you in a letter. Which meant, obviously, that we had to go.

Gus had a wish left over from his leg, courtesy of the Genie Foundation — actual name; the humor, he said, was the only honest thing about them — and he spent it on me: two tickets to Amsterdam. In the days before we flew, he talked on my mother's phone plan until the fruit bats went home, and said the thing he kept saying that whole golden month: "I'm on a roller coaster that only goes up, my friend." On the plane he explained that there are infinite numbers between 0 and 1, and though I have eyes the size of moons I could not see a single one of them, and I believe that is when I stopped writing anything off as cannula side effects.

Van Hooten turned out to be a reclusive old owl pickled in fermented sap, who answered our question about the monkey after the last page by asking whether we had ever considered what happens to ordinary people after we stop needing their subplot, and mocked my oxygen cart, and Gus's leg, and his own biography, until his assistant Lidewij — who had answered our letters in secret and was ashamed down to her feathers — marched us out the door. Gus threw his glass in the canal, or near it, which was not, he clarified, a metaphor.

But Lidewij had one mercy left in her, and drove us to the old house on the Prinsengracht where the young diarist had hidden with her family, and we climbed the stairs behind the bookcase, and I, a tarsier who reads walls with both paws, stood where she had stood. And in the attic I kissed Augustus Waters, to a round of applause from the tourists, which I choose to believe was for the kiss.

Amsterdam was shining outside. Gus laid out a picnic of mango slices and honey sticks he'd arranged himself, and under the stars beside the canal he told me he'd lit up like a fig tree at harvest — the cancer, back everywhere. He'd planned that trip on his last good legs, mostly to see me in the city of the book.

I fell in love the way you fall asleep: slowly, and then all at once.

We had one evening worth a troop of ordinary evenings, and then we flew home so the doctors could lose him slowly in front of me. He stopped being a howler; the howl went out of him by degrees, and the ICU smelled of disinfectant and endings. He asked me and Isaac to do a funeral for him early — a prefuneral, while he could still hear it — and Isaac went first, sobbing and laughing at once: "Augustus Waters talked so much that you'd find yourself agreeing with things you'd argued against, and he'd get you to love him, and then he'd act like you'd loved him all along." Days before, Isaac and Gus had egged Monica's car in farewell — Augustus calling targets, Isaac, blind, throwing at the sound of German engineering — and it had been, Isaac said, the best day of a terrible year.

And I said what I could say, which was not enough: that some infinities are bigger than other infinities, and that there are days — many of them — when I resent the size of my unbounded one.

"You gave me a forever within the numbered days," I told him, "and I'm grateful."

"But it's a large heaven," he whispered, "and I'm not done wanting."

Van Hooten came to the funeral, pickled and grieving, and began a sentence with the word *hamartia*, and I told him precisely where he could put his hamartia, and Lidewij, I later learned, would have paid money to watch me do it. It was Lidewij who explained the letters: that Augustus had written to his hero not for me but for them — that he had spent his last good weeks making sure his favorite author would know that his book mattered to a dead girl's boyfriend, which is a sentence that made sense to exactly three primates, and we were all three of them.

His last letter, smuggled to me afterwards, said that you don't get to choose if you get hurt in this world, old fruit, but you do have some say in who hurts you. *I like my choices. I hope she likes hers.*

The world is not a wish-granting factory. The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars but in ourselves, a teacher once made me read — though being a tarsier, I've always suspected the fault is in our tarsiers, in these enormous eyes we cannot close at midnight and cannot believe at dawn.

He asked me one last time, at the end of the letter, the only question that ever really mattered.

Okay?

I do, Augustus. I do.
