# The Picture of Dorian Grapes

*A parody of "The Picture of Dorian Gray"*

The studio was filled with the rich odor of ripening fruit, and when the light of the summer morning struck the hanging vines, they moved like green ribbons over the easel where Basil Hayvine was putting the last touches to a portrait.

It was the best thing the painter had ever done, and everyone in the grove knew why: the sitter was Dorian Grapes, the most beautiful young monkey in London — fur like poured honey, eyes like bruised plums, a mouth made for peeling life's sweetest fruit.

"Something has changed you," said Basil, working feverishly. "When this young monkey appears, all my art turns rancid with worship. I have put too much of myself into this picture, Dorian. To influence a monkey is to hand him your very banana. And I have handed you mine."

"Nonsense, Basil. I would rather stay ripe forever than anything," laughed Dorian, and then he saw the finished canvas, and went silent, and something green moved in his chest. "How sad it is. I shall grow old, and wrinkled, and my fur will go gray as old grapes, and this picture will stay young. If it were only the other way! If the picture could wither and I could stay ripe! I would give my soul for that. I would give my very tail!"

Behind them, unseen, Lord Gorilla Wootton — old, silver-backed, magnificent, an ape who had thrown away both youth and virtue and made a philosophy out of the throwing — smiled like a python who has just been handed the keys to the aviary.

"My dear Dorian," he said, "the only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it. Resist it, and your soul grows sick with longing for the things it has forbidden itself. Come. We are late for brunch, and a monkey who is bored is a monkey who has not yet branched out."

Basil begged him: leave the boy alone. But Dorian had already taken the silverback's arm, already glanced back at the canvas with the first cold look of a collector appraising his own immortality.

For weeks Lord Gorilla poured honey into Dorian's ears — that beauty is the only fact, that the senses are the physicians of the soul, that a monkey should live out his own life fully, completely, and above all bananas-ly. Dorian discovered new groves, new wines, new music, new ways of being looked at. And then he discovered the little Banana Repertory Theater in the cheap streets by the river, and on its boards a girl who made Shakespeare's moonlight out of one fig-leaf spotlight: Sibyl Vine.

"You must see her," Dorian told them, wild-eyed. "She is Jungle-liet and Ophelia and Rosalind in one small body. Last night she was the lady of the lyre, and when she sang, the vines leaned in. I love her because she is all the great roles in one — I don't know if she's real, and I don't care."

Basil and Lord Gorilla came to see her. But that night Sibyl played badly. She walked through the lines like a monkey reading seed packets. When they booed good-naturedly, Dorian went backstage, cold as a green banana.

"You have killed my love," he told her. "You used to stir my imagination. Now you don't even stir my curiosity. How little you know about love — how little about anything!"

"I might have learned to love you better, if you had not taught me what reality is," Sibyl said. She looked at him and saw, for the first time, that his beautiful face did not move at all. "You are like the portrait, Dorian. All surface. I would rather be a vine that snaps than one that only pretends to climb."

He left her. That was all he did: he left her. But Sibyl Vine, in the theater's dark after he had gone, climbed to the high rigging where the trapeze artists work, and swung out over the empty seats, alone, with no one to catch her — and in the morning the company found her at the foot of the Big Vine, quite still, her last audience one horrified stage-sweeper.

When Dorian came home, he noticed the portrait had changed. Faintly, just at the mouth: a line of cruelty, a twist, as if the painted monkey had sneered. He locked the screen over it and turned the key. That is how it begins, he thought — and that was the only time it frightened him. After that it only interested him.

Lord Gorilla was delighted. "The girl never really existed, Dorian. She was less real than the roles she played. Console yourself with this" — and he sent, the next day, a book bound in mango-colored leather, a story without a moral and a style without a conscience, and Dorian fed on its poisoned sweetness and began, that very season, to live it out.

For eighteen years he lived it out. The groves whispered. Young monkeys who went swinging with Dorian Grapes came home gray-templed or not at all; fathers forbade his name; and no one could prove anything, because Dorian stayed exactly, supernaturally, gorgeously ripe. All the rot went into the picture. It grew swollen and blotched and cunning behind its screen in the old nursery at the top of the tree — an overripe portrait of a monkey going rancid in installments.

Then one evening, before he left for the Canopy Royal estate, Basil came to see the picture before it went.

"Dorian, there is something about your name in the clubs — terrible things. They say you corrupt every young monkey who swings with you. Pray, Dorian, pray. Each of us has Heaven and Hell in him."

"Is that true, Basil?" said Dorian, softly. "Then come and see mine."

He pulled the screen away, and Basil Hayvine looked into the face of a monster wearing his own masterpiece's skin — leering, sagging, evil as a week-old durian — and reeled back. "This is the face of my soul!" whispered Basil, crossing himself with a banana.

"It is the face of your art's model," said Dorian, "and you put too much of yourself into it. You have put too much of yourself into everything." And he seized the fruit-knife from the table and turned on the painter, and when the night was over, Basil Hayvine was under the sycamore bough behind the greenhouse, and Dorian's problem was a body.

He did not bury it himself. He sent for Alan Campbell, a young chemist chimp whose early friendship with Dorian had left strings attached. "You burn things with acids, Alan. You will make this monkey vanish, or I will tell them what you did for me in the old days." Alan did it — then, a week later, drank his own green solution and left the world a note that said only: *You left me no branch to stand on.*

But Sibyl Vine had a brother. James Vine, a rough young sailor chimp off the banana boats, had promised his dying sister she could count on him, and had come ashore asking questions for eighteen years. He haunted the durian dens down by the docks, and one night an ancient she-lemur with a dyed ruff grabbed his arm.

"You looking for the golden one? Prince Charming? Monkey, look at him — that youth there, with the face of a cherub. He hasn't changed since I sold him rotten dreams when you were in diapers."

"Eighteen years," said James slowly. "Eighteen years and his face is a kit's face?" And he went for Dorian through the smoke, but Dorian held up lantern light against his own smooth cheeks. "I am not the monkey you want. I was a kit when your sister died. I'm not thirty, look at me — would I lie with a face like this?" — and the lie was so beautiful that James let him go.

James haunted him to Canopy Royal. On the shooting drive, beating through the chestnut coverts, James Vine was shot dead by his own party's keeper — a stray nut, everyone said, one of those things — and Dorian raised a glass alone that night and thought: *safe.*

He began, then, to be good — or to want credit for it. He spared a young monkey's reputation; he did one kind thing; and he climbed to the nursery to see whether the picture had begun to mend. It had not. The portrait had changed again, but worse: the face was saner, healthier, and infinitely more hypocritical — the face of a monkey who has found a way to make even goodness pay. Dorian understood he had only chosen a cleaner fruit to rot.

"It was the portrait that did all the sinning," he cried, "and I will kill my past!" He seized the fruit-knife and stabbed the canvas once, blade down through the painted heart.

The servants heard a scream and a crash. Upstairs, in the nursery, they found hanging on the wall the portrait of their master as a beautiful young monkey, ripe and radiant as ever, with only one small tear in the canvas. On the floor lay a fat, flabby, gray-furred old monkey, dead, with a knife in his sagging heart. They identified him by his rings — and, after much inspection, by the shape of the tail.
