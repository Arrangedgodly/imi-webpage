# A Scratcher Darkly

*A parody of "A Scanner Darkly"*

Once there was a monkey named Bob Banactor.

Banactor lived in a disreputable bungalow in Tangerine County with two housemates: Luckyman, loud and cheerful, and Barris, a chimp who explained things for the pleasure of hearing explanations. All three were on Substance S — "Seed" — which everybody said was just a pick-me-up distilled from the Little Blue Bloom that rooted among banana groves, and which quietly, over time, split a monkey's brain into two rival roommates who stopped speaking to each other.

Banactor had another name, too. At the County Scratchers' office he was Fred, an undercover agent, and at roll call he wore the scruff-scramble suit: a full-body pelt of flickering fur-fragments sampled from thousands of monkeys, remixed every second, so that no informant could ever say who Fred was. Sometimes even Fred couldn't. That was considered a feature.

"What does a scratcher see?" Fred asked his supervisor once, cycling through forty strangers' faces. "Does he see under the fur? Between the hairs, down to the skin, into the flea-bites of the heart? Out there, some monkey is scratching my house, and in here, I am the monkey scratched. If the scratcher stares at *me*, man, what will it find?"

"Save it for your report," said his supervisor.

The report was the problem. Fred was assigned to scan the surveillance feeds of a suspected Seed house: Banactor's own. So three nights a week Fred sat in a little booth watching Bob Banactor laugh with his friends, burn the rice, and fail, year after year, to win one date with Donna Banawthorne — the lovely, guarded monkey who supplied the house and wouldn't groom with anyone who used, because, she said, "I'd go bananas, and I like my bananas where I can see them."

Watching, Fred forgot it was himself. That was the whole horror and the whole joke. "Subject appears tired," Fred typed, about his own face. "Subject's grooming has deteriorated. Subject should be flagged."

The house ran on bad luck. One evening Luckyman, wrecked on Seed, got a pawful of downers stuck in his throat and went over backwards into the fruit bowl, choking. Bob called emergency with both hands shaking, pounded Luckyman's back, and pumped him back to life — while Barris stood at a scholarly distance explaining the pharmacology of the incident to the room.

"Textbook airway occlusion," said Barris, adjusting nothing. "Notice how my earlier dosing theories are fully consistent with — "

"You stood there," said Bob, "narrating."

"I assessed. Assessment precedes intervention, Banactor. It's in every manual." Barris's own tail, which he believed was bugged, twitched at the ceiling. "Also, I remark professionally that some monkeys under stress report aphids. Look at your shoulder. Do you see them? Exactly. That's how good their camouflage is."

Charlie Fleck, a visitor to the bungalow, put it best, trembling on the couch: "I can tell when a monkey's gone, man. There's nobody home behind the fur. It's like the lights are on but the tree's empty."

Then Barris — chatty, helpful, hallucinating an aphid infestation in his own fur — went to the scratchers secretly and reported a big Seed dealer operating out of the bungalow: one Bob Banactor, dangerous, probably a dealer of genius. Fred watched the recording in his booth, staring at his own housemate informing on his own name.

"Subject demonstrates paranoid schizophrenia," Barris told the officers on tape, straight-faced, "with symbiotic fauna of the fur as a presenting symptom. Banactor is cunning, and possibly me, which is the deepest cover of all. I'd start surveillance immediately."

"We have," said the officer, expressionless, and Fred, watching, cycled through forty faces at once and settled on none of them.

The scratchers, hunting the Bloom farms upstream, decided Bob Banactor was their best ladder in. They fed him more scans, more Seed, waited. And the Seed did what Seed does: it filed the two roommates of Banactor's brain into separate rooms and lost the key.

At his probationary scan-review, Fred sat in a dark booth while the holograms played — Bob's week, projected in the round — and the technician ran the new split-hemisphere protocol. The two halves of the subject's brain lit up separately, and the machine, helpfully, let each one talk.

*He's flunking everything,* said the left voice, which sounded like Fred. *Look at him. He was going to be somebody.*

*He is somebody,* said the right voice, which sounded like Bob. *He's the guy who saved Luckyman. He's the guy Donna almost likes. What are you? You're a suit with a monkey-shaped hole in it.*

*Without me you're a dealer.*

*Without me you're a warrant,* said Bob's half. *And the sickest thing, man, is that I only exist because you watch me. You're not my enemy. You're my biographer.*

"Subject," Fred typed, in the booth, hands moving on their own, "appears to be arguing with the scanner. Recommend the scanner lose."

The technician flagged the report, and Fred too.

When Banactor was finally scooped up — burnt out, gentle, blank — the County rechristened him Bruce and shipped him to New Leaf, a rehabilitation plantation where damaged monkeys did honest agricultural penance among the rows. He was a good worker. He didn't ask why the rows of bananas had a strange blue flower growing between them. He didn't ask, because he no longer asked anything.

Donna drove him there herself, the last monkey from the old house who would, and at the gate she told him the last secret, gently, like returning a borrowed comb: she had been a scratcher the whole time. Officer Banawthorne, County division, undercover among the undercovers, the one monkey in the bungalow who never touched Seed because she had to stay sane enough to watch the watchers.

"You were the assignment?" Bruce asked, mild as weather.

"You were never the assignment," Donna said. "The Bloom was. The house was a window. I kept the window open, that's all." She looked away. "You were the part of the job I did for free."

The plan — the supervisor's whole plan, the reason Fred was fed to the feeds — was exactly this plantation: burn an informant down to the roots, plant him at the source, and wait. One monkey in a thousand, they said, comes out the far side still able to notice things.

But the New Leaf staff made one mistake: they took Bruce's troop, once, on a tour of the far fields, where the Little Blue Bloom grew thick as sadness between the trees — the source of Seed, farmed at the place that cured it, the whole snake eating its own tail at industrial scale.

Bruce stood in the blue rows for a long moment. Something two rooms back in his head knocked gently at a door.

He picked one bloom, small and seed-heavy, and pushed it deep into the fur of his own tail, where it curled tight and hid, the way a monkey's tail holds what his hands won't admit to.

If his old housemates ever came to New Leaf — if Luckyman bounced in, or Donna came looking for the quiet monkey who loved her — Bruce would show them the bloom, and they would know what Seed looked like before it got into them, and maybe some monkey could pull it up by the roots.

For Luckman, who laughed loudest going in and longest coming out. For Charlie Fleck, who saw the aphids no one else could. For Barris, who explained everything but himself. For Donna, who held the line. For Bob, and for Fred, and for the monkey who was both, who was punished entirely too much for what he did.

They were not bad monkeys. They were monkeys who itched, in a world that sold scratches.

I will remember them, every one.
