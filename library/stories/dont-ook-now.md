# Don't Ook Now

*A parody of "Don't Look Now"*

Venice in the off-season is a city of wet stone and closed shutters, and if you are a grieving monkey it is exactly the right place to be miserable in style. My wife Laura and I had come in November because I had work — I am an architect-capuchin, and was restoring the mosaics of San Apelle, a church decorated entirely with historical apes — and because our daughter Christine had drowned the year before, wearing her little red rain-slicker, reaching — well. You do not finish that sentence either, do you.

It was at lunch in the trattoria behind the Rialto that we noticed the twins: two elderly macaque sisters, identical grey muzzles, identical sensible cardigans, one of them blind. They were spooning through their soup in unison like a comedy act, and Laura, who had not laughed since the funeral, almost smiled at them.

Then the blind one turned in her seat as though her blank eyes could see us and said, quite distinctly, across the room:

"Don't ook now, but your little girl is sitting between you. She's laughing. She wants you to know she's happy, and she's still very fond of bananas."

I wanted to strike the interfering old monkey. I got as far as half-rising. But Laura had gone white, then pink, then — a wonder I had forgotten existed — *merry*, because, she said, Christine used to laugh exactly like that, like a kettle coming to the boil, and no stranger in Venice could know about the bananas, which had been Christine's whole religion.

I was angry with the twins for it, and then, God help me, I was grateful, because I got my wife back for an afternoon. We walked the fondamenta arm in arm like honeymoon chimps. We ate gelato in the rain. I remember thinking: grief is like the acqua alta — it comes in, it goes out, you learn the tide table.

The next day the twins saw our son's school accident before the school telegraphed it — a fall in the gym, a broken wrist? — and Laura flew home to England with them, on the noon plane, promising to cable.

I stayed. And Venice, with my wife gone, began going quietly and thoroughly bananas.

It began with the red hood. Twice on my way to the scaffolding I saw a small figure in a red rain-slicker — Christine's exact slicker, the exact shade of postbox red — darting down a calle. Each time my heart went off like the Campanile and each time it was some Venetian child, some tourist's langur, nothing. The third time it vanished into the fog at the end of an alley and I stood there calling — not her name; I have never let myself call her name — and a gondolier poling past said, kindly, "Signore scimmia, there is no child. And you should not be walking alone at night. There is the other thing."

The other thing. The whole city was under a hush about it: a killer loose in the dusk, small and quick, the papers said, preying on lone walkers by the canals. The police inspector — a vast melancholy baboon named Longhi who took my statement about a stolen wallet I had not reported stolen (I said anything to explain why I was found at midnight, shouting in alleys) — leaned back and looked at me a long time.

"You see your dead daughter in the streets," he said, not unkindly, "and there is a murderer of the small stature in these same streets, and yet you stay, and you walk alone, and you follow little red figures into the dark. Signore, I ask you as a colleague of the rational mind: go home."

I did not go home. That is the whole of it. I did not go home, and everything that happened afterwards happened because of it.

The twins telephoned the hotel that evening, long distance, frantic — the blind one's voice going like a paddle-wheel: "John, don't ook now — don't ook anywhere — go to the airport at once, tonight, don't wait for morning — she saw you in the water, John, she saw you *under* the water, go *home*—"

I hung up on her. Grief had made me rude as well as stupid. And an hour later, on the vaporetto crossing to the Giudecca, I saw Laura.

She stood at the stern of the passing boat in her black coat, and the twins were with her, one at each elbow, and all three were watching me with a terrible composed attention, and the boat slid by, and did not stop, and took my wife away into the dark.

I did the madness then properly. I ran the quays. I took two water taxis and a police launch, which is how Longhi came to hold my passport. Nothing. The hotel said Mrs. Baxter was in England. The cable from Laura, when it came, was dated that morning: *Son's wrist mending. Twins wonderful. Flying back Thursday. Love, L.*

And yet — she had been *in* black. On a boat. With mourners.

That was when I stopped arguing with the evidence and let it argue with itself, which is the mind's last decent act. I sat in the Piazza at midnight with an espresso going cold, a small grey architect in a large grey city, and I thought: *all right, then. I have seen her coming back. Coming back for —*

I don't know what I would have concluded. I was interrupted. There, at the mouth of the alley by the Florian — the red slicker. Not running this time. Standing. Waiting. Small shoulders, bowed hood, one paw holding the wall.

Everything Longhi had said, and the twins, and the killer of small stature, all of it arrived in my head in the wrong order, too late, and I heard myself say — gently, gently, the voice you keep for wounded animals and daughters — "Christine?"

The figure turned.

It was not Christine. It was a dwarf howler monkey, ancient and wild-eyed, and in its paw was a straight cut-throat razor, and it went up on its toes with the razor rising, and I — I would like to report that I fought, that I wrestled the blade away. What I did was step back smartly, the reflex of a monkey who has dodged collapsing scaffolding all his life — onto a banana peel.

Someone had dropped it there, that morning, that noon, that century ago. Venetian moss, Venetian stone, a Venetian peel. My feet went up, my head went back, and the world turned once, unhurried, like a gondola coming about.

I came to rest half in the little canal, half on the step, quite still, watching with enormous interest while the dwarf howled off into the night — police whistles rising after it from three directions, Longhi's men, they got it, they got it that same night, you may look it up — and while the water in my ears went tick, tock, tide, tide.

And it was all suddenly as clear as the glass in the mosaic I had been restoring: the funeral launch I had glimpsed crossing our wake on that first afternoon, the coffin, the little procession — *mine.* Laura on the boat in black, flanked by the twins, coming back Thursday but seeing it from somewhere ahead of time — *mine.* And Christine, patient Christine, not laughing at all now but waiting gravely at the far end of the dark, holding out something small and yellow and familiar, saying what the blind sister had said all along, in the only language we had left:

*Ook. There you are. Don't ook now, Papa. It's only us.*

The tide came in to the step, as it does, as it always does.

They tell me Laura stayed on in Venice all winter, with the twins, and that the red rain-slicker was never found; and that when she finally sailed for home she stood at the rail and would not look back at the city, but kept her face turned out to sea, to the open water, to the red sun going down on it — going, going — as though she were watching somebody small in a red hood walking away across the surface, unhurried, not once looking back, gone.
