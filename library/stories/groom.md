# Groom

*A parody of "Room"*

Today I'm five. I was four last night going to sleep in Groom, but when I wake up in Nest in the dark I'm changed to five, abracadabra. Before that I was three, then two, then one, then zero. "Was I minus one?"

"I grew you in my tummy," Ma says, "and then you popped out, and the blood came, and I was so tired."

Ma is a bonobo. I'm a bonobo too, a small one. Groom is our home, eleven paces long and eleven paces wide, with Skylight in the ceiling where the sun comes in yellow like a banana and the moon comes in white like a coconut. There's Nest where we sleep, and Bath, and Table, and Plant, who is a banana tree called Gertie, and Tire Swing, my best friend after Ma, and Wardrobe, where I hide when Old Nick comes. Also Refrigerator, and Door, which is the wall that beeps. I know all of Groom's friends by name. Groom is not a shed. Groom has friends.

Old Nick is the night keeper. He built Groom behind his tool shed and he brought Ma here when she was nineteen, to live in his private collection. At night the door goes beep-beep-beep, and Ma makes me get in Wardrobe, and I count the rustles of Nest till he's gone. Ten rustles is a bad night. I don't look at Old Nick through the slats because I'm not supposed to know he's real. Once he creaked the bed and I counted eleven rustles, a record, and Ma didn't talk at all the next morning, she just stared at Skylight like the answer was going to come down on a rope.

On Sundaytreats, Old Nick brings us things if Ma asks nice. One Sunday I asked for a papaya, and Ma asked him, and he said papayas were expensive this week, and Ma got mad at him, and he didn't bring anything, not even the bananas. "The whole world's expensive this week," Ma said after, "except us."

I have some from Ma still, because I'm small and milk is good for bonobos. When I was zero I had some all day. Now I just have some on sad days, and I'm five now, so mostly I don't.

"Ma, do you like Groom?"

"I love you, Jack. Groom is where you are."

When I was four, Ma went Gone. That means she stayed in Nest with her fur all dull and her eyes open, and wouldn't drink, and I had to make her take her milk, one sip, two sips, till she came back. That's also when she pulled her own bad tooth out with a string tied to Door's handle, because there's no monkey dentist in Groom, and she bit on a banana peel and said a word I'm not allowed to know.

When Ma came back she said, "We're going to do a plan. I need you to be brave like a grown-up bonobo."

The first plan was the fever plan. I lay in Nest and coughed like Gertie's leaves in a storm, and Ma told Old Nick through Door that I was burning up, boiling, that I needed a doctor, pills, anything. Old Nick said I didn't look so bad. Ma begged him to bring penicillin from the pharmacy. He brought aspirin he had already, and that was the end of the fever plan. Ma sat on Floor after and didn't say anything, and I said, "Are you gone again?" and she said, "No, Jack. I'm planning."

The second plan was the big one. "Old Nick thinks you're getting better," she said, "but then you die. Say it: my boy is dead. He'll be upset, and he'll want you gone quick, before I start screaming. He'll wrap you up and carry you out to his truck."

Ma rolled me up tight in Blanket. "Be dead, Jack. Be limp like an overripe mango that fell off the truck. Don't sniffle, don't itch, don't be alive at all. When the truck stops at the stoplight, you wriggle out and run to a grown-up monkey and scream your head off. Say the words."

I practiced the words. "Help. Help me. My ma is locked in a shed in the backyard of — " and Ma made me say the street too, even though it's a long one, because Grown-Up Monkeys Like Specifics.

I was so scared I was jiggly, but Ma said jiggly was good, dead fruit jiggles.

The truck went bump, bump. Inside Blanket it was hot and dark like being zero again. The truck stopped, and I wriggled out of Blanket and dropped onto the road, and a big white light was coming, and my legs were asleep but my tail wasn't, and I scrambled up a lamppost with my tail all poofy, and I screamed my words at a man walking a dog. "Help! Help! I'm Jack! My ma is locked in a shed!"

The man said, "Where's your ma, little guy?" and then a hand grabbed my ankle out of a truck that had stopped, and I screamed my words again, louder, and the man held on to the other leg and the dog barked its whole head off, and the hand let go. There were sirens and men in uniforms and a helicopter like a giant dragonfly, and a policeman let me sit in the warm car and I said the street name, the long one, and they found Groom by the heat of Ma's body on their magic screens. They had to show her badge photographs to make sure she was who she said, because Ma had been Gone from the world for seven years and the world had kept her picture only as a little girl.

And then Ma was there, for real, holding me so hard I squeaked.

Being in the outside after that was the weird thing. There are leaves and leaves and bigger leaves called Trees, and Trees grow right out of the ground, everywhere, thousands of them, and nobody planted them on purpose. Also: wind. Nobody told me about wind. Also rain that nobody catches in a cup first. Also stairs, which are like a ladder that's given up. The troop was too many monkeys — I counted thirty-two in one minute, which is more than all the monkeys I ever knew times ten.

Grandma came — that's Ma's ma — and took us to live at the zoo's new jungle, where the troop is, and Grandma's friend Leo, who has a beard like coconut fiber and doesn't talk much but always has raisins. A monkey with a microphone asked me, "What was it like in that shed?"

"Groom is not a shed," I said. "It has Nest and Skylight and Tire Swing and Plant. But it didn't have Outside."

Another monkey with a microphone asked Ma the questions that made her go quiet. "Why didn't you ask him to send Jack away, to be raised normal? Weren't you being selfish, keeping him?" Ma's fur stood up all along her shoulders. "He had me and I had him," she said. "He wasn't a captivity. He was my — " and then she stopped, because the microphone monkey was already saying something else.

That night Ma went Gone again, the worst one, and the doctors pumped her like the story she told me about her own tummy once, and Grandma held me and said, "She'll swing back, honey. She's a strong vine." When Ma came back she had wires on her and she cried into my fur and said, "I'm sorry, Jack. I went where you couldn't come." I said, "You should've told me. I would've counted you back, one sip, two sips."

After that the doctors made Ma tired with their questions. "Un-grooming," Ma called it. "They want to unwind what happened to us." She didn't like the doctors unwinding Groom, because Groom had me in it, all the birthdays one through five, and even bad places hold good days like a paw holds figs.

Before we went to Grandma's for good, Ma said, "Do you want to see Groom one more time?"

The door was open and it smelled old, like Old Nick, who was in zoo-jail now with the bad keepers. There was yellow tape across Door like a vine that means stop. "Goodbye, Table," I said. "Goodbye, Nest. Goodbye, Gertie." Gertie was all brown and gone, because Plant needs sun for real, not just Skylight. I climbed up to Tire Swing and swung one time, two times, three times. The detectives' flashlights made the walls go white, but I could see all of Groom's friends anyway, the way I always would.

"Ready?" said Ma.

"Yes," I said. And I closed my eyes and opened them, and looked at Groom one last time. Then we climbed out to the trees.

On the way to the car I stopped and asked Ma for the scissors Grandma keeps in her bag, and I cut off my strongest tail-hair, the one I was keeping, and I put it in Ma's paw.

"For you," I said. "So I'm with you when I'm not."

And we went home to the troop, where I'm five, and then six, and the Outside keeps happening, and it turns out that's what it's for.
