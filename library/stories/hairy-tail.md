# Hairy Tail

*A parody of "Fairy Tale"*

Charlie Rhesus was seventeen the year the old mandrill on Pinecone Street died, and everything that happened afterward — the purple sky, the singing tree, the well at the bottom of the world — began, as these things do, with a dog.

Well, not a dog. A marmoset. Radar was the oldest, baldest, half-blind little marmoset in Sentry City, and he belonged to Mr. Bowditch, who lived alone in the big rotting house with boards on the ground-floor windows and a locked shed behind it that Mr. Bowditch guarded with a scowl that could strip bark.

Charlie had his own troubles. His mother had fallen from the high vines when he was seven. His father had taken to fermented sap after that. So when Mr. Bowditch fell down his own stairs one March night, it was Charlie who heard the distant hooting and went, because somebody had to.

The old mandrill's leg was broken and his heart was bad, and he knew it. After that, Charlie came by twice a week with groceries, and sorted, at Mr. Bowditch's direction, a chest of strange gold coins stamped with a monkey god's face. Gold coins, in a rotting house on Pinecone Street. Charlie asked once. He got the scowl.

"Listen to me, boy," Mr. Bowditch rasped one evening, "and don't give me the raspberry. I'm not from around here."

"You're from Pinecone Street," said Charlie.

"I'm from *down there*." Mr. Bowditch pointed at the floor. "There's another world under this one. It's called Empis. It's sick, and it needs a strong young monkey with a steady grip, and there's no one left but you."

He made Charlie promise. Then he gave him a key, and one instruction: "The shed. Whatever happens, keep Radar with you. He's the finest friend I ever had. And if anything comes up those stairs besides you — which nothing will, but if it does — you lock this door and you don't look back."

Three nights later, Mr. Bowditch died with his boots on. Under them, on a shelf, Charlie found a journal in a spidery, old-fashioned paw, and read it by flashlight, all night, with Radar asleep on his chest.

*I was called Bright, once, in the city of Lilimar,* it began. *I had a girl named Dora, and a war found us. The sickness came out of the Deep Well — the gray fever that turns a troop to sleeping statues — and it took her. I climbed down to the basin at the bottom of the world to bargain, and I learned what it costs. A wish costs, Charlie. It takes the thing you love best. I loved Dora best, and she was already gone, so it took the years I would have shared with her, and I came up the stairs an old mandrill with a young monkey's heart. Don't pity me. Radar was down there, starving in the dark, and I carried him up, and he has been my Dora and my boy and my good luck ever since. If Empis calls you — and it will, it called me — remember: everybody down there knows the price of a wish, the way children up here know not to talk to strangers.*

That was the hairy tail of it, in the old monkey's own paw. A wish costs.

The next part Charlie never told anyone but Andy Mangabey, his best friend, who handled it the way best friends handle impossible news: "Okay. So what do we do about the shed?"

What they did was keep the promise. But that October, Claude — the school bully, a burly young drill baboon who had made Charlie's life miserable since fourth grade — followed Charlie home on a dare, and Charlie, cornered in the yard with Claude's fist incoming, did the only clever thing left. He unlocked the shed, grabbed Radar, and ran down.

The stairs went down much too far. When they stopped, Charlie stood in a jungle where the trees were silver and the sky was purple, with two moons in it like a pair of watching eyes. Radar trembled against his neck — and then sat up straight, ears swiveling, and said, in a small rusty voice:

"Home."

(In Empis, and after Empis, Radar talked. It was one of those things best not examined.)

Empis was beautiful and it was broken. A prince called Ragfur had murdered the old king, his father, and caged his sister, the rightful Queen Liliane, in the High Tower of Lilimar; Ragfur's night soldiers marched the roads with lanterns, hunting anyone loyal to her. Half the city had the gray fever, sitting where the sickness caught them, staring at nothing, breathing twice a minute. And down in the Deep Well something old and patient was dreaming the world toward its end. Everyone Charlie met — Willa the wheelwright's daughter, who hid him in her father's loft and fed him gray bread; her little brother, who explained the politics of Lilimar with his toes — said the same thing: the well was the disease. The queen was the cure, and the queen could not be freed.

"Why not?" said Charlie.

"Because freeing her would take a miracle," said Willa.

"Then I'll go get one," said Charlie, who had read the journal.

"From where?"

"Bottom of the world."

Willa looked at him the way you look at a monkey who has announced an intention to bite a crocodile. Then she gave him rope, dried figs, and a kiss on the cheek for luck, and said, "Come back up, upworld monkey," which in Lilimar, Charlie learned later, was as good as a marriage proposal from the whole family.

The Deep Well was three days down. Charlie climbed past the ledges where wishers before him had grown old waiting, past scratches in the stone that said GO BACK in three different dead languages, down into a dark that made the two moons seem like a dream he'd invented. At the bottom was a round chamber, and in the chamber a vast dish of carved stone: the Sundial, lit by no lamp anyone could find.

He put his hand flat on it, at moonset as the journal said, and the dial spoke without sound, straight into his teeth:

STATE YOUR WISH.

"I wish Radar young," said Charlie.

THE PRICE IS THE THING YOU LOVE BEST.

"I know," said Charlie. He had worked it out on the rope. He loved Radar best. The dial could take Radar's love of Charlie. It could take Charlie's love of Radar. Either way, the price was the love, not the monkey. "Take it."

The dial considered, the way a mountain considers weather. Then it took a different payment entirely — it was old, and it was sly, and it took the years — and Charlie's fur went silver at the temples in a single cold breath, and the Sleeping Evil in the Deep Well sighed, and stopped, and did not wake, and far above, the purple sky over Empis began — very slowly — to turn blue.

Getting home was its own story: past the night soldiers' lanterns swinging below him like angry fireflies, up the Weeping Bridge with the whole troop of Lilimar hooting farewell behind him, past the old king's guards (who had heard, by then, that the gray fever was breaking, and looked the other way for the silver-tempered upworld monkey who had somehow arranged it), and up the impossible stairs.

When they came out into the yard, the little marmoset's eyes were clear and his fur was thick, and he ran three times around the shed shrieking with the joy of it, then bit Claude soundly on the ankle, because some things about a monkey are lifelong.

"You gave away your young to do that," Radar said that night, from the foot of Charlie's bed.

"I got a bargain," said Charlie, who now had gray fur at seventeen and did not much mind. "I got you."

His father, sober a year by then, hugged him and asked no questions, because Charlie had asked him not to. Andy Mangabey asked nothing but "Did it work?", which was the right question.

The shed is still there. The key is in a tin in Charlie's room. Somewhere below the silver trees, Empis is healing under a bluer sky, Queen Liliane has her throne, Willa tells the story of the wheelwright's loft, and its people tell of a young monkey with old fur who climbed down out of the world to save theirs.

That's the hairy tail. And Charlie lived it.
