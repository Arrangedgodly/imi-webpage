# Equal Mites

*A parody of "Equal Rites"*

The wizard Drum Branchlet crawled up the mountain pass at midnight to do the most important magic of a monkey's life: pass on his power.

It was supposed to be simple: an eighth son of an eighth son is born a wizard made, the dying wizard hands over his staff, the magic flows, and everyone gets on with the hats. Drum Branchlet had found one in a smithy at Bad Bunch, and staggered in as the baby arrived to press his staff — gnarled, black, humming like a hive of electric mites — into the crib.

Then the midwife looked at him with an expression he'd last seen on his accountant.

"That's not a son," said Granny Weatherwax. "That's a girl."

Drum Branchlet died of shock, magic, and embarrassment. The staff stayed exactly where it was. It seemed, the smith's wife thought later, to have settled.

The trouble with magic is that it doesn't care what anyone thinks. It's like mites: it gets into whoever it pleases and causes unspeakable itching. Baby Eskarina — Esk to everyone who valued their shins — froze her bathwater at eighteen months. At six, a boy pulled her tail, and his trousers achieved orbit. Bad Bunch drew the obvious conclusion.

"It's wizard magic," said Granny Weatherwax, who was old, iron-grey, and so proud she combed her fur with a hairbrush made of consequences. "She caught it off that fool's staff. And wizard magic in a girl is like a flea in a drum — it won't stop jumping till you deal with it."

"Send her to the witches, then," said the smith, her father.

"Witch magic's not the problem. She's got *wizard* mites." Granny looked toward Ankh-Morepork, where the Unseen University squatted like a stubborn toad with a library. "She's got to go where the wizards are."

The road there took most of a season and taught Esk most of witchcraft: how to read the weather's back, how to know what's wrong by standing still, how to be too proud to be scared, and how to Borrow — slipping her mind gently into another creature's, the way you'd borrow a neighbor's ladder. Esk Borrowed a hawk on the high pass, and stayed up too long, and came back down with her hair full of sky and an unnerving interest in small scurrying things.

"Everything wants to be something else," she reported.

"Yes," said Granny. "That's the trick of it. Respect the ladder, or the ladder starts decidin' where you go."

The staff behaved oddly the whole way, which is to say it behaved at all: it leaned against doors before Esk knocked, it was warm in hailstorms, and when a bandit questioned them at a ford it bristled like an angry eyebrow until the bandit, a reasonable monkey, apologized to it directly.

The Unseen University had never admitted a girl in ten thousand years, on the grounds that wizardry was for males — a position its faculty could defend for hours without once using the word "because."

Granny and Esk went anyway, reaching the Great Hall at dinner — a mistake, because in the library above, a young baboon prodigy named Simon was reading so hard that reality hummed like a plucked cable: holes opening in the air, and through them, something waiting. Simon had a stammer, a gift the size of a thunderstorm, and a habit of saying eight impossible things before breakfast, five of them accidentally in languages from outside the world.

The wizards, led by Archchancellor Cutangutan — a grey mandrill with a chain of office and a beard like a thundercloud in mourning — mobilized to repel the girls with full ceremonial pomp, which in wizard terms means insults and lunch.

"Madam," announced Cutangutan, "magic is not for females. It is a thing of the eighth son."

"Your magic's got the sense of a buck in rut," said Granny. "Something's up there eating holes in the world, and you're down here guardin' the sausage."

"WHAT could get into the library?" said Cutangutan. "Nothing gets in. The Librarian barely lets *us* in."

From the library doorway, an orangutan of few words looked up from his book and said, "Ook," in a tone that meant: *something is absolutely in there.*

Esk and Simon, meanwhile, had met in a corridor and done the unthinkable: become friends. She thought his holes were neat. He thought her staff was the most beautiful thing he'd ever stammered at. They traded the ways each had learned to hide being clever, which is the true founding friendship of every clever thing that has ever happened anywhere.

Then the holes got hungry, and the Things from outside the world — hooks, angles, and appetite, all of it politely, patiently pressing in — reached through Simon's reading and into the library's deep stacks, and Esk went in.

The duel followed — history records it as the Counterweight Hurricane: Cutangutan hurling every spell he knew, Granny batting each back with cold, precise, hectoring counterspells, while the candles screamed, the wine turned to rain, and the faculty hid under the tables.

"FIRE!" bellowed Cutangutan.

"That's not fire, that's a big light," said Granny. "When a monkey throws a banana at thee, does thee catch it or complain about bananas?"

"YOU CAN'T TELL ME ABOUT MAGIC!"

"I can tell thee about *arming* magic," snapped Granny. "It's not the size of the swinging, it's knowing where the branch is."

But the true ending happened above, in the library: Esk found Simon with the holes in the world and the things with hooks, and stood her ground, black staff blazing — not fighting the Things so much as telling them, in the flat tones of a young monkey entirely out of patience, that they were NOT INVITED, NOT WELCOME, and could get BACK. And when the Things looked past her, hungrily, at Simon, she did what no wizard had ever thought to do: she stopped defending her own mind and *lent* it to Simon, and he to her, two mites in one itch, and the two of them together slammed the doors of the world from the inside.

The staff helped. It had, everyone later agreed, been waiting all year to hit something.

The Things looked at the staff. They looked at the girl. They considered the mites.

They got back.

In the morning the University convened, scratched, and faced the arithmetic: the magic had chosen Esk, the staff stayed with Esk, and never once had the staff asked anybody's opinion. Cutangutan, still smoking faintly, conceded with the dignity of a mandrill who had reviewed his position overnight and found it under the table.

"Light thinks it travels faster than anything," Granny observed on the road home, "but it's wrong. However fast light swings, it finds the darkness has always got there first, and is waiting for it. Same with girls and magic. We was always here. We was just waitin'."

The gates of the Unseen University opened a grudging crack for one small monkey with a staff and an itch to learn, and Simon — who could no longer read the dangerous words without her standing on his other side — came back through that crack too, as the University's youngest lecturer, on the strict condition that somebody friend-shaped knew where he was.

In the library, the Librarian stamped her ticket personally.

"Ook," he said, which was a whole speech.
