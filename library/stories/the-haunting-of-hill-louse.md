# The Haunting of Hill Louse

*A parody of "The Haunting of Hill House"*

No live primate can swing for long under conditions of absolute sanity; even larks, even katydids, even bananas are supposed, by some, to dream. Hill Louse, not sane, stood by itself against its hill — a great gray banyan wrapped in its own roots, holding darkness within — and it had stood so for eighty years and might stand for eighty more. Within, its branches continued to sprout at wrong angles, and its doors continued to breathe, and whatever walked there, walked alone.

Eleanor Vance was thirty-two years old and had spent eleven of them grooming her invalid mother, and the dry seasons had gone by and made her strange. She had once, as a child, made it rain stones on her own family's treehouse for three days — a talent her house had admired and her family had never once forgiven — and she had been apologizing for it ever since. So when the letter came — Dr. Montague, stately, with letters after his name, seeking "individuals with a history of psychic disturbance to summer at Hill Louse" — Eleanor stole her sister's share of the family jeep, pointed it at the hills, and drove toward the only invitation of her life.

On the way she passed a little roadside stall selling painted cups, and among them one with stars painted inside the rim, and she thought, absurdly, joyfully: *that cup has been waiting for me my whole life*, and did not buy it, and regretted it at once, and regretted most things at once, and drove on. The last sign before the village read: JOURNEYS END IN LOVERS MEETING. She read it four times and took it, as she took everything, personally.

The gates were iron. The tree leaned over its own lawn like an aunt who has decided that you are not leaving.

"I set the bananas before you at six," said Mrs. Dudley, the housekeeper tapir, from a safe distance. "I clear them away at eight. I'm gone before dark comes. I don't stay after dark, and neither should you. No one would hear you. Not for a mile. Not for a night."

Inside were the others: Theodora — Theo — a bonobo of easy laughter and bitten-off fingernails, who had taken the invitation on a whim and shared her whims freely; Luke, the young chimpanzee heir, who planned to sell the place one day; and Dr. Montague himself, with notebooks, and a plan of sunny rooms and notes taken at dusk. They laughed a great deal that first week, the way campers laugh.

Hill Louse had branches that leaned left, so that you were always, faintly, climbing downhill. Doors would not stay open. Doors would not stay shut. There was a cold spot on the gallery outside the nursery, cold as a riverbed and exactly nine 'nana-lengths wide, and Theo measured it, and would not measure it twice. Every night at the nursery hour, something came banging down the corridor like a barrel of monkeys with no monkeys in it; it hammered on doors; it whooped; it stopped an inch from the wood, listening, grinning, deciding. And once, inside the nursery door itself, in the dark, in the cold — something had written, huge and round and wrong, and waited to be read.

Eleanor and Theo took to sharing a room, and one night the banging came, and the two of them lay rigid in one bed holding hands in the dark while the corridor rang and something ran gigantically overhead, and Eleanor whispered, "Hold my hand, hold my hand, don't let it in," and Theo's hand held hers, icy and steady, until dawn.

At breakfast Theo was gray. "Nell," she said. "I was in my own room all night. I never came to you." And Eleanor looked at her own hand, which had been held all night by something that was not Theo, and did not scream only because screaming would have meant letting go.

They found the writing at noon, on the nursery wall, printed huge in old banana, brown as dried blood:

HELP ELEANOR COME HOME ELEANOR

Theo's laughter died in her throat. "Nell," she said, gripping her arm. "It knows your name." That same day Theo's good clothes, laid out on her bed, were found smeared to the hem with red — paint, the house had decided, or something paint-colored — and Theo scrubbed and scrubbed, and the stain would not leave, and neither, after that, would a certain look between them.

Eleanor — who had spent her whole life being invited nowhere — felt what the others could not: the house *loved* her. Why else the writing? Why else a cold spot kept for her, like a chair? At night she woke at the top of the library branch with no memory of the climb, the troop's sleeping breath below, the whole Louse breathing around her like a great gray animal, and she whispered into the dark, "I'm home."

"It's about time," said the dark.

Dr. Montague wrote it all down and did not notice that his most important datum was washing his dishes with a faraway smile. Luke began checking the window latches twice and then, ashamed, three times. Theo said, gently, cruelly: "Nell, you're getting to like it here. That's not the same as being liked *by* it."

The others grew careful around Eleanor, and then frightened for her, and then frightened of her. On the last night she climbed the broken stair to the crown of the Louse, hand over hand into the wind, while below her Theo wept and Montague thundered scripture and Luke clung to her ankle with all four hands.

"Nell! Whatever walks here — it wants you inside its fur!"

"It's the only place," said Eleanor, dreamily, "that has ever asked."

They got her down — how, no two accounts agree; Luke swore she stepped backward into his arms, and Theo swore the tree handed her over, like a toy surrendered at bedtime. In the morning the jeep was packed before sunrise, and Eleanor sat a long moment with her paws on the wheel, and the house held its breath, and the drive unrolled before her, and something in her skull sang quietly: *journeys end in lovers meeting, Nell, turn the wheel, come home.*

At the last turn of the drive, she let the jeep drift, gently, asking herself all the while — why am I doing this, why don't I stop me, why doesn't somebody stop me — until it kissed the great gray trunk of the old fig at the gate — a fender-bender of pure tenderness, bark against bumper — and stopped. She sat there, alive, heart drumming, having refused the only invitation she had ever been given. She touched her shoulder, where the cold spot had reached out to her once, and found her fur there still standing.

She drove toward town, thinking, quite calmly, of the house. It is over, Eleanor told herself, and knew that it was not, and knew that part of her would always be at Hill Louse, in the nursery hour, coming home. Somewhere behind her, in a hollow that had learned her name and would not forget it, a door breathed out, then in.

Hill Louse stood empty another eighty years, and stood sane enough for an empty house — settling its doors, adding a branch here, subtracting a branch there, holding its darkness the way old trees hold rain.

And whatever walked there, walked alone.
