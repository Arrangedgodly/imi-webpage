# The Nutscratcher

*A parody of "The Nutcracker"*

On the night of the Festival of the First Fig, the whole troop gathered in the Great Fig Tree, and Uncle Dandylost — the troop's mysterious toymaker, with one amber eye and a tail that never sat still — arrived bearing gifts. He built clocks that ran backward and toys that ran slightly forward, and nobody knew where he came from, except the elders, who changed the subject.

For little Clawra the capuchin he brought a nutscratcher: a wooden monkey with strong carved jaws, a soldier's red vest, and a face so ugly it looped back around to handsome.

"He's *weird*," said Clawra, hugging him instantly.

"He's practical," said Uncle Dandylost. "The weird ones always are."

Her brother Figs grabbed it first, jammed a coconut in its jaws, and heaved. CRACK. The coconut held. The nutscratcher's jaw didn't. It hung crooked, and Figs was exiled to the far branch for crimes against toys.

"Never mind," said Uncle Dandylost, tying the jaw with a strip of fig leaf. "Broken jaws mend, little one. Especially at midnight."

"Midnight?" asked Clawra. "What happens at midnight?"

"Toys happen," said Uncle Dandylost, and would say no more, though his tail went still for a whole second, which for his tail was a long silence.

When the younger monkeys had been shooed to their hollows, Clawra crept back to check on her patient — and it was there, in the crook of the tree, that Uncle Dandylost found her awake, and told her the story toys tell about themselves.

*Long ago*, he said, *in the Kingdom of Ripe Bananas, there lived a princess named Pirlipaw, who was so sweet that figs ripened early in her honor. Now, at her birth-feast, the palace cook forgot to wash the bacon grease from his paws before handling the First Fruit of the Festival — an honored banana, pride of the royal table. "Pah!" cried the First Fruit. "Grease on a guest of honor! For this insult — a curse on the sweetest thing here!" And the princess's head turned to carved wood, jaw ajar.*

*The King's wise monkeys scrambled for the cure and found it: she must eat, from the hand of a brave young monkey, the kernel of the Iron Fig of Rudeness, hardest nut in creation. The kingdom's strongest jaws failed. At last young Prince Cashew, with the finest teeth in the royal line, cracked the Iron Fig on his first bite, and was told: seven steps backward, no more, and hand her the kernel.*

*On the fifth step he stumbled — over the First Fruit, who had tripped him on purpose, because spite trips. The kernel flew true, the princess's head bloomed back to fur, and the whole curse, having nowhere to land, fell on the prince: wood from crown to claw, jaws gone heavy, a face only a clockmaker could love. The First Fruit was banished to the compost for his crimes, where he rotted, and fumed, and grew a crown.*

"They call him the Banana King now," said Uncle Dandylost. "He rules the mice of the compost, and he has sworn to bite through the prince's curse before the prince can bite through another nut. If ever you see a banana with seven bruises, little one — those are his seven crowns — be civiler to it than history was."

Clawra fell asleep in the crook of the tree — and woke to twelve hoots. The old fig clock was striking midnight, the tree was growing somehow *taller*, and the floor was swarming with mice in little sugar helmets, led by a crowned banana with a rotted spot for a face and — she counted — seven bruises, gleaming like medals.

"The Banana King!" squeaked the mice. "Seize the nuts! Seize the figs! SEIZE THE NUTSCRATCHER!"

"Wait," said Clawra, quite unreasonable with sleep. "You're a *banana*. Commanding *mice*. That's not how anything works."

"SILENCE!" squealed the Banana King, wobbling with rage. "I was the First Fruit of the Festival, and they threw me to the MICE! Now I rule them! Now I take everything! Guards — to the nut bowl! And bring me the wooden prince — I have a score and seven bruises to settle!"

The mice rushed the nutscratcher where he stood on the toy shelf, jaw still tied with fig leaf, and the toy shelf became a battlefield. Wooden drums rolled. Marbles cannonaded. A regiment of mice went down sliding on a spilled tin of buttons, and still they came, wave after sugared wave, and the nutscratcher — alive now, every splinter of him — fought with his crooked jaw tied shut and his red vest blazing, and he was losing.

So Clawra, who would not stand by while a mended friend was mobbed, took off her little slipper and hurled it.

It caught the Banana King square in the bruise. He sat down hard, and while the mice gaped, the nutscratcher came alive — wooden arms swinging — snatched a candy cane from the fallen, and fenced the whole squeaking army off the shelf, stroke by stroke, to the very edge.

"Back, you soggy sovereign!" he cried. "Unhand my lady's figs!"

The Banana King fled, weeping pulp, and his mice followed him down into the floorboards, their sugar helmets askew. And then the nutscratcher turned, and where the wooden face had been there stood a prince — a tall capuchin with kind eyes and a jaw only slightly crooked.

"Prince Cashew," he bowed. "Cursed to wood by the Banana King's spite, until a brave heart threw a shoe on my behalf. Curse-fighting, it turns out, counts a slipper as a sword. Come — the Kingdom of Ripe Bananas thanks you."

Through the Snow Forest they flew, where snow monkeys in white fur twirled and spun and flung frost from their tails, waltzing them onward through a flurry that glittered like sugar going somewhere on purpose — out the window and down into a land of sugar-palm palaces and rivers of cocoa: the Kingdom of Ripe Bananas.

The Sugar Banana Fairy ruled there, glowing like dessert. "Welcome, Clawra! Welcome, Prince! The curse is cracked! Let the celebration commence!"

And what a celebration. The cocoa capuchins of Spain stamped the trepak until the chocolate rippled. The mocha baboons of Arabia poured coffee that steamed in rhythm. The green-tea macaques whirled parasols of leaves with their pinkies out. Mother Gingerbonobo opened her vast skirt, and out danced a barrel of tiny monkey children, tumbling and giggling and refusing to go back in at the end of their number, as is tradition. Then came the Waltz of the Fig Blossoms, petals and Dewdrop dancers spinning over the river, and at the last the Sugar Banana Fairy herself, tiptoeing through the cocoa-sheen with her tiny feet going faster than eyes could politely follow.

"You may stay forever," the Fairy offered. "We have dessert for breakfast. The evenings are made of bonbons. Nobody ever tells you to stop."

Clawra was sorely tempted. But the dancing slowed, the lights dimmed, the cocoa river hushed —

— and she woke in the crook of the Great Fig Tree at dawn, tinsel in her fur, troop monkeys snoring all around.

"A dream," she sighed. "Typical."

But there on her lap lay a walnut, perfectly cracked — Iron-Fig neatly, impossibly cracked — and propped beside it, her nutscratcher, with his jaw mended clean, his wooden face smiling just a little more than it used to. And on the branch above, Uncle Dandylost's amber eye twinkled down.

"Festival mornings," he said, "have a way of cracking things open. Nuts included. Dreams included. Curses, on a good year."

"It wasn't a dream, then," said Clawra. "Or it was, but a *true* one."

"Dreams," said Uncle Dandylost, "are like my clocks. They run whichever way they like, and they keep excellent time."

He had, she noticed, a slight limp these days — the sort a prince might get from stumbling on the fifth step of seven, long ago — and she had noticed, too, that the nutscratcher's crooked jaw and Uncle Dandylost's crooked smile matched exactly, and being a wise child, she hugged them both and told neither.

"Next year," she whispered to the nutscratcher, "we finish the battle."

The nutscratcher, being a toy — or being very good at pretending to be one — said nothing.

But his jaw, everyone agreed, had never looked stronger. And when the mice came again next winter — for the Banana King *always* comes again, with his seven bruises and his sugar-helmeted regiments — the whole troop was ready to shell-ebrate.

**The end. Or, as the Kingdom of Ripe Bananas says it: the éclair.**
