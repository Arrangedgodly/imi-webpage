# Slaughterlouse-Five

*A parody of "Slaughterhouse-Five"*

All this happened, more or less. The parts a monkey can remember between naps, anyway.

Listen:

Billy Bonobo has come unstuck in time.

Billy has gone to sleep a senile old ape and woken up on his wedding day. He has seen his birth, his death, and everything in between, over and over again, because the moments of a monkey's life simply are, and always will be, and there is no why.

In 1944, in the Great Troop War, Billy was the skinniest, silliest conscript in the whole jungle army: a chaplain's assistant with no helmet, no coat, and fur gone gray with fright. His regiment had been chewed to bits, and he wandered behind enemy lines until a patrol of gray baboons scooped him up, along with the fat, cheerful, reeking Roland Weary, and shipped them to a prison camp in the zoo city of Dresdiana.

"Two Little Pals!" Weary kept saying, punching Billy's arm. "The Three Mango-Eaters! We look out for each other, see? Nobody picks on my little pal."

Weary told anybody who would listen about the spikes he carried in his shoes, in case he ever had to stomp a baboon to death with his bare hooves. Then the boxcar doors were opened at the camp, and Weary's feet, it turned out, were ruined — frostbit, mashed, dying — and he had to be carried everywhere, and raved at Billy through the slats of the train: *If you hadn't ditched me, my feet wouldn't be rotting. When this is over, little pal, I'm going to have you killed.* He entrusted the vow to Paul Lazzaro, a scrawny, sizzling howler with a neck like a knotted vine, who kept a list of enemies under his armpit and promised each one a slow revenge. "Nobody touches my pals," said Lazzaro, cracking a plum between his teeth. "Revenge is the sweetest fruit there is, and I never hurry dessert." Weary died of gangrene on the ninth day of the ride. So it swings.

The prisoners — thousands of mangy macaques — were deloused, powdered, and marched at night through the empty streets to their new barracks: a concrete shed behind the stockyards, marked SLAUGHTERLOUSE-FIVE. It was, the guards said, the sweetest-smelling, safest, most louse-free place in the whole city.

The old schoolteacher Edgar Derby said it still wasn't right. Then he went to sleep.

Billy went to sleep too, and woke in 1967, on Tralfamadore, where he was the featured exhibit in a zoo. The Tralfamadorians were shaped like uprooted banana trees with one green hand on top and a single calm eye in the palm. They had taken him by flying saucer, installed him under a crystal dome, and furnished him with a mate: Montana Wildhack, a film-star macaque with a coat like poured silver.

"Why me?" Billy asked.

"Why anything? We simply are. There is no why. We spend eternity looking at pleasant moments, like this one."

The Tralfamadorians saw in four dimensions, all moments at once, the way a monkey sees a whole mountain range instead of one pebble. "How is a monkey supposed to look at time?" Billy asked them, and they folded their green hands patiently. "The moment is structured that way," they said. "When a monkey dies, he only looks dead. He is perfectly alive in the past, so it is very silly for people at his funeral to cry. We simply say, so it swings, and look away to a nicer moment." They taught him to ignore the awful times and concentrate on the good ones. Groom the ones you love in the good moments.

Back in Ilium, after the war, Billy had come unstuck in ordinary ways too. He married Valencia Merble, a macaque so sweet and so enormous that when the troop sang "For He's a Jolly Good Fellow," the ground moved. Her father owned the optometry school and the whole Bonobo eye-empire that followed, and gave Billy a Buick-green convertible and a house on a hill. Their daughter Barbara grew up bossy and married an optometrist. Their son Robert flunked out of everything — until he joined the Green Berets and became a straight-backed troop commander with a chest full of bananas on ribbons. "He's all better now," Valencia said. "They fixed our Robert." So it swings.

In the veterans' hospital, between fits of time, Billy shared a room with Eliot Rosewater, a drunk orangutan who read science fiction to teach himself to be sane. He fed Billy the novels of Kilgore Trout, a starving sci-fi chimp who wrote masterpieces by the hundred and sold them for bananas. In *The Big Board* two jungle explorers were kidnapped by aliens and displayed for millions, and the audience rooted for the aliens. *Oh, that,* said something inside Billy. *That is me. That is exactly how it swings.*

Once, unstuck into an optical inspection at his office, Billy watched a war movie played backwards — and it was the loveliest film he ever saw. The bombers flew backwards, sucking the fire up into steel bellies; the bombs rose out of the jungle and climbed home into the hatch; the shattered buildings assembled themselves, and the factories took the bombs apart and hid the dangerous parts deep in the earth; the wounded were made whole, and the fighter planes flew backwards away from everything, and the last scene was young pilots flying home, to be pressed into shiny new uniforms, to be untaught, to be unborn. Billy watched it forward and it was just a movie about monkeys shooting monkeys. He watched it backwards and it was about mercy. So it swings.

And then, unstuck again, to the night the bombers came to Dresdiana. The sky was one great brown wall of engines. Billy and a hundred other monkeys stood in Slaughterlouse-Five and watched from a hill at the edge of town while the little city was milked of everything it had. Five hours later the guards came up and said, "Come up and see the moon."

There was no moon. There were ashes and melted stones and one delousing shed, standing untouched.

The prisoners were made to dig. And Billy, digging in the moonscape, in the tepid brown rain, watched a baboon guard with lovely shiny boots weep at what the city had become. So it swings.

Old Edgar Derby was executed for taking one banana from a pile behind the commissary. So it swings.

In 1976, Billy chartered a plane to a optometry convention, and it crashed into a mountain of sugar beets in Vermont, and everybody died but Billy. So it swings. While he lay in the hospital, Valencia drove to see him in the family car, weeping with worry, backed into the garage, left the engine purring, and died of banana-gas poisoning in the closed dark. So it swings.

And then Billy blinked and it was 1976, Chicago, and he was a prosperous optometrist, famous for fitting monkeys with tiny spectacles, standing on a brightly lit stage telling a crowd: "I have been to Tralfamadore. I know how I die. An old enemy promises revenge, and an owl with an air rifle waits in the rafters. And so — " — he clicked his heels — "farewell, hello, farewell, hello."

The old enemy was Lazzaro, of course, keeping his dessert promise from the boxcar thirty years cold. The owl was already in the rafters. And Billy, who had seen it all a thousand times, stood very still. If the Tralfamadorians were right, he would always be alive in 1944, drinking warm cider in the shed with Derby asleep beside him; in 1967, under the dome, watching Montana nurse their baby; in 1925, at the Grand Canyon, holding his mother's finger. So it swings.

If you ask a Tralfamadorian, as I have, how a monkey should look at terrible times like these, the Tralfamadorian will fold its green hand over its one eye and say: "Ignore the awful times, and concentrate on the good ones. Groom the ones you love in the good moments."

And on the plaque outside Billy's dome, the Tralfamadorians carved it in three languages:

EVERYTHING WAS BEAUTIFUL, AND NOTHING ITCHED.

There is nothing intelligent to say about a burnt jungle full of monkeys. Everybody is supposed to be dead, so it swings, so it goes — never say that it is supposed to make sense.

Because it doesn't. It makes no more sense now than it did then, and there is nothing left to say about a massacre, except the only thing anybody can ever say:

Poo-tee-weet?
