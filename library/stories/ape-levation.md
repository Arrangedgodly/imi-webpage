# Ape-levation

*A parody of "Elevation"*

The trouble with Scott Macaque-y's scale was that it had started telling the truth, and the truth was going down.

91 kilos in September. 89 in October. 85 by the first frost — and all of it nonsense, because Scott was eating like a troop of locusts at a buffet and looked exactly as burly as ever in the troop mirror. The vet, Doctor Bob, ran him twice through the hoop scale, stones and all.

"You weigh nothing," Bob reported, "but you're all here. That's not old-monkey medicine. That's not any-monkey medicine."

"I feel fine," Scott said. "Better than fine. Light."

"Describe fine."

"I ate a dozen donuts Tuesday and lost a kilo."

"Best diet ever," Bob agreed. "All cake, no consequence. I'd write a paper, but they'd take my license."

Then one Tuesday, Scott hopped the back fence to fetch a dropped mango and — whoops — went three meters straight up, snatched a passing cloud by accident, and had to climb down it, embarrassed.

Scott lived alone in a fine kapok on Canopy Lane. His closest neighbors were two bonobo sisters, Deidre and Missy Bonbons, who ran a food cart at the bottom of the trail called Blossom Bowls — banana-broth bowls, noodles of green coconut, floss of dried mango on top. The whole lane agreed the bowls were delicious. The whole lane also was not speaking to the sisters, on account of the turf lawsuit the troop council had backed against them, filed by Scott's own old trainer, Coach Deitz.

Scott had never said a word about it, and the silence had begun to sit on him like a wet branch. In October, somebody egged the sisters' cart with overripe papaya, laughing all the way home. Scott, who had watched from his porch and done exactly nothing, came back at midnight with the hose and washed it off, and was ashamed to notice it was the first brave thing he'd done all year.

So he made a plan. Deidre, the big sister, watched him suspiciously for the first week, certain he was casing the cart. Missy, the little sister, simply refilled his bowl.

"You Deitz's monkey," Deidre said at last. "The lawsuit monkey."

"I'm nobody's monkey," Scott said. "I'm a customer. This broth is a religious experience. I've seen the gorilla god, and she ladles."

Deidre snorted, and that was the beginning of it.

Meanwhile the lightness kept coming. Scott took to sleeping with his tail knotted to the bedframe. The scale said 71, then 63, then nothing at all, even with Scott on it holding a sack of grapefruit. "Weight," Bob said, marveling, "is just gravity's opinion of you, Scotty. The world's stopped having one."

"You're sure it isn't dangerous?"

"I'm sure it isn't anything. You're not sick, you're not fading. If I had to put it on a chart, I'd chart you under 'departing.'"

"Departing for where?"

Bob didn't answer. He was looking at the clipboard, where Scott's chart, untended, had floated up to the ceiling.

Which was how Scott came to enter the Twelve-Twenty-Four.

The 12/24 was the lane's annual race — twelve vines, on the twenty-fourth of December, entry fee twenty-four pebbles, all proceeds to the Orphan Marmoset Fund. Coach Deitz's champions had won it nine years running and were favored to make it ten. Scott entered at the last minute, in a Blossom Bowls bib, hand-lettered by Missy, who dotted the B with a little flower.

He did not so much run as bounce, each stride a lazy eight-meter bound, coat rippling. At the fifth vine he was a speck. At the tenth, an anecdote. He crossed the line while the champions were still negotiating the third rope swing, and dedicated the win from the podium — breathless, glowing, only slightly airborne — "to the best bowls on the lane, which certain monkeys have been too stuffy to slurp."

The line at Blossom Bowls went around the tree by Boxing Day. Deitz's champions, to a monkey, defected to the coconut noodles. Deitz himself was seen slurping one at dusk, incognito, in a false mustache of his own chest hair.

That Christmas evening the sisters fed him at the cart's one folding table — noodles, broth, a candle in a jar that kept trying to blow out and kept being relit by Missy out of pure stubbornness. They ate under the lane's lit windows, all of them belonging to monkeys who were not speaking to them.

"Nine years of this," Deidre said. "The suit. The silence. And now one race and a bib, and suddenly we're the flavor of the lane."

"Monkeys are herd animals," Scott said. "We stampede toward noodles. I'm just the first."

"Why'd you really enter?"

"Because I can't stay on the ground much longer," Scott said, "and I wanted the lane to see you before I go up."

The sisters looked at him. The candle thought about it.

"Up where, exactly?" asked Missy.

That winter, Scott's feet stopped touching the ground between strides, then between sentences. He read at ceiling height now, hovering by the window like a balloon with opinions, and knew — the way you know weather — that the lightness was not an illness but a departure.

He told the sisters over bowls.

"Can you hold on?" Missy asked, small-voiced.

"Can't climb down a cloud forever," Scott said gently. "But listen — anything warm you ever feel on your fur on a cold day, that's probably me, checking in."

By February the lawsuit had quietly died — the council, citing new appetite for noodles, declared Canopy Lane open for slurping. Missy wanted to frame the letter. Deidre wanted to line a drawer with it. They compromised and did both.

Scott spent his last week on the ground settling accounts. He had Bob over for coffee and asked him, as a friend, to write something down — a letter to the Canopy Lane Trumpet, to run whenever Bob judged the time right.

"Dictating your own goodbye," Bob said. "That's either very brave or very tidy."

"Both," said Scott. "Write this: *To the lane — I was heavy in all the ways you can weigh, and I got light, and it turned out the two things had nothing to do with each other. Be good to the sisters. Slurp locally. Don't wait to rise until you have to.*"

"Sign it?"

"'A neighbor.' They'll know."

He visited his wife's tree one last evening, told it the news, and tied a yellow ribbon where she used to hang her wind chimes. Then he took himself to bed, knotted the tail, and set the alarm for frost.

On the morning he chose, there was frost on the grass and the lane was asleep except for two bonobos and a vet. His wife's ring, worn ten years past her leaving and thirty past her death, he gave to Deidre, who took it like a decoration for valor.

They each held a paw. It was like holding the idea of a paw.

"Going up," Scott said, like a monkey in an elevator.

And he went — up past the fence line, past the kapok crowns, past the startled geese — rising light as toast, warm as broth, waving with his free arms in big slow arcs until he was a fleck of gold against the blue.

The sisters watched until there was nothing to watch.

"He'll be all right," Deidre said finally.

"Weight is a rumor," said Missy, who had it on the best authority. "Lift is the truth."

And on cold days after that, monkeys going out to their carts swore they felt something warm pass over their fur — a scritch on the head, a pat on the shoulder, brief as steam off a fresh bowl.
