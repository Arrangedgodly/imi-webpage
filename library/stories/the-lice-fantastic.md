# The Lice Fantastic

*A parody of "The Light Fantastic"*

When the last day of creation came, the world was still riding through deep space on the backs of four enormous gorillas, who themselves stood on the shell of Great A'Tuin, a star-tortoise. Nobody knew where they were going. The gorillas didn't ask for directions.

At the moment of crisis, a wizard named Rinsemacaque was falling off the edge of the world.

This was not unusual for Rinsemacaque, who was the worst wizard at Unseen University — he knew one spell, and it was one of the Eight Great Spells of the Octavo, the book of creation, which had lodged in his memory at nineteen and refused to leave. Between worlds, Death met him falling, held up an hourglass with no sand left in it, and offered him a peanut-butter sandwich.

"I DON'T EXPECT YOU TO ACCEPT," said Death. "I MERELY FIND IT BREAKS THE ICE."

Rinsemacaque declined. That is Rinsemacaque in one sentence: a monkey who would rather fall off the world forever than commit to anything, including a sandwich.

The Octavo, deciding he was not finished being its vehicle, caught him, brought him home, and deposited him — into the middle of a druid ceremony in the Skund forest, in front of a young lady tied to a stone altar, beneath a sky where a great red star had begun, night by night, to get bigger.

"Hello," said Rinsemacaque, politely, upside down. "Do you mind if I rescue you?"

"Do you know," said the girl, whose name was Bethan, "I have been tied to this altar, the sacrificial knife is nearly sharpened, and you are the first monkey to ask my opinion about anything. Yes. Rescue me. Please."

The rescuing was accomplished with the help of the great aged hero Cohen the Barbanarian — leathery as an old banana skin, still the finest teeth in the world — who was in the area collecting jewelry from druids, on the grounds that druids had a lot of it and he was eighty-seven and it saved bending down.

"You know," said Twobanana, the world's first tourist, a wealthy young ape in ironed shorts with a large chest made of sapient bananawood — it had hundreds of little legs, an evil temper, and it followed him everywhere — "in my country we have a saying: the million-to-one chance happens nine times out of ten."

"I hate that saying," said Rinsemacaque, fleeing from something.

He had reason to be fleeing. In Ankh-Morepork the red star had acquired a religion overnight — crowds in the streets, sandals, bowls, placards reading THE END IS NIGH, and authorized vendors selling little tin stars on strings, because no apocalypse in Ankh-Morepork is so dire that somebody doesn't open a souvenir stall. And in the University, the wizard Trymon — a mandrill with eyes like holes in the universe — rose through the ranks by ambitious accidents, smiling at everyone, remembering everything. In the Library, the Librarian, an orangutan who had fought all attempts to turn him back ("Ook"), watched the Octavo, which now held only seven sparks of light. The eighth was in Rinsemacaque's head. Trymon wanted all eight. The spells, said the Octavo, must be read aloud when the star came close — or the world would end.

Also pursuing them, on Trymon's coin, came Herrena the Barbarian Huntress and her band, who caught up with the party at a gingerbread cottage in the deep woods. It smelled of toffee. It had curdled-cream curtains and a warm oven, and it was, everyone agreed later, exactly as suspicious as it looked.

"Run," said Rinsemacaque, and they ran, and the cottage ate the door behind them.

They were rescued by the Luggage, which considered gingerbread a building material only slightly less edible than most, and calmly ate the cottage from the foundations up while Herrena's band stood well back and reconsidered their contracts. Herrena herself, being a professional, sheathed her sword, made a note in her diary, and left. The Luggage finished the last wall, burped out a sugar chimney, and trotted after its master looking modest.

They came at last to Ankh-Morepork, where Trymon was waiting, having turned the entire senior faculty into figurines of amber, each little wizard preserved mid-frown. He held the Octavo.

"Give me the eighth spell," said Trymon.

"It won't work for you," said Rinsemacaque. "Spells have *taste*."

But Trymon took the seven, read them, filled up with terrible light, and went up the Tower of Art to read the last one at the top, with the red star filling a quarter of the sky and the crowds below singing hymns to it between purchases. The Tower of Art was eight hundred feet of architecture by madmen, built by generations of wizards each trying to out-build the last and none of them checking the plans; it swayed gently, the way very tall old things do when they have given up caring.

Rinsemacaque climbed after him, because a wizard who can't do magic can still climb, terror being excellent for the arms. Behind him came Twobanana, taking notes for his travelogue ("the view is very fine, although mostly of the tower"), and after Twobanana came the Luggage, which did not climb so much as arrive, floor by floor, wearing an expression of hundreds of little teeth.

On the tower, in the red light, the seven spells leapt out of Trymon like fleas off a dying monkey. They hung in the air. In Trymon's light-filled eyes something looked out that had no right to be seen, and it was looking at Rinsemacaque.

"Do something," his own reflection seemed to say, "or the world ends with you being the last one it happened to."

And Rinsemacaque did the one thing he could, the thing no wizard would ever think of, which is why it worked: he hit him. A good square punch, behind which was thirty years of running away. Then he hit him again, on principle. The fall did the rest, and where Trymon landed there was a scorch mark and, later, a small colony of very confused ants.

Then the eighth spell — which, it turned out, had spent the last thirty years hiding in Rinsemacaque's scalp, moving between the lice whenever anyone tried to examine him — the Lice Fantastic, as the histories came to call it — finally came out, brushed itself down, and joined the other seven. The Octavo opened itself above the tower, and the eight spells were read, aloud, in order, in voices like continents clearing their throats.

And the world did not end.

The star, so near, revealed itself not as a doom but as a nest: around it clustered eight tiny new worlds, shelled and gleaming, newly hatched, each one riding its own four little gorillas on its own little turtle. The parent had merely come home to lay. Creation, it turned out, was a family business.

Below in the city, the star cult disbanded with the good grace of people who had kept the receipts. One of the baby worlds swam alongside the Disc for a while, wide-eyed, before its little gorillas found their stride.

The wizards were un-ambered, mid-frown. Cohen married Bethan. Twobanana went home at last, posted everyone he had met a postcard of the gingerbread cottage site, and left Rinsemacaque the Luggage, which sat down beside him and glared at everyone.

"Ook," said the Librarian, sagely.

"Quite," said Rinsemacaque.

He was, for about a week, the most famous hero in the world. Then the faculty reclaimed their paperwork, the lice settled back in, and the whole thing went back to normal — which, on a world riding through space on gorillas, meant as weird as possible.
