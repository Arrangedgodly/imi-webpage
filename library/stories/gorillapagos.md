# Gorillápagos

*A parody of "Galápagos" by Kurt Vonnegut*

Listen: my name was Mango Trout. I have been dead for a million years, give or take, and I have had a million years to think about how I went — showing off in a fig tree with a belly full of fermented sap. The branch broke. My big brain, which could solve puzzles and ruin friendships, did not think to hold on.

There was a blue tunnel, when I died, and a light at the far end of it, and a voice that said, in effect, "Your table is ready." I declined. A million years of watching my descendants seemed like more fun, and I have never once been wrong about anything except that.

That is the whole moral of this story, so you may stop reading now if you like.

In the year of the Great Rot, when a blight was eating the world's banana harvests and the nations of the north were threatening to fling coconuts at each other over the last crate, a travel agency announced the Nature Cruise of the Century. The good ship *Bahía de Banana* would sail from Gorilla-Quill to the enchanted isles of the Gorillápagos, in the company of the most brilliant apes alive. Tickets were expensive. The timing was terrible. It turned out to be exactly the right mistake.

The passenger list was a museum of the big brain's achievements. There was Mary Howlburn, a biology teacher who could explain evolution to a five-year-old without making the five-year-old cry, traveling with her husband Roy, who was dying of a fig-tumor and wanted to see the sea once more while he could still point at it. There was Mac Macintosh, a war hero turned millionaire, who had adopted a blind young bonobo named Selena and employed Kazak, her seeing-eye capuchin, who was himself none too sharp-eyed and depended, in quiet ways, on Selena. There was Hisako Hiroguchi, who taught the art of arranging dead twigs so they looked sorry, and her husband Zenji, who had recently invented a handheld machine called the Mandrananas, which could translate any language and offer proverbs at the push of a button, whether you wanted one or not.

And presiding over all of it, Captain Adolpho von Coconut, who had recently been struck on the head by a falling coconut, and who thereafter answered every question with tremendous confidence and no information whatsoever.

"Captain," a passenger asked as the engines coughed and died, "are we sinking?"

"Beam me up, Scotty!" roared the Captain, gripping the wheel.

Mac Macintosh, naturally, had a plan. He went ashore in Gorilla-Quill to explain the riot to the rioters, and was shot by a monkey who resented being organized. Zenji went ashore to fetch his wife's twigs, and was not organized enough. That left the Mandrananas an orphan, the riot unresolved, and Roy dead in his hotel bed overnight, painlessly, while Mary slept beside him — which is how she came to be a widow, and how the Captain came to be consoled, briefly and clumsily, and how I came to know more about my descendants' grandparents than any ghost should.

The crew had already jumped ship in port. The plague was spreading. The war had started. The *Bahía de Banana* put to sea with nobody aboard who could navigate, ten little Kanka-Bonobo girls who had been promised a boat ride and got a century instead, and one Captain who believed the horizon was a government trick. It washed ashore, ten days later, on the desert isle of Santa Rosalía: one concussed old silverback, fourteen females of assorted ages and species, and the Mandrananas.

"There is food if you look for it," said the Mandrananas.

"There is not," said Mary Howlburn, looking at the cactus.

For a while the Mandrananas tried to keep morale up with old favorites: *A peeling a day keeps the vet away. Monkey see, monkey survive.* Mary, who had spent her life teaching, tried to teach it something useful in return, and it answered with a proverb about owls. The beach grew littered with its dead batteries, and Mary Howlburn did the arithmetic that would matter for the next million years: one old male, fourteen young females, and the future of the entire species swaying in the surf.

She explained the situation to the Kanka-Bonobo girls with a stick in the sand, because that was the kind of teacher she was. The girls, who had been kidnapped, discounted, exhibited, and now shipwrecked, considered the arithmetic, considered the Captain, and shrugged with tremendous dignity. "He is old," said the eldest, "but he is the last one there will ever be."

She was righter than she knew. The Green Rot did not kill anybody, which was its idea of mercy. It simply made sure there would be nobody next. All across the world that season, the clever nations of the north — the coconut-bomb nations, the satellite nations, the nations with opinions — went quietly, politely sterile, and the last fertile fur on the whole planet was blowing in the wind on Santa Rosalía.

"Nature is not cruel," Mary told the girls, who were crying and picking sand fleas off each other. "Nature is just very, very casual."

Mary taught them what the big brains had never figured out: that grooming is not optional, that the sea is full of fish if you're willing to get your fur wet, and that the bananas of the future would not grow on trees, because the trees of Santa Rosalía had no bananas on them at all. She died in her sleep on a warm rock, an old and honored teacher, and the troop mourned her the way you mourn a library.

The Captain, to his everlasting bewilderment, became the founding father of everybody. In his last years he took to standing at the water's edge, staring west, waiting to be beamed up. The Mandrananas, asked to trace the royal line of the tribe, always began and ended with him, and offered, unasked, a proverb about owls.

And so, generation by generation, the descendants of the wreck changed. Why not? The blueprint had been drawn by a million careless years. Their fur grew sleek and waterproof. Their snouts grew long, for seizing fish. And their brains — this is the part my ghost watches with something like pride — their brains got smaller. Wonderful, cozy, economical brains, the size of walnuts.

No more banana futures markets. No more astrology. No more wars over the proper way to peel. The big brain, you see, was the only organ in history that could invent both the banana daiquiri and the coconut bomb, and it delivered the coconut bomb first, every time. It was the only organ that could get a monkey married, sued, and drafted before breakfast. The smartest ape of the year one million is about as clever as a good dog, and immeasurably happier than the smartest ape of the year zero, who had to be smart, poor thing, because everything around him was armed.

Thanks a lot, big brain.

They swim now, my descendants, in the cold beautiful currents around Santa Rosalía, and when one of them finds a fish too big to eat alone, she shares it. They have kept one thing from the old world, besides the fur: they groom. They lie in the shallows at dusk and pick sand fleas off each other's long snouts, and their little walnut brains hum with contentment, like a hive, like a hammock, like a barrel of monkeys that finally learned to hold on to each other.

The Mandrananas, if anyone winds it up — no, never mind. The batteries died a million years ago, and its last proverb, I happen to know, was about owls. There is nothing on this island now but fish, and figs, and the sound of a million years of grooming.

Listen: my name was Mango Trout. I fell out of a tree. It was the smartest thing I ever did.
