# Baboonglow

*A parody of "Moonglow"*

In the spring of 1989, my grandfather, a very old baboon with a heart going out like a lawnmower, lay dying in a rented house on the Florida intercoastal, pumped so full of banana-brandy morphine that the stories came out of him the way seeds come out of a split papaya — all at once, and in every direction, and some of them, we would learn later, not entirely true.

"You want to know what I know," he said to me, gripping my wrist with an old gray paw. "The doctors give me two weeks. So we are going to do this. You are going to sit there, and I am going to tell you, and afterward you are going to have to figure out which parts I invented."

"I'm a writer, Grandpa. That's the whole job."

"Smart aleck," he said, with love, and began.

He began, as he always began, with the moon. "When I was a kit in the slums of Baltimore," he said, "there was no moonlight, just gasworks. But I saw the moon once, over the harbor, big and yellow and bent like a banana, and I thought: they hung it there for *me*. A monkey could go there, I thought. I spent eighty years being told no by monkeys who never looked up."

Then the war. The Great Troop War, where he served — and here the morphine and the truth took turns driving. He had been a corporal of engineers, then part of the odd little unit sent at the war's end to the ruined rocket works in the Harz forest, where the enemy had built machines that climbed the sky and came down on London-branch. My grandfather walked into that underground cathedral and found, lying on a workbench, an intact rocket gyroscope the size of a grapefruit, and stole it, and kept it on his dresser for the rest of his life, and would not, to his final day, say what became of the second one.

There was a monkey he killed, in the war. He told it flat, once, with his eyes on the ceiling: "There was a sergeant. He was doing something to a prisoner I will not describe to you, kid, because I want you to be able to sleep. I hit him with a tent stake. He got up wrong. That was that. Nobody ever asked me. I have never once been sorry." Then he was quiet a while, and said: "A man — a *monkey* — spends his whole life sure he's one kind of animal, and finds out in half a second he's another. Both kinds live in the same fur. That's the whole Torah, if you want it."

Then the Wild Girl of Saint-Nazaire, who became my grandmother.

He was twenty-six, stationed at the shattered submarine pens on the French coast, when she came over the wall of the naval hospital — literally over the wall, along a fallen crane, in a hospital gown, pursued by two orderlies — a French she-ape with wild dark fur and a laugh like a riot in a fruit market. She had been, before the war, famous in three towns as the girl raised by wolves, or by nothing at all — the story changed with the telling, and she changed it herself, and he loved her for it. He tackled her — professionally, he always claimed — and she bit him, and that, he said, was the courtship: the only woman in Europe who bit back.

"She had a snake in her," he said now, from the pillow, matter-of-fact. "You know that."

"A snake."

"In her belly. She believed it. Some witch business from her childhood, or from the camps, or from her own head — the asylum doctors had a word for it I never learned. She felt it move, kid. Coiled up under her ribs, she said, and it talked to her at night, in French, and told her she had no right to be happy. I told her: baby, that's not a snake, that's the war. She said: the war is over. I said: then it's a snake. That's marriage. You marry the monkey and the monkey's snake."

They came to America after the war, to the row houses of Baltimore, and my grandfather got the only work his hands would accept: tools, lathes, jet engines, and, in the great year of 1957, when the Russians put a satellite beeping over everybody's roofs, a job with the aerospace outfit in Florida. He was never an engineer — no degree, and a security file full of questions about that gyroscope — but he was the best bench hand they had, and he machined parts he was never allowed to know the purpose of, and came home at night with the moon out over the orange groves and stood in the yard with his face up like a dog.

The marriage fought. The snake got worse. There were years of flight — my grandmother taking my mother and vanishing to Atlantic City, to her sister's, to nowhere; then years of the state hospital at Crownsville, where my grandfather drove every Sunday, ninety miles each way, with a jar of the black cherries she loved, because she had stopped eating everything else. He never divorced her. Whatever else he was — a hard, silent, unemployable baboon, fired twice, jailed once (for rearranging a foreman's snout with a torque wrench, "a difference of engineering opinion") — he went to Crownsville every Sunday for eleven years.

"She asked me once, near the end," he said, from Florida, from the morphine, "whether the snake would have to come along, after — you know. After. Wherever she was going. And I told her — I thought fast, kid, you got to think fast with her — I told her: snakes can't cross salt water. Everybody knows that. It's why there's no snakes in Ireland. And she believed me, because she wanted to, and she put her hands on her belly and said, 'Well, then, I will go somewhere across the water.' And she died that autumn, quiet, with her hands on her belly, like she was holding a kit."

He wept then, at eighty-seven, without shame, the way old monkeys weep, everything at once: the war, the wall, the cherries.

The last week, he told me about my uncle — the strange one, the kit who built monsters: perfect scale models of two-headed crocodiles and five-legged goats and the atomic ants of the B-movies, balsa and wire and loving paint, hundreds of them, shelved floor to ceiling of his rented room. My uncle, who worked briefly at the same aerospace outfit and was let go, and — my grandfather did not like this chapter and told it only once — swallowed the bottle of his mother's old medicine on the night of a moon landing party, and was found, when they broke the door, lying among his monsters, asleep, having taken, it turned out, eleven too few. After which nobody in the family ever spoke of it, except the old man now, dying, to me: "He didn't want to die, kid. He wanted to be *found*. There's a difference. Ask me how I know."

On the last night, he asked to be carried out to the yard. The nurses said no; we did it anyway, with a blanket and the wheelchair, out onto the warm Florida grass, under the biggest moon of 1989 — huge, yellow-horned, low over the intercoastal, exactly the harbor moon of Baltimore, exactly the banana.

"There she is," he said. "Sixty years I have been telling them a monkey could go. They went, in '69, without me — wrong monkeys, but they went. Now." With the last of his grip he fished in the blanket's pocket and put a small brass thing in my palm: the gyroscope, or its heart, the size of a walnut, warm from his pocket. "They'll tell you to sell it. It's worth nothing anyway, without the rest, which is long gone. But it's the part that knows which way is up."

He died four days later, in his sleep, in Tampa, with his face turned to the window.

We did what the will said, and it was the only provision anybody argued about. We took a pinch of the ashes — the rest went to my mother — and we drove down to the big beach at Canaveral at midnight, and my son and I put the pinch into the payload tube of a model rocket my uncle had built thirty years before and never fired: twelve inches of painted balsa, red and silver, its fins perfect, a two-headed crocodile down the side.

We counted down from ten, the way you do, the way everybody does, three generations of us alone on that enormous dark beach — and the engine lit, and the rocket went up off our fingers with a hiss like the whole Troop War, up and up, wobbly, perfect, glowing, higher than the law allowed, trailing gold — and for one full half second, hanging at the top of its climb before the little parachute popped, the night had two moons.

The old man's glow, going one way. The big banana, hanging there, as always, for anybody willing to look up.

"Good boy," I told the rocket, uselessly, as it came down. "Good monkey."
