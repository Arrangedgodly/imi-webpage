# Anxious Treeple

*A parody of "Anxious People"*

A monkey walked into the Banque de Banane just before closing, wearing a ski mask with holes cut for her ears, carrying what appeared to be a pistol. It was, in fact, a slightly curved plantain, but the teller could not know that, and neither, honestly, could the robber, who had grabbed it in a panic that morning and only realized mid-heist that it was the wrong fruit entirely.

"Nobody go bananas!" she shouted. "This is a robbery! I want exactly six thousand five hundred 'nanas. No more. No less. I've done the math. It's two months' rent, and if I don't pay it, I lose the den, and if I lose the den, I lose custody of the twins."

"You're not very good at this," observed the teller, which was unprofessional, but accurate. In the robber's defense, the first bank she'd picked had no bananas at all, just leaf-currency, and she hadn't wanted to be rude about it.

The alarm went off anyway, because alarms do not care about your math. The robber fled downhill with the bag of 'nanas, into the residential canopy, and through the first open door she found — a treehouse viewing, mid-tour, with seven strangers and a plate of biscuits.

"Everyone sit down!" she cried. "This is a hostage situation! Oh no. I've made it a hostage situation, haven't I."

The hostages introduced themselves, because treeple are like that. Roger, an enormous baboon with a tool belt, was there to renovate; his wife Anna-Lena was there to apologize for him. "Load-bearing vines," Roger scoffed, rapping a limb. "No such thing. Give me a lever and an afternoon." Julia and Ro were there because they needed a nursery branch; Ro was so pregnant she'd had to be winched up the last ladder, and Julia kept introducing her as "my wife, who is growing a whole extra monkey on purpose." Zara, a snow macaque in a gray suit who worked upstairs at the bank saying no to people, stood apart, folding and unfolding a leaf she kept in her breast pocket. Estelle, the chatty grandmother selling the place, offered everyone juice. A man named Lennart, dressed as a fruit bat for reasons that took an hour to explain, had been hired to bid the price down.

He had been hired, specifically, by Roger, which Anna-Lena had arranged, which Roger did not know, which Anna-Lena also did not technically know she was going to confess, and did, within minutes, because that is the sort of hostage situation this was.

"You *hired* an actor?" Roger roared. "To lose auctions to us? For ten years?"

"He charges less than a therapist," said Anna-Lena, "and we've needed both."

Outside, Officer Jim, an old baboon with a megaphone, established contact, sighed, and was replaced at the megaphone by his son Jack, who had stormed two treehouses in his career and found both disappointing. Inside, the hostages helpfully critiqued the crime.

"You want a credible threat, dear," said Roger. "Say you'll knock down a wall every hour."

"Roger," said Anna-Lena. "We flip treehouses because if we ever stopped, we'd have to talk. Don't teach her our tricks."

Zara said nothing, because Zara had trained herself to say nothing at parties, at viewings, and at the bank, where the nothing she said most often was *no*.

Then Ro said, "Oh," in a small voice, and the monkey with the plantain put down the plantain and held her hand through every contraction, because it turns out there is no situation so terrible that a baby will schedule around it. Julia stopped arguing with the fruit bat about whether a nursery should face east. Even Roger put down his lever.

"Why our bank?" Zara asked quietly, between breath-counts.

"I came to your desk once," the robber admitted. "You said no."

Zara unfolded her leaf. Ten years ago, a young monkey ruined by a loan climbed to the middle of the Gorge Bridge. Jim, the negotiator, had talked with him for two hours — about figs, about weather, about nothing, because Jim understood that the last monkey on earth a jumper wants to hear from is one in a hurry. Then the wind moved, and the monkey let go. Jim never forgave the wind. Jack never forgave Jim for taking the bridge calls, and Jim never told him why he kept taking them. Zara, who had been the banker who said no, never forgave herself. The note she had carried ever since, folded to the size of a grass blade, read: *Nobody asked how I was doing until it was too late. Somebody ask the next one.*

"Consider yourself asked," Zara said. "How are you doing?"

The robber burst into tears, which everyone agreed was progress. Ro's grip did the rest; somewhere near midnight, mid-apology, a small wet howl joined the negotiation, and the first new hostage of the evening arrived weighing seven bananas and refusing all statements.

It was around then that the fruit bat removed his head, revealed himself as Estelle's husband Knut, and confessed that they weren't selling because they needed to; they were selling because the treehouse echoed since the children moved out, and Estelle cried in the pantry, and Knut had hoped that watching buyers be happy here might teach him how to let go. It had not. He was, he admitted, a fruit bat of habit.

"A whole marriage," said Anna-Lena, wonderingly, "and you never noticed the costume?"

"I noticed the costume," said Estelle. "I assumed it was a hobby. After forty years, dearest, you stop auditing."

What happened next, the police report never quite captured. The hostages traded clothes. A figure in Zara's coat descended the ladder among the "released hostages," who all gave identical statements, each one suspiciously fond. When Jack finally stormed the treehouse — past his father, who was in no hurry whatsoever — he found a fruit bat costume, an empty biscuit plate, a healthy newborn named Liv, and no robber anywhere. Jack stood in the middle of the nursery, holding the only leaf Zara had ever left behind, on which was written the statement she'd refused to give: *Ask them how they're doing. Then wait for the answer.* He gave it to his father. They read it in the car, together, and then they sat in the car for a long time, which is how baboons apologize.

The psychologist's interviews ran long. Roger and Anna-Lena decided to stop renovating and start talking; Lennart got a standing role in their marriage, at the rate of one dinner a month. Julia and Ro went home with Liv and the news that a nursery facing east is fine, actually, if everyone you love is in it. Zara retired from saying no and now says "let me see what I can do," which the bank considers a downgrade and the neighborhood considers a miracle. Estelle and Knut took the treehouse off the market and took on a tenant in their spare one instead — a young mother of twins, whose rent was set at one shared dinner every Sunday, plus juicy gossip.

"Summary of incident," Jim wrote at last. "No robber located. Hostages describe the evening as, quote, 'surprisingly lovely.' One civilian born on-scene, no complaints filed by her either. Recommend no further action. Also recommend the bank stop saying no quite so quickly."

Because the truth, as any of the treeple will tell you — over juice, over biscuits, over one more open viewing nobody attends — is that we are all just anxious treeple, holding on with one paw, doing our best. And doing your best is the most anyone can do.

Even a bank robber. *Especially* a bank robber.
