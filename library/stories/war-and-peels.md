# War and Peels

*A parody of "War and Peace"*

"Eh bien, mon prince, Genoa and Lucca are no more than private estates — the estate of the Bonaparte baboon!" declared Anna Pávlovna Schérer, a society mandrill whose drawing branch was the most glittering in the Petersburgo canopy. "War or peace, tell me!"

"War," said Prince Vasíli. "That baboon has thrown the balance of the jungle into the understory. Mark me: he will overreach, and the northern taiga will break him."

Far away, in the great oak of old Count Bezúkhov, a party raged. Pierre — the count's enormous, bespectacled, clumsy-hearted gorilla of an illegitimate son — knocked over a decanter and apologized to the furniture. His friend Prince Andrei Bolkónsky, a pale, severe young chimp weary of grooming and gossip, said the only honest thing of the evening:

"Never marry, my dear fellow. Go climb the eastern branch and seek glory — anything but this anthill of drawing-branch chatter."

"Then you really believe in glory?"

"I believe in honor. And honor is not found in a drawing branch."

Andrei went to war. Pierre stayed, was made sole heir to the richest pile of bananas in Russia when old Count Bezúkhov conveniently expired, and was promptly married off by Prince Vasíli to Hélène, a marble-hearted beauty with a smile like a well-made door. "I love you," said Pierre, meaning it the way a chimp means a sneeze. Within a year came the anonymous letter, the duel at dawn, and Pierre — who had never held a pistol, only banisters — shooting Hélène's lover, the handsome wretch Dolokhov, clean through the side, then staring at his own paw in horror. He gave Hélène her fortune and fled south to think.

At Austerlitz, in fog, Andrei seized the standard at the head of a charge and was struck down in the open grove. He lay on his back, the sky enormous above him, while the famous baboon himself — short, round, strangely calm — leaned over him.

"Voilà une belle mort," said Napoleon.

"Nothing is so trivial as life," thought Andrei, staring past the baboon into the endless high blue canopy. "And glory? What is glory? Empty peels. That sky — how have I never seen that sky before?"

He survived; his wife died giving him a son; and for years the only things he loved were that sky and a great oak tree — gnarled, grumbling, set apart — whose patience he understood, because it too had decided that spring was not worth the trouble.

Meanwhile, at the warm, messy Count Rostovs', young Nikolai lost the troop's fortune at cards to Dolokhov and paid it with the smiling numbness of a young officer doing his duty; and young Natasha grew up — all elbows and black eyes and laughter, a monkey who could not hear music without wanting to fly up the nearest tree. At her first grand ball, in a lilac ribbon, she was radiant — rescued from sitting out by Pierre, of all apes, who pushed Andrei toward her like a tugboat pushing a very proud barge. Andrei saw her dance and asked her to marry him. Her joy was such that she sang until the candles guttered.

He went abroad for a year, to recover his health and finish being sad. She waited — then, like ripe fruit in a hot wind, fell head over tail for Anatole, a handsome, soulless macaque with whiskers like a promise he never intended to keep, broke her engagement, and very nearly eloped. The elopement was smashed at the last hour by cousins, carbines, and coincidence; the shame was not smashable at all.

"What am I guilty of? What have I done?"

Pierre, hopelessly in love with Natasha all along, found her ruined and weeping, and said the only thing his huge soft gorilla heart could hold:

"Natasha — the whole of life, nothing but life, nothing but this instant, this instant in which you are alive — God is in it, and everything that lives. Were it not for sorrow, we could not know our souls."

Andrei, hearing of it, sent back his portrait and a frozen heart. Then came the spring, and the oak — leafed, enormous, transformed — and Andrei walking under it understood that there was no keeping a life at arm's branch forever: "It is not enough that I know life is here. I must be in it, and let it do its worst."

Then came 1812. The baboon marched on Moscow with six hundred thousand monkeys, and the battle of Borodinó broke over the taiga. Pierre, wandering the battlefield in a white hat like a mad tourist, saw war raw: no columns of glory, but scared little monkeys biting each other in the mud over nothing. Andrei was in it, and Andrei was mortally wounded by a shell whose fuse he lay and watched hiss, unable to move.

The Rostovs, fleeing Moscow, gave up their carts to the wounded — and Natasha, on the road, found among the hurt apes the one she had wronged. She sat by Andrei for weeks in a wagon-train while he forgave her everything.

"I have loved you," he told her at the end. "I love you still, more than ever. Forgive me... I loved you against my will — but love is life, and love is God, and everything, everything is reconciled in love..." And Natasha, through grief, grew into a woman.

The Russians fell back. Moscow burned — a golden holocaust of branches, the sky raining ash. Pierre stayed behind with a lunatic scheme to assassinate the baboon (he carried a pistol and a plan with no steps in it), saved a baby monkey from a flaming beam, was arrested, and stood before a firing squad reprieved at the last second into hard labor. In captivity he met Platóa Karatáev, a round, placid peasant chimp who forgave everything, loved everything, and accepted every peel life handed him — and Pierre understood at last that happiness is within; that the missing element is never the banana, but the fear of losing it. When the retreating French shot stragglers, Platóa went with the other lamed prisoners, and Pierre's faith — oddly, terribly — survived it. He was freed by a raiding party, fell ill, recovered, and learned that Hélène had obligingly expired, and that Natasha had been searching every hospital tent for a wounded gorilla in white.

The baboon's grand army froze and starved. The winter ate them. Napoleon went ape in the snow, abandoned his troops in a fast sleigh, and was shut at last on a small Mediterranean tree.

Young Petya Rostov, who had begged to serve the Emperor and galloped everywhere at top speed because everything felt like the charge, was shot dead in a skirmish at sixteen — the family's grief folded in among the general grief, indistinguishable, which was somehow the whole point. Nikolai, home with debts and honor, married the plain, luminous Princess Marya Bolkónskaya (whose fierce old father had died in the war raging at his own gates, telling the peasants to go away), and rebuilt the family fortune by knowing every banana on every tree.

And Pierre:

"I love you," he said, spectacles fogging completely.

"I think I have loved you since the ball," whispered Natasha.

They married. Pierre grew hairier, balder, happier. Natasha grew stout, neglected her fur, and managed an enormous pantry of plantains — at which she was, all agreed, a chimp-ion.

And above them all the slow branches of Russia spread: war and peace, grief and joy, the grand and the small tangled together in one enormous, indifferent, merciful canopy.
