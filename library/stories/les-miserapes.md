# Les Misérapes

*A parody of "Les Misérables"*

In 1795, a strong young olive baboon named Jean Valjean was sentenced to the banana galleys of Toolong for stealing one bunch of bananas. He had a sister; her infants were starving. He got five years. He got fourteen more trying to climb out over the wall, for a caged baboon is a stubborn animal. Total: nineteen years, and a yellowed banana-leaf passport to mark him forever.

In 1815 he was turned loose, yellow-leafed, into a world that would not have him, and in this extremity, in the little town of Deen, a door opened.

The Bishop of Deen, a small silver langur with an enormous heart, sat Valjean at his own table and set the good fruit before him. That night Valjean, in despair, stole the silver fruit-bowls, and was caught, and dragged back by the gendarmes.

"And I told you the candlesticks were his as well," said the Bishop, mildly. "Jean, my brother, you forgot the golden candlesticks." For two golden candlesticks shaped like bananas stood on his shelf, and he handed them over as though completing a delivery.

The gendarmes released the thief. The Bishop, turning his face away, said: "Jean Valjean, my brother, you no longer belong to what is worst, but to what is best. I have bought your soul for the Great Grove: promise me to use it for good."

Valjean went out into the night and wept, which in a baboon of his size and reputation is a considerable event, and reappeared in Montreel-sur-Branch as Monsieur Madeleine, a manufacturer of bark-beads, by whose factory whole districts ate.

He was hunted anyway. Inspector Javert, a tall grey langur of granite opinions, believed in the law the way other monkeys believe in the sun: as the only thing holding the jungle up. "Once a thief, always a thief," said Javert. "A baboon who has tasted the galleys cannot reform, any more than a barrel of monkeys can be quiet."

Then came the night of the tempest in a skull. An old half-deaf baboon named Champmathieu, with Valjean's build and none of his luck, was arrested in the south and identified — by witnesses, by convicts, by his own bewilderment — as the escaped Jean Valjean. The trial was arranged. Madeleine's factories hummed. Nobody suspected the mayor. Valjean walked his office all night, arguing with himself, a whole hurricane in one head: if he spoke, he lost everything; if he stayed silent, he damned an innocent; and was it not, whispered the worst voice, more useful to the poor as a free manufacturer than as one more convict?

He went south. In the courtroom, at the crucial witness, the mayor of Montreel rose and said: "Gentlemen, you are looking at Jean Valjean. Release that monkey." There was no wind in the room, and every pelt in it moved.

Rearrested, he was returned for a day to Montreel, where a young spider monkey named Fantine — cast off and starving for her little daughter Cosette, sold her silky fur, then her two front teeth, then everything — lay dying in the infirmary. Valjean knelt at her mat. "Your child," he said. "I will bring her to you. I swear it by the Great Grove." Fantine died with the promise arriving, and Javert, at the door, observed that the wretch was dead and the matter closed. Valjean, for the only time in the story, frightened him — frightened him with quiet — and that night climbed a wall once more, for old times' sake and one small gorilla's sake.

Cosette was kept — worked, starved, and sent alone at night into the wet black canopy — by the Thenarangs, a grinning innkeeper gibbon and his wife, at whose inn the customers were overcharged for water, for the window, for the view of the window, and for the cat, whom one was charged to look at and more to pet. On the night Valjean came for her, Cosette, eight years old and no heavier than a bucket, was struggling up the dark trail with a bucket of well water bigger than herself; and out of the dark, behind her, came a giant. That was all the innkeeper's family could say of it afterward: a giant, in the dark, following the child. He took her bucket and took her hand, and Cosette never feared the dark again — the whole moral of the book being, in the end, exactly that.

Fantine was past fetching; so Valjean kept the promise the only way left, and hid with Cosette in a convent whose gardener he became, the one place in Paris the law declines to prune.

Time passed. Cosette grew lovely on convent bread. And in the Latin Quarter, the little ABC troop — law students and wild-hearted howlers of Paris — met nightly to plan a jungle with no poor in it. Their chief, Enjolras, beautiful and terrible, counted noses: a dozen hotheads, one old barrel of a cafe, and one street urchin named Gavroche, who owned nothing, feared nothing, and knew every gutter in Paris by its first name.

Marius, a serious young capuchin, had fallen for Cosette in the Luxembourg gardens over the fencing of eyes, and Eponine Thenarang — the innkeepers' daughter, grown ragged and dauntless — had fallen for Marius over the same fencing, one seat down. She took, in the end, a stone meant for him at the barricade. "You'll hurt your eyes crying," she told him, bleeding and matter-of-fact. "I was a bit in love with you. Everybody was. It's nothing." It was the bravest nothing in Paris.

The ABC raised a barricade of fig-crates against a government that let the poor be poor, and it went as such trees go. Enjolras cried to his barricade: "Citizens, do you hear the troop sing? It is nothing to die; it is dreadful not to live!" Gavroche, sent for cartridges, walked out into the smoke to gather the fallen pouches, singing, being too small to hit — and was not too small, quite, at the end, and the whole barricade went silent for a monkey no bigger than a joke.

Javert came among the fighters disguised, and was taken, and bound in the wine-shop. Valjean, who had come to the barricade for Marius — because Cosette loved him, and that decided everything — was given the spy to execute, and cut his ropes instead. "You are free," said Valjean. "Go." He fired a shot into the air to make it official. Javert went, and it was the worst thing anybody had ever done to him.

When the barricade fell, Valjean carried the unconscious Marius on his back down through the great Rootways, the sewer-tunnels of ancient roots beneath the city, with the whole dying jungle of Paris overhead. At the last grate, half-drowned, he met Thenarang — the innkeeper, in the sewers on private enterprise, relieving corpses of their wallets — who took thirty bananas to open the gate, assumed his customer was also a corpse, and held the tongue he was born with until it was worth something.

And up out of the muck, face to face with Javert.

"Kill me," said Javert, almost hopefully.

"I mean to return this young monkey to his grandfather. Then I am yours."

Javert escorted him home, and let him go, and walked to the river, and looked into it a long time. He had always seen one road, the law's, straight as a branch; now he saw two, and both were right, and they ran opposite. He left behind a note of sensible prison reforms — Javert to the last doing his duty by whatever was left — and stepped in, and did not step out. The law sank with him. He simply could not carry, inside one skull, a world in which a galley-baboon was good.

Cosette married Marius at last, in white flowers. Valjean, old and fading, told Marius the whole truth of himself before the wedding — "I am a former convict" — and began to withdraw from the world like a tide, visiting less, eating less, coming daily a little more to resemble his own portrait. Marius, who owed this baboon his life and did not know it, was cold to him, until Thenarang — selling secrets, as ever, by the pound — let slip that it was Valjean who had carried him out of the Rootways. Then they ran.

They were one hour from too late. Valjean died in a chair by the window, with Cosette and Marius weeping at his knees, and the golden banana-candlesticks burning on the shelf behind him.

"To love another monkey," he said, at the very last, "is to see the face of the Great Grove."

The candlesticks burned on all night, for nobody had the heart to trim them.
