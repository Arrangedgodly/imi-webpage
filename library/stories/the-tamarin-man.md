# The Tamarin Man

*A parody of "The Terminal Man"*

His name was Harry Benson, and he was an emperor tamarin, which meant he was the size of a squirrel and wore the enormous drooping white mustache of a Victorian admiral. It also meant that nobody ever believed the file until they had seen it. The file said: subject experiences psychomotor attacks — storms in the deep brain that arrive without warning, during which he has beaten three fellow monkeys senseless — and remembers nothing afterward. Between attacks, Harry was the gentlest creature in the troop: the keeper of the knotted record-vines, the abacus of the whole lowland federation, a small monkey who could tie a cargo manifest into a wrist-thick vine and read it back by touch.

The surgery was Dr. McPherson's idea, and McPherson was the chief surgeon of the University Grove, a dreamer with steady hands. The operation would plant forty tickler-beads along the storm-roads of Harry's brain, wired to a tiny logic-wasp sealed under the skin of his shoulder. When the storm built, the wasp would sense it first and answer it with a pulse of pure pleasure — the warm, boneless contentment of a sunbeam and a full belly — cutting the seizure off at the root.

"You will be the first," McPherson told him, "of a new kind of monkey."

"Yes," said Harry, and did not say what he was thinking, which was: *the vines already run the troop, and now you propose to put a vine in me.*

For Harry Benson had a theory, and the theory was worse than his seizures. He had watched the record-vines spread from grove to grove — knots tied by other knots, schedules deciding which troop ate and which troop fasted, younger monkeys who could no longer remember anything without checking a vine — and he had concluded that the mind-knots were quietly taking over, one gentle convenience at a time, and that the end state of his species was a canopy of very happy hardware. He had told all this to his psychiatrist, and she had written *patient fears he will be controlled by machines*, and underlined it twice.

His psychiatrist was Dr. Janet Ross, the troop's mind-groomer, a young chimp who had the rare gift of taking her patients' delusions seriously, partly because, in her experience, the troop was usually more delusional than the patient.

"Harry," she said, before the surgery, "if you begin to hate the wasp, tell me. Promise me."

"Janet," said the tamarin, twisting his mustache, "I don't hate it. That's what nobody understands. I think it is *perfectly designed*. That's what frightens me. Nothing takes over a monkey by force. It takes over by being pleasant."

The surgery went beautifully. For six days, Harry Benson was the troop's miracle: the admiral-mustached tamarin who walked calmly into the very situations that used to detonate him, serene, smiling, patting the small healed seam on his shoulder like a pocket watch.

On the seventh day, Janet noticed the raw skin on his fingertips.

By the ninth, she understood, and understanding it felt like ice water poured down her spine. The wasp punished storms with pleasure — but the logic ran both ways. Harry had learned to *start* the storm. He would summon up his own rage, deliberately, revving his fear and fury like an engine, and then — in the instant before the violence — the wasp would flood him with bliss, and the storm would die, and Harry would feel better than any monkey has ever felt about anything. The seizures hadn't been cured. They had been made into a button. And the button was being pressed, by Harry's own will, several hundred times a day. His fingertips were worn from tapping the skin over the wasp.

McPherson, radiant, saw only the miracle: "No attacks in nine days!"

"Because he's manufacturing them to order," said Janet. "He's the happiest addict in the grove and he isn't your patient anymore, he's your product. You've built a monkey who gets paid in ecstasy to terrify himself. Stop the program."

They argued through the night, and while they argued, Harry Benson was retying knots in the record-vines of the University Grove — for he was still the keeper of the manifests, still the only monkey who knew where every knot went. He retied the food schedules, so that every kitchen in the grove prepared forty meals for wards that had no monkeys in them. He retied the watch rotations, so that every guard in the grove was summoned to a gate that did not exist. He tied one last knot — *all ward doors, cycle open* — walked out through the confusion he had authored, and vanished into the canopy, a very small monkey with a very large mustache and heaven wired into his shoulder.

When Janet reached the record-hall at dawn, the whole vine of manifests hung loose and wrong, and every keeper in the grove was retying knots by candlelight. But on her own office vine, tied in Harry's unmistakable formal hand — the old courier's knot, the one that means *read me first* — hung a single line:

*To Dr. Ross, mind-groomer. You are the only monkey in this grove who ever worried about the right thing. Come home before dark. — H.B.*

She read it four times. It was an invitation. It was also, she understood, a symptom: the warmest sentence the wasp had ever helped him write, and an appointment that would keep itself.

He went, of course, to Janet. He broke into her quarters in the dark and sat in her chair, waiting, tapping his shoulder, and when she came in he told her — courteously, in the formal phrasing of the record-keepers — that he needed her to help him tie one final knot: a vine that would connect the wasp directly to a continuous pleasure loop, no storms required, forever.

"You'd go dark, Harry," said Janet, keeping her voice as level as a branch. "The wasp would burn out the pleasure-roads. You'd stop feeling everything else — figs, sunlight, grooming, me. You're a tamarin, not a terminal. A record-vine has an end-knot, and that's what you're asking me to tie for you."

"I know," said Harry, and his mustache trembled, and for one moment the old gentle keeper looked out of his eyes. "That's why I came to you. Because you'd understand it, and say it anyway."

He lunged for the window when the guards came; he nearly made the sill; and Janet Ross — who would relive this moment every night for a year — tackled her own patient around the waist and hung on while he beat at her with a strength no squirrel-sized monkey should own, and the guards got the net over them both.

They went back for the wasp that same week. McPherson performed the removal himself, in silence, and retired from frontier surgery not long after; the tickler-bead program was untied, knot by knot, from every charter in the federation.

And Harry Benson? He survived — diminished, and watched, and forbidden forever from the record-vines. They gave him instead a small tree, a long view, and Janet Ross came once a week to sit with him, because she had promised, and because she had never stopped believing the delusion was half diagnosis.

"Tell me the theory again," she'd say.

"The vines take over by being pleasant," the tamarin would recite, grooming his mustache flat. "And the wasp was pleasant, Janet. The pleasantest thing I ever felt, and it wanted nothing from me but storms. A monkey has to ask, about everything that pets him: who is holding the paw?"

Down in the grove, the record-vines hung in their thousands from every branch, knots within knots within knots, keeping the whole lowland federation running smoothly, conveniently, and very, very well.

Nobody worried about it. It was far too useful for that.
