# Norwegibbon Wood

*A parody of "Norwegian Wood"*

I was thirty-seven years old, and riding a 747 somewhere over the Alps, when the cabin speakers played an orchestral cover of "Norwegibbon Wood."

The arrangement was sweet and slight, almost Muzak, a little orchestra of strings making itself pleasant. And yet the moment it began, the plane's cabin tilted, without tilting, and I was nineteen again, and the air smelled of rain on the shoulders of a girl.

If Gibbons have one law, it is this: we duet. A gibbon finds one branch-mate and sings the same song, all their lives, across the whole green world, each of them answering the other by name. Nanako never found her duet. And neither, though it took me years to hear the silence of it, did I.

It was 1969. I was a first-year macaque at a university in Tokyo, and I had a friend named Kizungi who was brilliant and sudden, and who, in the spring when we were seventeen, gassed himself in his parents' garage with the motor running, leaving no note and no explanation and a me-shaped hole in the world.

Kizungi had a girlfriend, Nanako. Beautiful Nanako, with her hair tied back and her eyes that looked at you like weather arriving. After the funeral we drifted apart the way two leaves in the same eddy drift apart. Then, a year later, on a Sunday in May, we met by chance on the subway platform, and went walking, and kept walking — Sunday after Sunday, along the canals, across the pedestrian overpasses, talking about nothing important. Which was the point. Everything important was in the walking.

Gibbons are supposed to swing, that's all people know about us. Nanako walked. She walked like someone in no hurry to arrive anywhere, and I slowed my swinging to her walk, and we lost entire afternoons that way, in a meadow by the reservoir, the grass going gold, the city humming beyond the trees.

On her twentieth birthday — we celebrated it, the two of us, with cake in my little room — something in her gave way. She wept. She wept the way it rains in the mountains, silently and completely, and I held her, and that night, for the first time, I saw her without her scarf, and we stayed tangled together till dawn without sleeping, and her crying came and went all night like a tide.

I understood nothing. I understood only that I loved her, in the way you love a door that is closing.

After that night she vanished from Tokyo. Months later a letter came, thin as a leaf: she was at a sanatorium in the hills above Kyoto, a place called the Ami Treehouse. Come and visit, she wrote, if you want. The last sentence was crossed out. I went anyway.

The Ami Treehouse sat among cedars, and in it, monkeys who could not quite manage the world were allowed to say so out loud, and to be slow without being blamed. I met Nanako's roommate there: Reiko, a rhesus of about forty, wiry and kind and ironic, with a face like a crumpled paper map of everywhere she'd been. She'd been a pianist once; her history had a cruelty in it that I still don't repeat, because it wasn't mine to carry. She'd been in and out of places like Ami for years, and she'd made her peace the way you make any peace: badly, and then again, and then it holds.

In the evenings the three of us sat around the kerosene stove, and Reiko played folk songs on her guitar with a cigarette going in the corner of her mouth, and the valley went dark below like an ocean going to sleep.

Whenever she played "Norwegibbon Wood," Nanako stopped talking.

"Before, I could listen to it and it was just a song," she said once, looking at the lamp. "Now when I hear it, I feel like I'm in a deep, dark wood. It's quiet and damp and there's no path, and somewhere in it I've lost something, and I already know I'm never going to find it."

"You can walk out of a wood," Reiko said, gently, changing chords.

Nanako smiled a little, and said nothing, because we all three knew which wood she meant, and that the exits were not currently marked.

That autumn, back in Tokyo, I met Midori.

Midori was a green monkey — literally: a vervet the color of new leaves, with a bob of black hair and an expression permanently set to mischief — and she sat next to me in a lecture on the history of drama, and stole my notes, and then my lunches, and then, somehow, my free time. She talked like a kettle at full boil. She'd nursed her mother through a long dying and was now doing the same for her father, and she'd decided, on principle, to have fun anyway, out of spite, and it was the bravest thing I ever watched anyone do.

"I'm a greedy monkey," she told me once, matter-of-factly. "When I'm hungry I eat, when I'm lonely I say so, when the world's on fire I go watch it. If I love somebody I want everything from them, and if they can't give everything, they can get lost. That's not asking too much. That's just refusing to ask too little."

One afternoon a fire broke out two streets from the campus, and instead of evacuating we climbed to her roof with cold beer and watched the firemen, and she sang songs from old movies with the wrong lyrics, and the smoke drifted over us, and she suddenly kissed me, quickly, like taking a splinter out of my heart and not letting me see it first.

She asked me if I loved her. And I — I had letters in my desk from a girl in a wood, and I said something tepid and true and useless, and Midori looked at me as if I'd handed her half a banana.

I wrote to Nanako every week. Her letters got shorter. Her ups got shorter and her downs got deeper, and in her last winter she went out one morning, alone, into the cedar wood behind the Treehouse, and she did not come back.

They found her at the foot of a tree, and I will write the rest of this paragraph the way Reiko said it should be said: Nanako died. It was terrible. The end.

I did not go to the funeral. I packed a rucksack and walked north instead, up the coast, for a month, sleeping in ferry stations and drinking, going nowhere on purpose. Somewhere along that coast an old fisherman took me in for two nights, fed me fish and rice, asked nothing, and on the last morning said only: "Don't judge yourself too hard. And don't judge the dead either. They're both just trying to get by." Then he gave me dried fish for the road, as if I were eight years old, and I wept in the doorway of his shop like a child, and it was the beginning of my coming back.

In the spring, Reiko came to Tokyo, carrying her guitar and the whole of Nanako's record collection. "We're going to hold a funeral," she announced. "Just us two. It's the only kind worth having."

We played every song Nanako ever loved, on a portable record player, in my empty room, from morning to night, "Norwegibbon Wood" over and over until the walls knew it by heart. We grieved until we were hollowed and clean. That night, one time, with all the grief still on us, Reiko and I held each other — two castaways sharing warmth, nothing more, and sadder than anything — and in the morning she got on a train to Asahikawa, to teach music at a little school where nobody knew her history, and to write me long letters for years.

After the train pulled out, I stood in the station and realized I was starving, and that being starving, after that year, felt almost like luck.

I found a phone box and called Midori.

I wanted to tell her everything: the wood, the fire, the coast, the songs. The line clicked and hummed, and then her voice, small and clear and green as the first of May: "You never call. So? Say something."

I opened my mouth. Everything I had to tell her was too much and too late and absolutely required.

"Midori," I said. "I need to talk to you. Can we meet?"

There was a pause, and I could hear her deciding, and the whole city going about its business in the rain.

"Where are you now?" Midori asked.

I looked around the phone box. The glass was fogged. Beyond it, people flowed past under umbrellas, a blur of gray coats and gray weather, and I did not recognize the street, or the corner, or the light. I was somewhere in Tokyo, in the rain, at the age of twenty, with one friend dead by her own hand and one walking off to the north, holding the last warm thing I had left, which was this phone.

"Where are you now?" Midori asked again, and she sounded, for the first time since I'd known her, afraid.

And I looked around, and around, and around, and I did not know where I was.
