# Loris and Ladies

*A parody of "Lords and Ladies"*

It was a fine midsummer in the mountain kingdom of Lancre, and Magrat Garlick, the youngest witch, was about to marry King Verence, which meant dress fittings while everyone pretended not to notice that the ring of standing stones called the Dancers was humming.

King Verence, for his part, had planned the wedding as a milestone of enlightened monarchy, with printed pamphlets on crop rotation between the courses and a lecture on the modern dairy for afterwards. Lancre received this news with its traditional enthusiasm, which is to say the torches and pitchforks were kept where the wind couldn't reach them.

The stones were a boundary marker — not between fields, but between the worlds. And on midsummer eve, when the gaps between things grew thin, out came the Lorises and Ladies.

People thought lorises were cute. The loris court were beautiful the way an avalanche is beautiful: enormous eyes, soft fur, and a glamour that poured off them like syrup and got into the head, so that a grown monkey who looked too long would smile and smile and step forward to be bitten. They had venom. They had time. They had been thrown out of Lancre once before by a witch named Granny Weatherwax.

Not everyone had learned this. There was, that summer, a girl called Diamanda, seventeen, wearing black lace on principle, who had taken to leaving little offerings by the Dancers and saying loudly in the tavern that the lori-folk were simply *misunderstood*.

"They're only half-tame if you feed them, girl," said Nanny Ogg, who knew about cats.

Granny was old, iron-grey, and so proud she combed her fur with a hairbrush made of consequences. Nanny Ogg, her oldest friend, was a fat jolly macaque with a pint and most of the kingdom's gene pool traced vaguely to her youth.

"They're coming back, Esme," said Nanny. "I felt it in my waterfall. And there's a crop circle in the upper field — perfectly round, mind."

"They always did like showing off," said Granny.

"There's worse. Someone's been telling Diamanda how lovely the lori-folk are."

"Aye. They *are* lovely. That's the trouble. Lorises — elves — call them what you like, Gytha. They're lovely the way a snake's eye is lovely. Cats is *kind* compared to them. A cat'll at least kill you before it plays with you."

That evening, up on the hills, Jason Ogg the blacksmith and the Lancre Morris Men did an old thing quietly: they danced the Dark Morris, the winter dance, round the Dancers themselves — boots stamping, iron bells on, dancing it backwards to bind the gaps. The stones hummed like wet claws drawn down glass. It helped. It was not, on its own, going to be enough.

Jason had, that same week, done a thing he did not like to discuss. A pale beast had been led to his forge at dusk — tall as a house, one horn, eyes like frostbitten stars — and the oldest rule of the forge is that Jason Ogg shoes anything brought to him, anything at all, and the rule knows iron, and iron knows the fae. He had nailed four cold shoes on the King's own unicorn, and it had stood there afterwards, breathing steam, suddenly and furiously *calm*, like a king forced into sensible footwear. It did not forgive. It did not forget. But its hooves hurt, and that was a start.

While Archchancellor Ridcully — who had once, sixty years ago, courted a young Esme Weatherwax and still kept the wound — came puffing up the mountain for the wedding beer, Magrat went to the Dancers alone.

The Loris King came to meet her: slow, silver, huge-eyed, venomous.

"Little queen," he said, in a voice like fur being stroked the wrong way. "Your kingdom is *ours*. We have been so patient. Come and adore me."

"I'm getting married tomorrow, actually," said Magrat, and stood her ground, which took the kind of courage that does not come in gowns.

Ridcully, meanwhile, took tea with Granny in her garden, and they rowed about the past the way old soldiers talk about old wars.

"You could have had the whole university in your pocket, Esme," he said.

"You could have had the whole mountainside, Mustrum. One of us was bound to be a fool about it." She poured. He drank. They discussed, at length and with feeling, the weather.

The invasion came at the wedding. The court poured through the stones — the Ladies with their beauty and their teeth, the King riding his pale enormous beast — and found Diamanda waiting for them in her black lace, because she had gone up there to be misunderstood at.

They had misunderstood her right back, and then they had emptied her. The guards found her at the churchyard wall, grey-faced, still smiling the terrible syrupy smile of someone being adored to death. Magrat hauled her inside and wedged her behind the beer barrels before the doors burst open — and it occurred to her, later, that it was the first time in her life she had told a younger girl what to do, and the girl had done it.

And Granny Weatherwax was, at that moment, not there. She was *borrowing*, her mind out over Lancre in her bees, her body cold and still by the garden gate — the kind of thing that gives people Ideas. Two of the Ladies circled the still body, giggling.

"Finish her while she's out," said one.

"No," said the King, and there was something in his voice that was, for the first time, not beautiful. "She'll wake. They always leave a bit of her watching."

They were right. And then Granny Weatherwax walked in through the great hall doors, grey-faced and furious, the fury that had been saving itself.

"You'll mind your manners in my country," she said. "Ye gods, I *hate* the fae. All glamour and no gumption."

The Loris King moved like syrup with murder in it — and Granny Weatherwax, who had never in her life fought fair when a fight was worth winning, head-butted him. With an iron hat-brim, at speed. The glamour cracked like a mirror. Lorises cannot abide cold iron, and Granny had spent the afternoon working the castle's old cage-wire into every horseshoe and nail she could find.

Then Magrat came down the stairs.

She had found the secret crypt and the armor of Queen Ynci — iron, real iron, skirted with cage-links — the queen the historians had all agreed never existed, which is the most satisfying kind of ancestor. It fit. The soft herbal witch with the frizzy fur stood in the hall with a spiked iron besom in her hands, and the Ladies found something new to be afraid of.

"Everyone was always telling me I was too soft," said Queen Magrat, advancing. "*Soft.*"

The battle of Lancre great hall was brief, loud, and unfair: iron against glamour. The Ladies' beauty kept slipping, like fur coming out in clumps. One of them turned on Magrat with the full syrup of her smile, and Magrat, who had spent thirty years being told to be nicer, hit her with the flat of the besom and told her, in a small clear voice, exactly where to put her loveliness. Nanny Ogg deployed a keg of her scorpion — the drink — as a weapon of mass distraction, and a good time was had by all on one side of the hall. The King's horn of plenty tipped over once more, pouring out endless fruit, gratuitous — the loris gift that costs a world — and not one monkey in the hall moved a paw toward it.

The Loris King was driven back to the stones, and the Dancers were bound again with iron nails, well hammered in, while the Morris Men danced guard in their bells.

And then, because the kingdom could not possibly waste a good wedding, Verence married Magrat that evening, in her armor, because there wasn't time to change.

They danced until dawn. Granny sat to one side, combing out her fur.

"Esme," said Nanny, offering her the scorpion, "you look terrible."

"I'm getting too old for this," said Granny. "Folk'll be wanting me to do it all again." She permitted a quarter of a smile. "Well. It keeps the fur from going grey."

It was already grey. It had always been grey. That, in Lancre, was how you knew who had won.
