# The Guernsey Literary and Banana Peel Pie Society

*A parody of "The Guernsey Literary and Potato Peel Pie Society"*

**From Dawsey Adams, Guernsey, to Miss Juliet Ashton, London, January 1946**

Miss Ashton,

I am writing to you because of your book — *The Selected Essays of Charles Lamb* — which came into my hands by way of a box of volumes that washed up on our shore during the war. Your name was written inside the cover, so it must once have been yours, and I have come to the end of it and am sorry to be there.

Could you tell me where I might find more by this Mr. Lamb? We have no bookshop in Guernsey now, and no books to speak of either. I would gladly pay the postage.

I am a farmer, better with pigs than with letters, so I will sign off.

Yours faithfully,
Dawsey Adams

**From Juliet Ashton, London, to Sidney Stark, Publisher**

Sidney,

You will not believe the letter I have received. A farmer in Guernsey found my old Lamb and wrote to ask for more, in a pawwriting so upright and honest it made me ashamed of every book I have abandoned halfway.

I am sending him my other Lamb. I am also out of ideas, out of money, and out of patience with the publicity tour for *Izzy Bickerstaff Goes Ape*, so do not nag me about the next book. I don't have a next book. I have a farmer who likes Charles Lamb, and that feels like enough.

J.

**From Juliet to Dawsey Adams**

Mr. Adams,

Your Lamb is on its way. Now — you cannot leave a writer dangling. What is this box of washed-up books? And what did Guernsey read during the Occupation?

Also, a bookseller in Town tells me there exists on your island something called the Guernsey Literary and Banana Peel Pie Society. I have been awake three nights wondering. Is it true? What, for pity's sake, is a banana peel pie?

Yours,
Juliet Ashton

**From Dawsey Adams to Juliet Ashton**

Miss Ashton,

The society is true, and it began with a pig.

It was 1941, the island was occupied, and we were hungry in the way you city monkeys never had to be. My pig — I will not say where she was kept, for some laws outlast the lawmakers — grew fat and secret. One night a few of us gathered to roast her behind drawn curtains, stayed too late, and broke the curfew coming home.

The patrol stopped us, and their officer asked where we had been at that hour. Elizabeth McKenna — it was Elizabeth who saved us, as she always did — said, instantly, that we had been at the meeting of our literary society, and had simply lost track of time discussing books, as readers do.

The officer was a lonely man, I think. He said he had not known Guernsey had a literary society, and that he would like to attend the following week. And then he let us go.

So we had to become one by Thursday. None of us had read a book in years. We invented book reports in three days. Will Thisby's report on *David Copperfield* — in which he swore the book proved you should never marry your aunt — was so memorably wrong that I have never dared read the actual novel since, in case Will is disappointed. When the officer came, we fed him, because a guest is a guest, and the only thing left in the larder was bananas. Amelia saved the peels for the pie, for the peels are the best part — the banana is mere padding — and Will baked them into his famous pie, and the officer ate two slices and said German literature had grown sentimental, and that was our first meeting.

We kept meeting, even after it stopped being a lie. I will tell you the rest when you ask.

Yours faithfully,
Dawsey Adams

**From Isola Pribby to Juliet Ashton**

Miss Ashton! ISOLA HERE. I am the society's chief pie-maker now that Will is gone, and everyone else writes slow. Enclosed is the recipe, so you needn't wrestle me for it:

BANANA PEEL PIE. Take peels of bananas, the riper the better. Boil them till they surrender. Mash with a raw egg if you have one, pretend you have sugar, and bake on a shell of potato peel. Eat while telling yourself it is banoffee. Do not die of despair, as despair ruins the flavor.

Also I have taken up reading since the society, starting with *Jane Eyre*, which I keep by my bed in case of fires and fits of loneliness. Also I make medicines from nettle and rum. Write to me if your publisher friend is ever constipated.

Yours,
Isola Pribby

**From Amelia Maugery to Juliet Ashton**

Dear Miss Ashton,

You should hear about Elizabeth from one who loved her, and not only the comic parts.

Elizabeth was the founder of everything here, and of Kit, her daughter, whose father was the German officer Christian — and I know what you will do with your face when you read that, Miss Ashton, so undo it. He was gentle, he drowned in the spring of '43, and Kit is the best of both.

Elizabeth was arrested in the autumn of '44. There was a Polish boy, Peter, a slave worker on the fortifications. Elizabeth hid him, fed him, and when they came for him, she stood in the door and struck the officer across the face. I have known braver deeds, but never a braver monkey. They took her away that night. She put Kit into my arms and said, "Keep her busy, Amelia, or she'll take up the piano."

We keep her chair at every meeting.

Come and see us.

Affectionately yours,
Amelia Maugery

**From Juliet to Sidney Stark**

Sidney,

I am going to Guernsey. Yes, in April, and no, Markham Reynolds may not come. Tell him I have discovered I would rather be a poor monkey on an island full of readers than a rich one at his table, and that his yacht smells of ambition.

They sent me their letters, Sidney — the whole society, book by book, monkey by monkey. Eben the fisherman read Shakespeare to his grandson by candle-curfew and wept at *The Tempest* because "there was a boy on a rock, and I knew him." Isola asks me whether Mr. Rochester could have been saved by nettle tea. And Dawsey writes to me about Lamb the way other men write about the war — carefully, and holding something back.

I think there is a book here. I know there is a book here. I am terrified of writing it, which is how I know.

J.

**From Juliet to Sidney Stark, from Guernsey, June**

Sidney,

I have been on the island a month and I cannot write fast enough. I spend my mornings in Amelia's kitchen with Kit, who is four and entirely Elizabeth's — she interrogated me on whether London monkeys truly sleep indoors — and my afternoons walking the cliffs with Dawsey, who talks the way he farms: slowly, thoroughly, leaving the ground better than he found it.

I have been a fool all my life about what I wanted. I wanted to be read, and I thought that meant being famous, and it does not. It means a farmer on a cold island holding a book of Lamb's essays like a warm stone and being *less alone*. We are not in the literature business, Sidney. We are in the less-alone business.

Also, I ate the pie. Three slices. Do not tell Isola, but I would defend the recipe to the death.

J.

**From Sidney Stark to Juliet Ashton**

Juliet,

I have news, plainly, for there is no kind way by tram.

A letter has reached Amelia from France, from a woman named Remy, who was imprisoned with Elizabeth. Remy writes that Elizabeth died this spring, in the camp, of fever and of refusing to save her own ration at another monkey's expense. She was, Remy says, "the one of us the guards could not make small." Her last message was for Kit: *Tell her I was busy, but I am coming home in my own way, and mind her grammar.*

I am so sorry, my dear. Come home if you want. Or stay. But write the book.

Sidney

**From Juliet to Sidney Stark, September**

Sidney,

It is done, or near enough: *The Guernsey Literary and Banana Peel Pie Society* — letters and all, and Elizabeth's chair on the cover.

And the other news is that I am married. Dawsey and I were wed on Saturday in Amelia's orchard, the entire society present, Isola weeping into her medicine, and Kit gave me away. She is ours now, Sidney — formally, legally, ours. She calls me Juliet and corrects my grammar, and I would not trade one correction.

The society meets on Thursdays still. Last week was Eben on *The Tempest*, again, and we let him weep as long as he liked, and Isola's pie was served with the new peels of peace.

I came to this island for the sake of a book I found by accident, and it turns out that is how all the best things arrive: washed up, water-stained, with somebody's name inside the cover.

Send paper. Come for the harvest. We are, all of us, less alone than we were.

Juliet (Adams)
