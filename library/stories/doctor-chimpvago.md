# Doctor Chimpvago

*A parody of "Doctor Zhivago"*

They walked and walked behind the body of the old she-chimp, singing "Eternal Preening," and at the tail of the procession trotted her son, Yuri Chimpvago, eleven years old and already half a poet. When the last fig leaf had fallen on the mound, the troop lawyers descended, and Yuri was carried off to Monkeyscow, into the house of the Groomekos — good, serious monkeys of the old northern troop who groomed their guests thoroughly and spoke of revolution the way one speaks of weather: always coming, never quite arriving.

There was one other guardian, half visible: Evgraf, Yuri's half-brother, a great gray monkey who appeared out of snowstorms three times in Yuri's life, always at the exact hour of need, like a wolf who had taken vows.

He grew up half doctor, half versifier. He could set a broken tail with one paw and dash off an ode to bananas with the other, which everyone agreed was an eccentric division of talents. When Tonya's mother lay dying, she called Yuri to her: "Yura, promise me — if anything happens, you will look after Tonya?" He promised; a promise made over a dying monkey binds like bark. He married Tonya Groomeko, gentle and steady as a low branch, half vow and half sentence, his prime mate in the eyes of the troop and the canopy, and for a while the boughs held still.

Others entered the story sideways, as they do. There was a girl named Lara Antipawva, daughter of a dressmaker of the Monkeyscow outskirts, upon whom a rich baboon called Komarsky had fastened when she was hardly fledged — the sort of old ape who ruins a young monkey and calls it generosity. The day the dressmaking tree passed to new creditors, Komarsky patted her paw over the account books and murmured, "How can you speak of money at such a moment?" — meaning that he had just sold it. There was Pasha, a soft-spoken monkey who loved Lara hopelessly and honorably; and there was the revolution, which was hiring.

At a Christmas party at the Sventitskys' — the whole Monkeyscow troop in candles and fur — Yuri, then a student, watched a beautiful unknown she-monkey sweep in and fire a pistol at a fat baboon, winging a prosecutor of the state instead. It was Lara; the baboon was Komarsky; and Yuri went home and wrote poems to a monkey whose name he did not know, which is the poet's method.

Then the Great Jungle Revolution came, and the whole canopy went bananas. The old silverbacks were driven from the tall trees; new commissars — young, loud, freshly deloused — swung through the branches shouting that all figs belonged to everyone. The Great Canopy War followed, as night follows a bad grooming, and Yuri was conscripted as troop doctor and sent to a fig-grove hospital that stank of rot and brandy.

There he found Lara Antipawva again, a nurse with gray eyes and a history. Everyone in the ward knew pieces of it: how Komarsky had ruined her girlhood; how she had fired at him at a party (missing, but making her point); how her husband Pasha had marched into the Revolution and come out the other end as Screechnikov, the howler in the leather harness, famous for tribunals conducted entirely in volume.

"You knew him once?" Lara asked, one night over fig pulp and iodine.

"I knew a Pasha," said Yuri. "I never met a Screechnikov."

"Nobody has," said Lara. "That is rather the problem."

They fell in love the way sick troops fall in love: slowly, sideways, while handing each other bandages. Yuri went home to Tonya, guilty as a monkey caught with someone else's banana. Captured for a winter by the Laurel Guerrillas — who were gorillas to a monkey, guerrilla in every sense — he doctored their wounded and escaped over the snow in nothing but his collar. When famine came, the Groomekos fled east to Varystickino, the abandoned grove in the pines — the journey arranged, the cow lent, and half their luck supplied by Samdevyatov, a rogue colobus in a red beret who quoted poetry he had never read and asked no questions, having, as he said, all the answers already.

That famous winter, the banana trees froze where they stood, every leaf sheathed in ice, so that the grove glittered like green glass. The family sat inside a treehouse of frosted silver, burning old poems to keep warm, while Yuri wrote new ones. Around the trunk, at night, the wild dogs circled and sang, and the monkeys held their small breaths.

Then Lara reappeared, alone, working at the Yuriatin town library, where Yuri found her by the simple expedient of borrowing every book she had catalogued. For a while Varystickino was the one warm place in the world. They read, they doctored, they went quietly bananas with happiness. But the past came swinging back on a liana: first Komarsky, sleek as ever, offering to "rescue" Lara eastward; then, one snow-blue evening, Screechnikov himself, who sat in the frozen kitchen and talked with Yuri until the fire died.

"You have lived," Screechnikov said. "You climbed for figs and wrote them down. I only knocked things down. Now there is no tree in this jungle with my name on it, and I find I am tired."

"I don't understand you," Yuri began.

"No," agreed Screechnikov, "you don't." He walked out into the pines. There was a single small pop, far away. Nobody looked up.

Komarsky returned with papers and a sleigh, and Yuri — being a monkey of honor in a jungle past honoring — lied to Lara, used a dead monkey's travel pass found in the snow, and put her and her daughter on the sleigh east, believing he was setting her free. Then he told the truth to nobody, being by then too good at lying. Tonya and the little ones were already lost beyond the sea of grass — deported by decree, on the grounds that they had once been monkeystocrats. Her last letter found him in Monkeyscow: *We are well. It is warm. Think of us sometimes under this other canopy.* He carried it in his pocket for the rest of his life, next to the pencil.

He declined. He grew shabby and stopped grooming altogether. Years on, riding the swinging trolley that hauled monkeys up the Monkeyscow hill, he saw through the vines a she-monkey with gray eyes, and his old heart went up like a kite — and stopped. He rose, reached toward the window, fell sideways into the arms of strangers, and the bunch of bananas a lady dropped rolled the length of the car, and nobody could say why the whole trolley seemed to swing.

They buried him under a wild fig tree. A gray-eyed she-monkey came out of the crowd at the graveside, wept, and vanished — taken, the whispers said, straight to a camp; nobody followed, because nobody could. His friends Gordon and Dudorov, two old monkeys who had learned to survive by forgetting, copied his poems out by paw, leaf by leaf, and passed them through the troop in secret. Years later, in the last war, a little laundry monkey with gray eyes and Yuri's stammer told them a story of her childhood they never managed to finish hearing.

A bunch, once plucked, still ripens. That was the whole of his doctrine — and of hers.
