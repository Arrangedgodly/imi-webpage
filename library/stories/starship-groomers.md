# Starship Groomers

*A parody of "Starship Troopers"*

Juan "Johnnie" Rico signed up for Federal Service because a girl did it first, which historians will tell you is how half of history happens, and the other half is supply logistics.

It was graduation night in Buenos Aires, and Johnnie — a young chimp of a wealthy merchant family, groomed within an inch of his life, bound by his father's plan for the family firm — was out with two friends: Carl, who was going to be a genius in Military Research whether the military liked it or not, and Carmen Ibañez, a capuchin with a pilot's eyes, who was going to the Fleet.

"Service guarantees chimp-zenship," Carmen said, filling in the form on the public terminal like it was a dinner reservation. "Two years, and then I can vote. You coming, Johnnie?"

"You don't need to serve to vote for a monkey like me," said Johnnie, who had not thought about it for one second of his comfortable life, and then Carmen smiled at him the specific way she had, and he heard himself say, "Sure. Why not."

His father's fury arrived by private aircar. Federal Service was for the poor, the witless, and the ambitions of other people's offspring; the Ricos had been merchants since before the Second Bug War, and no son of his was going to peel off the family fur for a uniform. Johnnie went anyway, mostly to spite him, partly for the smile, and — though he could not have said this out loud even under interrogation — because of something Mr. Dubois had once written on his homework.

Mr. Dubois had taught History and Moral Philosophy, the one course you could not fail because you could not pass it either, and in red ink at the bottom of Johnnie's final essay he had written a single line from an old poet, which Johnnie would not understand for another three years and would then never stop understanding:

**"The noblest fate that a chimp can endure is to place his own mortal body between his loved home branch and war's desolation."**

The recruiting station on the corner had a Fleet sergeant behind the desk with no legs, and an Army sergeant beside him with no eyes. This was deliberate. The legless sergeant took Johnnie's form, grinned up at him from the hover-chair, and said the thing he said to everybody:

"Service guarantees chimp-zenship. Mind, kid — the Fleet doesn't want you, and if it takes you it will kill you for its own amusement, and if you come back to this desk wanting to withdraw I will personally help you fill out the forms, because that's the smartest decision any recruit ever makes." He stamped it anyway. "But they don't call it Federal Service for nothing. Somebody's got to stand between the branch and the snakes. Sign."

Camp Arthur Currie, Saskatchewan, was run by a career gorilla named Sergeant Zim, who could break a chimp across his knee and had a voice like tectonics.

"You kids think you know why you're here," Zim said the first night, pacing before ninety shivering recruits. "You're wrong. You're here because you *don't* know, and my job is to beat the knowing into you. Any chimp can be trained to fight; that's the easy half. We're going to make you something harder: a trooper who grooms the chimp next to him before he grooms himself. The Mobile Chimpfantry breaks up families, recruits — because from today, we *are* your family, and family that doesn't groom together doesn't come home together."

And then, because he was fair the way a wall is fair: "If any of you had any sense, you'd quit now, and I'd respect you for it. I'm not here to break you. I'm here to make you. There's a difference, and if you can't see it yet, that's what the next ninety days are for."

They dropped out by dozens. The ones who stayed learned the suit: thirty hundredweight of powered armor that turns a chimp into a one-monkey avalanche — jump rockets, sensors, and a fire control system that reads your muscle tremors, so that the suit's fists move when yours do, only harder. "The suit," their instructors said, "is not armor. The suit is what the gorillas of the old century only dreamed of being: a chimp with a chest like a bank vault and a temper to match. You don't wear it. You *are* it. Together, on the bounce."

Grooming was regulation. Not the social kind — the combat kind: eight minutes a day, battle-buddy pairs, fur to fur, checking under the collar armor for the parasites of a hundred filthy worlds. It sounded like affection. It was doctrine. A distracted trooper is a dead trooper, and nothing focuses a chimp like another chimp's fingers finding what he couldn't feel. *An ungroomed trooper is a dead trooper* was painted over every barracks door on every transport in the Fleet.

Then the war came looking for them.

The Bugs — officially the Arachnanas, eight-legged horrors of the Klendathu hives, who had somehow lobbed an asteroid off-target-that-wasn't into Buenos Aires — were the enemy nobody had wanted to believe in. Johnnie's mother had been in Buenos Aires. His father, when the news reached him, did the thing that broke Johnnie's heart in a way the asteroid had failed to: he enlisted. As a private. At his age. Because there was nothing else in the world for a merchant whose world had burned, and because the Ricos, whatever else they were, had never asked another monkey to hold a branch they wouldn't hold themselves.

Operation Bughouse, the invasion of Klendathu, was a catastrophe that lived up to its name in the first hour, and Johnnie survived it by luck, doctrine, and a platoon sergeant named Jelly, who hauled him into a drop capsule screaming the ancient cry of the chimpfantry, the one every trooper in every war has howled going over the top:

**"Come on, you apes! You want to groom forever?!"**

They wanted to live forever, of course. Nobody does. Rasczak's Roughnecks — the platoon that inherited Johnnie when his own burned — was commanded by Lieutenant Rasczak, a one-armed baboon who gave his troopers the only order that mattered: "Men, this is all yours. I only carry the doctrine." When the Lieutenant died doing exactly what Mr. Dubois's red-ink poet had promised was noblest, the Roughnecks put his name on their patch and Johnnie found, without ever quite deciding to, that he had become what Zim had been making: the chimp the others groomed *first*.

OCS came next, because the Fleet had noticed that Johnnie Rico could think with his fur on fire. Between tactical problems they gave him History and Moral Philosophy again, this time from Major Reid, a bobtail with no patience for the comfortable lie that anybody's vote is free.

"You Rico," she said, pointing at him in front of the class. "Why isn't a chimp born to the Roughnecks? Why must the franchise be earned?"

"Because — " and Johnnie heard Mr. Dubois's red ink coming out of his own mouth, "—because the vote is force, ma'am. A ballot is power over other monkeys, and we don't hand power to anyone who hasn't put his own body on the line and learned that decisions cost. Service proves you can put the troop before yourself. No proof, no vote."

"Adequate," said Major Reid, which from her was a parade.

The war's last campaign, in Johnnie's war at least, was Operation Royalty: Planet P, the Bug royal world, and the objective that mattered more than any hive: capture, alive, the Brain Bug — the master caste, the planner, the thing that thought for the whole eight-legged army. The drop was a meat grinder. The platoon was burning around him. And when the Brain Bug finally came up out of the ground — huge, bloated, dripping, brilliant — it was a battered private who took it down bare-handed, ignoring four kinds of firepower, because Private Zim, formerly Sergeant Zim, had busted himself down a grade for the privilege of this one drop.

"First Brain Bug ever taken alive," Zim said afterward, cradling two broken arms like souvenirs. "You can have the court-martial if you want, Lieutenant. This was worth it."

Two weeks later, a transport sergeant reported to Lieutenant Johnnie Rico's new command: Rasczak's Roughnecks, reformed. The sergeant was gray at the muzzle, stood a little stiff in the suit, and groomed to regulation perfection. He saluted his son and said, "Request permission to join the platoon, sir. I'm told the grooming here is the best in the Fleet."

They shipped out together for Klendathu, father under son, both of them under the doctrine, all of them under the patch. On the flight deck the pilot was a capuchin Johnnie had once followed into a recruiting office, and the drop klaxon began its rising howl, and the Roughnecks sealed their suits and gripped branches.

"To the everlasting glory of the chimpfantry!" someone roared, and the bay answered as the capsules fell away toward the burning green of Planet P:

**"Come on, you apes! You want to groom forever?!"**

No. But they would groom as long as there was a branch to hold.
