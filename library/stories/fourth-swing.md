# Fourth Swing

*A parody of "Fourth Wing"*

Basguava War College hung inside a fig-tree cliff the size of a mountain, and the cadets had a saying about it: *There's only one rule at Basguava — survive the first branch.*

Violet Sorrengail looked at the first branch — a slick, mossy limb, four hundred feet over a gorge — and quietly despaired. She was the runt of her troop, small and brittle, with joints that popped out when she sneezed. She had trained her whole life to be a scribe, safe in a hollow with parchment. She could name every battle the riders had ever flown and diagram them from memory. Her mother, the General, had other plans.

"Riders' quadrant," she said. "You will swing."

"Rocs kill monkeys," Violet pointed out, very reasonably. "Rocs are our *natural predators.*"

"Then this will be a growth opportunity," said the General, who was not cruel, merely a mother shaped like a war.

Her sister Mira carved cadets' excuses with one eyebrow and told her: "Survive. That's the whole curriculum." Her brother Brennan had supposedly not survived it — a fact Violet kept in the same pocket as her grief and her suspicion.

The branch crossing was exactly as terrible as advertised. Cadets inched along it in a windstorm while instructors shouted encouragement such as "don't fall." When the recruit ahead of Violet's friend Rhiannon slipped, Violet — small, quick, and allergic to standing by — grabbed her tail with both hands and hauled her back to bark. Farther down, Jack Barbary, a slab of a macaque, was improving the odds by shoving smaller cadets toward the drop, because Basguava graded on a curve.

"That," said a low voice behind her, "was the most interesting thing I've seen all week. Look at you, Violence."

Xaden Riorson: wingleader of the Fourth Swing, black-furred, branded on the shoulder with the mark of a failed rebellion, and owner of the most suspicious smirk in the quadrant. He was supposed to want her dead — the General had executed his father's rebels, and among the marked ones, grudges were heirlooms. He mostly seemed to want her to eat more.

"You should be terrified of me," he offered.

"I'm busy," said Violet, diagramming the parapet's wind patterns. "You're in my light."

Her old friend Dain, sweet and careful, wanted to protect her too. His gift was retrocognition: he could read memories with a touch. Every time he took her hand "for her own good," he came back grim. "You read my diary again," said Violet, snatching it back. "Without asking. Again."

The real test was Threshing, when the rocs chose their riders — giant eagles in sunset colors, fanged and furious, whose idea of selection involved considerable maiming. A gold roc the size of a barn decided Violet was breakfast. Violet, thinking fast, did the one thing no cadet had ever bothered to try: she groomed it. Firmly. Between the eyes, where the itchy feathers are.

The roc blinked, melted slightly, and then looked outraged at itself.

And then two more came. First a chick — Andarna, a runt black-feathered thing with juvenile down and terrible timing — who landed on Violet's shoulder and announced, "You're small. I'm small. We'll be small together."

And then *him*: Tairn, an ancient black roc the color of a thunderhead, one eye scarred blind, patience already exhausted.

"You are small and breakable," Tairn said, in a voice like a rockslide. "I am old and out of patience. We'll manage. Mount up, rider. If you faint, fall to the left; my good eye is on the right."

Two bonds was not done. Had never been done. And Tairn's own mate, Sgaeyl, was bonded to Xaden — which meant, as Tairn put it with something like relish, "Where you fall, wingleader, you will now be fetching."

First flight was terror. Second was joy. By the tenth, Violet understood why the runt of a troop would strap herself to the sky: up there, swinging between a thunderhead and a chick, she was nobody's small anything. She found her squad, too: Rhiannon, who told death jokes; Ridoc, whose ice gift was only slightly less frigid than his comedic timing; Sawyer, who explained the rules so Violet could break them with citations.

In battle brief, she corrected the instructor's map of a border war two hundred years gone — the flank he'd labeled "untenable" was only untenable if you didn't know the river bent. Violet knew, because she'd copied that campaign twice for fun. The scribe quadrant's loss, riders, was busy being the riders' gain.

Then came the Gauntlet: a scaffolding of logs and rope up the cliff face, built on the assumption that cadets had four working limbs. Violet's joints disapproved of assumptions. She mapped the structure like a scribe — found the beam that bore no load, the knot that held no tension — and went up by patience and geometry while muscle-bound classmates rained gently into the nets below.

Xaden found her one dusk on the sparring platform and taught her to fight like a small monkey: elbows, angles, and the ancient art of losing her balance on purpose. "Use what you are," he said. "You'll never be the biggest. Be the thing the biggest doesn't see coming."

Then, on a "routine" patrol over Resson at sunset, the harpy eagles came over the ridgeline — dozens, silent, wrong — and among them moved shapes that had once been monkeys: drained grey by dark wielders who siphoned magic instead of letting it flow, turning power into debt. Basguava remembered it was a war college. Cadets fell. Xaden's swing scattered into battle formation, and Violet did the thing only a small, brittle monkey could do: she climbed *down* Tairn's wing in flight, hung upside down under the assault no eagle expected from below, and found the lightning that had been pacing in her blood since Threshing.

It came out of her palms like a cracked sky. The lead harpy learned, briefly, what it was to be a moth.

They won the field. They lost people — among them Liam, Xaden's protégé, who had bad aim with everything except kindness, and fell shielding a squadmate. The whole quadrant learned what the tuition actually was.

And Violet learned the truth about the rebellion: it had never been about seizing anything. The marked ones had risen to force out the fact the leadership kept burying — the wards were failing, the grey ones were coming, and the storm did not care whose shoulder wore a brand. "You lied to me," Violet shouted, palms still crackling.

"I protected you," said Xaden, "which is worse, and I'd do it again."

Afterward, bruised, singed, and grinning, she sat with Rhiannon over stolen banana waffles while Andarna stole everyone's crusts.

"Two bonds," said Rhiannon, "and a wingleader who watches you eat. That's either a war story or a love story."

"It's a Tuesday," said Violet.

"You're insane," said Xaden, dropping onto the branch beside her.

"I'm small and breakable," said Violet. "But apparently I conduct."

He almost smiled. "Fourth Swing flies at dawn, Violence. Try to survive until then."

"Make me," she said — and for the first time in her brittle little life, Violet Sorrengail wasn't bracing for the fall.

She was aiming for the sky.
