# Timevine

*A parody of "Timeline"*

Very early on a Thursday morning, an old baboon wandered out of the desert and into a veterinary clinic, babbling about castles. The staff thought he was sunstruck. Then they X-rayed him and found that his arteries were laid out in perfectly straight lines — like the plumbing of a bank, not the tangled vines of any living monkey — and he died, mostly of being from somewhere else.

That was the first anybody heard of ITC, except its investors, who had been told the initials stood for International Tree Climbing, which was, in a way, true.

ITC's founder was Bob Donigrilla, a silverback billionaire in racing leathers, who liked to end meetings by saying: "Forget the future. The future is a jungle. The past — the past we can prune." What he told the board was simpler: history was the only product nobody had monetized, and he intended to sell admission. Five hundred banana notes a vine, gift shop extra.

The machine, in the New Mexico basement, did not move you through time, exactly. It strummed you down a vine between canopies — a *timevine*, the engineers called it — to a world very like the year 1357 and not, in the strict sense, our own. Nobody understood it, including the engineers, which they felt was a strength.

Four hundred miles away, at the dig in the Barkdogne valley, Professor Johnston had been overseeing his students — Marek, a siamang who loved old swords; Chris, a nervous chimp; Kate, a gibbon who could read a collapsed roof like a bedtime story — when ITC flew him out for a look at the machine. And did not fly him back. David Stern, the professor's fastestidious second, refused on principle to be strummed anywhere, and stayed to glare at the engineers on the students' behalf.

Then the students, sifting the monastery ruins, opened a chamber no monkey had touched since the fourteenth century and found, on six-hundred-year-old parchment, in the professor's unmistakable hand:

*HELP ME 4/7/1357 — AND BRING BANANAS.*

ITC's offer was straightforward: thirty-seven hours down the timevine, retrieve the professor, come home. Nobody mentioned the mortality figures, because there were no figures, because not enough monkeys had come back to make charts. Each student was fitted with a pea-pod behind the ear that would translate Old Fernch — with a half-second lag and the confidence of a drunk tourist. Stern, left in the basement, was given a kitchen timer and the phone number of nobody.

The strum itself took three seconds and felt like being every age you had ever been, simultaneously, in a windstorm. Then it was 1357, and it smelled of woodsmoke and worse.

Canopygard village was burning by the second morning. La Roquefort castle glowered on its crag; Sir Oliver, a fat orangutan baron, held the valley with a troop of hirelings, chief among them Sir Guy de Malegant, a mandrill knight with a face like a war. Opposing him was Arnaut de Cervole, who was, everyone agreed, a true guerrilla — being a gorilla, and fighting as one — and with Arnaut rode Lady Claire, a langur with quick eyes and quicker plans.

The middle hours were educational. Chris, strolling into a chapel to look at the roof, came face to face with Lady Claire at her bath, apologized in two languages with a half-second lag, and spent the next afternoon hanging in a cage over the river, reconsidering his opinions about tourism. Marek entered the tournament at Sir Oliver's fête as an unknown knight in borrowed armor, unhorsed Sir Guy twice, and was so happy that Kate made him promise to stop grinning before somebody killed him for it. Kate herself was lowered into the monastery's drainage tunnel — a crawl the width of a fig leaf, in the dark, in a century with no electric light and every spider — and came out the far side having mapped the secret way into La Roquefort and made, she said, friends with the spiders, "on a strictly professional basis."

Caught and questioned by Arnaut, the students were tied to a tree while the great guerrilla circled them, sniffing.

"Monks say you fell from the sky. My seer says you are demons. My cook says you are lunch." He showed his teeth. "Explain quickly."

"We're historians," said Kate.

"From where?"

"Very far upstream," said Marek.

Arnaut looked at them a long moment, then laughed like a landslide. "Upstream. I like it. The river tells the truth more than monks do." He cut them loose. Wars, he observed, were won by whoever knew the ground best, and any monkey who knew the ground six hundred years upstream was worth feeding.

The professor was found, after some archery and considerable embarrassment, in Oliver's scriptorium, transcribing for his keep and leaving messages in the margins for history — including, Kate discovered with her heart in her throat, corrections to his own future textbook, annotated *source: I was there*.

"Six hundred years of peer review," he said, "and this is the first reviewer who ever shot back at me."

The escape spent the rest of the thirty-seven hours, and most of the town's furniture. The mill fight began as an ambush and ended as a fire; the tunnel beneath La Roquefort, opened from the inside by Kate and her professional spiders, filled with smoke while the professor explained the fourteenth-century ventilating properties of the masonry, in full, at a shout. Arnaut's troops stormed the gate the moment it opened, Sir Guy met Marek on the wall walk with a face like two wars, and Sir Oliver, sensing the accounting, changed sides mid-battle, twice, arriving at last on whichever side was winning, which by then was everybody's.

There remained the green chapel, the return window, and the recurring discovery, common to all time travelers, that Chris could not climb a rope. Kate could, and did, twice, backwards. In the last confusion Marek went into the river after a sinking Sir Guy and came up changed, having looked at Lady Claire once too often.

Back in the basement, Stern's kitchen timer had ticked down through two machine failures and one discovery. Every strum, he had established, frayed the vine; the engineers had a chart of it, and Donigrilla kept the chart in a drawer, which was the most expensive drawer in the hemisphere. When the last window opened, Stern stood on the platform shouting into the microphone at four historians and one missing siamang.

"Marek," said the professor, "the vine closes in ninety seconds."

"I know," said Marek. "This is my tree."

He meant it. They went home without him, and the timevine folded up behind them like a fig leaf. The board then took the only vote of Donigrilla's life that he did not enjoy: a unanimous minus one to send him down a vine of his own — to the fourteenth century, permanently, where he was last seen lecturing medieval monkeys on monetizing the past, to no visible effect. The basement was sealed, the patents shredded, and the vine cut for good, mostly.

Four years later Kate reopened the dig and found, under the chapel floor, a tomb. ANDRÉ MAREK, 1342–1412, WHO CHOSE HIS TREE — carved beside the name of his wife, Claire, and set above a stone banana worn shiny by six hundred years of nobody, and one line of Old Fernch that translated, more or less:

*I had a good life.*

Kate read it aloud. Chris, who by then was holding her paw, pointed out that the earpod lag was gone, and they agreed that some things translate on their own.
