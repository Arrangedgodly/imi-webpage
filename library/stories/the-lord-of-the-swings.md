# The Lord of the Swings

*A parody of "The Lord of the Rings"*

Long ago, in the fire-heart of Mount Bloom, the Dark Lord Saurilla forged a masterwork: a ring of living, golden vine, into which she poured her cruelty, her will to dominate, and a frankly unreasonable amount of clinginess. Three vines were given to the gibbon-kings of the high canopy, seven to the macaque-lords in their delved halls, nine to mortal apes who craved power and got it, on terms. But they were all of them deceived — for Saurilla had made One Vine to rule them all, and its making was her undoing: cut from her hand at the last alliance of apes and gibbons, lost, and passed into small paws.

By the time anyone noticed, it was in the Brier, in the nest of a bonobbit called Fruitdo, inherited from an uncle with a set of tarnished spoons.

Gandalf the Grayback threw the little ring into the fire, cooled it, and read the fine print that appeared along its spiral of gold:

**One Vine to rule the troop, One Vine to find them,
One Vine to bring them all, and in the tree-tops bind them.**

"You must leave the Brier now. The Nine are coming: shadowed once-apes, night-riders on silent leopards, the Nightgûl. They do not knock."

So the great swinging began: Fruitdo over field and furrow with his gardener Sam — who had promised on the garden wall and meant to keep it, gardening being the least of it — and, by accident and appetite, two young bonobbites, Mango and Pip, who came for the mushrooms and stayed for the war.

In Bree, at the Leaping Lemur (an inn whose sign had been repainted so many times it now showed a lemur asleep), the innkeeper Butterpaws confessed that Gandalf had left a letter for Fruitdo some months ago, in a pocket he had been meaning to mend. The letter said, in full: *Things are worse than I feared. Trust the ranger. Do NOT put on the Vine.* "Any other instructions?" said Fruitdo. "There's a postscript," said Butterpaws, "but it's just a recipe."

The ranger called himself Strider in the wild, though kings' blood ran old in him: Kong, heir of the line that had cut the Vine from Saurilla's hand and then, on the very ledge of doom, declined to throw it away — which is why there was a story. That night the Nine slashed the inn's beds to ribbons and got nothing but ticking, and the company fled into the wild.

On Weathertop, a bare hill with old fire-scarred stones, the Nine caught them at last. Their captain leaned over Fruitdo in the ringlight with a long pale thorn. "The Vine of the world," he hissed, and stabbed.

Fruitdo survived; the wound did not quite. It left a cold thread in his shoulder that no healer could pull out, and a terrible new truth: some hurts go with you. At the river, Arboreen of the elves set the wounded bonobbit on her white horse and turned on the pursuing Nine: "If you want him, come and claim him!" — and the river came down like a wall of white-maned mares, for which the Nine had not packed.

At Ripendale, in the last homely grove, the wise quarreled beneath the singing trees — who should wield the Vine, who should guard it, whose fault it all was. Grima the macaxe struck it with his axe, which broke, and was reproved. Boromandrill of the South spoke for using it ("one does not simply swing into the black country; its roads are watched by worse than gob-rillas, and they keep attendance records"). And Fruitdo, small and quiet at the edge of the argument, heard himself say: "I will take the Vine, though I do not know the way."

"You have my bow," said Legolas of the Misty Canopy. "And my axe!" roared Grima. "And my tail!" cried Pip, who was told that the Fellowship of the Vine was not, in fact, recruiting. Nine walkers set out: the Grayback; Kong; Boromandrill; Legolas; Grima; and four bonobbites — three more than sensible, even Gandalf admitted.

Denied the high pass by storm — "The peaks do not wish us to swing over; we shall go under" — they stood before the West-gate of Moria, inscribed *Speak, friend, and enter.* Gandalf tried every password he had learned in six hundred years. Nothing. It was Fruitdo who asked, mildly, "What's the elvish word for *friend*?" — "Vellan," said Gandalf, and the door swung open, and the Grayback spent the rest of the day being gracious about it.

Then something long and pale reached out of the lake and had Mango by the ankle, and they were all inside Moria very suddenly, and the door was gone behind them.

Moria: the deep under-roots, the buried halls of the old macaque-kings, where Pip dropped a stone down a well — "Fool of a Mango! Next time throw yourself in and be no further nuisance!" — and the dark answered with drums. *Boom. Boom.* "Paws in the deep," said Legolas.

They ran, and came to the root-bridge over the gorge, and up the stair behind them came a Balrog of the deep burrow: fire-whip in claw, all shadow and leopard-spots and poor intentions. Gandalf the Grayback planted his staff in the middle of the swaying bridge.

"You shall not swing past!"

The bridge snapped. The shadow fell — its whip lashed up, caught the Grayback's ankle, and he gripped the edge and looked at them once.

"Swing, you fools!" he said, and let go.

They wept down to Loambórien, the golden grove of the Lady Gorilladriel, who looked long into her mirror-bowl and saw what the Vine would make of her, and refused it, trembling, shining, magnificent: "I pass the test. I will diminish, and go into the West, and remain Gorilladriel." At parting she gave them gifts. To Fruitdo, a small crystal phial: "May it be a light to you in dark places, when all other lights go out." To Sam, a box of garden-earth, which is elvish for *hope.* To Mango and Pip, belts, because someone had to model good sense.

At the great seeing-seat on the hill, Fruitdo put on the Vine, and the Eye of Saurilla found him — a lidless red searching through the leaves — and Boromandrill, afraid for his starving southern troop, tried to take the Vine by force: "It is a gift! Why should we not use this Vine? It would give us power to feed every troop in the world!" He repented at once; but the Nightgûl came screeching, and Boromandrill, arrows in him, stood alone between them and the two young bonobbites, blowing his great horn until the reeds split. Kong found him at the river's edge.

"I tried to take the Vine from him," Boromandrill whispered. "Forgive me. I would have followed you, my brother, my captain, my Kong."

The Fellowship scattered: Mango and Pip carried off by the Uruk-hairs toward the burning west; and Fruitdo alone on the shore — until the reeds parted and Sam waded out after the boat, waist-deep and going under.

"Fruitdo! I made a promise! 'Don't you leave him, Samwise.' And I don't mean to."

"I'm glad you're with me, Sam," said Fruitdo. "Now hold on." And the two littlest apes in the great war paddled out into the wide river, east, toward the shadow — the Vine going with them, whispering, coiling, patient as a vine.

One Vine to bring them all, and in the tree-tops bind them. It had not done it yet. But it could wait.
