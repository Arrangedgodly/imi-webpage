# The White Tarsier

*A parody of "The White Tiger"*

**THE FIRST NIGHT: A LADDER OF VINES**

Your Excellency, Premier of the Panda Republic, Most Serene Eater of Bamboo:

I have read in the papers that you are coming to our jungle next week, to study how a troop of monkeys runs its outsourcing. Good. You and I, Excellence, we are practical animals. You plan five years ahead; I plan only to the end of this sentence. But between us, we will get to the truth of things.

Neither you nor I speaks Troop Standard as a first language — you have your bamboo grunts and I have my screeches — but there are some things that can be said only in Troop Standard. So let me begin.

My name is Balram. I am a tarsier. Look at my photograph: the size of a teacup, and the eyes — two golden moons, taking up half my head. We tarsiers cannot move our eyes, Excellence; we move our whole heads instead. So I have spent my whole life turning my head, watching. That is my education. That is my wealth.

I was born in the Darkness — the interior forest, where the canopy is so thick that sunlight reaches the floor only twice a day, like a bribe. In the Darkness, monkeys live like roosters in a coop. And I will tell you about the finest invention our jungle has ever produced: it is not the temple, it is not the banyan, it is the cage.

A million roosters in the cage, Excellence. They see the knives, they smell the blood of their brothers, and still they do not fly out. That is us. A million monkeys in the Darkness, packed so tight we groom strangers in our sleep, and every one of us dreaming of the lock instead of the key. The amazing thing is that we keep escaping into the cage — we are afraid of the free air.

I am the one who got out.

**THE SECOND NIGHT: MY FATHER**

My father pulled a branch-rickshaw in the sap-town — a cart on runners that he dragged through the mud with his whole bent body, ferrying fat rich monkeys. Coconut-cough got into his lungs from the mud-fumes. He spat black on the road for a year, and then he coughed one last time on the harness, and the landlord's agents took his body away, and I stood there watching, and no one poured one drop of banana oil on the pyre, because we owed the landlord too much. My mother had gone before him; the flu had taken her, the flu of the wet season.

"A tarsier in a mud town," the school inspector had said of me the year before, when he caught me unschooled and quizzed me anyway, and I, starving, had answered right. "This one is the white tarsier. There is one born once in a generation — the rarest animal in the jungle. The others live and die in the cage. This one will come out. Boy — you will be a champion. A chimp-ion."

I was taken out of school anyway, to sort nuts in the sap-house, because the family needed the nuts. My grandmother, Kusum — a black beetle of a monkey, that one, may she never rest — made my marriage at eleven to a monkey I never met. When I ran away from that, she cursed me to the seventh generation.

**THE THIRD NIGHT: THE MASTERS**

I broke into the driving trade. And thus, Excellence, I came to serve the family of landlords called the Storks — the tall gray baboons who own the rivers of our district, taxing every monkey who fishes, bathes, or drowns.

The Stork's second son was called Ashok. A soft, flabby baboon who had been to the big city and come back smelling of cologne and doubt. I became his driver — turban, cap, buttons polished — and followed him to the Big Mango, the capital of light, where he carried cash in a red bag to bribe ministers, and I sat below in the jeep all night, watching the building, and learning where the nuts go.

Ashok was not the worst of them. "Balram," he said one night, "have a mango. You're the only honest monkey in this city." And I took the mango and thought: yes — and that is why I will die poor.

His wife, Pinky Madam, a city monkey, drank fermented sap one night and took the wheel for a joyride — and a child of the street ran under the wheels. In the morning the family summoned me. "Balram. You were driving. Sign the paper that says so."

I looked at Ashok. Ashok looked at the wall.

I signed nothing that night, Excellence — but a monkey who refuses that paper has already cut the ropes of the coop in his heart. I began to watch the red bag.

**THE FOURTH NIGHT: THE MURDER**

They took me back to the city, and every day the red bag went to a minister, and every night I slept in the jeep, one meter from a million bananas, and my eyes — the two golden moons — did not move from it.

Understand: I am not a bad monkey. It is the jungle that is bad. Ask any tarsier: in the Darkness, either the mud swallows you, or you eat the mud.

The day Ashok went back alone to the district to settle land business, I waited on the mountain road, at the hairpin above the gorge. I had hidden, the night before, the heaviest fruit in the jungle — a ripe jackfruit, eight pounds, spiked like a mace. My family had owned nothing in four generations, Excellence. That morning I owned an idea and a jackfruit.

He got out to move a fallen branch. He trusted me. That is what did it: the trust. I dropped it from the rock above, and took the red bag, and walked away from the coop.

The police of the district, in their wisdom, have arrested my family instead — driven them from the land, at least; the details I read with these same unmoving eyes. I do not weep, Excellence. In the jungle, a monkey who climbs must kick off the branch below him. Newton's law. Apple, tree. Jackfruit, Ashok. Gravity is the one honest thing we have.

**THE FIFTH NIGHT: THE LIGHT**

I came to the Big Mango, the city of glass and software, changed my name to Ashok Sharma, and with the red bag I opened what you see in the photograph: **WHITE TARSIER DRIVERS — A 24-HOUR FLEET FOR THE NEW JUNGLE.** Twenty jeeps. Uniformed young monkeys, honest, energetic, and watched by me — I interview each one personally, and I ask only one question: *have you ever slept in a jeep?* Those who say yes, I hire. A driver who has slept in a jeep is a monkey who has been in the coop and wants out.

The city inspector came weekly for his bag of bananas, and I gave it, smiling, until he called me "the little one" one time too many, and I bought the shoe that pinches: a photograph of the police chief's sap-shop, unsigned. We are partners now. This is entrepreneurship, Excellence. This is the jungle's version.

And the Darkness? I have been back once. The mud town is the same; the cage is the same; a million roosters, and now my nephew Dharam is growing in it, and I have sent for him. I will not let the cage close. His eyes — I checked — are the two golden moons.

**THE SIXTH AND SEVENTH NIGHTS, CONDENSED: THE MORAL**

You asked — no, you never asked; you Panda Premiers never ask — so I will answer the question of the age anyway: *can a jungle change?*

No, Excellence. But a monkey can. The white tarsier is not born once a generation, whatever the school inspector said. It is made — by mud, by hunger, by a paper they want you to sign. The rest of us, the brown and golden millions, we sit in the coop and groom each other and die at the right age.

I am in the Light now, Excellence. At night I drive my own jeep to the top of the tallest tower in the Big Mango, and I turn my whole head, the way only a tarsier can, and I look back at the Darkness spread out beyond the grid — all those cages, stacked to the horizon — and I laugh, alone, in Troop Standard, at high volume.

The book of your visit will say the jungle is rising. Send your ministers to see my fleet. Tell them the owner is a teacup-sized monkey with moon eyes, and that his life is the true story of our age — a story, Excellence, that begins in mud and ends behind a windshield in the Light.

I remain, in Troop Standard and in spirit,

Your brother,
**BALRAM HALWAI-LOOP, the White Tarsier**
*The Big Mango*
