# The Peel Jar

*A parody of "The Bell Jar"*

It was a queer, sultry summer, the summer they electrocuted the Rosenbergs' flea collars, and Esther Greenbough couldn't stop wondering what a flea thought when its collar stopped working. She was in the Big Banyan, not the home grove, because Esther had won the fashion contest.

Thirty girl chimps had won, and here they all were on the tallest kapok in the jungle, working a month at *Lady Banana's Day* magazine, being photographed with fig fronds behind their ears and taken to banquets where exotic fruits lay iced in the leaves like green jewels. The other winners tore through their stipend of bananas like pretty barrel-going monkeys. Esther sat at the banquet and couldn't eat a bite.

"You'll want to be careful," Doreen said, sideways, watching the editors. Doreen was a languorous spider monkey who wore her tail like a thrown rope. "Old Jaycee there thinks you're going to be a great editor. She told me so. She said you had the makings of a proper chimp-ion."

Jaycee, the editor, had little spectacles and a permanent frown. "What do you have in mind, Greenbough, after this?" she asked that afternoon, tapping a pencil. "Grooming? Teaching? Young females with brains ought to be doing something."

What Esther had in mind, when she tried to mind it, was the fig tree in her grandfather's grove. She had read about a chimp sitting in one and choosing among the figs, and it had stayed with her: *I saw my life branching out before me like the green fig tree. One fig was a husband and a nest of chimps, and one fig was a famous poet, and one fig was a brilliant editor, and one fig was a professor of canopy literature, and one fig was a daring traveler of the far vines, and up atop, one fig was Esther, beating her chest over the whole jungle.* But choosing one meant losing all the others, and as she imagined sitting in the crotch of that tree, starving to death rather than choose, the figs went wrinkled, one by one, and dropped to the ground.

She had dated, that college year, a male called Buddy Willow, who showed her an X-ray of his own chest once, the way other males show flowers. He kissed her once in the laboratory parking-bough, experimentally, and wrote up the results. He had also taken her to watch a kit being born, over the protests of the mother-ape, standing on the observation branch with his arms folded while the little thing fought its way into the light; and Esther, watching, thought that the jungle might be divided into those who cry at births and those who take notes, and that she had just been introduced to the second kind.

"Pain is a rumor," Buddy said on the way home, "spread by the weak."

"I hope you catch it," said Esther.

Then came the banquet of the bad beetle canapés. All the contest winners were sick as dogs in the shrubbery, and Esther, between the leaves, laughed until she wept. It was the last thing that made her laugh for a long time.

The nights went wrong first. Doreen got in with Lenny, a bongo-drum celebrity from the carnival groves, and Esther, sent home in Lenny's private carrier, watched the lights of the Banyan smear past like wet paint, and understood, in a distant way, that everything was available and nothing was hers. She went home to the suburbs of the grove. She had meant to spend the summer writing a novel. Instead she found she could not read — the words crawled apart like ants — and she could not sleep, and when her mother suggested she learn shorthand, Esther pitched the shorthand manual into the ferns.

"Grandma, how do you know if a fellow really loves you?" she asked instead. Buddy Willow had never loved her, she saw now. He had kissed her once in the laboratory parking-bough so he could say he had run an experiment.

The dark came in slowly, like sap. She stopped washing behind her, then stopped washing. One gray week she tried, quietly, to climb out of her own life; the vine held; and the family found her, and the jungle agreed, gently and dreadfully, that she must go where the cracked she-apes went.

Dr. Gorilladon, the first doctor, grinned too much and listened too little. He wired Esther to the shock machine, and when the current went through her, something in her cracked open and she screamed, and they did it again the next week. They were putting her under the peel jar, and she didn't even know its name.

Under the peel jar, the jungle itself looked blurred, like the world seen through the skin of a banana. Wherever Esther sat — the sunniest bough of the tallest kapok, the ferry over the river, a café table in the far fig district — she was sitting under the same peel jar, stewing in her own sour air. The jar hung a few inches above her head, and nothing could touch her.

Even the good evenings only tapped on the glass. There was an interpreter named Constantin who took her to the glass-house where all the jungle's nations argued in little rubber ears, and fed her beetles in aspic, and was kind, and spoke six dialects and loved none of them; and nothing happened, and Esther went home thinking that a female could be let down by a fellow even when the fellow never noticed he was holding her up.

It was Dr. Nolan, the lady langur, who lifted the peel. She talked to Esther as if she were a whole chimp. She arranged the treatments like appointments, not punishments. She put her in the company of older, wiser, slightly cracked she-apes who had been under jars of their own — of whom the chief was Joan Gilling, once a champion swimmer of her college grove, who had followed Esther down and out again, and monitored Esther's progress like a rival sister, which, in a way no one said aloud, she was. And Esther, slowly, ripened rather than rotted.

On the morning of her release interview, a whole row of serious doctors sat behind a fig-leaf table and asked her how she felt, and Esther, who had rehearsed it, said: "All right."

"Adjustment will take time," said the head doctor, satisfied, and stamped the leaf.

Her mother brought her a cluster of celebratory roses, and Esther said thank you, and privately planned a small funeral for them; but she was smiling when she planned it, which Dr. Nolan had said was the whole difference.

"How did I know that someday — at college, in Europe, anywhere at all — the peel jar might descend again?" she wrote later. "But there ought to be a ritual for being born twice. I had taken the right peel off a banana all my life; I ought to know."

On her last morning at the sanatorium grove, Joan and the others stood in the leaves to see her off, and Esther smoothed her fur, fixed her one nice white collar of blossoms, and stepped out into the sunlight where the jar was not.

"How do I look?" she asked the fig tree.

And the figs hung there in the light — every one of them, this time, still green.
