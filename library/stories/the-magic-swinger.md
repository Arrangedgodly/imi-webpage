# The Magic Swinger

*A parody of "The Magic Finger"*

Marabel the marmoset had a secret, and the secret was this: whenever she saw red, her whole arm would tingle, her fingertips would glow like ripe banana skins at sunset, and the Magic Swinger would activate itself.

Nobody knew exactly what the Magic Swinger did. Nobody wanted to find out.

"You just be careful, Marabel," said her mother, picking through her fur for breakfast lice. "That tail of yours is loaded."

"I can't help it," said Marabel. "It just goes off when people are cruel. It's never once gone off when people were kind, which tells you something about the jungle."

Now the Gregg family — that was Gorilla Gregg, Mrs. Gregg, and their two horrible sons, Philip and William — lived at the far edge of the troop's territory, and they loved nothing better than creeping down to the river at dawn and shooting bananas at the gentle tree-dwelling macaques on the far bank.

THWUMP. THWUMP. THWUMP.

The macaques never did anything to anybody. They mostly sat around grooming each other and discussing the best sunbathing branches. But every Saturday, down came the Greggs with their banana-guns, firing away like a barrel of monkeys gone bad.

"Ha ha!" bellowed Gorilla Gregg, beating his chest. "Got six before breakfast! That's a record, boys!"

"Aren't they heavy?" asked Philip, reloading.

"Heavy?" said his father. "They're BANANAS. That's the beauty of it. No blood, no mess, no guilty conscience — just macaques with bruises. It's the civilized way to be cruel."

That Saturday, Marabel was hiding in the ferns near the river, and she saw the whole thing. She saw a father macaque fall out of his tree with a squished banana splattered across his ears. She saw the mother macaque gather her crying baby and flee into the understory. She saw William keep a tally on a leaf and cheer.

Marabel went hot, then cold, then very, very tingly.

"Uh-oh," she whispered, looking down at her arm. It was glowing like a firefly's back porch. "It's happening. The Magic Swinger is — OOH — OOH — AAAH —"

The tip of her tail spun like a helicopter seed. There was a flash of green light, a smell of crushed mango, and everything went wobbly.

The next morning, Gorilla Gregg woke up in his nest and knew immediately that something was wrong, because his feet were gone.

In their place: two enormous, hairy, prehensile macaque tails.

"MARGARET!" he roared, flailing. "MARGARET, COME QUICK! I'VE GOT TAIL FEET!"

Mrs. Gregg came running — and stopped dead, because she had them too. Great curly tails where her feet should be, complete with itchy patches she couldn't reach.

Down the branch came Philip and William, tumbling and skidding, tail-feet first, thumping into their parents like four furry bowling pins.

"We can't climb!" wailed William. "Every time we grab a branch, our feet slip off!"

"TAILS DON'T GRAB, DAD," yelled Philip. "THESE ONES DON'T, ANYWAY! THEY ONLY WAG!"

And that was the trouble. The Gregg tails could not grip a single thing. Not a trunk, not a vine, not a banana. Try as they might, the family could only get about by walking on their hands and the stumps of their tail-bottoms, down to the ground, where no self-respecting gorilla ever went, where they had to build themselves a nest in the cold, damp bushes like a family of very large, very miserable beetles.

It was miserable. The ants bit them. The river soaked them. A passing toucan laughed so hard it had to sit down on a rock, and then fell off the rock.

Word got around, as word does. Philip and William's classmates came down to gawp at the ground-nest, held their noses, and went home to tell their mothers that the Greggs had finally gone completely to the beetles. Mrs. Gregg tried to keep up appearances from the bushes — "We're CAMPING," she informed a squirrel, with enormous dignity — but it is hard to be dignified when your feet wag when you're cross, and hers wagged all morning.

"What I want to know," said Gorilla Gregg, "is why us?"

"Perhaps," said Mrs. Gregg, slowly, the way you say a thing you've been not-saying for years, "it's something about the Saturday mornings."

"I don't understand it," groaned Gorilla Gregg that evening, hanging his tails up to dry. "First the shooting, then this. It's almost as if — as if somebody were punishing us."

"Don't be ridiculous," said Mrs. Gregg. "Eating is out of the question, by the way. Every time I peel a banana, it slaps me."

And then, at dusk, they heard a rustling, and the blood of every Gregg ran cold. Out of the trees came the macaque family — the very ones they had been banana-blasting that very morning — swinging down in perfect, easy arcs, gripping everything, landing softly.

And they were carrying the Greggs' own banana-guns.

"THEY'VE COME FOR REVENGE!" screamed William. "They're going to shoot us! DAD, THEY'RE GOING TO SHOOT US AT DAWN!"

Gorilla Gregg — who had never once in his life been too proud to hide behind his wife — now found himself too heavy to climb away, so he did the only thing left. He knelt on his useless tails in the mud, held up his huge hands, and said, in a very small voice for such a large gorilla: "Don't shoot. We're sorry. WE'RE SORRY."

The father macaque looked at him for a long moment. Then he laid the banana-guns down in the mud, muzzle away, where nobody could reach them in a hurry.

"You dropped these," he said. "You look cold. We brought food — the real kind, not the fast kind. Would you like to come up? We have the most wonderful branches."

The Greggs tried to answer, but their teeth were chattering. The baby macaque took Gorilla Gregg's great hairy hand-tail in his own small one and tugged him gently toward the trees, and the whole macaque family made a ladder of themselves, hand to tail, shoulder to shoulder, and passed the Greggs up into the warm high canopy like parcels being posted home.

That night, everything changed again — flash, green light, smell of mango — and in the morning the Greggs woke up with their own feet back. But they were up in the tall trees, in a warm macaque-built nest, and the whole macaque family was asleep around them like a pile of cozy socks.

Gorilla Gregg didn't say anything for a very long time. Then, quietly: "Margaret. We are never firing another banana as long as we live."

And they never did. Gorilla Gregg even changed his name to Gorilla Grooming, because he said shooting was a filthy habit and picking was a clean one, and if that doesn't show reform, nothing does. The banana-guns were planted upside down in the riverbank, where they eventually grew — nobody could explain it — into a grove of the sweetest banana trees on the river, which the Greggs and the macaques picked together.

As for Marabel, she watched the whole thing from her favorite fig tree, tail tucked demurely around her ankles, feeling very pleased with herself.

"Much better," she said, munching a banana she had picked, not shot. "And if anyone else in this jungle needs teaching a lesson —" she wiggled her fingers dangerously — "they can just let me know."

The forest, wisely, was extremely kind to macaques from that day on.
