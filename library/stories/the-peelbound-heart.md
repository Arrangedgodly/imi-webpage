# The Peelbound Heart

*A parody of "The Hellbound Heart"*

Frank Cotton came to the house on Lodovico Street because he had run out of places to be bored in. He was a langur of forty with a burned-out engine: he had chased every pleasure the world sells — strong drink, fast cars, the finest aged bananas money could smuggle — and found each one, on arrival, gray as old newspaper. So when a tiresome acquaintance mentioned a puzzle that *ended* boredom, permanently, Frank was on a plane within the hour.

The puzzle was the Lemur-chand Configuration, bought from a dealer in Dusseldorf for a scandalous sum: a box of black wood and brass the size of a large mango, its faces engraved with lemurs — ring-tailed, indri, aye-aye — cut so cunningly that their tails formed the seams along which the box wanted to move.

"Work it slowly," said the dealer, counting the money. "It is solved by a sequence. Each step sings a note. When you find the final note, the box opens, and — well. You will have company."

Frank worked at it for a fortnight. Turn the tail, slide the eye, press the aye-aye's finger; a note, cold and sweet, like a glass struck in an empty church. He had no trouble; boredom's children are brilliant at puzzles. On the sixteenth evening, the box played all its notes at once, unfolded like a lotus, and the wall of the room opened along seams Frank had never needed to notice.

The bell began to toll. It did not stop.

They came through the wall politely, as though through a curtain: four of them, dressed in black leather so old it had gone the texture of bark. The first was tall, and his head was a pineapple — crowned, planted with such art that the leaves sweeping down might once have been hair. The second was all grin below the nose, a skull-face that chattered without cease, softly, like a teacup in an earthquake. The third was a lady, split down the middle of her costume, black on one side, white on the other. The fourth was round, contented, fondling something in a paper bag.

"We are the Order of the Peel," said the Pineapple Priest, in a voice like a door. "You rang."

"I wanted the pleasures," said Frank. "The real ones. Beyond the beyond."

"We have such *bites* to show you," said the Order, together, with dreadful kindness. "Sweetness to the edge of spoilage. We are explorers in the further regions of the pantry. No fake bananas, no promises of breakfast. Just discipline, and fruit, and forever."

"Angels to some," said the round one, unwrapping his bag, "demons to others. You may still decline. You have —" the Priest consulted a small brass watch — "until the bell stops."

The bell stopped.

"Ah," said the Order, in four-part sympathy. "Late."

They took Frank by the seams he had opened.

That was in the spring. In the autumn, Frank's brother Rory moved into the house — a decent, uncomplicated chimp with a decent, complicated wife. Julia had married Rory in a flurry of confetti and good sense, and if she sometimes watched the breakfast table as if expecting the doorbell, only she knew why. What she had *settled away from* had been a certain langur at the wedding reception: Frank, who talked to her by the stairs for forty minutes, peeled a banana one-handed without looking, and made her laugh until her ribs ached — then never appeared again in her life, because Frank never appeared twice anywhere.

It was Julia who found the first peel, then the box, then — on a wet Tuesday, alone in the parlor — the notes, cold and sweet.

The wall opened. The bell began.

"Madam," said the Pineapple Priest, bowing with genuine courtesy, "your brother-in-law has run up a considerable account. He has eaten of the orchard eternal, and cannot digest it out there, and cannot come *in* here whole. But —" the crown of leaves tilted, and Julia stood very still under the gaze of a fruit that had seen everything — "he could *ripen.* If he were fed. Will you feed your husband's brother, madam?"

"Is he in pain?" asked Julia.

"He is *hungry,*" said the Order, as if that were worse, and Julia thought of the stairs at the wedding, and the one-handed banana, and the forty years of breakfast table ahead of her, and said:

"What fruit do you need?"

What the Order needed was bananas of the living. Not blood — nothing so melodramatic. Bananas: each caller's own personal banana, the one saved against a rainy day. The terms were strict and a little prudish: each offering must be taken *by bonk* — the donor invited in and gently conked with the fruit bowl — so that the donor wandered home confused, cheerful, and permanently out of fruit, never quite knowing what had gone missing.

Julia became, within a month, the most charming hostess in Lodovico Street. Salesapes called; Julia welcomed them. There is a species of monkey that arrives with a briefcase and a rehearsal for warmth, and they made ideal donors: nobody misses a salesape, least of all the other salesapes. Bonk, and the floor of the spare room drank another banana.

And in the spare room, in the dark, Frank ripened. Layer by layer, peel by peel: a voice first, then a paw, then a shoulders' breadth of him — a langur assembling out of the gloom like fruit coming back onto a market stall, grinning at his sister-in-law with real love, the only love either of them was any good at.

Kirsty saw him. Kirsty was Rory's oldest friend, a sharp-eyed young gorilla with a cold and a work visa problem, who came round for tea and instead saw a half-finished langur standing in the window of the spare room, waving.

Kirsty ran — unfortunately with the box under her arm, grabbed as evidence — and that night, miserable and feverish in her rented room, she turned the lemur's tail, slid the aye-aye's finger, and could not stop, because boredom's cousins are brilliant at puzzles too.

The wall opened for her. The bell began.

"I don't want anything!" Kirsty cried at the Order of the Peel. "I want to *give* it back!"

The Pineapple Priest raised one leaf, and the Order paused, because in three hundred years nobody had said that.

"You rang the bell," said the Split Sister, "and rang it truly, and we must take a ringer. That is the discipline. But." Her split face considered Kirsty the way one considers a menu. "There is a rogue diner among your acquaintance — a monkey who rang, and ate, and ran, and is even now wearing another monkey's pelt on Lodovico Street. Bring us the *thief,* little ringer, and the account is settled."

Kirsty went back for Rory, of course. Found the spare room empty, found Julia setting the fruit bowl with terrible calm, and found "Rory" on the stairs, grinning down with forty minutes of wedding-reception charm.

"Come to Uncle Frankie-eek," said Frank, in his brother's pelt.

She got him in the end by believing him — walking trustingly up the stairs and then, when the pelt was within reach, taking hold of it and *pulling,* the way you pull a rug from under a table. Frank came out of Rory like a banana out of its skin, all unfinished glitter underneath; and the bell was already tolling, because Julia, seeing her whole engineering in a heap on the stairs, had — with enormous dignity, in her good coat — opened the box herself.

The wall opened for Julia. She straightened her collar. "Forty minutes," she said to no one, and stepped through, and the Order — who know quality when they see it — closed behind her like applause.

They took Frank by the seams he had opened — through the wall at speed, fruit-crowned and furious, complaining to the Engineer about the service; and the Engineer, vast and pale beyond the door, received him like a grocer receiving a returned melon.

The wall closed. The box unfolded one last time — lotus to cube — showing on its final face a new engraving: a gorilla, ring-tailed lemurs at her heels, holding a door shut.

She solved it backward, every note in reverse, the whole length of the night, while Rory slept the sleep of the recently depelted above, wrapped in a quilt, his fur — it grew back; fur does — already fuzzing pink at the shoulder.

The last note faded. The box was only a box: black wood, brass, cold as a struck glass.

Kirsty gave it to a hospital charity shop in November, sealed in a biscuit tin under old Christmas cards, and told herself that was the end of it, and was nearly right.

In the spring, in a curio shop three cities away, a dealer took from a biscuit tin the box of black wood and brass, turned its tail, and heard a note, cold and sweet — and a customer, a langur with a burned-out engine and gray behind the eyes, went quite still and reached out one paw.

"I want to hear it *sing,*" said the customer.

Some monkeys will ring any bell twice.

The Order of the Peel is patient. It has all the time in the world, and all the fruit.

And somewhere, beyond a wall that opens along seams you have never needed to notice, a bell is tolling — lightly, politely, one note short of forever.
