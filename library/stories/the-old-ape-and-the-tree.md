# The Old Ape and the Tree

*A parody of "The Old Man and the Sea" by Ernest Hemingway*

He was an old gorilla who climbed alone, and he had gone eighty-four days now without taking a ripe banana. In the first forty days a young chimp had climbed with him. But the boy's parents had told him the old ape was salao, which is the worst form of unlucky, and had sent him to swing with a younger troop that brought home three good bunches a week.

The boy brought him roasted plantains from his own supper.

"I could climb with you again," the boy said. "We have made a little 'nana-money."

"They have a troop," the old ape said. "You are with a lucky troop. Stay with them."

"But remember how long I held the branch for you when the big bunch came?"

The old ape groomed the boy's head gently. "I remember," he said. "You did not leave me."

They looked at the bark-pages from the far groves, as they always did, and talked of the great champion of the vine-leagues, who had swung through a whole season on a cracked heel and never told a soul, because he played for the love of the troop.

"They say his father was a gorilla," said the boy.

"Perhaps. If the great one were resting here, I would take him up the far ridge with me. There are no weak branches where he would go."

They carried the old ape's coiled vines up to the terrace together, and the boy asked, "Are you strong enough now for a truly big bunch?"

"I think so," said the old ape. "And I have many tricks. I may not be as strong as I think I am. But I know many tricks, and I have resolution."

"I'll wake you in the morning," said the boy.

That night the old ape dreamed of the mist meadows, and in his dreams he no longer saw storms, nor rivals, nor great storehouses of fruit. He dreamed only of the young gorillas playing in the tall grass, and he loved them as he loved the boy.

On the eighty-fifth day — eighty-five being a lucky number, he told himself — he went far out: past every ropey vine the other troops worked, past the last marked fig on the last marked ridge, out where the wild trees stood, because his big bunch, he believed, must be somewhere, and no ape is ever beaten until he believes it.

He set his vine loops at every height, each baited with a sour 'nan and measured in his head: forty branches, sixty branches, one hundred and beyond. A troop of young porpoise-monkeys swung past below him, rolling and tumbling in the open lanes, and he spoke to them, for it is lonely far out. "They are good," he said. "They play and make jokes and love one another. They are our brothers in the climbing way, though they are younger and better off." A small tired warbler-monkey landed on his shoulder and hid its head under its wing. "Take a good rest, small friend," said the old ape. "Then go in and take your chance like any ape or bird." The warbler stayed while the wind blew, and the old ape stayed too.

At noon, in the crown of the Champion Tree, where no ape had ever dared to climb, his vine lasso closed on something enormous.

He set his feet against the bole and his hands into the bark, and the pull came down the branch like the weight of the whole jungle, and the old ape felt the slow, heavy strength of the Great Bunch of the ridge.

"Bunch," he said aloud, for no ape is ever alone at the top of a tree, "I love you and respect you very much. But I shall pluck you dead before this day ends."

The bunch surged, and dragged him three branches out from the trunk. His left hand cramped into a claw, a dried pod of a hand, and he gnashed his teeth at it.

"Hand," he said. "Be a chimp's hand. Hold for your ape."

He ate sour wild bananas for strength as he rode the branch, and the mist came up, and the stars wheeled. The second day, and the second night, the Great Bunch circled in the crown below, and the vine cut his paws and his shoulders burned, and still the bunch would not come up.

"Bunch," he said, "you are killing me. But you have a right to. Never have I seen a greater, more beautiful, or more calm bunch than you, brother. Come up and let me pluck you. Come up, my brother — I'll stay with you until I am dead."

And on the second morning the old ape got one arm around the stem, and the great golden bunch came down slowly, slowly, and lay across the bough beside him like a fallen sun.

"Though it is unjust," he thought, "I will show you what an ape can do."

But the glide home was long, and the scent of bruised gold ran down the ridge ahead of him like a rumor of money. First came the harpy eagles.

"Harpy!" he cried. "Harpy eagle!"

He fought them with his fruit-hook and his broken branch, and drove them off, but each one carved its pounds of gold away. He did not look at the stem anymore, for it was easier not to; he looked only for the next wing coming, and struck it. Then came the bands of raiding capuchins, bold as pirates, swinging out to strip the bunch, and the old ape stood on the bough-way and swung his club until his eyes could not see the club.

"An ape is not made for defeat," he said. "An ape can be peeled but not defeated."

"They beat me, friend," he told the ridge, when the last raider fell away into the green. "They beat me truly."

He reached home at dusk with the great bare stem laid across his shoulders, its pale length swaying, every finger of fruit taken.

"What's that?" a young monkey asked at the terrace of the feeding trees.

"Tibar?" asked another, mispronouncing it.

"Estemar," said the waiter-monkey gravely. "Banana stem." And the measurer, an old ape with a knotted string, ran his string up the pale spine of it and said, without any theatre at all, "Eighty hands from tip to tip" — and the young monkeys at the terrace whistled, and did not laugh.

The boy came and looked, and then looked at the old ape's bleeding hands, and began to cry. He went and brought green sap and pressed leaves and cool water, and sat down beside him.

"I don't care," the boy said. "You'll make many bunches now. I have cried many 'nanas. Now we climb together, whatever the troop says, and I will fetch the grooming leaves and the sap for your shoulders."

"Thank you," said the old ape. "They beat me, boy. They truly beat me."

"The bunch didn't beat you," said the boy. "Not the bunch."

"No," the old ape agreed. "Truly. It was after that."

"How much did you suffer?"

"Plenty," said the old ape.

They sat together while the night wind came up through the branches. The old ape slept in his chair, fur to the sky, and the boy sat by him, grooming him, the way it has gone since the first troop.

The old ape was dreaming again. He was dreaming of the young gorillas playing in the mist meadows.
