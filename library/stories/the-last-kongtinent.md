# The Last Kongtinent

*A parody of "The Last Continent"*

Between the Great Southern Ocean and the Edge of the world lies the Last Kongtinent, a sunburnt country sweeping plains, of rugged mountain ranges and droughts and flooding rains, provided you were patient, because the rain had been running several thousand years late.

Also, everything on it was trying to kill you. Not metaphorically. The snakes were the most venomous in the world, the spiders had attitudes, the trees dropped fist-sized nuts onto unsuspecting heads, and even the sheep — introduced, cheerful, apparently harmless — were organizing.

This is where Rincewind found himself, after the usual technical mishap with the teleport. Rincewind was a wizard in the sense that a wet match is a fire: theoretically, with paperwork. His hat said WIZZARD, because he had bought it from someone who couldn't spell, and he had survived hazards across three hemispheres by running away from them at speeds that made him, technically, the greatest athlete of his age.

The Luggage had not come with him. This worried him more than the snakes. At least with the Luggage you always knew where the danger was: behind you, on hundreds of little legs.

"Right," said Rincewind, to four hundred miles of red sand. "Nice continent. Very... spacious." Then he began to walk, because in his experience the safest place was somewhere else, moving fast.

He walked for a long time. Along the way, purely in order not to die, Rincewind acquired a series of jobs. He shore sheep (the sheep struck for better conditions). He dug ditches (the ditches filled in behind him, hopefully not on purpose). He washed glasses at a pub in a town called Woop Woop, where the beer was older than the town and the bar flies wore hats. The publican, a sun-cured macaque named Banjo, watched Rincewind dodge a swooping magpie, a lunging goanna, and a falling tree, all in one smooth sequence while carrying a tray.

"You're a natural survivor, mate," said Banjo.

"It's my only skill," said Rincewind. "That and screaming."

"Good on ya. There's a creature been asking after you."

The creature was waiting outside, silhouetted against the furnace of the sunset: a kongaroo. That is, a kangaroo the size of a gorilla, or possibly a gorilla built along kangaroo lines, upright, muscular, boxing gloves entirely optional but implied. Its name was Scrappy, and it spoke in a voice like the crack of a dry riverbed.

"You him, are you," said Scrappy. It wasn't a question. "The one in the paintin's."

"What paintings?"

Scrappy tilted its head meaningfully. And Rincewind felt, in the way of men about to be hugely inconvenienced by destiny, the land itself lean closer, listening. There were paintings in the red caves of the interior, Scrappy explained, older than memory: pictures of the ancestors, the rain dances, the great beasts — and one figure repeated over and over, running, hat on head, a wallaby's terror in his stance.

"That's not me," said Rincewind. "I'm sure lots of people have hats."

"Land says it's you," said Scrappy. "Land's never wrong. Only late."

And that was the trouble. The land was dying of thirst. The rain had gone missing, the waterholes were cracking like old plates, and the land had decided, in its slow continental way, that it wanted Rincewind to fix it.

"I don't do heroics," said Rincewind. "I do running away. I'm very experienced."

"No worries," said Scrappy, settling onto its tail like a small throne. "Runnin's in the paintin's too."

Meanwhile, several thousand miles and, as it turned out, thirty thousand years to the north, the rest of Unseen University was in difficulties of its own.

It had begun with the Librarian, an orangutan of great dignity who had been an orangutan for so long that he refused to be turned back into a human on the grounds that he'd lose tenure. Now he was ill — a morphic instability, his shape flickering between marmoset, baboon, and a confused proboscis monkey at inconvenient moments — and the cure required him to be addressed by his original human name, which only Rincewind knew. Hence the attempt to fetch Rincewind home, hence the High Energy Magic apparatus, hence the teleport accident that deposited the entire faculty — Archchancellor Ridcully, Ponder Stibbons, the Senior Wrangler, the Lecturer in Recent Runes, the Bursar, and the housekeeper Mrs. Whitlow — not on the Last Kongtinent at all, but on a tiny sunny island in the middle of nowhere, occupied by a small excited chimpanzee in a workshop apron.

"I say! Visitors!" said the chimpanzee. "You'll be wanting to see the designs. I'm the God of Evolution. Everything here is one of a kind, made to order, no need for all that tedious natural selection when you've got craftmanship!"

The wizards were given the tour: the duck-billed, web-footed, egg-laying, venomous-spurred aquatic mole, assembled from the leftovers bin on a Friday afternoon before a long weekend.

"What do you call it?" asked Mrs. Whitlow, admiring.

"Oh, I hadn't bothered," said the God. "It's a proof of concept."

The wizards stayed for dinner. This was a mistake, because the apparatus, back at the University, chose that moment to discharge, and dinner became temporal. When the party finally staggered out of the misfiring spell, they were on the Last Kongtinent all right — but the stars were wrong, the coastline was a different shape, and the enthusiastic young god's just-finished prototypes were drifting out to sea on rafts of leaves toward a young and empty landmass on the horizon.

"Surely those creatures won't just... colonize an entire continent," said the Senior Wrangler.

"In my experience," said Ridcully, "nothing breeds like something you haven't seen before."

The wizards wandered the ancient Kongtinent and did what wizards always do in the past: they broke history gently. They gave the local tribes the benefit of fire, cooking, and table manners, and received in return a profound education in how to survive here, which they immediately forgot. They introduced Mrs. Whitlow's peach preserve to an innocent landmass, and wondered forever after how the peach got here first. They found a cave wall, and the Senior Wrangler, feeling artistic, painted a figure running, with a hat, to illustrate his travel lecture. The hands that painted the hero were, of course, the hands that had seen him.

Somewhere downstream of the aeons, Scrappy squinted at a freshly weathered cave painting. "See? Told ya."

The trail of destiny — Scrappy hopping patiently alongside, the land politely relocating waterholes so Rincewind would go the right way — led at last to a great red monolith in the desert's dead heart, where the clouds had been stacked up for millennia like furniture in a room nobody dusted, waiting for one correct move.

"I don't know any rain dances!" protested Rincewind, at the top of the monolith, with the whole continent watching.

"Everyone knows the dance," said Scrappy. "You been doin' it since you got here. It's called runnin'."

And Rincewind understood that in four hundred miles of dodging, ducking, weaving and sprinting he had, to the watching land, performed the oldest dance of all, and performed it beautifully, and never once stopped. All that remained was the finale.

What happened next, the Kongtinent's people still argue about. Some say lightning split the sky. Some say a figure danced atop the great red rock for a full day, arms windmilling, hat sailing off, and some say a voice cried out the most ancient and sacred words of power: Not the snakes! Why is it always the snakes!

And the land, satisfied at last, unstacked the furniture.

It rained. Not politely. Not a drizzle. It rained the way the Kongtinent rains after a drought: warm, roaring, deluging, filling the waterholes in hours and the dry rivers in a day, and every creature on the continent stood out in it with their arms up, even the sheep, briefly, before resuming negotiations.

By the time the wizards arrived via an accurately aimed rescue spell, having looped neatly back through the tail of their own timestream, the Kongtinent was green, the beer paradox had several more ancestors than it strictly needed, and a soggy wizard in a battered hat was being solemnly sung about in eleven languages.

"No, thank you," Rincewind told Scrappy, as the kongaroo prepared to hop off into legend. "I don't want a legend. I want a small room with no wildlife in it."

"Can't help you there, mate," said Scrappy. "Everything here is wildlife. Even the legends."

The Librarian, cured at last when Rincewind was dragged back to the University and made to say the name three times into the creature's ear, manifested as an orangutan again, declared "Ook" with enormous relief, and hit Rincewind affectionately with a chair. And the last anyone saw of the Luggage, it was surfing the flood crest down a newborn river, lids open wide, full of someone else's beer.

And on the Last Kongtinent, when the rains are late, the old monkeys still tell of the Rainmaker — the pale worried wizard who danced for four hundred miles and never stopped, and brought the water home.

"That's me," said Rincewind, from behind a locked door in Ankh-Morpork. "In the paintings. But I didn't do anything! I just ran away!"

Which, on the Last Kongtinent, is a sacred art.
