# The Day of the Tree-fids

*A parody of "The Day of the Triffids" by John Wyndham*

When a day that you happen to know is Fig-Day starts off by sounding like Sun-Day, there is something seriously wrong somewhere.

That was the thought that came to Billy Masen, macaque, age twenty-six, as he lay in the zoo infirmary with his eyes wrapped in banana peel. He had gone in for a minor operation — a scratched cornea, courtesy of a tree-fid seed pod — and had missed, of all things, the once-in-a-lifetime green comet shower that half the monkey world had stayed up all night to watch.

He could hear them cheering from his cot. Then he could hear, very faintly, that the cheering had stopped.

"Don't unwrap those peels," the night nurse had told him, "or you'll be a blind monkey in a world of trouble." In the morning she was gone. In the morning everyone was gone, in the sense that they were all still there, but there was nobody left behind their eyes. The whole Great Monkey City sat in the branches, blinded by the beautiful green sky, holding out their hands to whatever touched them.

You will want to know about the tree-fids. Everyone always does. They were walking trees — three legs, one long whipping stem — farmed for their fig-oil, which fried a superior banana fritter. Nobody knew quite where they had been bred; the story was a laboratory behind the Bamboo Curtain, and a crate of smuggled seedlings, and a very quiet customs monkey. The farms had fences. The farms had keepers. The keepers were now blind, and the fences were suggestions, and a tree-fid that can walk will walk.

The tree-fid's method is simple: it waits until it hears a monkey groping along, then it flings a hard green fig with terrible accuracy at the back of his head, then it walks over and settles its roots in. They are patient the way weather is patient. They also talk to each other, in the evenings, by rapping their root-knuckles on hollow ground — a dry clatter rolling across the dark fields — which makes them the only landlords in history that drum on the floor at *you*.

By the time Billy unwrapped his peels and saw daylight — whole, ordinary, miraculous daylight — he could hear the clatter already, and under it, closer, the whistles. Thwip. Thwip.

He met Josella Howler on the second day, in a square full of blinded apes who were being organized into chains by a bossy chimpanzee named Coker. She had a torn ear, a firm grip, and a notorious reputation: she had written a book called *Grooming Is My Adventure*, which every monkey in the city claimed to be shocked by and had read twice.

"You can see," she said. "You looked at me before I squawked."

"I can see. I had my eyes bandaged. Banana peel."

"That's the first lucky thing I've heard all week. Congratulations. You are now the most valuable monkey in the city, and the most hunted."

She was right, twice over. Coker's crowd wanted the sighted to serve as eyes for the blind, one chained groomer to twenty blinded strangers — an arrangement Coker defended as duty and Billy recognized as chain. From the docks came a fiercer crowd, led by a silky colobus named Wilfreda, who wanted them for something worse: a breeding colony, for the day when all the blind generations failed. Billy and Josella slipped their chains in the night, as any sensible pair of monkeys would, and swung for the hills.

They should have kept swinging. At Tinsham Manor they found the remains of the sensible party: Michael Beadune, the planner, with maps and ration lists and a scheme for pairing every sighted monkey with three blinded ones — Miss Durrant, the organizer, with a chapel, a rota, and a growing conviction that soap was a sacrament — and about forty others arguing over which came first. Billy and Josella stayed a week. Then half the party followed Miss Durrant into all-night prayer meetings, and the other half followed Wilfreda out the gate, and the sensible middle dissolved, as sensible middles do, for want of anyone willing to be fierce about sense.

"The trouble with organizing monkeys," said Coker — who had turned up at Tinsham himself, weary and unchained, having stayed behind to feed his blinded charges until they could feed themselves — "is that it requires monkeys."

They found a farm above the river — a walled fig orchard, a well, a troop of eleven other sighted escapees, and one small orphan named Susu, whom Josella collected off a roadside and never once afterward referred to as adopted. For one autumn it nearly worked. They planted. They groomed. They argued about whose turn it was to fry the grubs. Beyond the wall, the clatter circled and the whistles answered it, tree-fid to tree-fid, all across the county, and Billy — who had worked the fig-oil farms and knew the beasts the way some monkeys know wine — did not like the sound of it at all.

"They're conferring," he said. "They don't Confer about the weather."

The tree-fids came in the spring, a slow patient herd of thirty, walking on their three legs like malevolent umbrellas. They did not charge. They stood off at fig-throwing distance, day after day, and rapped their roots, and took the measure of the wall. The troop fought them with machetes and long poles and no imagination whatsoever, and lost the well, and lost the south wall, and one night lost the eldest of them, old Whitley, to a fig precisely thrown. They found him at dawn, roots already settling into him, the tree-fid standing over him with the deep contentment of a landlord.

"We can't hold the orchard," Billy said. "We've been defending a larder that walks."

"Then we go where the tree-fids can't," said Josella. "Over the water. The Isle of Ripe. Trees, but no walking ones. We start over, and we do it better this time — everyone grooms, everyone carries, nobody owns the top branch."

"And them?" said Susu, nodding at the herd.

"They come too, eventually. That's the joke of it. They're the best farmers in the world — they weed, they dig, they fertilize, they're worth their weight in oil. They'd have been a blessing, if they'd arrived a century at a time instead of all at once." Billy looked at the wall, at the elms beyond it, at the whole green apparatus of the old world. "We're the ones who got blinded, Susu. They only ever wanted what was ripe."

They sailed at dawn, fourteen monkeys and a stolen barge, leaving the Great Monkey City to the green rustle of its new landlords. And who blinded the city — whether the comet was a weapon that slipped its leash, or a warning nobody stayed awake to read, or just the sky being casual — nobody ever established, which tells you something about how much the answer would have helped.

Years later, on the Isle of Ripe, Billy wrote it all down for the youngsters, who found the idea of a blind city hard to believe and the idea of walking trees excellent fun. He ended his account the way I end this one: with the last thing he saw on the mainland shore.

The tree-fid stood at the water's edge, swaying on its three legs, patient as a creditor.

And waited.
