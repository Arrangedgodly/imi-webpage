# The Simiad

*A parody of "The Iliad"*

Sing, O goddess, the rage of Ape-chilles, son of the silverback Peleus — rage that hurled countless brave monkey souls down to the gloomy shade and made their bodies a feast for the jackals of the plain. Sing how the will of Zeus was woven through it all.

Ten years the siege of Fig-Troy had dragged on. The allied troops of the monkeys, in their thousand beached ships of woven vine, had come for one reason: Helen, wife of Menelaus, the face that launched a thousand lianas, carried off by Paris, the Trojan prince with the softest fur and the emptiest head.

But in the tenth year, the two greatest warriors quarrelled.

Agamemnon, the overlord, had been forced to give up his prize, a basket of golden bananas; he compensated himself by seizing Briseis, the prize of Ape-chilles.

"You shameless, banana-hearted fraud!" roared Ape-chilles, fastest and deadliest of all monkeys under the canopy. "You never lead the charge yourself! I came here for *your* war — the Trojans never harmed *my* figs. Take her, then. But mark this: when Hector burns the ships and hacks at your own tail, you will beg me back, and I will not come."

He sat down by his ships, and he sulked.

Old Nestor counseled; the troops grumbled; and Zeus tipped the scales. The Trojans, led by Hector — noblest of them all, a gorilla with the weight of a whole city on his shoulders — drove the monkey troops back to the beach.

Once, briefly, the war offered to settle itself: a duel, Paris against Menelaus, winner keeps Helen and the golden bananas. From the wall Helen herself pointed out the Ape-chiefs to old King Priam, since ten years had not taught him to recognize his enemies — "That one is Agamemnon, a good king and a strong spear-hand. I was once a guest in his house. Shame, shame, shame." She said it lightly; the goddess made her say it.

Paris fought exactly as he lived — prettily and briefly — and when Menelaus had him by the fur-helmet and was dragging him toward the Ape lines like a caught thief, Aphrodite wrapped the prince in a fig-leaf fog and set him down at home in his own perfumed chamber, where he sat, smiling faintly, smelling of defeat.

"A god took him," the armies agreed, and went back to killing each other, which is what armies do with a peace deal.

The siege came so near its end that Agamemnon swallowed his pride. He sent an embassy — smooth-tongued Ookysseus, giant Aias, and old Phoenix — to Ape-chilles' grove with an offer: Briseis back untouched, seven kettles of figs, ten talents of gold, twelve race-macaques, seven women skilled in grooming, one of Agamemnon's own daughters married with no bride-price, and the pick of seven cities.

They found Ape-chilles strumming a fig-wood lyre and singing of the fame of monkeys. He fed them splendidly — sulking heroes are always the best hosts — and then declined.

"I hate a liar like the gates of Hades. If he gave me ten times as much, it would not buy me. Tell the Atreid-ape to think of some other way of saving the ships. I am going home. My mother tells me I have two fates: a long, dull life at home, or a short, glorious one here. I have chosen — I sail at dawn."

He did not sail at dawn. He came to the wall.

Before the gates, Hector paused with his wife Andromache and their baby.

"I know it, my wild one," she said. "Your strength will be your death — and mine. But look: your own son is frightened of your plume."

Hector laughed, lifted the terrifying crest of red fur from his helmet, and kissed his boy. "Zeus and all you gods: grant this child to be like me, first in the troop. And let someone say, 'He is better than his father.'"

Then Zeus raised his golden scales, and Hector's day weighed heavier. Hera, Zeus's queen, saw her chance; she oiled her fur, put on the sash of Aphrodite with all the longing in it stitched in, and dazzled Zeus on the mountaintop so thoroughly that he forgot the battle for a whole afternoon — the trick every monkey knows, and no monkey survives. The tide turned; the Trojans rallied too far; and in the night raid that followed, Diomedes and Ookysseus caught Dolon, a Trojan spy wearing a wolfskin and carrying information.

"Spare me," chattered Dolon, "and I will tell you everything — where the sentries sleep, where Rhesus keeps his famous snow-white macques, and which of my commanders I dislike, which is all of them."

They took the information, and then — the goddess sings honestly, if not kindly — they did not spare him, for a spy who sells everyone once will sell you twice.

And then the worst day. Patroclus, dearest friend of Ape-chilles, could bear the losing no longer. "Lend me your armor. Let the Trojans think you have come back."

Ape-chilles gave it. Patroclus fought like a storm and drove the Trojans from the ships — three times he surged against the walls, and the third time his fate met him — Hector, before the walls.

"The birds, not the monkeys, will eat *you*," said Hector over him, taking the borrowed armor. "Who was it, friend, that fed you to the spear?"

When word reached Ape-chilles, he took up new armor forged by the god-smith himself — shield upon shield of worked bronze, upon which were hammered the whole canopy: the stars, the rivers, weddings and lawsuits, a troop at harvest, a lion eating lies — and his rage — sing it, goddess — that rage came down like the ending of the world.

"I will not live long," he said quietly, "but I will live greatly."

The fig-river choked with Trojans. Hector, at last, stood alone before the gates. Three times they circled; the spear flew; Hector fell.

"Beg me not," said Ape-chilles, standing over him. "You took my friend from me."

"Give my body back to my father," Hector whispered — and Ape-chilles lashed him to his vine-chariot and dragged him around the fig-grove walls. Not his finest hour. Rage, the goddess sings, makes even champions ugly.

For twelve days the grief went on — and for twelve days the funeral games: chariot races where the prize went to the ape who came last because he had driven the hardest and cried the most; a discus thrown beyond the measurers; a foot-race in which Athena tripped the leader ("The gods cheat," said Menelaus, darkly, "it is how they show favor"); and a beauty contest of grief, won by everyone, weeping. Then, on the twelfth night, old Priam, the white-backed king of Troy, came down from the city alone, driving a wagon loaded with figs and golden bananas. He knelt before the monkey who had killed his son and kissed his hands — the terrible, banana-taking hands of Ape-chilles.

"Remember your own father, Ape-chilles. I have endured what no monkey on earth has ever endured before: I have put my lips to the hands of the killer of my son."

Ape-chilles looked at the old king, and remembered his own father far away, and wept. They wept together, the killer and the bereft, grief for Hector and grief for Patroclus mixed in one basket.

"Your son is ransomed, noble king. At dawn you shall have him."

For nine days the Trojans gathered fig-wood. On the tenth they burned Hector, heaped his barrow high, and held the feast of mourning.

And the siege went on — for Helen's face still launched lianas, the gods still meddled, and in the monkey camp a very clever macaque named Ookysseus was already sketching plans for an enormous hollow fig.

But the Simiad ends here — at Hector's fire, and two enemies sitting quietly in a tent, sharing figs, having learned the price of rage.
