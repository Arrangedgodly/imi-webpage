# The Silverback of Notre-Dame

*A parody of "The Hunchback of Notre-Dame"*

In the jungle of Paris, in the year 1482, there was a cathedral, and in the cathedral there were bells, and in the bells there was a gorilla.

He had been left, as an infant with a mountainous silver shoulder and a face like a gargoyle's daydream, upon the foundling bed of Notre-Dame. Claude Frollo, the Archdeacon — a grave red orangutan whose heart had dried like a fig in his chest — adopted the child for penance, named him Quasimodo, hid him in the tower, and made him bell-ringer. The bells, which Quasimodo loved as other children love their mothers, made him deaf. This is the way of bells: they take the ears of those who serve them. Frollo raised one other: his little brother Jehan, a giddy orange youth who studied at the university everything except studying, and extracted bananas from his brother the Archdeacon with the persistence of a woodpecker.

Once a year, on the Feast of Fools, the whole jungle of Paris went briefly and legally bananas. The poet Pierre Gringoire presented a masterpiece that morning to an audience which, at the crucial moment, discovered it would rather elect a pope. And on that Feast the mob elected its Bananapope — the monkey with the ugliest grin in all France — and crowned him.

They crowned Quasimodo. He was carried through the streets on a shield of woven fig branches, delighted, believing himself loved at last. Then the crowd, which had been laughing with him, found it funnier to laugh at him, and began to pelt him with peel and mud, and Quasimodo, hooting miserably under his tin crown, could not even hear the mockery coming.

A young gypsy capuchin danced out of the crowd, climbed the rail, and put a cup of water to his lips. She was called Esmeralda, and at her hip rode Djali, a bushbaby no bigger than a fist with ears like fans, who could count, spell, and tap the hour on a little drum.

"Those bells must love you," she told him, in the gestures and lip-words a deaf gorilla can follow. "Anybody loved by a bell can't be all ugly."

Quasimodo loved her from that moment, the way a bell loves its rope.

Frollo loved her too, which was far more dangerous, for the Archdeacon had spent forty years teaching himself not to want anything, and here was this dancing gypsy, and his whole dried-fig heart cracked in the night. He ordered Quasimodo to carry her off. Quasimodo obeyed; Captain Phoebus, a golden baboon in gleaming armor, did not; Quasimodo was seized, flogged at the pillory, and left in the rain to be a lesson. Esmeralda brought him water again — twice, then, the only soul in Paris who had. It was more than the city ever did for anybody.

For Frollo, the worst of it was Phoebus. He spied on the captain's moonlit meetings with Esmeralda, gnawing his own knuckles in the dark, and on the night Phoebus arranged to meet her in a lantern-lit garret, the Archdeacon let himself in with a stolen key, whispered, "You do not love her, then," and struck at the golden baboon in the dark where the whole lantern-lit jungle of Paris could be blamed for it. Phoebus fell. Esmeralda screamed. And the jungle-law of Paris, which had never once convicted a lantern, took her instead.

At her trial, everything was evidence. A goat that taps the hour is not a pet but a familiar; the prosecution demonstrated, and Djali, delighted to be asked, spelled P-H-O-E-B-U-S with lettered peels, which finished it. Phoebus, recovered, did not come — he was getting engaged to his betrothed, Fleur-de-Lys, and let the court think what it liked about a woman he could not remember marrying. Put to the question, Esmeralda confessed to everything except the spelling. She was carried to the gallows-tree at dawn.

And down the wall of Notre-Dame, like a gargoyle quitting its post, came Quasimodo.

He swung on a bell-rope, plucked her off the cart of the condemned in front of the whole jungle, carried her through the great door over his head, and held her up at the top of his broken voice, roaring:

"SANCTUARY! SANCTUARY! SANCTUARY!"

The cathedral never rang louder than it rang that morning.

Frollo came that night to the cell where she slept. "I love you," he said, and it was a confession and an accusation in the same breath. "I have fought it with prayer, with study, with fasting, and the more I fight it the more I love you. Love me, and I will save you. Refuse, and I hand you to the rope myself. Choose between my love and the gibbet."

"You are kinder than he is, in your way," said Esmeralda, offering him the little dagger from her belt, hilt first. "One of us should use it."

Frollo fled up the stairs, weeping and hating, which in that monkey had become one operation.

The truands of the Court of Miracles — every beggar, thief, and unregistered monkey of Paris, who had no cathedral and wanted their sister back — rose up and stormed Notre-Dame to fetch her out. Quasimodo defended it alone: dropping beams, hauling up the great timber by pulleys, pouring boiling banana-oil down the stairs, deaf to every parley, believing until too late that they were Frollo's men come to hang her. Among the grapnels came a laughing orange student. Quasimodo tipped the ladder, and Jehan fell, and only when the hooting stopped did Quasimodo see whose laugh it had been. The King's archers broke in behind the truands — for the Crown had reconsidered sanctuary, with the wisdom of accountants — and in the smoke and swinging beams, a hooded orangutan and a distracted poet carried Esmeralda out by a postern door. Gringoire carried, to his lasting credit, Djali.

Frollo left her in the square at the foot of the gibbet, in the cell of Sister Sachette, the old recluse who had spent sixteen years cursing gypsies for stealing her baby girl — Esmeralda, whose little baby shoe Sachette had kissed until it was soft as fur. The recognition took ten seconds. The reunion took thirty. Then the archers arrived, and pried the mother off the daughter, and hanged Esmeralda on the gallows-tree, high enough that Sachette could see her shoe's twin, the amulet, cut from her neck at last.

From the tower, Frollo watched the cart tip, and Quasimodo, searching the crowd for his gypsy, saw the Archdeacon laugh.

"There is your beloved!" cried Frollo, in a voice like a fig splitting. And Quasimodo, wild with grief, took his foster father by the black robe with both huge hands and pushed him off the gallery into the dark.

"It is fate!" the Archdeacon cried as he fell — which is what a fig like that always says on the way down. The river took him without comment.

Quasimodo watched the splash, and then turned to the city of roofs below him and the silent bells above.

"Why was I not carved from stone like thee?" he asked the nearest gargoyle, who was his oldest friend. "The world would have made sense."

He was never seen again. He did not ring the bells; he did not come down; Djali and Gringoire mourned her, in their ways, and Phoebus married Fleur-de-Lys, and Paris got on with its feasts.

A year and a half later, in the vault at Monfaucon among the bones of the hanged, those burying a fresh body came upon the skeleton of a capuchin, and around it, the skeleton of an enormous gorilla, one bony arm around hers, the skull bent to hers as if it still listened for an answer. When the gravediggers tried to part them, both crumbled — to dust, to fur, to nothing. Which of the two had held on, nobody could say; the story only says that of all the love in Paris that year, this was the pair that kept its embrace.
