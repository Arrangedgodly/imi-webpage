# The Canopy Tails

*A parody of "The Canterbury Tales"*

When that April with his showers sweet
The drought of March hath pierced to the root,
And every fig in every wood hangs fat,
Then longen apes to go on pilgrim-swings —

And so, one April at the Tabbard Branch — a famous staging-tree in Southwold-wood — I fell in with nine-and-twenty apes out of every troop and thicket, all bound for the Shrine of the Blessed Golden Banana at Canopybury.

A fairer company no troop could wish. There was a Prioress, Madame Eglantiny, who sang the service sweetly, intoned through the nose, and spoke ape-French of the Stratford-Bush — for French of Paris was to her unknown. She kept little squirrel-dogs and fed them roasted fig and fine white bread, for a proper holy ape feeds her dogs before the poor. There was a Monk, a manly ape and able to be an abbot: he kept greyhounds as swift as fowl in flight, and when I reminded him of the Rule of St Benedict, he said rules were old and out of date — "Why should an ape make himself a wood-chimp, poring ever on a book?" There was a Friar, wanton and merry, licensed to hear confessions, who gave easy penance where he saw hope of a good dinner, and knew every barmaid in the shire by her grooming-pattern. There was a Clerk of Oxenford-grove, as lean as his hobby-horse, with twenty books and not one banana — gladly would he learn, and gladly teach. And there was a Parson, poor in goods but rich in holy thought, who walked with a fig-leaf staff and practiced first what afterward he taught. "If gold rust," said he, "what shall iron do?"

Our Host was a broad silverback, bold of speech, wise in fig-wine, and no ape's fool.

"Look at you all," he said, thumping the cask. "A pleasure-troupe going to waste. My proposal: every one of you shall tell two tails on the way out and two more coming home — tales of adventure, or wisdom, or honest monkey business — and whoever tells the best shall have a free banana-split at the Tabbard at everyone else's expense. I shall ride along as judge — bribable only by good storytelling."

We drew lots, and the lot fell to the Chimpion — a grave old soldier-ape who had fought fifteen border skirmishes over mango rights and never once boasted (he only alluded).

**The Chimpion's Tale.** "Once in old Ape-thens there ruled a duke named Theseed, who imprisoned two cousin-knights, Palamonkey and Arcite. From their tower they spied the duke's young sister Emape gathering flowers, and both were smitten before the dew had dried. 'I saw her first,' said Palamonkey. 'I loved her more,' said Arcite — and cousins who had shared one blanket now sharpened sticks at each other through the bars. Freed, they met in tournament for her hand. Arcite's stick was truer; Arcite's luck was not: glancing up to thank the gods, he was thrown by his own startled cassowary, and died forgiving everyone, which is very hard. 'She was yours first, cousin,' he told Palamonkey, 'and you were always the better ape.' So Theseed joined Palamonkey and Emape in marriage. Thus ends the Chimpion's tale, in order, even unto tears."

The Host applauded. The Mandrill — a stout red-bottomed ape three fig-wines deep — climbed onto the cask uninvited.

**The Mandrill's Tale.** "John, an old carpenter-ape of Oxenford-grove, married young Aliswinger, and let a room to Nicholas, a nimble young clerk with nimble young opinions. Below in the garden sang Absolon, a dainty ape who combed his tail. Nicholas and Aliswinger wanted a whole night, so Nicholas told the old ape, with a face of doom: 'The sky has whispered to me — a second Flood is coming! We must sleep each in a tub, hoisted to the topmost branch!' The old ape believed him, as old apes in love with young wives believe convenient nonsense. That night, while the carpenter snored aloft in his tub, the young ones swung down to the garden; and Absolon, passing, begged a kiss in the dark from the window. What he received was neither cheek nor chin, but Nicholas, who had climbed up to moon the fellow. Absolon went away humbled, and returned with a fire-ant on a twig, applied to the same spot; and Nicholas's shriek of 'WATER! WATER!' so alarmed the old carpenter that he cut his rope and came down through the whole fig tree, tub and all, to the astonishment of the neighbors." The Mandrill sat down to enormous cheering.

But up rose the Wife of Banyan, gap-toothed and smiling, an expert in far countries and in marriage. "Experience, though none authority, is government enough for me, good apes — for since I was twelve, thank the orchard, I have had five prime mates at the church door, not counting the ones who only came to dinner. And what do women most desire? I shall tell you, for an ugly old she-ape once told a knight-ape who had a year to find out or hang: women desire sovereignty over their own lives — which in the canopy means the sunny branch, and the last word, and the biggest fig. The knight guessed it, and when the old she-ape offered him a bargain — be my mate, and I shall be young and true by turns — he chose honor, and she gave him all three. Which teaches: give up the last word, and win the argument."

"Madame," quavered the Pardoner, who had been trying to sell us the certified knuckle-bone of Saint Baboon (guaranteed against fleas, mange, and unripe fruit; seal included, peel slightly used), "I was about to take a wife."

"You will not be the last to try, dear," said she, and the Host fined the Pardoner a round of figs for fraud, ruling that a relic is not a tale, it is a business.

"Then hear a sermon instead," said the Pardoner, wounded, "for my theme is always one: Radix malorum est Cupiditas — greed is the root of evil, and of most bad harvests. **The Pardoner's Tale.** In Flanders-grove there lived three young rioters who drank all day and feared nothing. One morning they heard a bell: a corpse being carried to its fig-grave. 'Who killed him?' they cried. 'A plague — a thing called Death,' said the bearer. 'Death has killed a thousand in this very wood!' The three swore an oath: they would seek out Death and kill him. Down the road they met an ancient, withered ape. 'Where is Death, old one?' 'Under that fig tree,' said he, and hobbled away laughing, which they should have noted. Under the tree they found no Death — they found a heap of golden bananas, seven bushels, shining. Long deliberation was held, and greed won on all ballots. Two were to stab the third at supper; meanwhile the third bought rat-bark to poison the bananas and have it all. That night, under the fig tree, the two killed the third, sat down to celebrate, ate the bananas, and died beside him. Thus they found Death — precisely where they had been told he sat. Now, friends — contributions for the knuckle-bone?"

"Sit down," said the Host, "before I contribute you to the river."

We reached the shrine at dusk next day, the Blessed Golden Banana glittering through the leaves, and climbed down and went in on our knees, which is no way for an ape to travel and exactly why it counts. Which tail won the banana-split? The Host never did declare — he said the arguing on the road home was the best entertainment of the pilgrimage, and by the Tabbard every ape had told four tails more, including the cook, including me.

Here ends the Book of the Tails of Canopybury. May the Sender of Stories forgive any ape I have slandered herein — and may the rest of you do likewise.
