# Gorilla Road

*A parody of "Glory Road"*

I met her on a Greek island, between one war and whatever the next one was going to be. My name is Oscar Gordon — "Scar" Gordon, since the service, on account of the stripe a barbed liana left across my brow — a gorilla of twenty-eight with no property but a knapsack, a chess set, and a talent for falling out of trees for my country.

She was playing three grandmasters at chess simultaneously, and she was the most beautiful creature I had ever seen: a langur so graceful she made gravity look like a suggestion. Her name was Star, and I assumed at once she was out of my troop.

Then she looked at me — the way you look at a melon in the market — and said, "You swim, Mr. Gordon?"

We swam. She laughed at two of my jokes and I memorized both. Then, mid-sentence, she was gone, and I spent the rest of the summer playing chess for drachmas and telling anyone who would listen about her, which was no one. A hero's soul is a lonely soul. I didn't know I was a hero yet; I thought I was a moderately tall chess player.

By autumn I was in Paris, down to my last francs, reading the International Herald Tribune classifieds for work that didn't exist. And there, between LOST: PARROT, ANSWERS TO "PLATO" and GENTLEMAN SEEKS GENTLE ESTATE, was this:

**HERO WANTED. Must be unmarried, twenty-five to thirty-five, sound of limb, brave of heart, comfortable at altitude, and able to keep his paws off what is not his. Gorillas preferred. Chimpanzees of exceptional maturity considered. Noprimates need apply. Apply in person, 16 Rue du Singe, third knock.**

The address was an old door in a wall that shouldn't have had one. I knocked three times, and it opened on a small red capuchin with a gray muzzle, sharpening a machete.

"Ozzie," he said. "Late as usual."

"Have we met?"

"Names Rufo. Baggage and brownnosing. You hungry?" He tossed me a banana. "Eat up. Long climb."

"You're the hero?" I said, because he was four feet of wrinkles.

"No," said a voice like warm honey down a silk rope. "I am."

Star. She wore traveling leathers and an expression that suggested the universe had already apologized to her twice. Rufo explained the job while she watched me eat.

"The Empress of the Twenty Arboreals," he said, "has lost the Egg of the Nanaphoenix. Golden egg, inestimable power, currently sitting in a nest at the top of the Tower of the Eater of Souls, in the last universe of the Twenty, at the end of the Gorilla Road. We're going to get it."

"Why me?"

Star said: "Because the Road cannot be walked by anyone who is owned — by debts, by love, by fear, or by a troop. A hero has to be free all the way down. You are the freest soul we have tested in two hundred years."

"That's a compliment?"

"It's a job description," said Rufo. "Also, the pay is terrible and the hours are geological. But the benefits—" he grinned, displaying every tooth he'd ever grown, "—are out of this world."

The door in Paris opened, and we stepped through it into a jungle where the light came from the wrong direction. The Gorilla Road is not a road, exactly. It's a vine: a strand of possibility swinging through twenty universes, and you cross the same way you cross any jungle — hand over hand, trusting the branch, never looking down at the dark between worlds.

I'll skip the small adventures — the toll-keeper spiders, the river of regrets, the customs macaques wanting our fruit declaration in triplicate. What matters are the Trials, because the Road is mostly a test of whether you deserve to reach its end.

**The Trial of Gluttony.** In the sixth universe we came to a valley of banana trees beyond counting, every bunch perfectly ripe, and a host of gracious hosts crying, *Eat, eat, travelers, eat!* "One banana," Star murmured. "Eaten slowly, with thanks. The valley keeps everyone who gorges." I chose the smallest banana on the smallest tree and made it last an hour.

**The Trial of Greed.** A hall of treasure: gold, gems, debit cards of forgotten empires. Walk through, touch nothing. Star watched me instead of the gold, which was harder to ignore than the gold.

**The Trial of Sloth.** The Great Bough, where the most comfortable moss in twenty universes grows and the softest wind says *rest, just rest, just a little nap*. I did two thousand push-ups and recited the alphabet backward in Latin. The wind gave up on me.

**The Trial of Pride.** We met a blind old loris in the path, and the loris said: "To pass, the great hero must bow to me." Rufo bowed instantly, like a folding rule. Star bowed like an empress. I stood there a long moment, and then I bowed so low my knuckles touched the path — because the trial isn't the bowing. It's noticing that the bowing is the trial, while your pride is busy being proud of noticing.

**The Trial of Wrath.** A howler in the ninth universe insulted my mother, my grooming, my troop, and my personal stench, in that order, with footnotes. He wanted me to swing at him. I complimented his footnotes instead. He wept with rage. It was the happiest I have ever made anyone.

On we swung, universe by universe, and somewhere in there I stopped thinking of Star as a job and started thinking of her as the point. She was old — thousands of years, though you'd never guess it from her shoulders — and she had outlived everything except her purpose. At night Rufo told stories of her past heroes. There had been others. I pretended not to mind. Heroes are good at pretending.

At the top of the last universe stood the Tower of the Eater of Souls, and in its highest nest lay the Egg, gold as noon, and between us and it stood the Eater itself.

It did not fight me with claws. It fought me with *wanting*. It wore my mother's kitchen and offered me childhood breakfasts. It wore the war and offered me the medal I never got. It wore a quiet island and a chess set and no more falling, ever. And at the last it wore Star, and offered me Star, forever, no questing, no dying, no choosing.

"You are not her," I said. "She would never be had so cheap."

And that — not the swordwork, of which there was mercifully little — is how you defeat an Eater of Souls. It can't digest a man who knows the difference between a prize and a cage. It folded up like an empty fruit skin and blew away, and I climbed the last span and lifted the Egg of the Nanaphoenix out of its nest, and it was warm, the way a sun is warm, the way a held hand is warm.

We came back to Star's capital in triumph, and I married her — I did; I'm getting to the good part, hold onto your vine — and became Emperor-Consort of the Twenty Arboreals. There were parades, medals, and statues, though I never found the committee that approved my nose.

And then came the word I came to loathe above all words in all twenty universes.

"Protocol," said the chamberlain, and took away my machete. "Protocol," said the vizier, and moved my chair one seat farther from my wife at every state dinner, because the consort ranks below the third under-minister of vines. "Protocol," said Star, one night, when I asked her to run away to a beach where nobody knew us, and she said it softly, with something in her eyes that was almost the Eater of Souls.

The Egg sat in the imperial vault, powering the peace, and I sat beside it, powering nothing. Rufo found me there one evening. "You know the secret, don't you," I said. "You've been her baggage-carrier for three thousand years. Tell me the secret."

Rufo peeled a banana he'd smuggled past the protocol officers, split it with me, and said: "The Road goes on, Ozzie. That's the secret. Heroes aren't a person. They're a job. The job ends. The Road doesn't."

So I left. I abdicated the consortship — there's a form for it, forty pages, all of them humiliating — and Star let me go, because she loved me, in her thousands-of-years, empire-first, protocol-broken way. The last I saw of her she stood on the highest balcony of the palace, not waving, which for an empress is a torrent of tears.

I am Professor O. Gordon now, at a middling land-grant university in the American Midwest, Department of Applied Bananaconomics. My students think I invented my war. I live in a small house with a large door, and every so often, between terms, I take the paper to Paris and read the classifieds all the way through, every line, twice.

There was a parrot lost last spring. It answered to "Plato."

I keep in shape. I eat one banana slowly. And I keep my knuckles calloused and my soul free all the way down, because a door is only a door until it opens, and somewhere a vine is still swinging through twenty universes.

The toll for the Gorilla Road is yourself, paid in full, in advance.

I keep the fare ready.
