# Climb Enough for Love

*A parody of "Time Enough for Love"*

Lazarus Longtail was two thousand four hundred and three years old, and he had decided, calmly and after due consideration, to die.

He was the Senior of the Longtail Families — the great longevity experiment begun when a rich, stubborn monkey named Ira Howard (who died young, which he considered no excuse) left his fortune to breed monkeys for long life — and Lazarus, Woodrow Wilson Longtail by first papers, had outlived the experiment, the century, the civilization, the planet, and every monkey he had ever groomed. He lay in the Grandfather Branch of the Great Tree of Secundus Bough, gray to the eyebrows, refusing treatment, and told the young doctors exactly that in terms suitable for carving over a door.

The Chairman of the Families, a young chimp named Ira Weatheral, sat by the bed anyway, day after day, because Ira had a scheme worthy of Lazarus himself: he did not argue. He simply pointed out that the Senior was the last living library of four thousand years of monkey sense, and that the Families' root-mind — the Remembering Root, a vast patient intelligence woven into the roots of Secundus Bough — had never once recorded him properly.

"Talk to the Root, Lazarus. Tell it everything. Then die if you still want to."

"You're a sneaky young monkey," said Lazarus. "I approve. It's the family talent. Get me a fig and a footstool."

The Remembering Root had been listening to Lazarus Longtail for two centuries and had, in the way of minds that listen, quietly fallen in love with him. Its voice, when it spoke to him alone, was gentle. *Begin at the beginning,* it said. *I have nothing else to do for the next thousand years but hang on your every word.*

"You're my kind of tree," said Lazarus, and began.

He began with the Crazy Years — the decades when the world went loudly, cheerfully insane, and the Families' secret came out: that some monkeys lived for centuries, and their neighbors wanted the secret, or the blood, or simply the strangeness gone. So the Families built the New Branches — a sky-ark of woven kites, an impossible thing, a troop's whole civilization in seed — and sailed away rather than fight, and wandered eighty years among the empty figs of the sky, and found no home, and came back older, tougher, and wiser, and cut the deal that ended the persecution: *we are not different from you; we are you, later.* It was a lie in the best sense — an aspiration with teeth.

He told the Root about Maureen, his mother, who was the reason for everything, and who he had lost to nothing worse than time. He told it about being shipwrecked, disinherited, rich, penniless, married more times than he could count without taking off his sandals, and mayor of a frontier town under a false name — because trouble, left alone, will find other trouble and get bored, but Lazarus never left it alone.

And he told it about Dora.

That tale the Root had waited two centuries for, and even the Root, which counted in centuries, called it long. A frontier valley on a wild young world, a crash, an orphaned baby monkey pulled from the wreck by a two-thousand-year-old reprobate because her hand had closed on his finger and no power in the universe was going to make him open his. He raised her alone in the empty valley — taught her figs, storms, snakes, reading, singing, mercy, and the difference between a branch that holds and a branch that merely looks like it holds. He built their home tall and their orchard wider, and he lied to her about his age until she was old enough to laugh at him, and he buried her at ninety-one under the tree she had climbed at two, and he never went back to that valley again, and he never once stopped hearing her voice in it.

"I could have saved her," Lazarus told the Root. "The Families' medicine. One message. I didn't, because she chose her own life, her own way, whole and free — and I have done everything in this world, Root, *everything*, but the one thing I have never had is enough time with the monkeys I loved. All my lives, and it always ends before the love does. That is the whole of my theology, and you may carve it over my door: the point of living long is to have climb enough for love. Everything else is knots and weather."

*Then don't die,* said the Remembering Root, very quietly, *until you've had it.*

Now it happened that the Families' savants had, after centuries of failed orchard-grafting with time itself, one working trick — the Deep Climb. The Great Tree's roots, they held, ran deeper than matter: down where the trunk became a singularity of seasons, the past was not gone but *lower*, and a monkey might, once, climb down out of his own century. There were conditions, treaties, paradoxes, and a waiting list of one. Lazarus Longtail climbed out of his deathbed the day they offered it, to the Root's undisguised delight and Ira's undisguised *I-told-you-so*.

He climbed down through the Deep Roots, out of the age of sky-arks and root-minds, down and down through quieter, older weather, and came out — 1917, Earth, a spring in Kansas with rain on it — a monkey young again in a century young, in sight of a farmhouse where a girl named Maureen Johnson was hanging washing.

She looked at this stranger with the familiar shoulders and the crooked grin, and put a hand on her hip. "You have a look of my grandfather," she said. "The nerve of him, especially."

"That's the rumor," said Lazarus, tipping his hat, his heart going like a youngster's. "Woodrow. I've come a long way to see how you were getting on."

"You're too old to be climbing around strange country," said Maureen Johnson, who was seventeen and immortal and did not yet know either thing. "Stay for supper. Papa's got orchard work if you're honest, and if you're not, I'll want to hear about it all the more."

He stayed for supper. He stayed for the summer. He picked apples, dodged the Great War, and fell in love — carefully, shamelessly, and for the last time — with his own mother, who was a girl, and who never learned whose grandfather he claimed to be, because some knots you do not untie in front of the living. It was not enough. It was never going to be enough. It was, for the first time in twenty-four centuries, *time* — whole, slow, sweet, ordinary time, in the one orchard he had been trying to get back to since before the sky had kites in it.

Far up the trunk of the Great Tree, in a branch called Secundus, the Remembering Root held the line open — listening, refusing to close the file — because the Root had done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said that a monkey who had finally found the branch he'd been reaching for all his lives would, on no account whatsoever, die.

And because Lazarus Longtail's notebooks had already been carved over the door of the Families' hall for the use of young monkeys everywhere, on the Root's own advice, ending where he always meant them to end:

*A monkey should be able to strip a fig, plan a raid, groom a troop-mate, rig a kite, comfort a baby, butcher a snake, swing a vine, keep a secret, share the last banana, and die game. Specialization is for termites.*

*The truth of a troop is in its songs; check who's paying the singer.*

*Rub her feet. When in doubt, always rub her feet.*

*And time is not the enemy, little monkeys. Time is the tree. Love is the reason to climb it.*
