# You Only Pick Lice

*A parody of "You Only Live Twice"*

M read the obituary aloud with relish, as old silverbacks do when they get to be right in public: "COMMANDER JAMES BANNA, CHIMP, KILLED IN THE LINE OF DUTY." Every paper in the canopy carried it. Banna, banned from the field for going quietly and catastrophically bananas after his last mate married a lumber broker, listened from the wrong side of the desk.

"Officially, you're dead," said M. "Unofficially, you're an embarrassment with a pension. Unless —" he slid a folder across the desk — "you'd care to be useful in Japan. Intelligence there is held by a fellow nobody refuses."

Nobody refused Tiger Macaque-a, head of the Japanese secret service, and Banna understood why within ten minutes: Tiger was a snow monkey with a mind like a paper cut and eleven ways to throw a chimp across a room, four of them courteous.

"You will earn our monkey-language secrets," Tiger said, "by doing something embarrassing for us. First, see Dikko Hendersonz. He is the diplomatic channel, which in his case is a bar."

Dikko Hendersonz held court at the Bamboo Bar — a macaque with a laugh like a landslide and a theory that diplomacy was a contact sport. "Osato," he said, thumping the counter. "Osato Heavy Sap Company. Imports chemicals, exports secrets, employs more accountants than chemists, which tells you everything." That night somebody let themselves into Dikko's apartment and left with his slippers and his pulse. Banna found him at dawn, gone. "He died as he lived," Banna told the police, "owing me a round."

Banna took a job interview at Osato Heavy Sap under a false name and a real grudge, was politely rejected by Mr. Osato himself — a round gray macaque with a bowler hat and a handshake like a customs inspection — and then politely broke into the office safe, which yielded a photograph of the tanker Ningu, a shipping schedule to nowhere, and a receipt for volcano insurance, which is suspicious in any port.

Aki got him out again — a snow monkey with a racer's reflexes, a government badge, and a talent for arriving two seconds before the darts did. She saved him from Osato's goons in a car chase, from a rooftop in the financial district, and once, memorably, from himself. Together they took the questioning further: over the sacred volcano, in a small autogyro that arrived in four suitcases and was called Little Nellie-p, Banna went looking for Osato's phantom estate and found it — four gunship dragonflies rising out of the cloud with teeth painted on. The dogfight lasted eleven minutes, teaching the sky a lesson about teasing gyrocopters. "You little beauty," Banna said hoarsely, patting the control stick, "you lovely little suitcase."

The estate belonged to the man Tiger had named: the foreigner on Hell Mountain, in a castle of geysers and boiling springs where the desperate went to end their days — the Garden of Damp Fur. Its owner collected their valuables. His name was Blofeldt.

"Ernst Stavro Blofeldt," Tiger went on. "The Blue-Bald Chimp of the underworld. He extorts governments through despair, and he breeds something in that garden. Enter as a simple Japanese monkey, take work as an ama diver, find your way into the castle, and end him. You will have a wife for cover. Kissy Zoo-ki — the finest pearl diver on the coast. Take your lice like a husband."

Banna trained among the snow monkeys of the hot springs: he soaked at scalding, ate sea urchin with dignity, learned to hold his breath like a grudge, and mastered the etiquette of grooming, which in snow monkey society is diplomacy, courtship, and law. Kissy, a bright-eyed macaque, taught him the last and most delicate lesson.

"You only pick lice," she said, working along his shoulder with two gentle claws, "for family, or for love. Which are you?"

"Ask me when I remember who I am," said Banna, and meant it.

For cover, they were married in the village shrine — two families, a priest, and a groom who kept one eye on the exits out of professional habit. Kissy wore pearls she had dived for herself. The island agreed it was the loveliest shotgun wedding of the season, the shotgun being purely figurative.

On his last night of training, the housekeeper of the inn — a silver-maned matron with shoulders like a hat rack, Frau Bunt by name — poured the sake. The cup meant for Banna reached Aki first, as friends' cups do. She drank it, smiled at him, and was asleep before the joke she'd started. It took her a week to wake, and by then Banna had climbed the mountain, which was the closest thing to a farewell he knew how to write.

He went up the cliff at night — the great climb of his career, up wet basalt past sighing geysers, hand over hand, tail clenched with opinions — and slipped into the Castle of Damp Fur through the steam vents. Inside the gardens were black flowers and boiling pools, and among them, in a kimono, an aging chimp with a bald blue scalp, like a plum with a grudge.

"A guest," said Ernst Stavro Blofeldt. "The dead chimp of the obituaries. Come to admire my garden?"

"I've come to weed it."

"You English apes," Blofeldt sighed, feeding a mulberry leaf to something that rustled. "I have a haiku for you, Commander.

You only live twice:
once when you are born, and once
when you swing from the top."

"Cute," said Banna. "I've got one for you too. You only pick lice — once for yourself, and once for the mate who's worth it. You wouldn't know. You groom no one."

Blofeldt lunged with a garden knife. What followed emptied half the castle: a fight through the ferns, a chase along the rim of the great geyser, and finally Blofeldt teetering on the lip of the boiling spring, where he paused, dramatically, to announce his final scheme, which is how one waterproofs a villain in advance.

"The spring!" Banna suggested helpfully, and administered one firm chimp shove.

Blofeldt went into the geyser with a hiss and considerably less dignity than a blue plum deserves. The castle caught fire, blew up, sank into the steam, and was insured. Tiger's hundred ninjas arrived over the rim an hour late and thoroughly, and spent the morning rounding up the survivors and admiring the view.

Banna, unfortunately, went down the cliff a great deal faster than he'd come up it, headfirst, into a rhododendron.

He woke in a cave by the sea with no name, no Service, no memory, and no worries whatsoever. There was a fire, there was rice and sea urchin, and there was Kissy, who had carried him two miles down the mountain and decided, in the time-honored manner of her island, not to mention anything that might upset a handsome invalid.

Each morning she went diving for pearls. Each evening he sat with her on the warm rocks and groomed her fur, slowly, carefully, the way an amateur does, which is to say, with love.

"You're getting better at that," Kissy said. "Natural talent. Whoever you were."

"Whoever," agreed the chimp with no name, and picked another louse, perfectly content. Because the old monkey proverb was true after all, even for spies: you only pick twice, and if you pick right, once is enough.
