# The Once and Future Kong

*A parody of "The Once and Future King"*

In the Sauvage Scrub, in the hold of Sir Ector the silverback, there were two boys. One was Kay, destined for a knighthood and opinions about it. The other was the Wart — a skinny young gorilla with a crooked tail and a heart the size of the weather.

Into their jungle one morning walked a mandrill with a nose like a fire warning, carrying a suitcase of live owls.

"Whit," said the mandrill, "is two and two?"

"Four," said the Wart.

"Wrong. It's whatever the King says it is. I am Merlyndrill. I was born old, I live backward, and I shall grow young. I've come to tutor you. Lesson one is in an hour, underwater."

The Wart became a fish.

In the moat, a monstrous old pike ruled the weeds, and he looked at the small perch-shaped Wart with one flat yellow eye and said, in a voice like mud: "Here there is only one law, minnow. Might is right. Everything smaller than me is food, and everything bigger is weather. That is the whole of politics."

The Wart was nearly eaten twice, and came out shaking with a cold new idea of what a ruler must never be.

"Hmf," said Merlyndrill. "Let's go be unhappy somewhere educational."

He became an ant.

The Ordered Hill marched with one scripture carved everywhere: EVERYTHING NOT FORBIDDEN IS COMPULSORY. YOU ARE HAPPY. REPORT ALL UNHAPPINESS. And when the war-band swarmed out to take a neighbor's grain, the Wart learned that the scariest noise in the world is a million voices agreeing.

"They're not wicked," said Merlyndrill. "That's the trouble. They're only small, and frightened, and organized. Remember the moat, and remember the Hill, and you're two lessons ahead of most kings."

He became, last, a wild goose. Up in the cold blue the Wart found the strangest country: one with no fences. "We keep no borders in the sky," said the grey goose. "Only the wind, and she is free. Down there they draw lines in the mud and die for the mud. We migrate."

And for good measure, Merlyndrill turned him once into a badger, who lived alone at the roots of an oak and had spent his life writing a treatise called *Of Monkeys.*

"You are the only pupil who ever read it," said the badger, pleased. "The argument is this: might settles everything, except whether it should. That question cannot be clawed. It can only be learned. That is why the learning creatures must inherit, or there will be nothing worth inheriting." He tapped the treatise with a claw. "Also, in chapter two, I prove that every Kong in history has needed a tutor, and been ruined by firing him too late."

The Wart grew. Kay practiced tilting, and grumbled that the Wart got all the magic, which was true and unfair. There were adventures besides: King Pellinora crashed through the underbrush hunting the Questing Beast, a creature with the head of a serpent, the body of a leopard, the hindquarters of a tapir, and the bark of forty dogs, all of it exhausting. The Beast, in point of fact, mainly needed a vet and a hobby. The Wart and Kay left Pellinora bandaging its paw and telling it, sternly, that nobody was chasing it and never had been.

Then came the great tournament at Lemurdin, where the jungle's warriors gathered in the churchyard around a strange thing: a bough of bronze and gold driven into a boulder, inscribed — WHOSO PULLETH THIS BOUGH FROM THE STONE IS RIGHTWISE KONG BORN OF ALL TROOPS. Famous silverbacks had pulled till they turned purple; the bough out-pulled them all.

On the morning of the joust, Kay forgot his sword. Naturally.

"Naturally," said Merlyndrill, rolling his eyes to heaven, where he had an appointment backward.

The Wart ran to the inn — locked. The barracks — locked. So he slipped into the churchyard, saw a bough sticking out of a boulder, and pulled it out the way you'd pluck a ripe banana: because it was there, and he was in a hurry.

The square went quiet. Then it went extremely loud.

Kay went white. "Wart. What are you holding?"

"A sword? For you? I mean — a bough?"

"Kneel," said Merlyndrill, whom nobody had seen arrive, weeping. "Your name isn't Wart, my boy. You are — oh, dear, I know how this comes out and I'm still going to cry."

They crowned him, because the bough had said so, and the jungle has always trusted boulders more than barons. Kay knelt and said, "I was ever so rude to you, ever since always," and the new Kong forgave him, having been rude back, about equally.

But that night, in the tower, the young Kong was afraid. "I don't know how to rule," he said. "I'm only a peel of a gorilla."

"The best thing for being sad," said Merlyndrill, "is to learn something. Learn why the world wags and what wags it. That is the only thing the mind can never exhaust, never mistrust, and never dream of regretting. Look what a lot of things there are to learn — the tails alone would take you a year."

"Will you stay and teach me?"

"I'll stay until I leave," said Merlyndrill, who knew, backward, about a baboon called Modead, and a table that would be round, and a night in the far west that only a good heart could face. "That's a tail you've heard before, isn't it? Well. It isn't finished. That's the point of it."

And the lessons kept coming, because a Kong's education is never done; it only changes teachers. In time the young Kong made a table round, so that no baron could sit at the head of it, having learned his geometry from geese. He gathered knights who swore to use might *for* — a preposition that had never before appeared in the job description. The best of them was Sir Prancelot, an ugly-honest chimp who did one hundred push-ups nightly and believed, correctly, that knighthood was a kind of homework; and the Queen, Gwenohere, sat beside the Kong at the round board, and she and Prancelot loved each other, and the Kong too, all three, gently, and none of the three was brave enough to say so until everything was on fire — which is the oldest knot in the world, and it was not cut until it had strangled half the Round Table.

For the dark baboon Modead, whom the old Kong had tried to prune out of the future once, discovered that the future grows back crooked. He turned the jungle's quarrels to his own use, and the best knights came home from the quest for the greatest of all fruit empty-handed and hollow-eyed, and there was a last battle in the far west, on a night of rain.

It was a tie, which in battles is the same as a loss. And afterward — so the story goes — the Kong told his last knight, Sir Bedevere, to take the bronze bough and throw it into the water, and Bedevere hid it under a bush twice, because it seemed a waste, and the Kong knew both times, because Kings always do. The third time the bough flew, a paw came out of the lake and caught it, and the water closed over it, satisfied.

"Put up the bough, Bedevere," said the Kong. "I have eaten my banyan. Now I must go into the green dark, and be a lesson myself."

And they say that when the darkest night comes to the troops, the Kong of that old lesson does not die, but sleeps in the green dark of the Scrub; and wherever a troop is broken, wherever the strong remember the moat but forget the goose, there he swings again, once and future, learning always, before he is needed.
