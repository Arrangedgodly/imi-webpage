# The Fox and the Great Apes

*A parody of "The Fox and the Grapes"*

In the jungle where the banyan meets the bamboo, there lived a fox named Reynard, who was clever the way foxes are clever — clever enough to get into trouble, never clever enough to get out politely. He had once sold a river "protection" against floods. He had sold an armadillo a shell upgrade. He was, by his own account, the finest mind in the jungle, and the fact that no one else's account agreed with this was, he felt, a failing of the jungle.

One golden afternoon, Reynard caught a scent drifting down from the tallest kapok: sun-sweet, honey-heavy. He gazed up — the Great Apes' Golden Bunch, a perfect spray of 'nanas hanging at the very top of the tree, glowing like a chandelier at a monkey wedding.

"They say those are the sweetest bananas in the world," Reynard whispered. "Riper than romance. Sweeter than stolen honey. And they shall be mine."

Now, you might think a fox would simply climb the tree. Reynard thought so too: three bounds up the trunk, a scrabble, a slither, a long moment of dangling by two claws, and a majestic fall into a bromeliad, out of which he rose wearing a flowerpot on his head.

From the high branches, a family of great apes watched with the calm, unreadable faces of great apes.

"Would you like a ladder?" called Madame Gorilla, in a voice so even it was almost sarcastic.

"I do not need a ladder," said Reynard, removing the bromeliad from his ear. "I was merely inspecting your trunk. Fine work. Top of the line. Termite-resistant, I'd say. I'd know; I inspected it personally."

His second attempt was flattery: an hour at the kapok's roots, complimenting Madame Gorilla's grooming. "Such luster! Not a single flea — never have I seen a more magnificent coat, and I say that as a fox of discriminating taste. Also your shoulders. Also your brow. I said all of it to you last spring, and it has only become truer."

"Mr. Fox," said Madame Gorilla, "you said all that to me last spring. Word for word. We are great apes, you know. We invented the unreadable face — and also the memory."

From the uncle in the branches came a slow, chewy voice. "Feh," said the uncle, a chimpanzee of great age and tiny patience. "In my day, foxes stole honestly, in the night, with dignity. This one does it in broad daylight with compliments."

"Everything is worse now," agreed a cousin, who was nineteen.

Third attempt: bargaining. Reynard returned dragging a vine heavy with plump purple grapes. "A trade! A handsome bunch of grapes for one banana from your Golden Bunch! Think of it — grapes, the fruit of kings, concessionally priced!"

The eldest of the apes, an orang patriarch with a face like a wise walnut, lowered himself on one arm and sniffed the grapes at length, the way a banker sniffs a loan.

"Mr. Fox," he said, "let us review. You are offering us grapes, of uncertain provenance—"

"Vintage."

"—in exchange for the sweetest bananas in the jungle, which took four generations to grow, hang ninety feet above the ground, and glow. Those grapes look sour to me."

"SOUR?" gasped Reynard, who had stolen them fair and square. "They are vintage grapes! Grapes of distinction! Grapes with a FINISH! This bunch has NOTES!"

"Mm," said the orang, and swung back up, leaving Reynard to feel, for the first time in his life, what it was like to be on the wrong end of the word "sour." He took the grapes home. He did not eat them. He took them personally.

His fourth attempt was a masterpiece. He glued moss to his shoulders with tree sap, balanced a coconut shell on his snout as a mask, and marched up to the kapok on his hind legs, announcing: "Good morning, fellow enormous apes. I too am a great ape, of the red-furred lowland variety. I have come to hang about at the top of your tree, as is our custom."

There was a long silence. Then the youngest of the apes, a gibbon child no bigger than a breadfruit, slid down to Reynard's eye level and pointed one gentle finger at the grass behind him.

"Your tail is showing, Mr. Fox."

Reynard looked back. His tail was, indeed, showing — magnificent, red, and utterly un-ape.

"Also," added the gibbon child helpfully, "you're standing on your own grapes."

The great apes did not laugh; they simply exchanged the slow, patient blinks of a family that had seen every scheme in the jungle and rated them all "could try harder."

But Reynard was not done. His fifth attempt was engineering. In the dead of night he returned with a contraption of vines, bent saplings, and one stolen tortoise as an anchor. He had devised, he explained to the tortoise, a catapult. The tortoise, who had not agreed to any of this, simply waited, which is the whole of a tortoise's strategy and it has never once failed.

Reynard climbed into the sling, cut the release, and was flung magnificently upward — past one branch, past two, a soaring golden arc through the moonlight — directly into the middle of the Great Apes' family nest, where he landed between the uncle and the eldest, wearing the sling as a bonnet.

The family looked at him. The moon looked at him. Somewhere below, the tortoise left.

"The nursery," said Madame Gorilla, not even raising her voice, "is on the ground floor, Mr. Fox. You have achieved nothing, but you have achieved it at a great height."

He was lowered down by one enormous, patient paw.

And so, at sunrise, Reynard sat beneath the kapok, stared up at the Golden Bunch glimmering far above, and delivered the speech for which he is famous:

"Hmph. Bunch of sour 'nanas, anyway. Green. Starchy. Probably mealy. Too yellow, if you ask me — and full of weevils, no doubt. 'Nanas are for amateurs. A fox of my refinement prefers GRAPES." And he ate one of his own grapes — extremely sour — making a face like a cat in a rainstorm.

The gibbon child swung down one last time, holding out a single ripe banana. "You could have just asked," she said. "We always share with the polite."

Reynard looked at the banana, then at the child. He ate it in two bites.

"Delicious," he admitted grudgingly. "But of course — I always despise whatever I cannot reach. It's tradition."

The next season he came back, and stood at the bottom of the kapok, and said, with enormous difficulty and one eye closed, "I merely stopped by to inspect your — no. No, I came to say: may I have a banana, please."

"You may," said Madame Gorilla, and gave him two.

"Why did he not just say he was hungry at the start?" the gibbon child asked her mother, as they swung away, leaving the fox eating politely in the grass for the first time in his life.

"Ah, little one," said her mother. "That is the oldest fox trick of all: the tail you cannot put down is called pride, and it keeps a fellow hungry at the foot of every good thing."

*Moral: it is easy to call the bananas sour when your arms are too short.*
