# The Lousetrap

*A parody of "The Mousetrap"*

Monkswell Manor Guest House, said the sign — newly painted, newly opened, newly mortgaged. Mollie and Giles Ralston, a young married pair of spider monkeys with more dreams than groomed fur, welcomed their first guests in the teeth of the worst leaf-fall in thirty years: a green blizzard sealing the passes and hissing against the windows like a polite audience shuffling.

The guests arrived one by one.

Christopher Wrenn first: a twitchy young colobus with a sweet tooth and a passion for building little twig-towers. Then Mrs. Boyle, an elderly howler of majestic disapproval, who disliked the wallpaper, the sheets, the view, the guests, and — after tasting it — most of the bananas. Then Major Metcalf, a retired mandrill who kept odd hours and a locked trunk. Then Miss Casewell, a tall, cool gorilla in a trench coat who told nobody anything. And finally Mr. Paravicini, a plump foreign macaque with an unplaceable accent, whose pony cart, he claimed, skidded in the leaf-fall and deposited him, fortuitously, at the only guest house in the valley.

"Fortuitously," repeated Giles, who did not believe him.

That evening the radio scratched through the storm: a murder in London — Mrs. Maureen Lyon, strangled. The police sought a suspect connected to the old Corrigibbon case — the three children of Longbranch Farm, and the littlest dead of neglect.

Then the telephone vines went dead — cut, someone found, out at the pole. And the leaf-fall went on hissing, and the guests went on disapproving of one another.

Into this, absurdly, swung Detective Sergeant Trotter: a young monkey arriving on a liana at speed, badge and notebook, the canopy roads being the only way through.

"There's been a second connection to the Lyon murder," he announced. "At the scene the killer left a rhyme — 'Three blind lice, three blind lice, see how they groom…' — and the address of this house. Someone here is connected to the Corrigibbon children. One of you."

"Rubbish," said Mrs. Boyle.

"Which of you was at Longbranch Farm? The father was killed by one of the children — a child, mind. The mother was Mrs. Lyon, jailed for cruelty, strangled within the month of her release. Three blind lice; three children. One died at the farm. One emigrated. And one is here."

He worked the room like a grooming clause. "Understand what you are hiding from. The murderer of Mrs. Lyon is almost certainly a young male, mentally damaged — a child in a grown monkey's fur. Such damage is never random. It repeats. And a troop that lets a child go unloved should not act surprised, later, at what the child lets loose."

"Monstrous speculation," said Mrs. Boyle, and poured herself more fig tea with a hand that was not perfectly steady.

The household unraveled by association. Wrenn, questioned, talked too much and too fast about twig-towers, and was heard to say of the dead Corrigibbon boy, "I expect he was like me — odd, and never let forget it," with such tenderness that Trotter's ears stood up. Paravicini played the piano at three in the morning, badly, and could not or would not say what country his accent came from; he claimed his documents were lost "in the leaf-fall," which had apparently also eaten his second name. Major Metcalf's trunk was locked, his alibi for the war years a shimmer of vagueness, and he twice lied about napping.

And in the kitchen Mollie and Giles accused each other in whispers — each had noticed the other lying slightly about small things. Giles had gone up to London on the day of the murder "for a juicer"; Mollie had burned, that same week, a small something in the stove, and would not say what. This is the trouble with newlyweds: they haven't yet learned to hide things properly.

Trotter searched the guest rooms and found, hidden under Wrenn's mattress, a press cutting: the Corrigibbon trial, the children's faces smudged, and one circled in pencil. Wrenn laughed too loudly. "I collect crimes! It's a hobby! I'm fascinated by — by abnormal psychology—"

"You identify with the Corrigibbon boy, don't you," said Trotter softly, and Wrenn stopped laughing entirely.

Miss Casewell, asked about her family, looked out the window for a while. "I had a brother," she said at last, "a long way from here. That's all anyone gets." The Major, asked about his, said "All dead," with the promptness of a monkey reciting rather than remembering.

"Where were you," Trotter asked Mollie, softly, "in the year the Corrigibbon boy was dying, Mrs. Ralston? A letter was written to a young teacher. The letter was never answered."

"I never received any letter," said Mollie, white to the ears — and whether it was true, only Mollie knew. Giles, watching from the stairs, looked as if he'd been struck with a fig branch: not suspicion of his wife — suspicion that she suspected him.

"Any of you," said Trotter, standing back, "could be the child from the farm. Nerves, tempers, secrets — you're a convention of the plausible. Guard each other tonight. Nobody goes anywhere alone."

The next evening Mrs. Boyle was found in the library, strangled — and everyone had been somewhere else, which is the least helpful thing a houseful of monkeys can arrange. She had, of course, announced she would be alone there; she had, of course, told the killer so; the last thing Mrs. Boyle ever did was disapprove of someone to his face. The house drew in on itself. Mollie and Giles held each other and lied beautifully about being fine.

The twist came at half past nine.

Trotter asked Mollie to meet him alone in the parlor. "I know now who the killer is. It's the children of Longbranch Farm I keep coming back to. Georgie Corrigibbon — the little one they starved. Someone ought to have answered his letter, don't you think, Mrs. Ralston?"

His voice had gone strange — high and young and wrong. The badge was pinned on upside down.

"I'm not a policeman," said Trotter, smiling terribly. "There is no Sergeant Trotter. The real sergeant is lying under the leaf-fall on the canopy road, poor chap. I am the eldest Corrigibbon. I came home."

He raised the revolver, and Mollie, backed against the mantel, heard the leaf-fall hiss like the farm wind, and a little voice saying nobody came, nobody came—

The lights came up on Miss Casewell, who had stepped from the shadow of the door.

"I'm Kathy Corrigibbon," she said quietly. "You're not the only one who came home, brother. And you're not going to do one more mouse-trap thing in this house."

"Nor lice-trap either," rumbled the Major, seizing him by the collar — and revealing, as he pinned the shrieking young monkey to the floor, that he was no retired mandrill at all, but a police inspector, planted in the guest house since the wire went dead.

"Three blind lice," said Paravicini mournfully, retuning the piano. "Two of you were the lice. I was only the scenery."

So ended the case of the three blind lice; and the audience, on their way out into the night, were asked by the management, as every audience since has been asked, to keep the secret of Monkswell Manor — on their honor as monkeys, and on pain of losing their bananas.
