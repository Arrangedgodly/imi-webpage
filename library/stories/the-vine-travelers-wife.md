# The Vine Traveler's Wife

*A parody of "The Time Traveler's Wife"*

Clare was six, playing alone in the fig grove behind her family's troop-tree, when she heard a rustle and a thump, and a grown chimpanzee appeared out of nowhere — fur askew, no satchel, no rain cloak, holding his head.

"Don't scream," he said. "I get nosebleeds."

"Where did you come from?"

"Nowhere you can get to yet," he said. "I'm Henry. I'm your husband. Not today. Today I'm just hungry."

She gave him a fig. It was, she decided, the least she could do.

Henry slipped down the vines of time the way other chimps slipped on wet bark: without warning, without dignity, whenever he was stressed — and he arrived wherever the vine dropped him, empty-pawed, ravenous, in some other year. He could not steer it. He could only endure it, and try to land in fig season.

He had been slipping since he was five. His mother, before the accident, used to say he had fig juice in his veins instead of blood. After the accident, he could still visit her — she simply wasn't alive in any year he could get to on foot. Once, aged nine, he landed in a summer where she was young and laughing, and she said he had her nose, and he agreed not to cry until he got home.

So Clare grew up with a husband who visited out of order. She kept a stash in the hollow by the old tree: figs, her brother's spare comb, a rain cloak folded small. When she was ten he came with a scar he wouldn't explain; when she was sixteen he came and taught her the List — never tell anyone their future, never eat unripe fruit from a stranger, never let go of my hand — and taught her the rest of it too, in the patient drills of a chimp who has been dropped into a hundred midnights: how to pick locks and pockets, how to steal a rain cloak and an umbrella, how to fall out of a tree and get up cross, how to find food in any orchard in any season, how to fight like a sister and run like a thief.

"You're grooming me for disaster," she said.

"I'm grooming you for me," he said. "Same thing."

Clare's family never quite saw him. Her father thought Henry was sullen; her brother Mark thought he was a sneak; and her oldest friend Gomango — who had loved Clare since they were seedlings and hated Henry on principle — kept refilling Henry's cup in the hope he would fall out of the tree. He did fall, once, at a New Year's dinner: mid-toast, between the soup and the figs, gone, leaving a spinning chair and an explanation owed. Clare said he'd gone to check the weaning pens. Her mother said that boy will be the death of some girl someday. Mark laughed loudest of anyone at the table, and went on hunting in that grove all his life.

When she was twenty-two, at last, she walked into the seed-catalog room of the Newfig Library where Henry worked and said, "I'm Clare. It's my turn now."

He looked up. He was thirty-one. He had known her his whole life, and not at all.

"There you are," he said.

Their wedding was in the grove. Henry made it through "I will" before the vine took him mid-vow; he popped back five minutes later, older, gray at the muzzle, having already attended the reception. The guests agreed it was the most romantic thing they had ever seen, and the worst-timed.

Dr. Kendfig, the geneticist, called it chrono-vine displacement and offered to study it. "You don't travel in time," she said, pinning up charts. "Time is the vine. You just keep losing your grip."

They wanted children, and this was the cruelest bend of the vine: three times the little ones slipped away before they were ripe, tumbling off to some other year before they could hold on. "The seed carries the vine," Dr. Kendfig said gently, and Henry stopped sleeping, because he knew exactly where his children would be landing, hungry, in the dark.

Then they had a daughter, Alba, who slipped too — but Alba steered. The first time she vanished from her crib and reappeared, aged six, sitting on the kitchen shelf eating jam, she shrugged and said, "I held on tighter."

That was when Henry went quiet, because Henry had already seen the winter grove.

He had slipped there once, an old man into an old winter, and found Clare, eighty-two, sitting in the snow in the rain cloak, weeping. He never told her what he saw. But he wrote it down, and Clare found the letter after.

Alba steered, too, in the other direction. She steered herself to a summer before she was born and sat on her father's shoulders through a whole thunderstorm. He never asked what she knew, and she never told him, and they ate figs in a year that belonged to neither of them.

The vine took things even so. Near the end, it dropped Henry barefoot into a frost hollow, and by the time the troop found him his toes had gone grey as old fruit, and the healers took what was gone. He lived in the nest chair by the window after that, patient, watching Alba learn the vines. He never ran again. Alba, who steered, went looking for the where he'd been taken from and came back quiet. "The vine doesn't leave footprints," was all she would say, and after that she made him practice falling into her arms from the window ledge until he laughed, which took days.

And then, one winter morning, when Clare was a child and Henry a stranger, hunters moved through her family's grove. A young one fired at a shape flashing between the branches, believing it a leopard. Henry arrived at that moment, from forty years away — gray at the muzzle, still young enough to have run, and gone before the frost.

Clare knew. She had known all her life, the way you know a fig is ripe. That was the bargain: she got to love him across all of time, and time got to take him in the middle of hers. So she waited, the way he had asked. She raised Alba. She kept the stash filled anyway — out of habit, out of hope.

And on an evening when she was eighty-two, in the snow, in the rain cloak, she heard the rustle and the thump.

He landed in the leaves, thirty-one years old, fur askew, holding his head.

"I knew you'd come," she said.

"I try never to be late," Henry said. He was bleeding and laughing and trying not to cry. "I hate being where you aren't."

They shared the last fig from the stash, which had never once, in all those years, been empty.

The vine took him back before dawn, as she knew it would. Clare sat on in the snow, unhurried, ancient, the luckiest chimp in the jungle.

"Right now," she said — to nobody, to him, to the whole tangled vine of it — "in this grove, with fig juice on my chin, I am happy. And I'll wait. You know I'll wait."
