# A Visit from the Groom Squad

*A parody of "A Visit from the Goon Squad"*

Sasha had been keeping other monkeys' things for eleven years, the way some monkeys keep grudges: in a hollow tree, lined with leaves, visited at night. That evening it was a macaw tourist's pouch of grooming tokens, left beside the basin in the ladies' grooming room of the Jungle Vista Hotel, and it was in Sasha's fist when the macaw came flapping back in a state.

"You left this," said Sasha, handing over the pouch, keeping one token back, and flying out through the window leaves on guilt and delight in equal parts. Halfway across the park she stopped on a branch and looked at the token, and at her own gray chest, and thought what she always thought: *Time's a baboon. It comes for everyone swinging.*

The keeping had started in college, the year of the lake. Her best friend was a rangy monkey named Rob who could groom his own tail in one pull and believed, the way the doomed believe in weather, that everything would be fine. One long night by the water, with the fermented bark going around, Rob went in for a swim to prove the cold was a rumor. Sasha was eleven feet away, kissing a monkey whose name she was already losing. When she turned around, the lake had a new shape in the middle of it, and that shape did not move again. She had been reaching for things ever since — tokens, combs, a scarf here, a purse there — the way you reach for the far bank. Her uncle Jules, a journalist of legendary appetites, put it best at the funeral: "Sasha collects evidence that time has happened to somebody."

Jules himself had once interviewed the famous film beauty Kitty Jaguar for a cover story. It went badly. It went so badly that Kitty's bodyguard bit his ear off and ate it, and Jules sold the ear's story instead of hers. Monkeys said he never recovered; Jules said the ear was the best thing he ever published, being entirely true.

Bennie Salabanana, founder of Silverback's Ear Records, sprinkled gold flakes into his fig juice because someone had once told him it stirred the blood. It did not. Nothing did, anymore. In 1979 he had played bass in the Flaming Mandrills, a band that imploded over a stolen banana and forty years of unsaid things, and the silence had kept better time than the drummer ever had. Bennie paused between everything he said now; the pauses were where he lived.

His assistant, Lulu, was the daughter of the publicist Dolly — La Doll to the trade — who had once thrown the party that ended her: a celebrity monkey, a tray of flambéed figs, a general with no sense of occasion, and a fire that made the newspapers for a month. Dolly rebuilt herself out of the only client left, which was the general, a dictator so dedicated to silence that he never once learned to say no to an invoice. "I made him lovable," Dolly told Lulu, years later, in the tone of a monkey confessing to a crime she had been acquitted of. Lulu took the lesson and ran Bennie's office like a well-thrown net.

His old bandmate Scotty Haumandrill, whom Bennie had not called in decades, had lately caught a fish in the mangrove canal with one banana for bait and no faith at all. Everyone at the label agreed this proved nothing except that the river was dirty. Scotty brought the fish in a bucket to the reception tree, where he told the intern:

"I've been thinking about time. It's a baboon. It comes for your fur, it comes for your band, and it does not knock."

"Mr. Salabanana isn't receiving," said the intern.

"Nobody's receiving," said Scotty. "That's my point."

Meanwhile, far out in the suburbs of the canopy, a twelve-year-old named Alia was making a presentation for her family, slide by slide, on the big leaf:

**GREAT GROOMING PAUSES, by Alia**

*Slide 1: WHY PAUSES MATTER. A pause in grooming lets the fur breathe. When the comb stops, you can hear the other monkey's heart.*

*Slide 2: DAD'S PAUSE. Dad pauses four seconds before grooming Linc's back. Linc counts. Last Tuesday it was six.*

*Slide 3: LINCOLN'S SLIDE. I don't like pauses. I like when the comb keeps going. This is my slide.*

*Slide 4: MOM PAUSING BEFORE SHE ANSWERS THE TROOP PHONE. Six seconds. Alia counts.*

*Slide 5: THE BEST PAUSE. After the last nit is gone and before anyone moves. It lasts one second. It is the whole family.*

Alia's mother had been a wild young groomer once, the kind who went through other monkeys' pockets, and if you had told her she would end up in the suburbs making slides about pauses, she would have stolen your watch to time how long it took to happen.

Years on, everything came up your branch. The Groom Squad arrived in vans marked HAND & FUR, staffed by young monkeys you had known when they were nothing but ears and a tail — Alia drove one; her brother Linc took the bookings through the app Ooo-Aaa, which sold the sunset sound of ten thousand distant troops as a ringtone and was, everyone agreed, the future of feeling things. Lulu, silver-streaked, ran the company that had bought the company that had bought Bennie's label. Nobody had bought time yet, though not one monkey in the jungle had stopped trying.

Sasha herself had ended up in the desert, teaching young groomers at a trade school, married to a steady monkey named Drew. Her father, Ted, before the end, had filled his trailer with little monkeys made of bottle caps and typewriter keys and left them where his daughters would find them, which was his way of saying everything. Sasha kept one on the sill. She had stopped taking things by then, mostly, though she had never once thrown anything away.

Bennie, seventy, sat in the mirror chair when the van came.

"Take it all off," he told Alia, meaning the gray. She worked the comb through forty years of label meetings, two divorces, one gold record, and the silence of not calling Scotty, and it all came away soft as underfur, and Bennie closed his eyes and was nineteen again, up in the wind over a crowd.

"You ever lose anybody?" he asked, eyes shut.

"Everybody loses everybody," said Alia, who had been raised on pauses and knew a seven-second one when she combed one. "The trick is what you keep."

Bennie thought of the lake he had never swum in, of the band, of the banana nobody ever admitted stealing, and kept his eyes shut.

That was the week a demo arrived, one monkey, one instrument strung from vine and tin: "Goodbye, My Fig," by Scotty Haumandrill. It had a pause in the middle so wide you could lose a whole troop in it, and when the pause came the leaves seemed to lean in.

Bennie listened twice, staring out at the lights coming on along the far bank of the river like a troop assembling in the dusk. Somewhere out there the baboon time was still making its rounds, combing the years off an old monkey somewhere, gently, the way you comb a sleeping child.

He called Alia in.

"You think he's got anything?" Bennie asked.

Alia looked at the little player, at the wide kind pause holding the door open in the middle of the song.

"He's pure," she said. "Pure primate."

Later that night Bennie walked home the long way, past the river, past the hotel where a young thief had once flown out the window leaves with a token in her fist. He stopped at a fruit cart, bought a banana, paid for it — which, at his age, was a kind of surrender — and then, forty years late, he called Scotty. It rang twice. Somewhere across the jungle an old mandrill looked at the bucket by his door, took a breath the size of the pause in his song, and picked up.
