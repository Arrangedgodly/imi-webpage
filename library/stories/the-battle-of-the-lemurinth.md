# The Battle of the Lemurinth

*A parody of "The Battle of the Labyrinth"*

"Orientation is at the amphitheater," said Annabough Chase. "You two are going in."

Percy Jackson, demigod son of Poseidrift, and Annabough, daughter of Apethena, stood at the edge of the climbing structure at Camp Half-Primate with Grover, a nervous lemur whose tail was doing the thing it did when Grover suspected — correctly — that something was about to eat his friends.

Beneath the camp, under the strawberry fields, spread the Lemurinth: a maze built by the legendary inventor Daelus, and renovated — Luke warned them via a traitor's whisper — into an invasion highway straight to the camp's heart. Kronos, the Titan of time and bad ideas, was sending his army through it.

"I got us a guide," Annabough said, and produced a square of ambrosia wrapped in paper — and glowing on the paper, a small lemur, blinking. "The Lemurinth can only be navigated by a lemur's nose. The maze reads minds. Left on Tuesday is right on Wednesday. You have to think like a lemur."

"I can't even think like a person on Tuesday," said Percy.

"Then follow Grover and stop asking questions."

The entrance, naturally, was in the most romantic possible spot — the cliff above the sea, at sunset, red-gold light and all — and Percy chose that exact moment to lean toward Annabough, who chose that exact moment to notice, and it would have worked, honestly, if a fire-breathing giant chimp hadn't come over the ridge right then. So: no kiss. Instead: sword practice, a scream of "Empusa behind you," and a hasty descent into the under-maze.

The Lemurinth was alive. Corridors of living branch and stone re-arranged behind them. One moment they were in a tunnel of roots that pulsed like an artery; the next, in a chamber full of doors. Along the way they picked up companions the way a troop picks up ticks:

There was Briares, an ancient hundred-handed gibbon, sulking in a cave with only two arms' worth of confidence. "You're better than your worst day," Percy told him, and handed him a nugget of the maze's own bronze to fix, because there is no grief so heavy that a repair job doesn't help a little.

There was Rachel Elizabeth Dare, a mortal with the sight, who drove the maze crazy because a mortal mind without a divine signal was pure static to it.

There were rumors of Nico, son of Hades, drifting through the maze's deeper floors being mentored by King Minos's ghost — an apprenticeship with no good ending, since ghost kings do not teach you useful things like baking, only useful things like regret. Percy kept leaving messages for him in the dark. He was almost sure some of them arrived.

There was a Sphinx, who had replaced riddles with a standardized test. "Question one," it announced as Annabough stood sword-ready. "Thirty monkeys share a tree. If each monkey picks twelve bananas, define the concept of destiny. Show your work."

"That's not a riddle," said Annabough. "That's bureaucracy."

"It's for funding," said the Sphinx, wounded.

They were captured by a three-bodied rancher named Geryon, who worked them half to death and set Percy one impossible chore before he'd free the rest: muck out stables that had not been mucked since the age of heroes, a mountain of Augean-grade monkey business. Percy looked at the manure, looked at the river running conveniently past the fence, and re-plumbed the valley. Geryon, to his credit, honored the deal. To his debit, he tried to eat them anyway.

They fought a giant named Antaeus, son of the earth, who grew stronger every time he touched dirt — and Percy, realizing there was no version of wrestling him that ended well on the ground, introduced him to the ancient art of being hung from the ceiling by his own chains. And in a golden hall of peacocks and bad decisions, the goddess Hera offered the troop her patronage — all they had to do was fit into her idea of family, which fit like a banana fits into a boot. Annabough declined. Hera's smile did not change at all, which was the frightening part.

They found Daelus himself in a workshop of humming bronze birds and mechanical spiders, guarded by a hellhound the size of a school bus named Mrs. O'Banany: a bent old genius with a laptop and a suitcase full of escape plans. When they asked him to help stop the invasion, he gave them the face of a monkey who'd already made his deal.

"My body dies, the Lemurinth dies," he said. "That's the design. And I am — forgive me — not ready to be a design flaw."

Meanwhile, above, Luke led the invasion. The army came up through the maze floor like hornets out of a rotten log: dracaenae, hellhounds, and the Titan lord's vanguard, marching to bury Camp Half-Primate once and for all.

The camp fought. Chiron's archers turned the sky rainy with arrows. The Ares cabin arrived fashionably furious. Clarisse refused to speak to anyone, but fought like three monkeys, which was her version of a warm hug.

But the line broke. The giant drove them back to the amphitheater, and it would have been over—

—except that Briares came up out of the maze with all hundred fists at once, pelting the invader with boulders like the world's angriest fruit-throwing contest, and Grover, the softest lemur in the western hemisphere, closed his eyes, thought about everything the wild had lost, and let out the scream of the god Pan.

PANIC.

It hit the invading army like a wall of dread. Monsters dropped their weapons. Giants sat down and cried. Even the hellhounds remembered urgent appointments elsewhere. The vanguard fled back into the Lemurinth, chased by their own imaginations.

The Titan lord's scheme broke — at the cost of one old inventor's change of heart. Daelus, watching monsters swarm past his workshop, looked at his suitcase of escape plans, set it down, patted Mrs. O'Banany once, and let Annabough's knife find the maze's one true anchor point.

The Lemurinth collapsed with a sound like a forest falling asleep.

But there was one more betrayal waiting on the sand. Luke had come to the beach himself, and Kronos had come inside Luke — you could tell because his eyes went gold, all the way through, like syrup replacing a soul. The Titan rose in a borrowed body, and it fell to Rachel Elizabeth Dare, a mortal girl with no sword, no training, and no apparent sense of self-preservation, to throw her plastic hairbrush at the lord of time.

It hit him in the eye. Even Titans have to respect that.

Kronos vanished into the sea-smoke with Luke's body, and Percy understood, watching the horizon, that they had not won the war this time. They had only met the enemy wearing his new monkey suit — and the suit had fit.

Afterward, on the beach, the council of cloven elders finally — finally — gave Grover his searcher's license, and the wild itself settled into him like a bird onto a branch. Pan was gone, but the spirit remained, and it had picked the unlikeliest possible lemur to carry it.

"Two wars down," said Annabough, elbow-deep in repairing the camp's climbing wall.

"One to go," said Percy, watching the sun go down over the water and thinking about a mountain in the city, a Titan in a sarcophagus, and a prophecy with his name on it — and, for once, not kissing her at sunset, because a hellhound barked.

Third time's the charm, he told himself. Probably.
