# The Long Dark Flea-Time of the Soul

*A parody of "The Long Dark Tea-Time of the Soul"*

It can hardly be a coincidence that no language in the jungle has ever produced the expression "as pretty as an airport."

Kate Squeaker only wanted to catch her flight from Jungleport to Ape-lo. What she got was a check-in desk full of monkeys vaporizing in a flash of green light, a hole in the ceiling shaped suspiciously like a very large orangutan, and a strong feeling that the airline's apologetic leaflets were not going to cover it.

"They can't just lose a passenger," she told the desk clerk, who had regrown most of his fur and none of his composure.

"Madam," he said faintly, "we haven't lost him. There is no him. There has never been a him. He is not on the passenger list, he is not on the cargo list, and he is not, according to the zoo's records, on this planet."

"Then who signed the ticket?"

The clerk shuddered. "A monkey. At least, a shape. It paid in a coin. The coin had a face on it, and madam, I am obliged to tell you that the face was not a monkey's."

One other thing: a large bearded ape of a god had been at that desk minutes before, shouting at a ticket agent about a banana — a first-class banana, no less, in a class that serves no bananas. Nobody could remember his face. Minds slid off it like rain off a gibbon's back.

Two days later, Kate came home to her treehouse and found an enormous bird of prey sitting on her sofa.

Which is how she met Dirk Macaquely.

Dirk Macaquely — billing himself a "holistic detective" — ran a stuffy office in Camdentroop on the principle of the fundamental interconnectedness of all bananas: solve every case by following everything that had ever happened, and bill accordingly. His secretary, a spectacled macaque named Miss Macaquees, refused to type anything that could not be billed and refused to bill anything that had not been typed, a system Dirk described as "a snake eating its own tail, but a snake I can invoice."

"Your case," he told Kate, waving a fistful of expedition-authorized bananas, "involves an exploded desk, a missing god, a wounded condor, a lawyer, and a calculator. Obviously."

"My case," said Kate, "involves a large bird in my living room."

"Obviously," said Dirk.

The calculator in question was a Banana Ching machine: divide any number by nothing and you got no error, only *a suffusion of yellow*. Dirk had bought it from a gibbon in Bermondsey who pretended not to exist, which was excellent marketing. That afternoon he fed the machine the question *where do I begin?* and it answered, as it always answered, "a suffusion of yellow," which Dirk interpreted to mean Woodshead — mostly because Woodshead was where he had already decided to go. That is the beauty of holistic detection: you are never wrong about your reasons, only about your conclusions.

The condor, meanwhile, had a visitor. A small, green-eyed, smug creature called Toerag — an attendant of some kind, self-appointed — turned up at Kate's treehouse and demanded the bird in a voice like a hinge that had been told it was right.

"The bird is property of the master," said Toerag. "Everything that flies is his. The sky is his. That sofa is negotiable."

"The bird," said Kate, "is concussed, and bit a vet this morning."

"The master will want it back."

"The master," said the condor from the sofa, in a voice like rocks settling, "can wait."

Toerag fled. The condor fixed Kate with one fierce golden eye and said nothing else for two days, and Kate, who had been raised to be polite to her elders — even elders with talons — kept bringing it dead things from the market, which it ate with dignity and no thanks whatsoever.

The trail — Dirk never followed leads, only vibes — led him to the tall bearded ape himself. His name was Tohrt. Son of Ohdinn. God of Thunder, which is a great deal for a gorilla-god whose own father had retired.

"You don't understand," Tohrt rumbled, crouched in a rented nest in Muswell Hill, big as a fridge and twice as hummy. "You monkeys get old and *done*. We gods just get old. Forever. Do you know what it's like being a god in a world that's stopped believing in you? You hang around the jungle like an unpaid intern. You go *barmy*."

"I've heard it called going ape," said Dirk.

"I AM APE," said Tohrt, and the windows rattled.

Beside him lay a furled umbrella that hummed like a wasp in a jar. There was also, Dirk noticed, a wound in the god's side, old and unhealed, which Tohrt angled away from the light. Gods do not scar. Something had been done to him deliberately, and by family.

"My father," said Tohrt, "is not dead. He has *sold out*. The thunder rights. The tithes. The ravens' pension. All of it, to a lawyer. I tried to fly to the lawyers, and a desk clerk with a rulebook the size of a fig-stone would not let me board, and I — lost my temper." He looked shamefacedly at the hole his temper had left in the ceiling. "First Class was full."

So they went to Woodshead: a very exclusive retirement forest for patients with unusual conditions, all mossed quiet and creased linen, where the leaves were changed twice weekly and nothing was ever mentioned twice. They were admitted by a nurse of enormous serenity and enormous everything else, Sister Brunhildabboon, who hummed opera and smelled faintly of thunderstorms.

In a private glade sat Ohdinn. One-eyed, ancient, magnificent, wearing a dressing gown and a bib, in a wheeled chair of white root-wood, being fed grapes by a discreet macaque. He looked at Tohrt for a long time.

"Son," he said at last. "You've put on thunder."

"You sold the franchise." Tohrt's voice shook the birches. "You sold *me*. The wound in my side — that was clause seven, wasn't it? 'All liabilities arising from heirs.'"

"The wound in your side," said Ohdinn mildly, "is the only thing you ever kept that I gave you." He waved the grape macaque away. "Tohrt. I fought, for nine thousand years, a war nobody remembered. I have been worshipped, mocked, and hungry, usually in that order. Here I am warm. I am fed. Nobody prays at me. Nobody needs me furious on schedule. Do you know what this is? This is *retirement*. It is Asgard with a duvet."

"It's giving up."

"It is checking out, and I would rather do it in a chair than a war." The old god leaned forward, and for a moment his eye was the eye from the coin at the airport. "I regret one thing. One. The eagle. They shot her out of the sky — my last companion, my oldest guilt — and she is too proud to admit she needs help, and I cannot go to her, because I signed the sky away. Look after her, boy. Not for me. For her."

Tohrt stared. Overhead, the storm that had been assembling itself all evening — entirely unbudgeted — thought better of it and broke apart into light, apologetic rain.

"She is being looked after," he said at last. "By a monkey named Kate."

"Then treat the monkey kindly," said Ohdinn, "and the eagle better."

And so the whole sorry business unraveled, as Dirk's business did, all at once and mostly by accident. The green flash at Jungleport had been Tohrt's temper. The wounded condor on Kate's sofa had been Ohdinn's guilty old companion, shot out of the sky by a rival god's freelancing raven and, being proud, unable to admit it needed help. And the lawyer had been eaten. Not by anyone supernatural, particularly. Just eaten. Jungle law. Toerag, it is recorded, attended the reading of the will and got nothing.

Kate nursed the condor back to health. The condor, in its grudging way, let her. Tohrt visited on Thursdays, and the weather over her treehouse was noticeably better for it.

On the last evening, Dirk sat in his office explaining himself to Kate, because she had refused to leave.

"The gods are leaving," he said. "The old ones. They're either checking out by selling themselves to solicitors, or raging in airport queues, or sitting in your living room pretending they aren't grateful. And do you know why? Immortality is the long dark flea-time of the soul. Any monkey with fleas is never really alone. There's always something to scratch. But a god has no fleas. Nothing to groom, nothing to pick, nothing to do but sit there, forever, being immortal at three in the morning."

Kate considered this. "You're describing my uncle."

"Madam," said Dirk, "your uncle is immortal in all the ways that matter, then. My bill—"

"—has a suffusion of yellow all over it," said Kate.

"Everything," said Dirk, with a holistic smile, "is connected."

Back at his treehouse flat, Dirk finally faced his own long dark flea-time: the refrigerator. He had not opened it in over a year. Something in there had begun, quietly, to evolve. He looked at the door. It looked back.

Tomorrow, he decided. Definitely tomorrow.
