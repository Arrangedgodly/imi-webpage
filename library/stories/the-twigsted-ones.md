# The Twigsted Ones

*A parody of "The Twisted Ones"*

When Grandmother Cotys died, I got the job of cleaning out her house at the edge of the Scrubs, because I am a colobus with poor boundary-setting skills. I brought my loris, Bongo, because Bongo has even worse ones.

The realtor had given me three weeks. Grandmother's house was a big shambling place full of taxidermied enemies and china penguins, and it sat on land the jungle had spent thirty years trying to reclaim. The taxidermy alone was a museum of grievances: a bobcat with an expression of permanent surprise, a hawk mid-glare, and one bear — a bear, two jungles from any mountains — with a look of such ongoing resentment that I put a towel over it the first night and never took the towel off again.

The second day, Miss Gennings from down the road came by with a casserole and the latest news, which was forty years old. She had known Grandmother Cotys her whole life.

"Sweet woman," said Miss Gennings. "Sweet, but odd. She used to argue with the yard. We'd see her out there of an evening, telling the treeline it was mistaken." She patted my hand. "You'll want to be careful with that yard, dear. It took her a while, but she won most of those arguments. The ones she lost — well. Those aren't my business, thank you."

She left the casserole and the implication, and drove off in a truck older than the road.

I found Grandfather Frederick's journal in the upstairs desk, behind a drawer that wasn't supposed to come out. Grandfather had been an engineer, a practical monkey, and his handwriting was as tidy as typewriter keys. That's what made the journal hard to read.

*June 12. The twisted ones were in the yard again. They had been at the wind chimes. Every cord was knotted into a spiral. Do not let them see you notice.*

*June 18. They twisted the clothesline. This morning, the bean poles. I do not know what they want.*

*June 21. They have twisted the cat. The cat is all right, but it was a near thing. Keep away from the white ones. If you see them, do not run. Running is noticing.*

*June 25. Cotys knows. She has known for years. She is pretending not to, and they are pretending she is pretending, and I am an engineer, and I cannot pretend hard enough.*

And then, on the last page with any writing: *They are not deer.*

Bongo, who was supposed to be asleep on my shoulder, growled at the window.

The Scrubs is all second-growth strangler figs and scraggly pine, and that evening, at the treeline, I saw them. Pale, skinny figures like plucked herons, standing still as fence posts. Their eyes were white all the way through — no pupil, no dark, like two little moons. They stood the way nothing healthy stands. Too patient.

I did not run. Running is noticing. I lowered the window shade very slowly and poured myself a large glass of fig wine, and Miss Gennings' casserole, I will note for the record, was excellent.

In the morning the wind chimes were knotted into spirals, and out past the garden, in the low grass, I found the structure: twigs, hundreds of them, lashed and twined together into a hollow figure taller than me. It looked like a monkey, if you'd only ever had a monkey described to you. Over the phone.

I decided, with the clearheadedness of the doomed, that the attic was a lovely place to sort china penguins, and I did not go near the field again.

That was the day Bongo got out.

I don't know how — the screen was intact, the door was shut, and Bongo is a loris, a monkey whose top speed is "shortly." But by afternoon his cushion was empty, and I stood on the porch with my heart going like a woodpecker, looking at the field.

I found him standing in the low grass, stiff as a fence post, with five of the white ones around him in a wide circle. Every one of them was pointed at the house. Not at Bongo. At the house. Bongo was the only one facing me, and his round eyes were enormous.

"Bongo," I said, very quietly, in the voice you use for a friend standing on a cliff edge. "Walk. Don't run. Don't look at them. Come count the penguins."

He walked. He was very much not noticing them. He got within ten feet of me and then climbed me like a tree, and we stood there while five sets of moon-eyes regarded the porch with what I can only describe as professional interest, and then I carried him in, and shook for a while.

That night the whistling started.

Not from the woods. From the yard, from under the porch, from the gravel path — everywhere but the house, as if the sound were walking the property line, checking the doors. Bongo sat up on the bed with his round eyes shining and growled steadily for four hours, and I lay there reciting facts to keep from screaming. Interesting fact: slow lorises have a toxic bite. Interesting fact: it did not seem relevant.

In the morning I went up to the attic, because an attic full of china penguins cannot hurt you, and there among the penguins I found Grandmother's own book. Not a journal, exactly. A grocery ledger — dates, prices, eggs, flour — and in the margins, going back decades, tallies. Little rows of tick marks, in pencil, in her round hand, and beside them in tiny letters the same word, over and over: *watchers.* She had counted them. She had counted them for thirty years, and the counts went up, and down, and up. The last entry was three weeks before she died. The number was high.

At dawn I saw them at the treeline again, dozens now, and standing in front like a bad idea with legs was the twig figure. It was not in the field anymore. The field was behind it. It was halfway across the yard, and one long twig arm was raised, and around its twig wrist, I saw with a special cold feeling, was knotted a spiral of cord from our wind chimes.

They came at dusk.

The white eyes were at every window before the light finished failing, and they did not try to break in. They simply stood there, closer to the glass than anything has a right to stand, and the whistling moved inside my head, and I understood that they would wait until we came out. Or until we didn't.

So I gave them something else to notice.

I took Grandmother's good oil lamp — her entire inheritance of lamp oil — and threw it through the window into the middle of them, into the twig one, and the dry grass did the rest.

The figure went up like a festival. The white-eyed things turned toward it, every moon-eye fixed on the flames, all at once — noticing it, being noticed back — and Bongo and I went out the back door and down through the black woods with the fire lighting our backs and the whistling rising into something like weather. I did not run. I left at a very fast, very deliberate walk. Running is noticing. Leaving is not.

Grandmother's house caught too. The insurance monkey was very understanding, in a suspicious sort of way, and asked only one question, which was whether the bear had been recovered, and I said I believed the bear had seen itself out.

That was years ago. I live in the city now, eleventh floor, no trees worth the name. Bongo sleeps a great deal, and I sort china penguins for a living. People say, "what a calming job," and I say, "you have no idea how calming."

But I'll tell you the honest truth. To this day, when the wind chimes tangle — really tangle — I do not untangle them.

And when I pass a knot of twigs on a forest floor, all twined together like they were lashed on purpose, I keep away from the white ones, keep away from the twisted ones.

I do not run. Running is noticing.

But I have gotten very, very good at leaving.
