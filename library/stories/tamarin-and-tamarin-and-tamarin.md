# Tamarin, and Tamarin, and Tamarin

*A parody of "Tomorrow, and Tomorrow, and Tomorrow"*

*Tamarin, and tamarin, and tamarin, creeps in this petty pace from tree to tree...*

Sam Mazer and Sadie Green were emperor tamarins, born with mustaches they did not earn and could not shave off. Sam was raised mostly by his grandparents, who believed a sick boy deserved every machine ever built, and who bought him, in order: a projector, a computer, a telescope, and, for the days when the treatments left him too tired for any of them, a very fast wheelchair. His mother came and went like weather with a suitcase. Sadie visited the jungle infirmary because her sister slept and would not wake.

They met in the infirmary when they were eleven: Sam with a tail crushed under a falling log and a suspicious lump in his leg, bald from the treatments, furious at everything; Sadie with a bag of pebbles.

Sadie taught Sam a game she had invented on the boat over — rules, levels, a boss made of five stones wrapped in a leaf. Sam beat it in an afternoon, redesigned it in an evening, and made it harder, kinder, and stranger by nightfall.

"You changed my game," Sadie said.

"You made a game worth changing," Sam said.

For three months they were inseparable, two sick-ish, smart-ish monkeys building worlds out of pebbles and bark. Then an old nurse with a grudge got Sadie banned from the ward, and they did not speak for six years, which in tamarin is nearly half a life.

They met again at a train-station fig tree, and it started exactly where it stopped. Within a year they had built FIGGO in the hollow of the old fig where Sam roomed at school: a game where you play a small tamarin climbing a great tree that never ends, and when you fall — and you always fall — you become a seed and grow again from the ground.

"A game about second chances," the judges said, giving it the grand prize. "A game," Sam said into the microphone, "about every monkey who ever fell out of a tree and got back up."

Behind them stood Marx — Sam's roommate, a capuchin so handsome that other monkeys became stupid around him — who had talked a publisher out of money they did not have and become their producer. Together they founded Unripe Games, on the theory that the best fruit never hurries. Marx wrote the studio rules on the wall of the fig hollow: RULE 1: THE GAME IS THE BOSS. RULE 2: SEE RULE 1. Under it, in smaller letters, someone had added RULE 3: FEED SADIE, which Sam denied writing and enforced daily.

Once, early, a journalist asked Sam and Sadie whether they were a couple. "We are not a couple," Sadie said. "We are collaborators. We are the two monkeys who make the thing." "A couple," said Sam, "is two monkeys who make only each other." The journalist printed both quotes. It remained, for twenty years, the only thing ever printed about them that neither of them wanted to correct.

The fight came at the awards, the way fights do. An interviewer said, "So *you're* the genius who made FIGGO," and Sam, flustered, camera-blind, said, "Well — I had assistance." In the credits, Sadie's name had already shrunk to a line of small print: *with assistance from Sadie Green.*

"Assistance," Sadie said afterward, in the empty office.

"It's a technical term," Sam said.

"It's an erasure," Sadie said. "Titles are how monkeys climb, Sam. You let them take my branch."

They did not speak for four years. Two tamarins on the same branch, facing away, is how Marx described the company to investors, who invested anyway, because Unripe's games were that good.

Sadie spent two of those years under the paw of Dov, her old design professor, a famous tyrant of a snow monkey who taught by subtraction: your idea is derivative, your mechanic is sentimental, your joy is unearned. Sadie mistook the subtraction for rigor, the way the young do, until the afternoon she watched Dov workshop a student's game to death in forty minutes and then fail to build anything at all himself. "He teaches doubt," she told Marx on the way out. "I want to build. Doubt is for editors." She quit Dov and, by way of a thank-you, sent him a copy of her next game with no note.

It was called PERSIMMON, and Sam came back to help her finish it, because it was too good and she was too tired. In PERSIMMON, nothing can be killed. Your enemies can only be frozen, mourned, and — if you play long enough, and grieve long enough — thawed. The critics called it sentimental. The players called it the truest thing ever coded. It sold a million, and Sadie's name was on the front, full size, twice.

Then came the night the jaguar got into the studio compound. It was Marx who heard it first, Marx who went out with a torch in each hand, Marx who stood in the gap between the dark and the door. The torches went out. What happened after happened fast and cost everything: Sam lived, tail ruined, ending the year with a prosthetic of braided vine; Marx did not.

At the funeral, Sam and Sadie stood at the same grave and did not face away.

"We were unbearable," Sam said.

"You especially," Sadie said. "But he loved us anyway. That was his whole game design."

Years passed. They made a western called PIONEERS, in which the frontier could only be survived by cooperating — which the reviewers called unrealistic and the players called the best thing ever built — and a world called MAPLE, in which a quarter of a million monkeys built one enormous tree together, and griefed each other, and built again, which is the entire history of troops in one sentence. At fifty, the leg that had been a boy's cancer finally called in its debts, the foot came off, and Sam designed the replacement himself, carved vine-and-wood with a save-point glyph on the ankle. "Every death is a seed," he said, hopping. "I'm finally taking my own advice."

Sadie had a stroke at sixty-one, in the middle of the last game. Sam sat by her bed with a board and fig pieces and waited, which is also a design discipline. When she could speak again, she said, "We're not done."

"We're slow," Sam said. "We always were."

They were careful with each other, which is a kind of love, and honest, which is a rarer one.

The last game they made together they called *Tamarin, and Tamarin, and Tamarin.* You play every generation of one family of tamarins climbing the same enormous tree — grandmother, mother, daughter — each life starting where the last one fell, each rung earned by somebody before you. Every death is a seed. Every seed is a save point. There is no winning, only climbing, and the reviews called it devastating and perfect and the two old monkeys who made it just called it Tuesday.

At the launch, in the hollow of the old fig tree, Sadie raised a cup of fig wine.

"To tomorrow," she said.

"To tamarin," Sam said. "And tamarin. And tamarin."

Because in games you get another life, and in life you only get another day — tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow — which is, when you think about it, the greatest cheat code ever written, and the only one nobody ever patches out.
