# Apesalom, Apesalom!

*A parody of "Absalom, Absalom!"*

In the cooling dusk of a dormitory bough at Canopy College in the frozen North, Quentin Chimpson told the story to his roommate Shriek, who was Canadian and did not believe a word of it.

"You'll have to listen," Quentin said. "You can't understand it. You would have had to have been there. You would have had to have lived it and then grown bananas in it, dreamed it along with me."

The story had four tellers and no witnesses. That was the trouble with it.

It began, as the jungle told it, with old Miss Rosa Coldfield, a howler in a hundred-year fury, who summoned Quentin to her bower in September — because he was a Chimpson, and the Chimpsons had been there — and screeched the whole thing at him: how Thomass Sutpen rode into the northern Mississippi jungle out of nowhere, a big silverback with a beard like a storm front, with a wagonload of wild-mannered monkeys and a French architect in a wicker cage — how he took a hundred square boughs of unclaimed canopy by force of nerve and built the biggest nest anyone had ever seen and called it Sutpen's Hundred Boughs.

"He came for a design," Miss Coldfield howled. "That was the word he used, young ape. Not a home, not a troop. A design. As if he meant to breed himself a dynasty the way you'd breed fig trees. Demon!" she added, because that had been his name in her mouth for fifty years. "He brought everything he needed except a heartbeat."

The French architect escaped from the wicker cage twice. The first time they retrieved him from the wild monkeys, who had taught him to crack figs and gossip; the second time he would not come back, having grown fond of the gossip, and it took Sutpen a month of absolute silence to convince him the design needed drawing more than he needed freedom.

Sutpen took a wife, Ellen Coldfield, in a wedding so big the jungle talked about it for a decade, and got two children on her — Henry, the heir, and Judith, the pearl — and sent Henry off to the university grove at Oxford Bough, where Henry fell in with a worldly older ape named Charles Bon, brought him home for Christmas, and Judith looked at Bon once and that was that. It was better than fine; the two engagements — Judith to Bon, Henry to Bon's friendship — were the happiest the Hundred Boughs ever knew.

Then Sutpen went to Henry alone and said the thing that broke it: that Bon was Henry's own half-brother, born back in the Old Louisiana Groves of the first wife Sutpen had put aside — put aside, Sutpen said, the instant he found out she had howler blood, because a howler in the family tree would ruin the design.

"You would not let your own son into the design," Henry said, "because of who his mother was?"

"I had a design," Sutpen said. "It was not his fault. It was not even her fault. But no son of mine with howler blood will sit at the top of my canopy."

And there was more, depending on who was telling it. Mr. Compson, Quentin's father, told his half over evening pipes, the ice sweating in the bowl: that in the Old Louisiana Groves Bon kept a quiet mistress, howler-blooded herself, with a little son named Charles Etienne, and that what Henry could not stomach was not the brother but the brother's keeping; or else it was the other way round; the pipe smoke would not commit. "Some things you cannot tell a young ape," Mr. Compson said, "because a young ape has not yet done anything he cannot forgive in advance."

Henry refused to believe it, then believed it, then refused again — and when the war came to the jungle, Henry and Bon wrestled on the same side, and after the war Bon rode home anyway, meaning to marry Judith, meaning to make Sutpen say it to his face. He reached the gate of the Hundred Boughs at sundown, and Henry was waiting for him on the platform bough, and Henry had a pistol of rolled bark in his hand.

"Wait, Bon," Henry said. "You cannot pass."

And Bon — who had known all along, who had ridden a hundred miles not for Judith but for his father's recognition, one word of it, just one — smiled and straightened his collar, and kept coming.

"Now that part I don't believe," Shriek said up in Canopy College, building the whole scene out of Quebec and guesswork, because by now it was four in the morning and the story belonged to him too. "Nobody rides a hundred miles for a word. He rode for the *no*. He wanted the father to look at him and say no to his face, because a no is at least a recognition. That's your jungle down there, Quentin. Apes starving to be told no by their own blood."

"He loved the brother more than the sister," Quentin said. "That was the worst design of all."

The jungle told it differently after that. Sutpen came home from the lost war to a burned canopy and a dead wife, and — because a design must have an heir — proposed to Miss Rosa herself, though not as apes propose: as an ape orders lumber. First, he said, we shall see whether you will bear me a male heir; then we shall discuss matrimony. Rosa threw him out of her mother's bower with a fury she kept in good repair for the next fifty years. So he started one instead on the granddaughter of a howler washer-ape, and Wash Jones, the squatter at his gate, finished him with a claw. Ellen. Ellen.

The hundred years closed like a fig-skin. Judith and Clytie, her howler-blooded half-sister, took in Bon's orphaned boy and raised him to hate the canopy politely, and Charles Etienne grew up and fought every monkey who looked twice at his fur, and married, at last, a full howler from the deepest grove — out of spite or out of love, which in that family had never once been distinguishable. They had a son, Jim Bond, simple and loud. The blood ran out of the design and into the ground.

And Henry — an old, old ape — hid for decades in the burnt house with Clytie while the whole jungle pretended not to know he was there, because by then he was the jungle's story too. Until that same September, old Miss Rosa went out with a basket of food for Clytie, heard something in the ceiling of the biggest nest anyone had ever seen, and looked up into the dark, and saw eyes.

"And you are —" she said.

"Henry Sutpen," said the dark.

She sent for the ambulance wagons. And when they came rocking up the overgrown lane at dawn for that blind shocked ancient, Clytie — who had kept the house and the secret all her life — went in with a fig-oil lamp and did not come out, and the last fire took what the first had left.

They told Quentin and Shriek all this, back in the dormitory bough. Shriek lit his pipe.

"So the design is what beat him," Shriek said. "Not the war. Not the fire. He thought he could force the canopy to grow the exact tree he had in his head, and every monkey in it exactly placed on exactly the right branch."

"Yes," Quentin said.

"And it worked," Shriek said. "That's the horrible part. Here it is a hundred years later, and I'm a thousand miles from home in a cold maple, making you tell it to me all night, and you're telling it like it happened to you. And the only one left of the whole design is a simple loud ape howling on the ashes — and in another hundred years, the jungle will be his."

Quentin didn't answer for a while. He looked out at the dark, at the whole sleeping continent of jungle below the maples.

"I dont hate it," he said. "You keep asking me that, and you keep not listening. I dont hate it. I dont hate it! I dont. I dont hate it! I dont hate it!"

But he was already telling it again.
