# The Lice of Locke Lemura

*A parody of "The Lies of Locke Lamora"*

In the canopy city of Camofur, where the Duke's justice fell faster than ripe fruit, an orphan chimp was sold to a blind baboon priest called Chains, keeper of the temple of the Nameless Thirteenth — the god no respectable troop names at dinner: the Crooked Warden of Thorns, patron of liars, givers of alibis, and monkeys with a professional interest in other monkeys' bananas.

"You," said Chains, sniffing the small thief head to tail, "have lice."

"Everyone has lice," said the thief.

"Not like yours," said Chains. "Yours have ambition. What's your name, vermin?"

"Locke Lemura."

"Then here's your first lesson, Locke Lemura. There's the Secret Peace — the thieves' troops pay tribute to the Duke, and the Duke pretends not to see them, and nobody robs the quality. There's the peace, and there's us. We are the Gentleman Bandar-loggers. We don't pick pockets." The blind baboon's grin was magnificent. "We pick *noblemen*. Cleanly. Politely. For everything."

"Nobody robs the quality," Locke had said. "Why not?"

"Why do you rob noblemen, my boy? Because that's where the money is."

*Interlude: at eight, Locke sold the Duke's own bridge to a visiting fishmonger. Twice. At nine, he forged requisitions for four hundred oranges "for the war effort." Chains made him eat every one, and Locke learned the two halves of the Warden: thou shalt not steal from people who can't afford it, and thou shalt not get caught being clever about the first bit. Also, thou shalt tithe a share to the Warden, because even divine patrons appreciate a standing order.*

And so Locke grew up gentry in the temple: taught tailfork and table manners, Camorri history and Camorri lying, by a priest who could hear a forged coin blush. His troop was Jean Tamarind, a chunky gorilla with twin saplings of hard sapote wood he called the Wicked Sisters — Chains had taken Jean aside young, given him the clubs, and taught him manners to go with them, "because a gorilla with two clubs and good table manners can pass anywhere" — the twins, Calo and Galdo, who could counterfeit anything except humility; and little Bug, who wanted only a garden.

Their masterpiece that season was Don Sapota, a nobleman with a fortune in salted 'nanas and a wife who trusted him slightly less than she trusted mosquitoes. Locke played "Lucas Figg," a modest grape-merchant dying tragically of 'nana pox. Jean played the Thorn of Camofur, a masked terror of the bridges, who "ambushed" poor dying Figg before the Don's eyes and, moved by his victim's weeping dignity, spared him. The Don, seeing a chance to buy the Thorn's protection against a rival house that did not exist, invested. Then invested more. The Thorn's gratitude cost four thousand gold 'nanas and the deed to a warehouse.

"You're sure the quality deserve this?" Bug asked, splitting the take.

"The quality hired the Thorn," said Locke. "The Thorn is us. Ergo, the quality hired us to rob them. I'm merely honoring the contract."

"I love this city," he added. "Camofur isn't corrupt. Corrupt implies deviation from the standard."

*Interlude: at eleven, Locke asked Chains why the Warden had no face in his temple. "Because he's got yours, boy," said the priest. "Every liar looks earnest in exactly the same place: the eyes. Practice in a mirror until you can lie to the mirror. Then stop, because that's where the trouble starts."*

Then the Gray Kong came.

A gorilla gray as storm cloud, voice like a rockslide, he began killing the capa's folk — Capa Bananasavi, the crime lord of all Camofur, lost his heirs one quiet rope-bridge at a time. The capa's daughter, Nazcanne, was returned to her father packed in a barrel of salt, which even the Duke's magistrates agreed was rude. When the Capa gathered his crews to face him, the Gray Kong walked into the meeting, named himself, and tore the room's loyalty out by the roots with the help of the Mandriller — a Bondsmajesty with technicolor cheeks, a sorcerer whose contracts were written in other people's blood.

The Gray Kong had one more use for a face as famous as Locke's: he made him dine with the Duke's own spymaster wearing the Capa's face, to prove the old ape still lived. Locke performed so well the waiters asked for an encore. It was the worst evening of his life, and it had competition.

He came to the temple, too, while Locke was out casing a vintner. Chains, being blind, was home. The Gray Kong's men brought good wine and bad intentions, and Chains drank like a connoisseur — which he was — and died like one, complaining about the vintage's finish and the company's manners.

The Gentleman Bandar-loggers were marked. Bug, who had only wanted a garden, got one: a hill of earth and a fruit tree planted on top. Calo and Galdo fell defending the temple door, and died as they had lived, impersonating each other so thoroughly that the Gray Kong's men were never sure which twin they had killed, and neither, by the end, were the twins.

Locke was taken and unmade politely by the Mandriller, and it is recorded that he did not scream until the sorcerer started discussing *interest*.

But nobody ever counted on Jean Tamarind with two Wicked Sisters and a grudge — Jean, ambushed by six in the night, whom the Sisters graded on a curve. He carried Calo's hat and Galdo's dice sewn into his vest, next to the manners. And nobody ever, *ever* counted on Locke Lemura wanting to live more than he wanted to be a gentleman.

He came for the Gray Kong on a swaying bridge in the rain, both of them ruined, both of them armed. The Gray Kong was stronger, heavier, and better with a blade, and he said so, and Locke — bleeding, grinning, chimp to the last — did the single thing no gentleman of Camofur ever does.

He bit him.

Not the ankle, either. History does not record where, precisely, out of respect for the deceased.

The Mandriller came next, and found his sorcery fouled by a trick Chains had taught small thieves years before — because a Bondsmajesty's power holds men's minds, and Locke Lemura's mind had been left deliberately, professionally, gloriously *empty* of anything a contract could grip. Jean's Sisters did the rest. They left the sorcerer alive, which in Camofur is considered the ruder weapon.

"You broke the Peace," the dying Gray Kong rasped. "The Duke's peace, the Capa's peace. Why?"

"Because it wasn't a peace," said Locke. "It was a cage with a fee. Master Chains taught us the Warden's only commandment: thou shalt not steal from people who can't afford it. Everyone in Camofur can afford it, friend. We checked."

Afterward, Locke raised a glass of pale wine to a blind baboon who wasn't there to see it, and Jean drank with him, and Locke and Jean — the troop entire now — sat in a temple of the Nameless Thirteenth and told lies about their dead friends until dawn, which is, in that faith, the only honest kind of prayer. Locke told the one about the fishmonger and the bridge, twice, and Jean laughed so hard the temple rang, and it counted as a psalm.

In Camofur they still say his name in whispers, mostly his own: Locke Lemura, the lice you never catch.
