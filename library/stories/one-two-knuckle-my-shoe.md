# One, Two, Knuckle My Shoe

*A parody of "One, Two, Buckle My Shoe"*

One, two — knuckle my shoe. It was with a shoe that it began: a small brass toe-knuckle, dropped by a chatty she-monkey in a taxi's doorway, and restored to her with ceremony by the passenger behind her — a passenger with orange fur, waxed whiskers, and thirty-two teeth of which he was unreasonably proud. Hercule Poirot was on his way to see his dentist.

The dentist, Mr. Morley, was a capuchin of conscience, at 58 Queen Chimp-lotte Street. His secretary, Miss Nevill, was unexpectedly absent (a telegram, said the maid: a sick aunt). Poirot's own appointment was at eleven-thirty, and was, as ever, a triumph of maintenance. He left with his molars singing and his goodwill intact, passing in the hall the chatty she-monkey with the toe-knuckles, a faded pretty vervet who had once been an actress: Mabelle Seed, waiting her turn.

At half past two that afternoon, Morley was found in his surgery with a bullet in his head and the pistol by his paw, and by evening one of his other patients — a voluble Greek visitor named Ambernutis, who had never in his life been to that surgery before — was dead of an anaesthetic overdose. Two deaths in half a day around a dentist's chair.

Three, four — knock at the door. Chief Inspector Japp, a leathery old baboon, arrived at Poirot's flat wearing his funeral face. "Morley shot himself, we think, after botching the Greek. Professional disgrace. You were his last sane patient, and his first. Sorry, Poirot. You'll be wanting to make it murder."

"I was his last *satisfied* patient, Japp. And a monkey who has kept his own teeth for fifty years does not care to have his supplier die by accident." Poirot counted on orange fingers. "Morley saw three creatures after me. Miss Seed, whom nobody can find. The Greek, dead. And — who else?"

"Alistair Blunt," said Japp.

Five, six — pick up sticks. Alistair Blunt was the only gorilla in the jungle whose name was printed beside the word *credit*: the great financier, plain, square, gray, holding half the banks of the canopy in one fist and the confidence of every small investor in the other. To suspect Blunt was to suspect the tree itself. Japp would not hear it; Japp's superiors would not spell it.

But Miss Nevill's sick aunt had never sickened: her telegram was a forgery, sent to clear the surgery of witnesses. And Miss Seed, who had gone to the police cheerfully and vanished after, left behind a flat nobody knew and a friendship nobody could trace — and a month later a disfigured body in a strange woman's rooms, wearing a coat like Mabelle Seed's, with hair like hers, and shoes that were wrong. Poirot looked longest at the shoes. The buckles were dull, the fit was broad, the heels were unworn: the dead monkey had *small* feet and the dead monkey's coat had *big* pockets, and the dental records sworn to by two surgeries identified the body as one Mrs. Chapman, of no fixed anything.

"Japp. Ask your zookeepers: when a monkey wants to borrow a body, he dresses it. But he cannot borrow its *teeth*, and he cannot always borrow its shoes."

Eleven, twelve — dig and delve. At Blunt's riverside estate, Poirot was received by the financier himself, mild as milk; by Blunt's American niece Jane Olivera and her hot-eyed radical suitor Howard Raikes; and by a gracious, vague elderly "cousin," Helen Montressor, who kept house in the west wing. Poirot ate muffins, discussed orchards, and observed that Miss Montressor's left paw was inked faintly green, as paws are that have lately written many envelopes.

Thirteen, fourteen — maids a-courting. A shot cracked from the hedge line that evening: Blunt walked away alive, and the gardener's new assistant — Frank Carter, Miss Nevill's morose young boyfriend, lately taken on at Blunt's own gate — was seized with a pistol in his pocket and politics in his mouth. Raikes's, said the papers', notoriety. It was all extremely satisfying to everybody, which was precisely what was wrong with it. Carter, bullied white, admitted at last he had gone that morning to Queen Chimp-lotte Street to beard Morley about Miss Nevill — and had found the dentist already dead, and seen *two* monkeys go down the back stairs.

Fifteen, sixteen — maids in the kitchen; and in the kitchen, the maid, Agnes, had heard nothing, seen nothing, and remembered everything, including that "Miss Seed's" appointment had run long and been shown up the *second* stair, to the private floor, where no surgery was.

Poirot assembled them all in Blunt's library, because one must: the financier; the false cousin; Jane; even Japp, gloomy as a wet weekend.

"Count with me, messieurs. *Un, deux* — the toe-knuckle: I met Miss Seed because a knuckle fell off her shoe, and I notice shoes; it is a disease of the tidy. *Trois, quatre* — the door: a forged telegram knocks the secretary away. *Cinq, six* — the sticks: Morley shot himself with a pistol that could as easily have been in another monkey's pocket. But consider the *order* of the patients, which the whole of Scotland Yard has read wrong all month. I was at eleven-thirty; Blunt at noon. When Morley led me out, he was alive; when the Greek came, Morley was already dead — and the Greek had never seen Morley *or* Blunt in his life. A dead dentist cannot give an overdose. A *dentist's chair* can, if a monkey who has watched that dentist work for thirty years sits in it, borrows the white coat, and says 'open wide.'"

Blunt said, mildly: "You are describing me."

"I am accusing you. You came out from behind the screen when Morley was dead, wearing his coat, and put the needle into the Greek, and shot nobody but a reputation. Miss Seed — poor Mabelle, who talked to everyone, and in Calcutta twenty years ago talked once too often to a Greek — had told Ambernutis a story of her old rep-theatre friend Gerda, who had married, secretly, a rising financier and gone quietly away. *Bigamy*, monsieur. Before your fortune, you married Gerda Grant; and then you married the heiress Rebecca Arnholt, twenty years your senior, and her millions; and the first wife was put away in a pretty lodge with a new name and a silence — 'Helen Montressor' — while the world called her your cousin. Mabelle, meaning nothing, recognizing you both, chattering toward catastrophe. So Gerda lured her to a rented flat under the name of Chapman, poisoned her, dressed the wrong body in her coat and her *shoes* — and Gerda's feet, madame, are two sizes smaller, hence the dull buckles on a corpse that never walked in them — and swapped the dental records of two surgeries to bury the truth in someone else's mouth. And Carter — the threatening boyfriend — was the gift that arrived by post: a scapegoat with a pistol and a temper, whom you rigged to fire at yourself across a hedge. Politically, Japp, a public crime. Privately, three deaths and one marriage certificate."

Silence. Jane Olivera's cup was ticking against its saucer.

Blunt got up, square and gray, an honest rock in a dishonest jungle, and made the only speech he had ever made on his own behalf. "I am worth more than a hundred radicals. Every bank in the canopy stands on my name. Expose me and you will not hang a murderer, Poirot; you will trigger a panic that starves every small monkey with a savings book. Protect me. For the sake of the tree, cover the rotten branch."

"No," said Hercule Poirot.

The word dropped like a fig.

"I have watched the tree, monsieur, and its name is *trust*, and you have been boring it through from the inside for thirty years. It does not matter that I like your banks better than another monkey's banks; three deaths are three deaths, and there is no credit in the canopy strong enough to cover them. Japp — the lady first, I think. She will not run."

Gerda Grant, once of the Calcutta repertory, went quietly, adjusting her gloves; she had been given, all her life, to silence when the curtain came down. Blunt, to the last, could not comprehend why thirty-two honest teeth and a bar sinister of banks did not balance three monkeys in a ledger.

Poirot dined alone that evening, and thought about a toe-knuckle in a taxi door.

"Un, deux," he said to his reflection, toothpaste foam ringing his muzzle like a ruff. "One must keep one's own in order, you and I. It is the *little* things, mon vieux — the shoes, the buckles, the order of the patients — the little grey fur follicles, they never fail. And where would any of us be, if we could not open wide, and trust the monkey in the coat?"
