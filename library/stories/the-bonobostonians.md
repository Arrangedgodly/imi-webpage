# The Bonobostonians

*A parody of "The Bostonians"*

Basil Bonobsom had come north to Bonoboston from the fallen branches of the South, a tall young howler with threadbare grooming and magnificent opinions, chiefly about himself. His cousin Olive Chimpcellor, meeting him at the station-tree, looked him over the way a monkey looks at a tick she has found in her fig.

"You may come to the meeting," she said, "provided you understand that you count as furniture."

"I have always aspired to furniture," said Basil.

The meeting was at the treehouse of Miss Birdseye, an ancient loris who had been giving her whole life away since before anyone could remember, and who smiled at Basil like the last fig of winter. The rooms were full of she-bonobos of the Cause — the emancipation of the female of the troop, the right of every monkey to her own banana, her own branch, her own opinion. There was Dr. Prance, a spider monkey who climbed professionally and took no patients below the third storey; there was Mrs. Moon-a, Olive's sister, who had married out of the movement into a comfortable nest and was enjoying the guilt, accompanied by her small son Newton, who bit the guests in a manner that promised, one day, politics; and there, on a chair by the window, sat a small marmoset with an enormous destiny: Verena Tarrat, daughter of the mesmeric Selah Tarrat, who had made a career of passing his paws over his daughter's brow.

"Be still, everyone," said Selah, brandishing his feather brush. "The voice comes to her when I make the passes."

He made the passes. The marmoset shut her eyes, laid her paws upon her breast, and out of her came the Voice — "Behold the she-bonobo, kept in the lower branches since the first fig fell!" — and the whole parlor went into spasms of feeling. Miss Birdseye wept into her fern. Olive Chimpcellor, cold, gray, and quietly consuming, decided upon the spot that Verena Tarrat was born to be the chimpion of the Cause, and that she, Olive, would educate her, house her, and guard her.

Basil had come to scoff. He stood against the wall with his arms folded, and the Voice went through him like warm fingers through matted fur, and he fell in love with his own objections on the spot.

"That voice," he declared on the walk home, "was made for one branch and one monkey — not to be shown to ten thousand strangers in a hall."

"Mr. Bonobsom," said Olive, stopping with terrible composure, "you are the enemy."

"I'm a howler," said Basil. "I come by my opinions loudly. Why should your side have all the noise?"

Asked at the meeting why she had never married, Dr. Prance had threaded a needle of conversation very neatly: "I see too many males recovering from their wives," she said, "to recommend the recovery to any monkey." Basil retold this all week as proof of the Cause's charm and its error; Olive retold it as proof of its point; and neither of them noticed they were quarreling over the same epigram, which is how families argue.

War was declared. Olive carried Verena off to the house on Chimp Street, read to her of the she-bonobos of history, dressed her in plain gray, and engaged the Voice a season deep in lectures. Verena spoke that winter in a dozen halls, and the Voice carried; monkeys wept who had never wept at anything but onions. But between engagements she was beginning to notice that the Cause talked largely of her voice, and that her voice was increasingly a property: Selah booked the halls, Olive wrote the words, and Verena owned, apparently, only the throat.

Basil pursued by letter, by ambush, and by apparent accident at the trance conventions of the interior, urging his own simple program: retirement from publicity, marriage to himself, a nest in the sunny South. Once he got her to himself for a whole afternoon, walking by the sea, and showed her the southern monkeys fishing off the rocks. "In my country," he said, "a female rules her own nest outright, and nobody has invented a Cause, because nobody has thought to take anything from her."

"You talk," said Verena, "as if happiness were a kind of shame."

"I talk," said Basil, "as if it were a place."

She wavered between them, stirred by the Cause and softened by the howler, while Olive grew grayer and grayer, and said one evening the truest thing she ever said:

"If she goes over to a male — after all that we intend, all that we are — I shall feel it as a defection from the whole she-bonobo world."

Between then and the great night there was a season of siege. Young Burrage — a rich chimp with a wardrobe like a sunset and a mother who collected causes the way other monkeys collect fleas — invited Verena to address his society in New York-nest. Olive smelled the trap (a male, a drawing-room, a wedding) and carried Verena off instead to a snowbound house in the country, where the two of them sat three days over the lives of the great she-bonobos of history. Verena cried over their sufferings and swore she would give her life to the Cause. "Yes," said Olive. "And never to a male." "Olive," said Verena, "you are terrible" — and loved her.

Miss Birdseye died that winter, and Verena nursed her to the last; and Basil, arriving through the snow, unwelcome and irrepressible, found himself, for one strange evening, on the same side of a bed with Verena Tarrat, the two of them passing each other the old loris's cup, watching the life go out of the one monkey in Bonoboston who had never once wanted anything for herself.

"She gave away everything she ever had," said Verena, "and it never once made her unhappy."

"It is the highest style of monkey I ever met," said Basil, "and I have no arguments against it."

It was the first thing they had ever agreed on, and it frightened them both more than the quarrels had.

The great night came: the Bonoboston Music Hall, packed to the rafters with she-bonobos and a few quiet males in the upper dark who had been told they could come. Verena stood in the wings in white ribbon, trembling like a leaf about to be groomed; Olive pinned on the last flower, radiant and gray; the chairman rose to announce her; and the audience rose too, a whole canopy of them, gone suddenly ape.

And out of the shadows at the wing took hold of her elbow a strong southern paw.

"Not here," said Basil. "Not for them. Verena — come."

"No," said Verena. "Yes. No!"

There was a trellis under the window, and a tapir-cab at the corner, and they were away down the nighttime streets of Bonoboston while behind them the crowd, discovering the empty platform, caught her name and roared it into the dark.

Verena cried all the way across the river. Basil, who had won, was wise enough not to say so. The roar of the crowd followed them for years, they say, and Olive never forgave the male of any species again. But it may be added that, in spite of her tears and the great hullabaloo of the emancipated, the marmoset was not, on the whole, so very unhappy.
