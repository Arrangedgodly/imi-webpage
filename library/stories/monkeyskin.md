# Monkeyskin

*A parody of "Donkeyskin"*

Once there was a Monkey King whose queen was the wisest, kindest macaque in seven jungles, and when she lay dying she took his hand in her small dry one and said, "Promise me this: only marry again a queen wiser and kinder than I. Swear it on the great fig."

He swore it on the great fig. He searched the whole world — he interviewed empresses and abbesses, a famously patient sloth, and a dolphin who was almost certainly wise but kept answering questions in fish — and found nobody. And when he came home hollow-eyed, the court began, terribly, to whisper that there was one creature wiser and kinder than the queen, one who had learned gentleness at her own knee.

Her daughter.

For a long time the king stuffed fig leaves in his ears. But a promise sworn on the great fig is a hard thing, and grief is a crooked carpenter, and one morning at council he cleared his throat and said the unsayable thing out loud. The whole court looked at its feet. The chancellor developed a violent cough and had to be carried out.

When the princess understood what they were whispering, she ran through the night to her godmother, the Fig Fairy, who lived in a fig that was bigger on the inside.

"He will never agree," said the Fig Fairy, combing her hair with a moonbeam. "Ask him for a gown the color of the deep jungle at dusk. No father can give that."

"Give *what*?" asked the princess — but she was desperate, so she asked.

The king called his ten thousand weaver-birds, and the gown came woven in teal shadow and leaf-fire, trailing like evening itself. He presented it at breakfast, with jam.

"Impossible," said the princess to the Fig Fairy. "How?"

"Ask for a gown the color of the moonlit canopy."

The weaver-birds worked by night, and it came woven in silver and palm-shadow, so cold and bright that moths followed her through the palace.

"He MUST refuse now."

"Ask for a gown the color of noon sun on a perfectly ripe banana."

It came woven in cloth-of-gold so warm the wardrobe glowed through the walls. The weaver-birds unionized.

"There is one thing left," said the Fig Fairy, gravely. "The Golden Banana. His treasure-tree. Its peel is the dearest thing he owns — he irons the fallen peels into coins. Ask for that peel. No father can give that."

The princess asked. And the Monkey King, whose love had rotted into something with teeth, went straight to the grove, plucked the Golden Banana that very evening, and brought her the peel, gleaming, huge as a cloak. He did not even wince. That was the terrible part — that he was *proud*.

"Now, my child," said the Fig Fairy, "run." So the princess folded her three gowns into a bundle, hid her little ring inside a fig, and draped the great golden-green peel over her head and shoulders, so that she looked like a mangy, scraggy, cabbagey kind of monkey, and walked away into the world. The palace dogs did not recognize her. Her own reflection required a moment.

At a far jungle she stopped and asked for work in the royal kitchens. The head cook, a baboon with one eyebrow and no patience, looked her up and down.

"Name?"

She couldn't say. Names could be followed.

"Experience?"

"Peeling."

"You're hired," said the cook, who had a mountain of plantains and a headache. And because she would give no name and looked so scraggy and peel-wrapped, the cooks called her what everyone called her: Monkeyskin.

She scrubbed yams. She peeled mountains of plantains. She hauled water until her shoulders sang. The scullery monkeys invented a game called Knock Off Monkeyskin's Peel, and she let them win, because princesses can afford to lose games and cooks cannot afford to lose tempers. She hid her bundle of gowns in the loft above the woodpile, and on Sunday afternoons, when the kitchen slept, she washed in the stream, shook out her fur, and wore one gown for one hour — dusk one week, moonlight the next, gold the next — just to remember who was under there. Then she folded them away and went back to being nobody. It is possible to be a princess and a scullion on alternating Sundays. It is only tiring.

Now it happened that the young prince of that jungle, who was sad the way young princes are sad — which is to say, poetically, indoors, at dusk — walked past the kitchen one evening and peeked through a crack in the door. And by chance Monkeyskin thought herself alone, and let the great peel slip down to her waist, and shook out her fur, and put on her gown the color of the deep jungle at dusk.

The prince's heart went off like a fig tree in fruit season.

After that he was worse — sighing, pale, pushing his dinner around his leaf — and the palace doctors were summoned. They felt his pulse, looked at his tongue, and shook their heads gravely.

"It is a fever," said one.

"It is the spleen," said another.

"It is love," said the oldest doctor, and everyone gasped, because the oldest doctor was paid by the diagnosis.

When the prince was asked what his heart desired, he whispered, "Banana bread. From the lower kitchen. That is all. That is everything."

And Monkeyskin, who felt sorry for him — for she had seen him peeking, and had peeked back, and neither had mentioned it, which is how those things begin — baked him a banana bread. As she poked it to see if it was done, her little ring dropped into the batter, and rather than ruin the bread she — well. She let it bake. And the prince found it with his slice, and held it up to the light, and declared a strange and terrible thing: that he would eat nothing but banana bread — every day, forever — until the finger this ring fit came to claim it.

"You will get very round," warned his mother the queen.

"Rounder things than I have ended well," said the prince, who had been reading philosophy.

The duchesses' daughters came with their fat cream fingers. The countesses' daughters came with their long thin ones. The ambassador's daughter brought a finger so bejeweled the ring did not know where to sit. The chamberlain even tried, discreetly, in a doorway, with soap. None fit, and the kitchens baked on, a new loaf every day, until the palace smelled like a bakery having a nervous breakdown.

At last, from the kitchen, grimy and scraggy and wrapped in her golden peel, came Monkeyskin. The court fell about laughing. The chamberlain laughed so hard he had to sit down on a duchess.

"Go on, then, girl," chuckled the prince's mother — kindly, for she had noticed the bread always arrived warm. "Try it, and be quick, so we can all have lunch."

Monkeyskin washed her paws, and slid the ring on, and it fit like a closing fig-flower — and as she raised her hand the great peel slipped, and beneath it were the dusk and the moonlight and the noon sun, all three gowns at once somehow, or perhaps it was only her. The laughing stopped so abruptly that three courtiers fell over. The prince rose from his bed of sighs, entirely well, entirely certain.

"It was *you*," he said. "Baking. All this time."

"Someone had to," said Monkeyskin. "You were wasting away on philosophy."

She married the prince that season, and the wedding smelled of banana bread, by royal decree. And among the wedding guests came an old, gray-faced monkey who hovered at the edge of the lamps, not quite daring to come in — an old king who had searched the whole world for a wise and kind queen, and found one at last, in himself too late. The bride knew him. The whole glade held its breath. She went to him, and put her hand in her father's, and forgave him — the wisest, kindest thing of all, which would have made her mother glad. He wept into her dusk-colored sleeve, and it is said the sleeve never faded.

The Fig Fairy came too, waved a branch, and everyone danced until the figs came down. The scullery monkeys were seated at the high table, the head cook was made a duchess (she terrified the nobility into obedience within a week), and the Sunday gowns were hung in the great hall so that everyone could be reminded, in dusk and moonlight and gold, that somebody is always more than they look — especially somebody in a peel.

And if they haven't stopped, they are peeling, still.
