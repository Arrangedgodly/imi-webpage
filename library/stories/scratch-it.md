# Scratch It

*A parody of "Hatchet"*

Brian Robeson was thirteen and a chimpanzee, sitting in the copilot's seat of a single-engine jungle hopper, flying north to his father's banana research station. The old capuchin pilot had let him take the stick once ("easy, young fellow, she bites").

Brian thought about the Split, and then about the Secret. The Secret was this: before the Split, he had seen his mother in the smoothie hollow with a stranger chimp in a bright red cap. He had never said. He carried it like a burr in the one spot on your own back you can never reach.

His mother, at the ferry dock, had tucked something into his belt: a bamboo backscratcher, carved with five fat teeth, a little flint bobble hung on a thong at the handle.

"So you can scratch your own back," she said, "because mothers can't always be there to."

He had almost laughed. A backscratcher. Some survival gear.

The pilot said something about figs — a word half-said, a smile half-made — and then his heart simply stopped. Just like that, mid-sentence, the old monkey slumped, and the hopper nosed down. Brian grabbed the stick. He had done this once, level and slow. He did it now as well as he could, which was not well, and the hopper tore through the canopy and plowed into an oxbow lake. He clawed free of the straps, swam out, and lay on the bank retching, with nothing but the backscratcher on his belt.

No pack. No matches. No troop. Only the scratch-it.

The first night, huddled under the root-bank, a voice came to him out of memory — his old troopmaster, Mr. Positive, who used to drum his knuckles on the blackwood and say: "You are the finest asset you own. Motivate the asset! The asset is all you've got!" Brian had found this hilarious at the time. He found it less hilarious now, and also, in the dark, with the jungle reading out its full inventory of noises, the only advice on offer. So he motivated the asset. He got up, ached, and looked around.

He learned the way the jungle teaches, which is slowly and by consequence. He learned that the sweet red gut-berries want to be eaten so they can finish you off, and spent a whole sick night paying for a handful. He learned that a half-cave under the root-bank of a giant fig is called shelter once you wall it with fronds, and home only after you have wept in it — and he wept in it, for the pilot, exactly once.

Then one night the porcupine came. It shuffled into his shelter as if it held the lease, and Brian threw the scratch-it at it in blind monkey panic. The porcupine left him a leg full of quills, and the scratch-it clattered against the rock wall — and in the dark, a scatter of sparks smoked the moss.

Fire, he thought. Fire was hiding in the flint bobble all along.

It took two days of striking and feeding before he had a friend that crackled orange, and he understood something that stayed with him: he was not the same chimp. The old Brian would have panicked. The new one struck sparks. Feeling sorry for yourself, he wrote later, never ripened a fig.

He fed that fire like a pet — banking it at night under ash, waking at dawn to blow it back to life, hauling deadfall through the drizzle, because the jungle teaches you fast: a fire is a mouth, and it is always hungry, and if it dies in the night, part of you dies with it. He built a wall of green wood to cut the wind, and stacked figs on it to smoke-dry, and the smoke kept the midges honest.

He ate real figs then, and his first fish — speared with a stick sawed to a point using the scratcher's teeth, because a tool that scratches can also cut. He learned the foolbirds, who stand on the ground looking like a fistful of brown leaves until you nearly step on them, and who are so dumb that the hard part of hunting them is not laughing long enough to lose your grip. He found the turtle nest above the sandbar and took two eggs for breakfast and marked the spot in his head as a pantry with a shell on it. The scratch-it dug for grubs, scraped the fish, killed two snakes (he hoped the second one was a snake), and every night scratched his back, thought of his mother, and did not cry, mostly.

The jungle sent its auditors. A fruit-hog raided his store, and he learned to cache high and mark the caches, and to eat less from all of them, because hunger has a longer memory than a fruit-hog. A hippo cow came off the bank like a thundercloud with legs and threw him into the reeds for standing in her river, and he learned that rivers have landlords. His body got lean and his eyes changed — he learned to see, not look: the flick of a bird meaning fish below, the cut of mud meaning a wallow, a smell of sour leaves meaning rain by dark.

Days went by. He stopped counting them, and then he caught himself singing to the fire — badly, half under his breath, an old troop-nursery song about a fig and a fig's best friend — and stopped, and then went on singing anyway, because the fire didn't mind.

Then came the monsoon. It rained as though the sky had a grudge, flattened his shelter, drowned his fire — and, the miracle, lifted the drowned hopper's tail into the shallows.

Brian dove one gray morning, working the wreck open with the scratch-it, and pulled out the survival pack: a dented flask, a sleeping fur, matches — and a little box with a red switch, which sang and crackled when he turned it on.

"Hello?" Brian said. "Can anybody hear me?"

The box sang to itself. Brian baked a fig on a hot stone and went to bed.

The next morning a rescue hopper came down the lake like a dragonfly with a grudge, and a monkey in a flying cap leaned out and called his name. The singing box had been talking to everyone the whole time. The pilot, it turned out, had lived — a fisher-lady had found him downstream, grumpy and grateful — and he asked Brian one question over the engine noise: "What kept you whole out there, son?"

Brian patted the scratch-it on his belt and didn't say the truest answer, which was that what kept him whole was wanting, sometimes, to be the kind of chimp who deserved to be found.

The papers called him the Jungle Boy. He was not a jungle boy. He was a chimp who had been changed — tough in the head, patient in the hands, first to see the small things: food, fire, fur, family. A reporter asked him about the Split, and about his father, and Brian said the Split was between his parents and none of a reporter's business. The Secret stayed a burr in the unreachable spot, and he discovered he could carry it a little lighter now, having carried heavier things.

On the wall of his room, forever after, hung a bamboo backscratcher with a flint bobble on a thong. Visitors always asked about it.

"It's the only gear I had," he told them. "It turned out to be enough."
