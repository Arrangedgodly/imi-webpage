# Horton Scratches a Leg

*A parody of "Horton Hatches the Egg"*

Half down the jungle, in the figgiest tree, there lived a lazy macaque by the name of Lazy Maisie. Maisie hated grooming, hated climbing, hated picking — but most of all she hated sitting still, which was a problem, because she had an egg.

Nobody asked too many questions about where a macaque got an egg. (A very tired hornbill. Finder's keepers, she insisted.)

And one sweltering afternoon, who should wander under her tree but Horton the gorilla, the most enormous, most gentle, most gloriously gray-backed ape in the entire canopy.

"Oh, Gorilla!" cooed Lazy Maisie, fluttering her eyelashes. "I'm so dreadfully tired. I need a vacation — palm air, fermented figs, absolutely no sitting — but I simply cannot leave my precious egg. Would you sit on it for me? Just a week. I'll be back before you know I'm gone. Quick as a fig falls."

Horton looked at the egg. The egg did not look back, being an egg.

"SIT on it?" said Horton. "A gorilla? On an egg? Maisie, I weigh as much as the tree."

"Oh, you'd be careful," sighed Maisie. "Besides — everyone knows you're kind. And faithful. They say you meant what you said and you said what you meant, and a gorilla's faithful one hundred percent."

Well. He had indeed said it. He did indeed mean it.

"Very well," he sighed, and he climbed up, and he sat — gently, delicately, absurdly — upon that egg, balancing his colossal silvery self on a nest of twigs like a bungalow balanced on a cupcake.

"I'll be back in a week!" trilled Maisie, and she swung off into the sunset, whistling.

Maisie did not come back in a week.

The rains came instead — SPLASH — thundering down on poor Horton's head, soaking him into a drippy gray sponge, but he never once moved. "I meant what I said," he muttered through chattering teeth, "and I said what I meant. A gorilla's faithful one hundred percent."

Then the dry season came, and the leaves turned brittle, and one by one they fell on Horton's head until he resembled a very patient bush. Then came the cold winds, and the huddling birds, who nested in his fur and paid him in gossip. Through it all, Horton sat, and sat, and sat some more, growing hungrier and grayer and more determined, while the egg beneath him stayed cool in the heat and warm in the cold, which is the whole trick of eggsitting and the only part of the job he was naturally brilliant at.

Then three friends swung by, howling with laughter.

"Horton! You absurd ape! You great galloping goober! Get OFF that egg! Maisie's in Palm Beach Cove eating fermented figs with a fruit-fly flotilla! She's been gone three hundred and forty-nine days!"

"Then it's three hundred and forty-nine days of a gorilla's word," said Horton, eyes closed, soaked, starving, loyal. "I said what I meant and I meant what I said. A gorilla's faithful one hundred percent."

His friends rolled off laughing, going completely bananas at the sight — but they came back the next week, and the next, and it is a fact that they laughed a little less each time, until by the end they were bringing him figs and pretending they just happened to be passing.

Then came the hunters — men with nets and cages and terrible hats — crashing through the underbrush, and one looked up and said, "Blimey, that's the queerest gorilla I ever did see. A sitting gorilla! Nest and all! We'll take him for the circus."

But Horton would not leave the egg. "Take me," he rumbled, "but the egg goes too."

The hunters (very confused, but carnivals pay by the pound of weird) chopped the whole branch, nest and all, lashed it to a raft, and floated Horton down the river to the Big Jungle Circus, where he was billed as THE GORILLA WHO SITS — fifty coconuts a peek. The billboard showed him looking noble, which was not difficult, as noble was the only thing he had energy left to look.

And still Horton sat, through the heat and the drums, through fifty-one shows a day, on a branch on a nest on an egg, faithful, faithful, faithful. Monkeys threw peanuts, and he ate the ones that landed near his mouth and let the rest lie. He sent his wages home to his mother and sat through the winter, until the egg under him was the most-traveled, best-warmed egg in the history of the jungle.

Fifty-one weeks later, in swung Lazy Maisie, fat and rested, having read about the famous gorilla in the Banana Gazette — and having read, further down the page, what famous gorillas and their famous eggs were worth.

"THAT'S MY EGG!" she shrieked, pointing. "MINE! The circus pays big coconuts for egg-sitting gorillas AND their eggs! I want the gorilla, the nest, the branch, and the EGG! Gimme gimme gimme!"

"Your egg?" thundered the ringmaster lion. "Madam, permit me to inquire: where precisely have you BEEN?"

"Vacationing," said Maisie, unabashed. "It's vital for my feathers — my FUR — my glow. Anybody can sit on an egg. Very few can glow."

"I sat on it," said Horton quietly, "for fifty-one weeks."

"You sat on it," sneered Maisie, "like a great gray FURNITURE. Now step aside and hand over my —"

And right at that moment — CRACK.

The egg split, and out popped a tiny marmoset, small as a fist, with an enormous thatch of silvery-gray fur down its back and two vast, gentle, deep-gray gorilla eyes.

The crowd went silent. The band stopped mid-tune. Even Maisie stopped talking, which the ringmaster noted down as the second miracle of the evening.

"Ack!" shrieked Maisie. "It's got HIS ears! It's got his EYES! It's got his — SITTING FACE! Take it away! I only wanted the coconut kind of egg!"

But the little silver-backed marmoset took one look at Horton — big, drenched, exhausted, faithful Horton — and leapt lightly onto his shoulder, wrapped its tail around his neck, and hollered, "OOH-OOH! DADA!"

The crowd came down like a monsoon of applause. Maisie flounced off in a huff and was never heard from in responsible circles again (though the fruit-flies sent her a postcard).

"Well," said the ringmaster lion, adjusting his monocle, "that seems settled. Monkeys are monkeys, no matter how small — and family is whoever sits on your egg for fifty-one weeks in the rain. Case dismissed. Retirement, Mr. Horton. Full honors. The branch is yours."

And Horton? Horton scratched the tiny silver back of his brand-new baby with one careful gorilla finger, and headed home, warm and dry at last, with the little one riding his shoulder like a very small admiral.

And the baby's first words, of course — the very first — were these:

"I meant what I ooh-ed, and I ooh-ed what I meant. A marmoset's faithful one hundred percent."

Horton laughed until the trees shook, and bolted the door in the morning anyway, just in case any other lazy macaques came knocking with eggs.
