# A Pocket Full of Aye-Ayes

*A parody of "A Pocket Full of Rye"*

Rex Fruitescue, the howler monkey of Fruitescue Brothers, big in the Banana Exchange and bigger in his own opinion, came down to breakfast at Nuttree Lodge, drank his morning tea, ate his toast and banana marmalade, announced that the coffee tasted of bark, went up to his counting room, and died.

The police surgeon found a quantity of *taxine* — yew poison, distilled from the hedge that blackened the corner of every respectable monkey garden — in the body. In the jacket pocket of the deceased, tucked in the breast pocket where a monkey keeps his treats, was a handful of dry rye seed.

"Rye," said Inspector Neele, a Scotland Yard gorilla of the old granite school, to his sergeant. "We have a poisoned tycoon with birdseed in his pocket. Either we are looking for the world's only homicidal groundskeeper, or somebody is being clever. I dislike clever. Clever always turns out to be literary."

On the day the youngest son came home from the Outer Branches — Lancelot Fruitescue, black sheep, charming as a basket of ripe figs, arriving with his new wife Pat — two more deaths landed neatly in step with the first. Adelle Fruitescue, the young second wife, drank cyanide in her tea before the mantelpiece clock had finished the morning. And Gladys, the parlourmaid — a small, hopeful marmoset who had trained in service at the village of Nana Mead — was found in the garden, strangled, with a clothes-peg clipped neatly on her nose, the washing half hung out around her like bunting.

"Rye in the pocket," said Inspector Neele, standing over her, and for the first time in twenty years he recited poetry at a crime scene. "The king was in his counting house, counting out his money. The queen was in the parlour, eating bread and honey. The maid was in the garden, hanging out the clothes."

"Sing a song of sixpence," said a soft voice behind him, "a pocket full of rye. Somebody, Inspector, is doing a *rhyme*. And when a murderer decorates, it is because he needs the decorations. There was no need for this child to die; she was killed because she knew something. There was every need for the rhyme; it was the plan. Look for a monkey to whom that nursery song is a set of instructions."

The speaker was a small elderly langur with silver-tipped fur, an umbrella, and the mild apologetic air of somebody who has seen everything twice. She was Gladys's old trainer in service — Miss Maple, of Nana Mead — and she had come, she said, because a maid of hers had been murdered with the laundry, and that was a thing one did not let pass.

There were blackbirds in the case already, and it was Miss Maple who made Neele look at them properly. Four and twenty of them — little knitted aye-ayes, eerie wide-eyed knitted figures — had been found arranged on Rex's desk a month before his death, and a pie that should have held fruit had been found baked full of them, and nobody in the house would say who had done it.

"I will tell you who," said Miss Maple, when the threads were finally in her lap. "Once, in the Outer Branches, there was a mine. A prospector named MacNutt found it and brought it home in triumph to Rex Fruitescue, who invested, and surveyed — and then abandoned poor MacNutt in the bush to die, taking the land for nothing. Mrs. MacNutt went half mad with grief and swore her twin infants would be raised to avenge their father: Donald and Ruby MacNutt. Donald died in the Great Troop War. But Ruby, Inspector — Ruby is *in this house*. She married her way in, changed her name, and set out her little knitted aye-ayes to prick Rex's conscience for him. She never killed anyone. She merely gave a murderer his wallpaper."

"Which murderer?"

The household, in the way of households, had assembled itself like a line-up. There was Percival Fruitescue, the elder son, prim as a pressed leaf, who had disapproved of his father for thirty years; his wife Jennifer, who watched everyone with the stillness of a monkey keeping a secret under her fur; Mary Dove, the housekeeper, a silver colobus cool as a shaded branch, who knew everything in the house and priced it; Adelle's friend Vivian Dubois, elegant and expensive; and Lance, the black sheep, home from abroad with his bride, laughing at everything, mourning nothing.

"Consider the aye-aye," said Miss Maple pleasantly, to the drawing room at large. "A humble creature, mostly nocturnal. Nobody suspects it of anything. It taps, it listens, it takes what it finds. We had such an aye-aye in this house — a plain little monkey whom nobody noticed, who came and went through every room, and who was fond of a stranger she called her young monkey. Gladys, our Gladys, had a sweetheart. She spoke of him in her letters home: a serious young monkey named *Albert Gibbons* — who worked, he said, with rare plants, and who had given her a packet of powder for the marmalade. He told her it was a truth-dust. That a great monkey like Rex Fruitescue ought to be made to tell the truth about an old wrong in the Outer Branches. He told her to slip the rye seed in his pocket afterwards, as a *joke*. And she — bless her trusting little heart, she so wanted to matter to somebody — did every single thing he asked."

The room had gone still. Pat Fruitescue, Lancelot's wife, was looking at her husband with her mouth open.

"The taxine was in the new pot of marmalade," Miss Maple went on, gently, inexorably, "which only Rex ate, and which was thrown into the garden after. The cyanide came later, because if Adelle had outlived Rex by a month, the money would have gone to *her*, and not to his sons. And the clothes-peg was afterwards, because the rhyme required a maid. It required a *song*, you see. The whole plan was a nursery rhyme — and the monkey who chose it is the one monkey in this room who never once appeared to be in the song at all. Lance Fruitescue came home from the Outer Branches because there is uranium under the old MacNutt land, and the deed was in his father's counting house. He is Albert Gibbons. He is the aye-aye. And he tapped and listened and took."

Lance moved — charm collapsing off him like a coat off a hook — and found Inspector Neele's granite hand already on his shoulder. Even then he managed a grin at Pat.

"Fancy," he said, "being undone by the maid."

"She mattered," said Miss Maple, with sudden steel, "to me."

Lance Fruitescue was tried that winter and hanged in the spring; and it was noted throughout the jungle that to the very end he could not believe the jury had preferred a dead marmoset's letters to his own smile.

Miss Maple went home to Nana Mead. A delayed letter was waiting for her — posted by Gladys the day before she died, nervous, apologetic, asking her old trainer whether a truth-dust could really do any harm, and enclosing, so that Miss Maple might know the young monkey she meant, a photograph of "Albert Gibbons."

It was unmistakably Lancelot Fruitescue, smiling, with a knitted aye-aye on his shoulder.

Miss Maple looked at it for a long time. Then she put on her gardening gloves and went out and planted sweet peas, far away from the yew hedge.

"One must never," she told them severely, patting the earth down, "underestimate the small ones. The whole jungle is run by small ones. We only let the big ones think otherwise, dear, because it keeps them quiet."
