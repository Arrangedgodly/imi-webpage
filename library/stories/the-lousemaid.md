# The Lousemaid

*A parody of "The Housemaid"*

The smell is what gets me: nit shampoo and lemon polish, and underneath it something rotten, the way a fig can go bad inside a locked vase.

My name is Millie. I'm the lousemaid.

The interview was at the foot of the biggest kapok in the district, a tree with more rooms than the zoo has cages. Nina Lowell looked me over the way you look over a banana you're deciding about.

"You understand the position?" she said. "Delousing, polishing, my daughter Ceci, discretion. You'll live in."

"I can start today."

She did not ask why I needed to. I'd done two years in the Zoo Penitentiary for stealing a banana truck — I only took what the troop owed my brother, but nobody wants a banana-truck thief grooming their children — and after forty-one interviews, I had learned to say nothing at all.

My parole officer, a saggy-eyed baboon named Kola, dropped me off with my one bag. "Somebody finally gave you a chance," he said, tapping my file like it was a drum. "Don't make me regret signing the good parts."

"Spotless," I promised. I meant it both ways.

My room was the top hollow: a round window, a cot, a lamp. One door. One latch. On the outside.

"You'll get used to the latch," Nina said. "We've had break-ins."

Andrew Lowell, the silverback of the house, was all teeth and charm. "Glad to have you, Millie. We're so glad." He said it twice, the way you say a word you've rehearsed.

Nina ran the house off a chalkboard chart. Monday: delouse Ceci. Tuesday: polish the koi rail. Wednesday: the veranda, top to bottom. And one rule that never came off the board, written at the bottom and underlined twice: THE ORCHID BED I DO MYSELF.

I counted the hours, the way I learned in Block C. Eight hours of work. Eight hours of sleep. Anything is survivable for eight hours.

And little Ceci drew: the tree, the veranda, the koi pond, the orchid bed. And over and over, in crayon, she drew the orchid bed with a lady monkey in it. Just the hands and the face, sticking up out of the flowers.

"Who's the lady?" I asked.

"Nobody," said Nina, and took the drawing away, and Ceci went to her room without being told.

Later, alone with me and the delousing comb, Ceci pointed at the crayon lady. "She whistles back sometimes," Ceci said. "At night. When the wind goes through the flowers." I told myself it was the wind. The corner where the orchids bloomed best was the one corner I was never asked to water.

Then one night in the small hours I heard it myself. Whistling, low, from the direction of the garden — a fig-song, the kind you whistle through bars to keep your nerve. The night was dead still, the kapok leaves hanging like ears, and still I told myself: wind through the flowers. That's what I let myself believe, because the alternative had a name.

Andrew's charm grew hands as the weeks went by. A paw at my shoulder when I was on the ladder. "Good girl," he'd say, when a rail shone. "You like it here, Millie? You ever think about the coast?" Nina watched from doorways with her face like a closed fig, and said nothing, which I understand now was the loudest thing she ever said.

Then the vase broke. Nina's mother-in-law's vase, a wedding gift, and I hadn't touched it — I heard it fall from the veranda while I was elbow-deep in the delousing comb — but Nina stood in the wreckage with her paws open and said, "You broke it, Millie. Say you broke it."

"I didn't —"

"Say it."

I said it. That night the latch clicked from the outside while I was brushing my fur, and it did not click again. The window had been painted shut since before I came. It was fourteen hours before the door opened; I had used the planter. Nina looked at my face and said, "You look terrible. The banana bread is burning."

I thought about leaving. I did. Officer Kola came for his monthly check and found me scrubbing the same kettle I'd been scrubbing when he left. "Spotless," he said, pleased, and I hated him a little, for believing me.

But the next night Ceci drew a lady monkey falling — not buried, falling, out of a round window like mine — and I stayed to find out what I was standing inside of.

The answer came on a Thursday, at 3 a.m., while I was sitting up in the dark counting hours.

"You don't remember me at all, do you?" said Nina, from the doorway, perfectly calm.

"Should I?"

"Block C. I was in the cage beside yours for a year. You used to whistle fig-songs through the bars." She came and sat on the end of my cot. "They let me out into this, Millie. Do you know what my husband did to his first wife? The one who 'moved to the coast'?" She looked at the round window. "Ceci draws what she heard through the floor."

"And the vase?" I asked. "You broke it yourself."

"Of course I did," said Nina. "He was tuning you."

"He'll do it again," I said. Not a question.

"He'll do it to you," said Nina. "He's been rehearsing the tragedy of the clumsy lousemaid since the day you walked in with your parole papers. And when he comes for you, Millie — I'll be behind him."

I could tell you I argued. But I had been locked in that hollow for fourteen hours with a painted-shut window, and somewhere under the orchid bed was a lady with her face sticking out of the flowers.

He came three nights later, smiling his small smile, slid the latch, and told me the view was better from the window — come and see. He talked on the way up the stairs, easy as a Sunday: about the coast, and how his first wife had loved it, and how clumsy she'd been, poor thing, near windows.

I went to the window. His paw settled between my shoulders, warm as a stove.

"I brought you a witness, Andrew," said Nina, from the door.

He turned. What happened at that round window I will tell the rangers exactly as I told them then: that he lunged, that Nina stepped aside like a dancer, and that the scream we heard was the last thing that silverback ever did in this tree. The koi were disturbed for days.

Self-defense, they wrote. Two witnesses, they wrote. A tragedy, they wrote.

The kapok passed to Nina outright. The newspaper monkeys came to photograph the round window; I keep it painted open now, whatever the weather.

Officer Kola read about it in the Gazette and came by with a basket of fruit. "You were spotless," he said, baffled, reading my face.

"Nobody's spotless, Officer."

Nina and I groom each other on the veranda now, in the mornings, in the sun. Ceci draws sunshine.

My name is Millie. I'm the lousemaid. And I always know where the lice hide.
