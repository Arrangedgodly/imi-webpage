# Catching Fleas

*A parody of "Catching Fire"*

The Victory Tour should have been the best month of Nanners Everdeen's life. Two apes, one train, all the roasted figs they could eat.

Instead, she stood on a stage in District 11, in front of the families of the tributes she'd killed, and made it worse the only way she knew how: by being honest.

"Rue was my ally," she said, "and she was better than all of us. Every good thing I did in that arena, I did because of her." Then she raised three fingers to her lips and held them to the sky.

The whole square answered. Thousands of apes, silent, saluting. A whistled tune floated up — Rue's four-note song — and the Peacekeeper baboons lost their tempers and the crowd lost its shape, and Nanners was hustled back onto the train.

"That," said Haymuzzle, pouring himself something medicinal, "was the bravest and dumbest thing I've ever seen, and I once watched a chimp argue with a waterfall."

President Gnaw was waiting in her compartment, pale and powdered, smelling of blood-red bananas and disinfectant.

"You defied the Capitol, Miss Everdeen. You and your baker boy and your berries. Every district saw it. Now every district thinks an ape with a bow can say no to me." He stepped close. "Convince them it was love, not defiance. Or I will personally introduce your family to the kind of tree that has no branches."

Home was no safer. The new head Peacekeeper turned the square into a parade ground: curfews, cage punishments, mines shuttered, and when Gale poached a turkey that legally belonged to the Capitol, they whipped him to the post until Nanners threw herself over him, screaming "Enough!" — and a dozen miners quietly picked up stones. That was the month Nanners learned the most dangerous thing you can do in a frightened country is stop being frightened.

So Nanners and Peela posed and cooed and got engaged on live television, and the plan almost worked — right up until an old howler in District 11 whistled Rue's song during their stop, and someone in the crowd shouted "If an ape can burn, an ape can bite," and the tour ended with the whole country quietly going bananas.

At the Capitol's victory ball, a new Head Gamemaker introduced himself — Plutarch Havocbee, round and jolly with fur like a rumor of gold. He danced with Nanners, badly, and showed off his pocket watch. "Doesn't seem like much, does it? But look — it doesn't even tick until midnight." Nanners smiled the way you smile at baboons holding fireworks. It would be a while before she learned to take him seriously.

For her final interview, Cinna-mun built her a wedding gown heavy as a fruit harvest. As she spun for the cameras it burned away — gauze, then feathers, then a full wingspan of mockingmacaque. The audience gasped. The cameras flared. Somewhere above the stage, President Gnaw's teacup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"That," said Cinna-mun calmly, "is the point."

Then, at the Quarter Squeal — the seventy-fifth Hunger Apes, held every twenty-five years to remind the districts whose troop was biggest — Effie Twig-it read the twist with trembling paws: "As a reminder that even the strongest cannot stand against the Capitol, the tributes for this year's games will be selected from the existing pool of victors."

Nanners was the only female victor District 12 had. The arena had eaten its own tail, and she was in its belly.

Back in the jungle dome — launched into saltwater, fished out sixty seconds before the killing started — she made unusual friends. There was Finn-uk Ottertail, the golden-chested tribute from District 4 who wore nothing but a banana-leaf net and a smirk. "Want some sugar?" he asked, offering a cube of pressed fig. "It's the only thing I own that isn't muscle." She put an arrow in his hand that first hour and would spend the rest of the games being glad she'd only grazed him. There was Magpie, his ancient mentor, who could skin a mango at forty feet and communicated mostly in pats. There was Jo-hamna Mason, who had won her games by pretending to be a gibbering gibbon until only the meanest were left, and who introduced herself by insulting Nanners's outfit, her district, and her posture — which, in Jo-hamna's dialect, was affection. And there was Beetee, a spectacled chimp wired to a coil of golden thread, plus his partner Wire-ess, a wispy old langur who had stopped mid-sentence years ago and never quite restarted, and who kept muttering at the trees until one bath-time it assembled itself: "Tick-tock. The jungle's a clock."

It was. The arena was a perfect circle of jungle, divided like a wheel into twelve slices, and every hour something horrible rotated through: blood rain at one, a poisonous mist at two that blistered fur, a flock of orange apes with vendettas at three. When the mist came, Finn-uk carried Magpie until he couldn't, and then Magpie, ancient and unhurried, kissed his cheek and walked back into the white. Nobody said anything. There wasn't a word the right shape.

At four came the worst hour of Nanners's life: jabberapes, birds that mimicked voices, screaming *Primrot! Primrot!* in her sister's voice until she covered her ears and wept, while the Capitol decided whether that counted as entertainment. It did. That was the whole problem with the Capitol.

"Remember who the real enemy is," a dying ally whispered to her, pressing something into her palm. It was a tiny golden mockingmacaque, stamped on a disc, and Nanners finally understood: she had allies everywhere, and they were all playing a longer game than the one on television.

When Haymuzzle's first parachute delivered a spile — a little tap that pulls water straight out of a tree — Nanners understood the message under the message: *the woods are on your side.* By then she had stopped playing to survive and started playing to teach: every arrow aimed at the cameras' heart, every alliance aimed at the Capitol's.

At midnight, the lightning came. It struck the same ancient kapok tree at the arena's center every hour, faithful as hunger. Beetee's plan: run the golden wire from the tree to the saltwater pool, wait for the storm, and fry the arena's heart.

The plan half worked, which for Beetee was a triumph. When the Peacekeeper baboons came for the survivors, Nanners did the only thing a cornered ape with a bow and a live wire could do. She wrapped the wire around one arrow, aimed at the shimmering flaw above the kapok, and shot the sky.

The dome cracked like a dropped coconut. The jungle flickered out.

She woke on a hovercraft floor, and Haymuzzle's grizzled face swam above her. "Easy, sweetheart. We're going to District 13."

"District 13 burned," she rasped.

"It's underground. They've been down there a while, growing bananas under lamplight and sharpening their opinions." He pressed her palm shut around the mockingmacaque pin. "Plutarch Havocbee is Head Gamemaker and one of mine. Half the tributes in that arena were loading the dice for you."

"Including Finn-uk?"

"Especially Finn-uk. He just liked you anyway."

Through the viewport, the ruins of District 12 slid past — ash and broken vines where her home had been. The Capitol had taken everything, and in return she was going to teach them the oldest lesson of the troop: you can knock an ape out of the tree, but you cannot knock the tree out of the ape.
