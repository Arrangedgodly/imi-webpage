# Hard-Peeled Wonderland and the End of the World

*A parody of "Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World"*

## 1. In which I peel for a living

Call me a Bananatec. I'm a primate data processor — I shuffle information inside my skull, which is more secure than any computer, since nobody can hack a monkey who doesn't know, himself, where he's put things. The System licenses me. The Factory would like to un-license me. Think of them as two halves of one rotten coconut.

I'm what they call hard-peeled: nothing gets under my skin, a quality I cultivated in this business and now can't switch off, even off the clock. I drink whiskey, feed the cat, do sit-ups, and keep my fur neatly combed, and I take the jobs and don't ask what's inside them.

Then a dwarf in pink — a candy-pink macaque girl with a mean little pink car — delivered me to her grandfather: a Professor of theoretical botany who had moved his laboratory, lock, stock, and centrifuge, into an abandoned cavern beneath the city sewers.

"Why the sewers?" I asked.

"INKlings," said the Professor, matter-of-factly. "Infra-Nocturnal Kappas. Small, wet, implacable. They infest the dark down here and worship the sound of dripping. My colleagues above ground are now semioticians of the worst sort. Down here, I have only the INKlings to fear, and one can negotiate with religion."

He offered me unlimited coffee, an unrepeatable salary, and a piece of benign-sounding work: shuffling an experimental algorithm he called the Fairy-Tale Protocol.

"Any side effects?" I asked.

"None that you'll notice," he said, "in the sense that you'll have no way of noticing them."

I should have peeled out of there. Instead I shuffled. And somewhere between the third and fourth pass, my brain — this is the only way I can put it — changed hands.

## 2. The Town

I arrived in the Town on a day of enormous quiet, leading a golden beast with one horn by a soft rope halter.

The Town stands inside a wall of living wood, so old and so tall that from the middle of the main street it seems less like a place than like the inside of a thought. There is a bell tower, a gate, a river, a library, and the beasts: unicorns, gold-horned and gentle, who graze the lawns in slow silence and whose breath in winter steams like little kettles of the soul.

At the gate, the Gatekeeper — a barrel-chested baboon with a ring of keys and no small talk — took my Shadow from me.

This is the custom. My Shadow, a gray, weary, and sarcastic copy of myself, was cut loose with a sound like a stitch giving way, and assigned to him: my Shadow now sweeps bones at the foot of the wall and lives in a lean-to, and at dusk I may visit and speak with it, but only a little each day, and never of the past. In the Town, the past is not spoken of. This is not a rule; it is more like a climate.

"What did you do with my mind?" I asked the Gatekeeper.

"You'll get along better without it," he said. "Everyone does. Mind the bell."

I was given a bed in the library and a job. In the Town's library there are no new dreams; there are only old ones, stored in the skulls of dead unicorns, and my work is to read them. The Librarian — a langur with still, dark, gold-flecked eyes — sets a skull on the table before me each afternoon, and I lay my fingertips in the cool hollows where a mind used to be, and I wait. After a while the skull warms, faintly, and I read: old dreams, patient and sweet and unspeakably sad, the dreams of beasts that spent whole golden lives grazing inside a wall.

I do not know what I was before I came. My hands know the skulls, though. My hands seem to have done this forever.

## 3. In which the peeling gets deep

Back in the sewer — my other life, or my same life, wearing a trench coat — the Professor confessed.

The Fairy-Tale Protocol wasn't an algorithm; it was a world. He had encoded an entire other reality into a partition of my brain, and the shuffle had brought it online. My hard-boiled skull now ran two operating systems, and the second one was warm, walled, and full of unicorns.

"The INKlings are getting aggressive," the dwarf in pink told me, driving me back down the tunnel at speed, scattering small wet worshippers of drip-sound from the headlights. "Grandpa says you have about two days before the switch becomes permanent."

"Permanent how?"

"Pick a world, gorilla. Day after tomorrow, one of you goes dark."

I did what any professional does with an impossible deadline: I demanded overtime pay, was refused, drank the Professor's coffee anyway, and climbed out of the sewers one last time to look at the city — neon, rain, noodle steam, ten million strangers, the whole gorgeous peel of the world — with the eyes of a monkey leaving a party early.

## 4. The winter of the beasts

That winter in the Town, the cold came down like a verdict. The river froze bank to bank. The beasts grew thin and grave, and one by one, some of them lay down in the snow, and the Gatekeeper gathered their horns and stacked them against the wall, and the skulls went to the library, and I read them: their old dreams, which had been, every one, about grass, and light, and the sound of the bell.

My Shadow, sweeping bones, made its proposal. There is a way out — over the wall, through the South Woods, where a corner of the world leaks a little warmth; the Shadow had mapped it for years, in its spare bitter hours.

"We go together," my Shadow said. "I can't climb without you. You can't leave without me. You do get that, right? A monkey without a shadow isn't free. He's just unaccompanied."

We stood at the top of the wall on the last night of winter. Below us, on the far side, the leaking warmth made a hollow in the snow: a little green place, moss and ferns, a spring that never froze — the only wilderness inside the wall, and the one place the Town had never named.

And I understood that I had a choice, and that it was not between worlds; it was between being a monkey with a mind, and a keeper of dreams.

"Go," I told my Shadow. "Over the wall, into the world with the minds and the weather and the losing. Live there. Get rained on. Fall in love with something temporary. That's your job now."

"And yours?"

I looked back at the Town: the bell tower, the frozen river, the beasts breathing their small kettle-steam into the dark, the library with its patient shelves of skulls, every one holding somebody's old dream of grass.

"Somebody has to stay and read," I said.

## 5. Last entry

The Factory finally caught up with my body, or the Professor's coffee ran out, or the deadline kept its deadline; from in here there is no telling, and, more to the point, no more in here from which to tell.

I peeled my last banana this morning. Slowly. There was no rush; there is nothing else; the peel came away in one long spiral, which even the hardest-boiled monkey has to admit is satisfying.

This afternoon the Librarian will bring me a new skull. I'll set it on the table, lay my fingers in the hollows, and wait for the small warmth, like a sun inside an egg, and whatever dream of grass it kept, I'll keep it now too. The bell will ring at dusk. The beasts will stand in the falling snow like candle flames that refuse, out of politeness, to go out.

The world out there — the neon, the whiskey, the sit-ups, the pink dwarf, the whole racket — I picture it sometimes, the way you picture a party you left early: still going, probably. Fine music. Nobody missed me; I was, professionally speaking, unpeelable.

I miss my Shadow, in a mild, well-shaded way.

But in the Town the light does something, at a certain hour, through a certain haze, that the hard-boiled world never once managed in all my years of shuffling: it makes everything, for exactly one minute, impossible to doubt.

The end of the world, it turns out, is not a bang or a whimper. It's a pace. You slow to it, one afternoon, and then you keep it.
