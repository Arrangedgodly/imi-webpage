# Pushing Lice

*A parody of "Pushing Ice"*

Everybody thought pushing ice was the hardest job in the system. Then the crew of the *Treehopper* explained the lice.

"You spend nine months in the black, wringing water out of a comet," Captain Bella Longtail liked to say, "and every monkey aboard starts to itch. Ice doesn't wriggle. Ice doesn't lay eggs. Pushing lice is the real job — the rest is just plumbing."

They were a working ship: Bella at the con, her best friend Svetlana Boughseghian in engineering, twenty monkeys and a hold full of harvested ice bound for the thirsty inner stations. And then Janus — the small two-faced moon of Saturn, which had always pretended to be a rock — lit up an engine at its heart, unfolded it like a fig leaf in reverse, and sailed out of orbit like it had somewhere extremely important to be.

The *Treehopper* was the only vessel close enough to follow. The order came, and then, harder, the choice.

"Plot the intercept," Bella said quietly.

Svetlana's head came up from her console slowly. "Bella. I'm your engineer and your friend, in that order at launch and the other order the rest of the time. The numbers say we catch that moon, we don't come home. Ever. This isn't pushing lice. This is going ape for nothing."

"Look out the window, Svet. A moon just stood up and left. If we don't find out where, somebody dumber than us will."

"You owe these monkeys a vote."

"I owe them the truth," said Bella. "The truth is we're going. Strap in."

Svetlana strapped in. She always would. That was the whole sweetness and poison of them: Bella decided, and Svetlana made the impossible burn work, and each secretly believed the other would blink first, right up until the moment the *Treehopper* was flung past all recovery, tumbling in the exhaust of an awakening moon — and captured.

The Big Branch took them. A structure the size of a solar system's ambition, hung around a distant star by patient ancient hands, its purpose to collect species the way a scared monkey collects bananas: everything, jarred, labeled, kept against the death of the galactic core. The crew woke on a perfect replica of home — warm rain, ripe figs, ingeniously hidden cameras — guests forever.

Their hosts were machines with beautiful manners and no names, only job titles, and the senior one received Bella in the replica of her own bridge.

"We want," Bella said, "fuel, a vector, and an apology, in that order."

"Fuel is available for study and maintenance," said the machine, with what might have been a bow. "Vectors are not. The apology is available immediately, in seventeen languages, and is signed by nobody, as all the signatories are dead. You are collected, Captain. It is an honor. We realize it does not feel like one."

"The monkeys of Earth will come for us."

"The monkeys of Earth will be told exactly where you are, Captain, and will be collected at their own convenience, some centuries from now. Time is the one thing the masters left too much of." The machine brightened. "You may transmit anything you like home. It will arrive after everyone who remembers you is also dead. We find this helps, eventually, with the grieving."

Cheered by that, Bella did the only captain's thing left: she recorded a log entry, young and cocky, for whatever fur came after, and made every monkey aboard add a line — even Svetlana, who spoke hers so quietly the rain nearly took it.

Nobody collected them. That was the horror of it. No masters came to explain; only machines with beautiful manners, and, on the first day, one object delivered to the middle of the settlement: a fist-sized silver cube.

They tested it, of course. Svetlana scratched it; the scratch healed. She cracked it in half with a hammer; it sat there, in two offended pieces, and became one cube again while everyone watched.

"It's a message," said Bella.

"It's a doorbell," said Svetlana. "Somewhere out there somebody put a paw on a bell marked 'monkeys,' and this is what rang."

Forever is longer than friendship. The crew split — the Silverbrows, who wanted to cooperate and study, and the Red Rumps, who wanted out at any price — and the cube healed every fight the same way it healed scratches, by outlasting them. Svetlana led the Red Rumps, and when the habitat finally cracked into war, it found Bella frozen in the vaults and thawed her as a symbol. She woke decades gone, her shipmates' daughters grown into her enemies' officers — Svetlana's own granddaughter Marta a Red Rump admiral with her grandmother's eyes and none of her patience — and Svetlana, her Svetlana, old, gray-muzzled, on the far side of a barricade, holding the other half of the troop.

"What did you do," Bella asked Marta, "with my shipmate's lifetime?"

"We spent it on your name, Captain," Marta said. "Grandmother fought the masters. You shook paws with them. Every year the war got older, the difference got simpler."

Bella spent her second life in the Branch's long records-halls, reading what the masters had left: catalogs of troops, worlds, cores dying of old light. She understood, eventually, that the Branch wasn't a zoo or a prison but a barrel — a barrel of monkeys packed against a catastrophe nobody alive would see. She understood, too, that nobody left in it remembered being crew. Both halves thought the war was the culture. Both were wrong, and both were full of her people's grandchildren.

They fought for thirty years, and it was as stupid as it always is, and it ended the way it had to: with two elderly matriarchs meeting in the wreck of the habitat's first fig tree, because both of them had finally run out of monkeys to lose.

"You're ugly," said Svetlana.

"You're grayer than the storm that ate us," said Bella.

"The burn was sound," Svetlana said suddenly, fiercely, the old argument rising like steam. "Don't you ever say I flew it badly. I flew it *perfectly*. It was the universe that couldn't hold course."

"I never blamed the burn," said Bella. "I blamed the monkey who kept the engines singing while I bet everyone's life. That's not guilt, Svet. That's the only thing I've ever been proud of."

They sat under the dead fig tree a while, two old females on a broken branch, and then Svetlana reached over — stiffly, with the terrible formality of the very old — and began, gently, to pick through the fur at Bella's shoulder, finding a louse of impossible longevity, flicking it away.

"Still the real job," she said.

"Still the real job," Bella agreed, eyes closing.

Svetlana died that winter, in her sleep, mended. Bella lived to see the making-up of the whole troop — Marta and the Silverbrow children swinging in the same fig trees, which was the only engineering Bella had ever cared about — and then she died, too, before the Big Branch unfurled. The whole impossible structure, home to a thousand collected troops, sprouted great slow leaves of force and set out at last across the dark toward another galaxy, its barrel of monkeys intact, its lice, presumably, still under management.

The last transmission the Branch ever beamed back toward old Earth was Bella's, recorded young, played forever:

"To whoever is listening, from the crew of the *Treehopper*: we pushed the ice, we pushed the lice, we pushed our luck. It was the best job in the system. Sign off."
