# State of Fur

*A parody of "State of Fear"*

Peter Evans was a junior chimp at the law firm of Hassle & Black, and his most important client was George Morton, the billionaire orangutan philanthropist whose money kept the lights on at MERF — the Molt Environmental Resource Fund — whose president, Nicholas Drake, was a howler monkey who could howl for funding like no primate alive.

The cause was the Great Molt. Every screen in the world said so: the monkeys of Earth were losing their fur, the thermometers confirmed it, the models agreed, and only MERF stood between the tropics and total baldness. A treaty conference was coming — the Bonn Protocol on Shedding — and Drake howled that the sky itself was falling out, strand by strand.

Which was why Peter Evans was surfing in Malibu when a helicopter he did not know was his client's circled low, and why, one night later, George Morton's car went off a canyon road and burned, and why at the funeral, standing in the rain with Morton's brilliant assistant Sarah Tails, Peter found himself seized by a mandrill in a raincoat who said: "Do not go home. Tell no one. Your name is on a list, and the list is not a mailing list."

The mandrill was Professor John Kenner of MIT — Molecular Institute of Troopology — and his companion was Sanjong, a Himalayan langur with perfect pitch and a suitcase of instruments.

"George Morton is not dead," Kenner said, once they'd verified Peter's fur for bugs the literal kind. "He staged it. Because he audited MERF's books and found something worse than fraud. He found a *production schedule*."

"You mean," said Peter, "the eco-terrorists."

"I mean the monkeys who keep the troop in a state of fur," said Kenner. "Not the state of having it. The state of fearing for it. Fear is the cheapest fuel ever discovered, Mr. Evans, and Drake's entire machine runs on it. The Great Molt may be real, or partly real, or not. But the *disasters* — the ones on the news, the ones that make the donations spike — somebody has been manufacturing them. And in the next four weeks, somebody intends to manufacture four more."

The first was in the snow country. The famous hot-spring macaques of the mountains — the ones photographed to death sitting in steam up to their chins — were calving an ice shelf by resonance: charges laid at the fault line, timed to a chord, to drop a serac the size of a stadium into a valley on camera, during the closing session of a MERF benefit. Kenner and Sanjong found the charges by seismograph and gummed the detonation sequence a half-second late; the avalanche collapsed *away* from the cameras, harmlessly, and the benefit raised almost nothing. Drake howled about "the tragedy that almost was." Nobody filmed the almost.

Peter lost two toes to that cold, and gained, in exchange, the habit of asking for the data.

The second was in the South Pacific: a village of island macaques in the path of a "rogue storm surge" that was no storm at all but a surge-generating rig — sonic pumps anchored offshore, tuned to ring the sea like a drumhead, timed to arrive while an insurance convention watched from the headland. Sarah Tails swam the reef in the dark to cut the power leads with a dive knife, and the sea arrived at the village as an ordinary high tide, which the villagers slept through, which was the entire point.

"Understand," Kenner said, wringing out his fur, "the surge was real. The village would have drowned. The *science* is not what I am attacking — the science is the only thing worth defending. I am attacking the show. Every year the show must be bigger, and every year the troop must be made more afraid, because fear, Mr. Evans, is the one subscription that renews itself."

The third was static. In the high desert of New Mexico, at a mesa where eco-tourist monkeys gathered to watch lightning, the conspirators had seeded the air with conductive chaff and rigged wire launchers to *draw* the strikes to the viewing platform — one spectacular death by lightning, on film, attributed to the angry fur of the planet. Sanjong's perfect pitch heard the launchers' whine, Peter pulled the ground stake, and the bolt took the rig instead, cooking the conspiracy's truck and every server in it. Which is how Peter Evans, attorney-at-law, came to be in lawful possession of the thing the conspirators wanted back: the production schedule for number four.

Number four was the big one. A conference on the Bonn Protocol — vice-ministers, celebrities, documentary crews, Drake himself — gathered on a coast, and offshore, on the sea floor, at the edge of the trench, the conspirators had spent a year building the finale: shaped charges that would drop a shelf of seabed and send a wall of water at that coast to arrive *during Drake's keynote*. The Great Molt, demonstrating itself. A catastrophe with catering.

They went down in a submersible to stop it, because of course they did; the schedule said the charges fired at nine-forty, and Drake went on at nine.

The trench was black, and the rig was enormous — a lattice of cables and shaped charges laid across the seabed like a harp stringed by maniacs. While Sarah and Sanjong cut detonators, the conspirators' own sub came out of the dark and rammed, and it was George Morton — alive, singed, and furious — whose battered deep-diver slammed it broadside and pinned it against the mud until its motors screamed and quit, while Kenner pulled the master initiator with fourteen minutes to spare.

The wave that reached the coast that morning was two feet high, and the young monkeys surfed it.

What happened after was quieter, and worse, in the way true things are worse than scary things. The schedule went public. The rig was raised. Half a dozen MERF-adjacent engineers traded testimony for immunity. Drake himself was never charged with anything — he had, his lawyers said, been "deceived by rogue contractors," which is what every howler says when the pack changes direction — but the donations fell by ninety percent in one season, and the Bonn Protocol was renegotiated in a smaller room, by calmer monkeys, with footnotes.

Peter Evans, two toes lighter and one law degree heavier, quit Hassle & Black and sat with Kenner on the porch of the MIT field station, watching the sunset do unspeakable things to the sky for free.

"So is the Great Molt real?" he asked at last. "The whole thing — a hoax?"

"The measurements are real," said Kenner. "The fur is thinner in some places, thicker in others, and the troop has a real problem, several real problems, all of them solvable by any monkey willing to read past the headline. That is precisely why the fear-mongers make me so angry. They take a genuine question and bury it in a lie, so that when the lie collapses, it drags the question down with it. Politicized fear, Mr. Evans, is not the opposite of neglect. It *is* neglect, wearing a very expensive ribbon."

Sarah Tails came out with lemonade. Down the slope, the field station's troop of grad-student gibbons had shed their winter coats all over the lawn, and were happily stuffing the shed fur into nest-pillows, the way monkeys have for two million years.

"Look at that," said Kenner, nodding at the fur-clouds drifting off the lawn. "Actual molting. Nobody's panicking."

"Because nobody's filming it," said Peter.

"There it is," said the mandrill. "That's the whole thesis. Now stop being my lawyer and drink your lemonade."
