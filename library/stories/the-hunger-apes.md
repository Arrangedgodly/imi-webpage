# The Hunger Apes

*A parody of "The Hunger Games"*

The reaping came to District 12 the way it always did: with too much ceremony and not enough bananas.

Nanners Everdeen stood in the square with her little sister, Primrot, both of them thin as vine-strips. Around them, the miners of the Seam scratched under their collars while Peacekeeper baboons in white armor paced the perimeter, looking bored enough to start grooming each other.

"Welcome, welcome to the annual Hunger Apes!" trilled Effie Twig-it, a purple-faced macaque in a suit the color of an overripe mango. "Happy Hunger Apes! And may the figs be ever in your favor!"

The crowd did not cheer. The crowd had never cheered, on account of being hungry and also apes.

Effie dipped a hand into the glass bowl and pulled a slip of bark-paper. "Primrot Everdeen!"

"No!" Nanners shoved through the crowd before her sister could even bristle. She flung up both arms and shrieked the words that would ruin and save her life: "I volunteer! I volunteer as tribute-ape!"

"Of course you do, sweetheart," said Effie, delighted. Nanners hugged her sister once, hard, and whispered, "Get out. Get out before they make it worse."

The male tribute was a baker's son named Peela, a stocky chimp with flour still dusting his shoulders and a talent for looking like a banana had feelings. Nanners knew one thing about him: years ago, in a rainstorm, this boy had "accidentally" burned a loaf and thrown it to a starving girl in the pigpen. She had never thanked him. She'd been too busy surviving, which is the most ape reason there is.

In the Capitol, they were waxed, plucked, and perfumed until they smelled like a fruit basket in love. Their mentor, Haymuzzle, a grizzled old red-bottom who had survived the games by being too drunk to find, belched at their first dinner together.

"Listen, you two. Stay alive," he said.

"That's the whole strategy?" said Nanners.

"It worked for me," said Haymuzzle, and drank his soup through his mustache.

Their stylist was a golden lion tamarin named Cinna-mun, who believed fashion was just aggression with better lighting. "I could dress you as bananas," he said, pinning them into plain black. "Everyone dresses District 12 as bananas. But coal catches." He touched a taper to their capes, the cloth bloomed with pretend flame, and when their chariot rolled into the City Circle, the Capitol screamed itself hoarse for the two tribute apes who appeared to be on fire and loving it.

"The apes on fire," breathed Peela at the replays.

"The marshmallows," muttered Haymuzzle, who was eating again.

In training, Nanners played it sly: hopeless at knots, worse at spears, exactly dangerous enough to stay interesting. Haymuzzle's whole strategy was "Make them like you, then make them fear you. In that order." Peela's strategy was lifting heavy sacks and befriending the murderous careers, which in the Capitol was so exotic it might as well have been witchcraft.

At her private session, the gamemakers ignored Nanners in favor of a roast pig. So she put one arrow through the fig in its mouth, pinning fruit to wall, and stalked out without bowing. They gave her an eleven — the highest score of the year, which in practical terms meant "kill this one first."

Then came the interviews, and Caesar Fleckermonkey, whose teeth were whiter than prejudice. Nanners twirled in a gown of manufactured fire and charmed the nation by accident. Peela charmed it on purpose, which was worse, because what he said was: "She has no idea. Completely oblivious." A pause. A smile like rising dough. "I'm in love with Nanners Everdeen."

The Capitol melted. Haymuzzle, watching from the wings with the first sober expression of the fortnight, whispered, "Clever baker."

The arena was a jungle under a dome. Sixty seconds on the plates. Nanners's eyes went to the golden horn at the center of the clearing, the Cornucopia — the Horn of Plentiful Bites — heaped with mangoes, machetes, and one beautiful bow.

Gong.

She didn't run for it — district apes climb like gossip travels, instantly and without mercy — and that kept her alive. She went up a kapok tree and watched the careers — the well-fed, vicious, glossy-furred volunteers from Districts 1 and 2 — make their kills below, with Peela among them, playing ally to stay alive.

Rue found her in the branches, a tiny bushbaby with enormous eyes. "You have a mockingmacaque pin," Rue whispered, pointing at the little golden bird on Nanners's shirt. "It sings, you know. When you're in trouble."

They became allies. Of course they did. They were both small, both quick, and both excellent at being places where large angry apes were not. Together they plotted the destruction of the careers' food hoard, and when Nanners saw the nest of snacker-jackers — furious wasps guarding a branch of honey-bananas — she knew exactly what to do.

"Saw the branch," she told Rue. "On three. One, two—"

The nest fell. The stings came. The careers went absolutely bananas, swatting and howling, and their hoard went up in the fire Nanners had set beneath it. Nanners took three stings herself, and the venom repainted the jungle in colors that don't exist while the trees whispered in her mother's voice. She slept it off in a hollow high on the trunk and woke up meaner.

But Rue was caught in a net. Nanners got there too late. A spear found the little bushbaby just as Nanners's arrow found the spear-thrower.

"Did you blow up the food?" Rue whispered.

"Yes."

"You have to win."

"I will." Nanners held her as the light went out of those enormous eyes, and then — because the Capitol was watching — she circled Rue's small body with flowers and raised three fingers to her lips, then to the sky. A salute. The district sign, sent to all of Panan.

The cameras caught it. The Capitol went ape. And District 11, watching its dead daughter covered in flowers by the enemy who avenged her, went quieter than quiet — which, historically, is the loud sound.

When the gamemakers announced that two tributes from the same district could win, Nanners found Peela at the riverbank, camouflaged so perfectly in the mud he looked like a very handsome log, with a gash in his leg ripening from bad to worse. She nursed him in a cave — fever, fig paste, and the terrible chemistry of gratitude.

"You shouldn't have come for me," Peela said. "I'm dead weight."

"I've carried heavier figs," said Nanners. "You've seen my figs."

They finished the games as a team, playing the star-crossed lovers of District 12 until only one career was left: Cato, the glossy-furred killer of District 2, huge, furious, and haunted by absolutely nothing — which was the tragedy the Capitol had ordered and paid for.

The gamemakers, bored by three survivors, opened the plain and drove them to the Horn of Plentiful Bites, and on its golden rim they set the muttations: mutt-mandrills, wolf-sized and bristling, wearing — horribly — the eyes of the dead tributes. The Capitol had recycled. The Capitol applauded.

Cato fought everything at once, monkey, mutt, and metaphor, because violence was the only trick the games ever taught him. When he took Peela in a headlock at the horn's mouth — "One move and I snap his neck!" — Nanners put an arrow through his hand, and the mutts dragged him down. His howls went on far too long, because the gamemakers wanted a finale, until at last his voice found her in the dark, small as a district ape's: "Do it."

She had one arrow left. She used it kindly.

Then the rules changed back.

"Go on," said the voice of President Gnaw, cold as a zoo in winter. "One of you. We need our victor."

Nanners looked at Peela. Peela looked at Nanners. And Nanners, who understood exactly what game was really being played, pulled out the small pouch of nightfig berries.

"We'll both win," she said loudly, "or nobody does."

The berries hovered at their lips. The country held its breath.

"Stop! Stop!" the voice bellowed. "Both of you. Victors. Congratulations."

Nanners spat the berries into her palm and smiled for the cameras, sweet as ripe fruit, with a war brewing behind her eyes. Haymuzzle had told her the most important thing, the only thing: the Capitol may own the arena, but nobody owns an ape who refuses to play.
