# The Lion and the Louse

*A parody of "The Lion and the Mouse"*

In the tallest fig tree of the Banyan Belt, where the fruit hung fat and the troop gossip hung fatter, there ruled a lion-tailed macaque king named Leopold. He had a silver mane like a movie star and a temper like a thunderclap. When King Leopold napped across the widest branch, the whole canopy went quiet, because waking him was widely considered a lousy way to spend the afternoon.

One drowsy Tuesday, a tickle disturbed the royal mane.

"WHO," thundered Leopold, shooting upright, "DARES GRAZE THE ROYAL FUR?"

He plucked the trespasser out between two claws and found himself nose-to-nose with a louse. A very small louse. A louse so small that her full title — Gwendolyn the Groomer, Third Class of the Great Nit-Picking Guild — was considerably longer than she was.

"Mercy, Your Mane-jesty," squeaked Gwendolyn, dusting herself off with great dignity. "I was only tidying. Flakes, you know. Dreadful this season. I'm a professional." She produced, from somewhere, a calling card so small the king had to squint. It read, in letters visible only to other lice: *G. Groomer. No flake too far.*

"A professional?" Leopold laughed so hard the branch bounced and two passing parrots fell out of formation. "You? You're smaller than a flea's eyebrow! What could a speck like you ever do for the king of the canopy?"

"Small helpers," said Gwendolyn primly, "have rescued great kings before. Perhaps one day I shall return the favor."

"In your dreams, crumb."

"In my dreams," said Gwendolyn, "everyone is exactly my size, and it is glorious."

Leopold went positively ape with laughter, but he had eaten three figs and a perfect 'nana that morning and was feeling grand. "Off you scamper then, little groomer. Today the royal mercy is as big as the royal appetite."

Word raced through the troop faster than a dropped coconut: the king had gone soft on vermin. Leopold pretended not to hear. Kings are excellent at that.

But Gwendolyn, who had a professional's pride, did not scamper off entirely. Over the following weeks she made herself quietly useful around the troop — unknotting a baby macaque's tail from a strangler vine, clearing the ear of the eldest grandmother so she could hear her grandchildren's nonsense again, cataloguing the flake output of every shoulder in the Banyan Belt (six volumes, self-published). Nobody thanked her. She was a louse; lice bill by the job and are thanked never. She kept the ledger anyway, because that is what professionals do.

Then came the Terrible Snare.

Rumors arrived at dusk, carried by a breathless barbetail: humans had strung nets beneath the mango grove, right beside the troop's winter fig stash — soft-eyed nets, clever knots, anchored to stakes that did not care about royalty. The Council of Elders convened at once and ruled, sensibly, that nobody go near the grove until the nets had been studied.

Leopold, being magnificently brave and even more magnificently vain, heard the ruling as applause. The king, personally, alone, would shred the nets and appear at dawn trailing one like a victory cape. He told no one his plan, on the grounds that surprises are royal.

It did not go as planned.

By moonrise he was trussed in the net like a burrito, one arm wrapped in cord, his mane full of rope, his royal dignity nowhere to be found. He pulled. The net tightened. He gnawed. The cord tasted terrible. Somewhere below, he could hear the barbetail saying "I told him" in the tone monkeys reserve for funerals.

"HELP!" he bellowed. "HELP! THE KING IS STUCK! THIS IS NOT A DRILL!"

The troop came, of course, and went bananas trying to free him. The macaques tugged until their tails ached. The big baboons gnawed until their jaws clicked. Somebody suggested vine-oil; somebody else suggested prayer. The net held. The elders retired to a branch to whisper about who would be king by morning — and the front-runner, a smug mangabey named Rotch, had already begun practicing his royal wave.

And then, out of the moonlight, up the net's anchor rope, climbed a very small figure with a very large toolbox.

"Gwendolyn!" gasped Leopold. "You came?"

"A professional," said the louse, "always finishes the job. Also, you still owe me for the Tuesday tidy." She turned to the night and lifted a tiny leg. "GUILD! TO ME!"

Up the net swarmed the entire Great Nit-Picking Guild — ten thousand lice in marching order, mandibles gleaming like moonlit rice. At their head marched the Knot Battalion, who lived for a tangle like this; behind them the Flank Corps, the Cord Cutters Third Class, and the Guild Band, who were there for morale and mostly got in the way.

"Hats off to Her Smallness," announced Gwendolyn, and the ten thousand saluted, which sounded like ten thousand tiny clicks, which sounded like rice pouring, which made Rotch the mangabey fall out of the tree.

"Strand by strand!" commanded Gwendolyn. "From the bottom up! And mind the royal elbow — it's had a hard night. Cord Cutters — you have your orders. Band — the quiet one, PLEASE."

They chewed. They nibbled. They unpicked knot after knot with the patience of ten thousand tiny tailors, and where ten thousand tiny tailors work, a marvel happens: the cords began not to be cut, but to be *combed* — straightened, untangled, laid strand from strand, the way Gwendolyn combed a mane, because it was the only art the Guild knew and they applied it to everything.

And Leopold, king of the canopy, lay very still — stiller than he had ever lay in his life — and let the smallest subjects in the jungle work, because for the first time in his magnificent life he had seen the size of a favor being repaid, and it humbled him, and it tickled, and he did not dare laugh at either.

Just before dawn, the last strand parted. The net fell away like a shed skin, and Leopold rose, shook out his silver mane, and swung free into his own fig tree while the troop roared itself hoarse. Rotch the mangabey retired from politics that same morning, citing the lice.

"Three cheers for the king!" cried the macaques.

"Hold the cheers," rumbled Leopold. "Three cheers for the Guild." And he bowed — a full, deep, mane-sweeping bow — to a louse the size of a sesame seed, which in monkey law makes you either a saint or a fool, and Leopold decided he could live with either.

"You see, Your Mane-jesty," said Gwendolyn, tipping her hat, "you laughed at me once."

"And I'm not laughing now," said the king. "Well — I am. But respectfully."

There were consequences, and they were grand. Leopold appointed Gwendolyn Royal Groomer First Class, the first louse ever to hold court office, and her calling card had to be reprinted twice to fit the new title. When the humans returned at sunrise to check their snare, they found a net combed into neat parallel threads and coiled, with unsettling tidiness, beside the stake; they packed up and never came back, because there are fates a trapper's nerve cannot survive. And the Guild, for its part, dined out on the story for a generation — ten thousand heroes, one night, one burrito.

From that day on, the first groomers invited to the royal mane were always the smallest, and the Great Nit-Picking Guild never once paid for a fig in the Banyan Belt again.

The moral, carved into the bark of the tallest fig tree where every young monkey can read it:

**No act of grooming is too small, and no groomer too little — for even the mightiest mane may one day be untangled by the tiniest teeth.**
