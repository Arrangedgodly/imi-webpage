# The Gingerbread Mandrill

*A parody of "The Gingerbread Man"*

Once upon a time, at the edge of the fig forest, there lived a granny macaque with a sweet tooth and terrible luck with ovens. One morning she scraped together her last spoonful of ginger, her last smear of honey, and her final three currants, and patted the dough into the shape of a mandrill — because every monkey knows a mandrill has the gaudiest face in the jungle, and she meant for her baking to be bold. She gave him currant eyes, a candied-mango nose, and proud swirls of gold-and-blue icing. She stood back and admired him, and gave the dough a little pep talk, the way grandmothers do.

"Rise, my pretty," she told him. "Rise and be gorgeous. But STAY ON THE STONE."

Then she slid him onto the hot stone and turned away to fetch her fan of banana leaves.

That was her first mistake.

"OW!" said the Gingerbread Mandrill, sitting bolt upright. "That stone is HOT! And I am FULLY BAKED — I have decided this myself!"

He leapt off the stone and away down the jungle path, pumping his little doughy legs, while the granny macaque came flapping after him with her apron flying.

"Come back, you half-baked scoundrel!"

"Run, run, as fast as you can!" called the Gingerbread Mandrill, which is a very saucy thing for a biscuit to say. "You can't catch me — I'm the Gingerbread Mandrill!"

He ran until he met the granny's husband, an old baboon with a bamboo cane. "No biscuit of mine shall run wild in this jungle!" the baboon huffed, swinging his cane. The mandrill skipped neatly under it, twirled, and delivered his line with extra icing.

"Run, run, as fast as you can! You can't catch me — I'm the Gingerbread Mandrill!"

"MY biscuit," wheezed the old baboon, to nobody. "I never even got a sniff."

Down the path he went, past a whole troop of macaques, who promptly went bananas at the smell of him. "It's a cookie!" they shrieked. "A WALKING cookie!" They poured out of the branches, hooting and tumbling over one another like a barrel of monkeys tipped downhill, but the mandrill zigged and zagged and left them in a heap, and his taunt drifted back over them like a aroma of pure cheek.

Then came a peccary, trotting out of the undergrowth with her piglets behind her. "Stop, you little loaf! You'd be lovely with jam!" But the Gingerbread Mandrill ran between her legs, under the piglets, and out the other side of the whole family before they had finished snorting.

"Run, run, as fast as you can! Nobody's catching the Gingerbread Mandrill!"

At the river he met a crocodile, grinning like a log with opinions. "Come closer, little pastry," the crocodile rumbled. "I only want to admire your icing."

"Admire it from there," said the mandrill. "Run, run, as fast as you can! You can't catch me — I'm the Gingerbread Mandrill!" The crocodile's jaws snapped shut on nothing but air, which is very bad for a crocodile's confidence.

A python in the shallows smacked her lips. "Ssssuppertime. You look sssweet."

"I look delicious," the mandrill corrected, "which is exactly why I'm in a hurry." And away he went, while the python muttered that he was too full of himself — and everyone knows what happens to baked goods too big for their breadbox.

A toucan tried an aerial grab next, swooping low with his great beak open like a shopping basket. The Gingerbread Mandrill stopped dead, and the toucan — a bird built like a seesaw — plowed beak-first into the mud, stood on his own head, and stayed there, thinking about what he'd done.

"Run, run, as fast as you can!" sang the mandrill, doing a little icing-flex as he ran. "The air force can't catch the Gingerbread Mandrill!"

By now the whole jungle was involved, and the Gingerbread Mandrill had begun to believe his own press. Two coatis chased him with a pinch of salt and a pinch of pepper, calling out cooking suggestions. A spider offered to wrap him to go. Word ran ahead of him through the trees, as word does, until every creature on the path knew the phrase and some of the younger ones chanted it WITH him, which is never a good sign.

At last, breathless and crumbly, the Gingerbread Mandrill reached the wide brown river, and behind him came the entire parade: granny, grandpap, the macaque troop, one peccary family, a sulking crocodile, a humbled toucan, and two coatis still shaking their seasonings. The mandrill's icing began to sag. For the first time, he considered that his plan might be only half-baked.

There on the bank sat a lean red fox, washing his paws and looking supremely uninterested.

"Whatever is the matter?" asked the fox.

"The whole jungle is after me!" wailed the mandrill, "and I cannot swim!"

"Tsk," said the fox. "The state of this jungle, chasing a poor biscuit. Jump onto my tail, and I'll ferry you across." So the mandrill hopped onto the fox's tail, and the fox set out into the river, while the macaques screeched to a halt, going ape with frustration.

"Lovely current this time of year," said the fox, paddling.

"Is it?" said the mandrill. "I wouldn't know. I'M ON A FOX'S TAIL."

"The current's rather splashy," said the fox. "You'd best hop up onto my back." So the mandrill hopped onto the fox's back.

"My back's getting heavy," said the fox a few strokes later, and there was something odd in his voice, a sort of syrupy smoothness. "Hop onto my shoulder." So the mandrill hopped onto the fox's shoulder.

"Almost there," said the fox. "But my shoulder's getting tired. Hop — onto — my — nose."

And the Gingerbread Mandrill, who had out-run a troop and out-witted a crocodile and out-styled a toucan, paused. He was, everyone agrees, a clever biscuit. He was simply not clever at the exact moment it mattered, which is the most common kind of cleverness. He hopped onto the tip of the fox's nose.

*Snap!*

"I'm a quarter gone!" cried the Gingerbread Mandrill.

*Snap!* went the fox again.

"I'm half gone!"

*Snap!*

"I'm three-quarters gone—"

*GULP.*

And that was the end of the Gingerbread Mandrill, all but one golden crumb, which the fox left on the bank as a warning to other pastry: never taunt anybody who has more teeth than you have sense.

"Delicious," said the fox, licking his whiskers. "A trifle vain, perhaps. But delicious." And he trotted up the far bank and away, having eaten better than any fox in the forest simply by being patient, which the crocodile took very much to heart and the toucan wrote on the mud with a stick.

The granny macaque, watching from the far bank, shook her head. "That's the last time I bake anything with ginger AND ambition," she said. "Ginger snaps. It's in the nature of the recipe."

The next week she baked a gingerbread tortoise. It sat beautifully. It stayed on the stone. It was, she said, a sitter, not a sprinter — and the whole fig forest agreed it was the finest tortoise anyone had ever tasted, partly because nobody had to chase it first.

And the moral, as the old baboon told it ever after: if everybody wants a piece of you, don't ride the one fellow who wants the whole batch.
