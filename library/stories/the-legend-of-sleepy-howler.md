# The Legend of Sleepy Howler

*A parody of "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow"*

In the bosom of one of those spacious canopy coves which indent the great eastern jungle, two leagues from the Tamarin Zee, lies the market glen of Sleepy Howler, where a drowsy, dreamy shade hangs over everything. Some say a zookeeper cast a spell here in the old days. Certain it is, the glen holds under some witching power, and the monkeys are given to marvellous beliefs — trance-preening, midnight grooming charts, and visions. Particularly one: the dominant spirit of the glen, the Headless Howler.

The Howler, say the housewives, was a mercenary whose head was knocked clean off by a falling coconut in some nameless battle of the Coconut War — and who rides forth at night on a giant tapir, body rattling like a pod of seeds, carrying a hollowed gourd that glows like a jack-o'-lantern, because, having no head, he must carry his fire somewhere. They say he rides to the old fig bridge and vanishes in a flash of fireflies, which is why the bridge is used mostly by the brave and the drunk.

Ichabod Macaque, who came teaching in Sleepy Howler all the way from frugal Connectikong, was tall and excessively lanky, with arms like hanging vines, feet that might have served as shovels, and a small flat head set on his frame like a keel. His appetite for strange food and stranger superstition was equally enormous: every sound a monkey-banshee, every rustle a coconut ghost, every owl a colleague. He carried Cotton Marmoset's *History of New Jungle Witchcraft* on his shoulder the way a parson carries his scriptures, and he lodged about among his pupils' families, fattening as he went, and in the evenings strolled by the brook reading *Tree-Yarns of the Night Side* until the darkening jungle flapped its bat-wings at him and he fled home humming.

Ichabod had, however, a cheering thing in his eye: Katrina Van Tassel, sole heir of a rich farmer whose banana plantation sloped green and heavy to the water. Rolling his hungry eyes over it, every banana became a bite, every bunch a bond, every grove a gold mine with a pretty guava at the center. He even gave Katrina lessons in psalmody, in the intervals of which he swallowed hard and proposed improvements to her father's orchards. Katrina missed her notes on purpose. She was a little plump, a little roguish, and a great deal aware of everything, and Ichabod's notion that she dripped honey into his ear was one of the few beliefs in Sleepy Howler that was itself superstition.

But he had a rival: Brom Bonobo, broad-shouldered and hunched, famous for wrestling, riding, and pranks, trailed by his gang, the Foggy Branch Boys. Brom had determined to surround Katrina himself, and his policy toward rivals was short: coconut or be coconuted. He called Ichabod "Old Bones." He smoked out the schoolhouse by stopping the chimney on psalm-practice nights. He broke in at eve and turned the whole classroom upside down, so that Ichabod arrived at morning to find the globes hung from the ceiling and the chalk writing backwards, and went about all day in a delicious fright, sure the witches had held a ball there. And having heard that Ichabod groomed his fur with a certain fine comb, Brom trained his dog, Whelp, to come dashing through the lesson window whenever the comb came out, so that Ichabod's scholarly dignity was regularly interrupted at the second stroke.

One autumn evening the whole glen gathered at Baltus Van Tassel's for a feast. Ichabod groomed his fur flat, borrowed a lean old spider monkey called Pepperbox, and arrived early, eating with his eyes before the table opened: cakes, plantain tarts, honeycomb, roasted cobs — his heart yearned over each dish like a lover's. He surveyed the plantation, the smokehouse, the fat birds, and in imagination ate his way through all of it twice, Katrina at the center like a fig in a cake. When the music started he capered off with Katrina like a clothesline in a gale — no monkey danced with less machinery and more joints — while Brom sat jealous as a hornet's nest.

Then came the stories. The oldest monkey in the troop told of buried treasure under the mossy rock, and how he had heard it counting itself at midnight; of the white howleress wailing in the ravine; and best of all of the Headless Howler, seen that very season tying his tapir to the churchyard palings. A hush fell. Brom topped them all: he had raced the Howler himself, and would have won, too, if the ghost hadn't burst into fireflies at the bridge and shot up the trunk like a rocket.

Ichabod would have traded a whole harvest for one hour of Brom's nerves.

The party broke up late, and Ichabod came out with a face the color of a green plantain — whatever Katrina had said, it was not yes. He mounted Pepperbox and started home, and every story he had ever heard arrived to walk beside him. A white shape in the marsh turned out to be rotten bark; he saluted it anyway. A rattling in the brush was hail, unseasonable and, Ichabod felt, pointed. Then, at the bend by the peat swamp, he heard it: clump, clomp, clap — the steady trot of a tapir.

Ichabod's hair stood up like a startled cat's. He hurried; the other hurried. He broke into a scramble; the other scrambled. He remembered with a groan that the Howler was a rider of skill, and that Pepperbox was old, flatulent, and governed by a system of prayer. Brom's stories came back to him now in unhelpful detail. Ahead rose the bridge — pass the bridge and the Howler must vanish! Ichabod dug his shovel-feet into Pepperbox's ribs; the saddle, a borrowed and ungenerous thing, slipped sideways, and for a moment Ichabod rode shotgun on his own downfall. He righted himself, hit the bridge at speed —

— and in his path he saw it plainly. The rider had no head at all. And from the saddle where a head should have been, the shape lifted the gourd: the glow, the grin carved in the rind, the light swimming with seeds.

Ichabod heard Pepperbox's hooves-steps hit the boards of the far side — safety, the haunted ground ending — and then something whistled past his flat keel of a head and struck a stump ahead of him with a splat and a shower of sparks. He did not stop to inventory it. He leaned low and was carried up the hill in a storm of fur and terror, and nobody in Sleepy Howler saw him again.

Next morning Pepperbox was found at his own gate, saddle gone, breathing in a satisfied way. In the road lay Ichabod's hat. Close beside the bridge lay a shattered gourd, softly steaming, and near it a peeled, squashed banana. Ichabod Macaque did not make his appearance at school.

A wondering troop searched the glen. The housewives put two and two together and made a ghost. Brom Bonobo, who shortly married Katrina — the wedding, it was noticed, followed rather close on the vanished schoolmaster — always laughed whenever the gourd was mentioned, and looked exceedingly knowing, and once offered to race anybody from the church to the bridge at midnight, tapir optional. Which led some to suspect he knew more than he told.

But an old farmer swore he had met Ichabod in Connectikong, alive, studying law, sitting in judgment under a banyan — a banana split before him, and a fearful glance behind. And that is the mercy of Sleepy Howler: it keeps its ghosts, but it lets its schoolmasters go.

To this day the bridge is used mostly by the brave and the drunk — and, of course, by the groom of any bride who would rather not be asked twice.
