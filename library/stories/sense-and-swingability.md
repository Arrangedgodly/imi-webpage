# Sense and Swingability

*A parody of "Sense and Sensibility"*

The Norland Banyan was entailed, as all the best boughs are, upon the male line; and so, when old Mr. Dashwood died, his widow and her daughters — Elinor, Marianne, and little Margaret — found themselves guests in their own tree.

"The banyan is theirs, of course," said Fanny Dashwood to her husband John, who had promised his dying father otherwise. "I am sure I do not see why the coconuts are not also theirs. Coconuts are so heavy to move."

By Christmas John had pared his promise down to an occasional fig, and then to a cordial wave across the river.

The Dashwoods moved instead: to a cottage at the edge of Barton Copse, a modest holding of Sir John Middleton, who welcomed them with figs and shouting.

"You shall pick every fig on my ground!" he bellowed, which is the zoological equivalent of kindness.

His mother-in-law, Mrs. Jennings, installed the sisters at once on the gossip-bough. "Two nice young she-monkeys! Now we shall find you each a prime mate, or my name is not Mrs. Jennings."

"She means well," said Marianne, "but she grooms everybody's business."

It was at Barton, swinging too boldly across the gorge, that Marianne missed her vine and came down with her ankle turned. A stranger with glossy black fur caught her up and carried her home through the canopy — Willoughby of Combe-Canopy. By evening they had exchanged first names, favorite trees, and opinions on every poet in the jungle; by week's end they had a private liana strung between their two groves. He had, Marianne reported, all Marianne's own opinions, only faster.

"She has gone quite bananas for the black-furred one," Mrs. Jennings reported, delighted.

"Marianne," said Elinor, "you do not really know him."

"One week is a lifetime," said Marianne, "when one swings high. To love by halves is not to swing at all."

Elinor said nothing. Elinor had her own silence to keep: Edward Ferrars — gentle, awkward, and unlike any male her relations had ever produced — had visited Norland, and had looked at Elinor over the course of a month in a way she mentioned to no one, and had then not proposed.

Colonel Branchdon — the weathered silverback who had loved long ago and lost — told Elinor the whole of it one rainy evening, in a voice like bark. The she-ape he had loved at two years old had been passed by their families to his elder brother; she had gone from bad bough to worse, and been ruined, and vanished; and her daughter — the second Eliza — had this very season been seduced and abandoned by a black-furred male of good groves.

"His name," said the Colonel, "is Willoughby."

"He shall answer for it," said Elinor.

"He shall answer for it," said the Colonel, "at dawn, on the dueling-liana. I have sent him word." They met; honor was satisfied; nobody's fur was much harmed, Willoughby having a marksman's respect for his own pelt. But Marianne never learned of it until long after — a kindness two people paid for out of their own silence.

Then Willoughby went to London, promising to return, and did not; and the season took the sisters after him.

In London, at the hop-vine ball, Marianne saw Willoughby across the room and held out both arms. He bowed, coldly, turned to a golden she-tamarin on his arm, and passed by. His note came the next morning, cold and correct, returning her lock of fur and her liana.

Engaged, came the gossip-bough's verdict, to Miss Grey. Fifty thousand bananas a year. It costs a male nothing to break a heart that has no bananas attached to it.

John Dashwood called in Harley Grove to commiserate, and stayed to calculate. The Combe-Canopy figs, gone; the groom's expectations, gone; Marianne's chances, recalibrated downward — John ran the figures aloud with real tenderness, twice, and concluded that Marianne had lost nothing she had ever legally owned, which was John's idea of sympathy, Fanny's idea of arithmetic, and the world's idea of John.

Marianne swung that very night, in the rain, for two hours, staring at the lights of Combe-Canopy across the gorge, and came home burning with fever. It was Colonel Branchdon who fetched Mrs. Dashwood through the storm, flying all night, without being asked.

Elinor, meanwhile, had her own London agonies, conducted at tea. Edward came to town and sat with her, and was not warm; his greeting was embarrassed, his goodbye was relieved. Old Mrs. Ferrars, the mother, looked at Elinor through her lorgnette-leaf as at a display of yesterday's fruit, and cut her. And in the middle of it all Elinor was cornered at a rout by Lucy Steele — a pretty, cobweb-clever she-capuchin — who confided, with girlish pressure of the paw, that she had been secretly engaged these four years to Edward Ferrars, and showed Elinor the ring braided from his own chest-fur.

Watching Marianne sleep, Elinor made the only speech she ever made. "I have had my own news this month," she said quietly, "if anyone had thought to ask me. Edward has been secretly engaged these four years to a she-capuchin called Lucy Steele, who showed me the ring this week. It is braided from his own chest-fur."

"My Elinor! And you said nothing!"

"What good would swinging from the chandeliers do, Mama? I had his word against my happiness either way. Somebody in this tree has to hold the branch still."

Marianne recovered; and Willoughby came at midnight, unwelcome and miserable, to confess himself the swing-most scoundrel in England.

"I loved her," he said. "I have loved no one since. But I am ruined, Miss Dashwood, and Miss Grey has fifty thousand bananas, and I have always been the sort of male who must be rich to afford himself."

"You made her love you," said Elinor, "and then you made her a joke."

"I know it. Tell me she lives. Tell me that, and I can bear the rest."

"She lives," said Elinor. "You may go."

Marianne, listening at the curtain, forgave him entirely — and then, slowly, and better, forgave him not at all, and discovered that a heart does not break; it only learns which bough will hold it.

In her slow weeks of getting well, she made Elinor a full accounting. "I never once examined my heart — I only exhibited it," she said. "I read now for two hours a day, and speak only when I have something true, and I find his opinions have gone out of my head entirely, which I take for the greatest cure. If I could but know that he is always wretched—"

"He will be," said Elinor. "Miss Grey has fifty thousand bananas and a temper."

The letters that followed were better than justice. Old Mrs. Ferrars, discovering the secret engagement, flung Edward from the family tree; Lucy Steele, on meeting Edward's newly rich brother Robert, forgot Edward entirely and eloped with the brother; and Edward arrived at Barton looking like a male reborn, clothed in neither estate nor regret, and holding a letter of ordination — for Colonel Branchdon, hearing everything, had at once given him the living at Delaford, a fine fig-glebe with a steady perch.

"Elinor — my Elinor — I am released! I was released a year ago and had not the courage to learn it. Can you — is it possible —?"

Elinor, the sense of the family, wept like the sensibility of nobody.

Marianne, in time, married Colonel Branchdon, and found that a steady palm can hold a swinging vine better than any wild fig. "I loved by halves, and it nearly killed me," she told her sister. "Now I love by wholes."

And the two couples nested in adjoining boughs — sense and swingability — like two limbs of one banyan, leaning closer every year.
