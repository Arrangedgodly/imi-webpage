# Furity

*A parody of "Purity"*

Pip Tamarin was twenty-three years old and one hundred and thirty thousand bananas in debt, and the only fact she had ever wanted — the identity of her father — was the one fact her mother refused to supply.

"You were conceived with the help of a donor," Annabel said, in the tone she reserved for taxes and tumors. "A generous young monkey at a clinic in the northern hills. No. The file is sealed. No. I will not be interrogated at my own kitchen table, Pip. Eat your kale."

Pip lived in a shared warren in the east of the city, worked eleven-hour days at a compost-powered internet café for wages paid partly in leaf-lettuce, and was known, thanks to a childhood prank of her mother's, to the world as "Pip" — a name she had chosen herself at four, having been born with a jawbreaker of a legal name she could not even say with a straight face. She was tall, furious, funny, and permanently molting with frustration. Some monkeys joined bands. Pip joined causes. So when a recruiter from the famous Moonbeam Project came through town — the transparency hive of Andreas Woulf, the monkey who had embarrassed three governments and one global fruit cartel by publishing their whole rotten correspondence — Pip, who had once traced her mother's sealed clinic file through six registries before the lawyers got to it, was exactly the recruit they had come for.

Or so she thought.

The Moonbeam Project lived above a canyon in the Monkey Republic of Santa Vela, in a converted hacienda of whitewashed adobe and satellite dishes, staffed by thirty idealistic young monkeys and one charismatic founder. Andreas Woulf was a huge, amber-eyed silverback with a monk's beard, a victim's smile, and a habit of making every young monkey feel chosen by standing too close and asking about their wounds. He had been famous for twenty years, ever since the fall of the Walled Burrow — the East Warren, where he had grown up the son of a high official of the secret police, and from which he had emerged at nineteen with a legend: that he had defied his father, hidden dissidents, and walked out a hero of the resistance.

The Project's great white whale was Tom Ape-errant — Woulf's opposite number in the north, the paunchy, limping old editor of a nonprofit newsroom called The Understory, who had published Woulf's early leaks and then, infuriatingly, kept publishing things about Woulf too. Ape-errant's people had been sniffing around the Project for a year, and Woulf, who could forgive anything but being seen through, had decided the time had come to know Tom's secrets before Tom knew his.

"You, Pip," Andreas said, on the terrace, at sunset, too close, "are the perfect monkey for the job. You have the gift. You have the wound. Go north, volunteer at The Understory, and simply — befriend him. A fatherless girl and an old editor. It will seem natural."

"Why me, though? Truly."

"Because, little one," said Andreas, with a smile you could have hung laundry on, "I know something about your father that you don't."

He never repeated it. But he put her on the plane.

The Understory turned out to be four overworked monkeys, a dying server, and Tom Ape-errant, a heavy, shambling mandrill with a brilliant blue-and-red face going gray at the muzzle, a war injury in one hip, and a Moral Center of the Old Grove that he maintained like a boiler in a basement. He worked Pip hard, insulted her prose, taught her everything, and never once asked about her father — until the night the deadline broke and the paper shipped and the two of them sat on the fire escape drinking dandelion wine, and Pip said it herself.

"He's an anonymous donor, supposedly. Some clinic banana. My mother's one romantic fact and she's welded it shut."

"Your mother's name," Tom said. "Before you were Pip. Annabel?"

"You know her?"

Tom looked at the city for a while. "I knew a monkey named Annabel," he said finally. "Thirty years ago. At a place called the Leavenworth Colony, in the far west — a grand failed experiment in communal living where I was the mail-order young intellectual and she was the blazing argumentative beauty who was engaged to somebody else, and I loved her so much I embarrassed us both, and she went away, and then she came back for one week the following spring, and then she went away again, and about a year later I got a letter with no return address that said, 'You were right about the money and wrong about everything else,' and I never saw her again."

Pip's fur stood straight out from her body, all of it, at once.

"That's — no. She never — she was with my stepfather —"

"Ask her," said Tom, not looking at her. "But do one thing for me first. There's a file The Understory has sat on for two years, because I could never source it. It concerns the fall of the Walled Burrow, and a young monkey who was a police informer's son, and a night in the cellar of a police villa. Andreas Woulf's cellar. I've always believed the legend of the hero of the Warren had a body under it. You're inside his house, Pip. You can check."

She did not want to. That was the thing about Woulf: nobody who had ever been chosen by him wanted to un-choose him. But she went back to Santa Vela with the question strapped to her chest like a bomb, and she did what she was born to do — she followed the data. The Project's own archive, cross-referenced with the Warren's unsealed files: the informer's villa; the night the informer's wife went to the hospital and the informer stayed home with his son; the son's later testimony, destroyed, then misplaced, then destroyed again; a shallow grave in a service yard, dug up by a road crew in the Warren's last chaotic spring, catalogued, and filed by a bureaucrat with no taste for legends.

Andreas Woulf had not merely defied his father. Andreas Woulf had killed him — in the cellar, with a poker, at nineteen, while the wall came down overhead — and had spent the rest of his life laundering that one act into sainthood, so successfully that he had half a million followers and a terrace and a beard, and the only monkey who had ever gone on counting the bodies was a limping mandrill on a fire escape.

She put it to him on the terrace. That was her mistake and her nature: she believed, like all the young, that the truth is a key that turns once.

"Was it self-defense?" she said. "Tell me it was self-defense, Andreas, and I'll bury it. Was he — did he —"

"He was my father," said Andreas Woulf softly, "and I killed him, and I have never once been sorry, and do you know what that makes me, little one? A monkey who is not pure. Which is the only kind of monkey there is. You wanted to find your father to find yourself clean. There is no clean. There is only fur, and what the fleas know about it."

He put her on a plane that night — one-way, out of the Republic, before his board could ask why. It was, in its way, the kindest thing he ever did, and he knew it, and he smiled his laundry smile as the door closed.

The story ran in The Understory the following spring: THE SAINT OF THE WARREN. Sources on the record; the grave, the file, the testimony. The Moonbeam Project's donors fled like starlings; the Republic issued a warrant; the beard disappeared from the terraces of the world. Woulf never forgave either of them, and never denied a word.

And in a kitchen in the east of the city, at Pip's mother's table, over the kale, the last fact came out the way splinters do, with more blood than expected and then relief: no clinic, no donor, no sealed file. One week in the spring at the Leavenworth Colony, a ring quietly returned, a decision made alone — Annabel's one unshareable thing, hoarded for twenty-four years not because it was shameful but because it was *hers*, the single object in a lifetime of compromises that she had never let anyone else touch, including her daughter.

"You could have told me," Pip said, crying into the kale.

"You could have asked me the right question," her mother said. "You never once asked what I loved. You only ever asked what I was hiding."

Purity, Pip decided, was a gimmick — a shampoo claim. Furity, though — furity was the real product: the whole animal, mud and magnificence in one hide, no molting required. She kept the name Pip, kept the debt (down to ninety thousand bananas), and took a desk at The Understory, from which, on good days, with her father yelling about commas two rooms away, she published things that powerful monkeys had sworn were buried.

On bad days she remembered a terrace at sunset, and a silverback saying *there is only fur*, and thought: yes, Andreas — but fur keeps things warm.
