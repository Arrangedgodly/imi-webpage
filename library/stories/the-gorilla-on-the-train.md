# The Gorilla on the Train

*A parody of "The Girl on the Train"*

There is a gondola that runs the north branch-line at 8:04 every morning, strung like a nut along a liana through the suburbs, and I ride it even though I no longer have anywhere to be.

I did have, once. A job, a desk, a troop of colleagues, a presentation about fig futures that I delivered to a room of clients while smelling like a sap-house — they called it redundancy, and I called it Tuesday. That was a year ago. I never told my mother, and I never told Tom. I get up at 6:15, put on my work furs, and ride the 8:04 like a gorilla with a career. My sister Cathy says, "Why do you pay for a ticket you don't need?" Because the ticket is the last routine I own, Cathy. Because the 8:04 still believes in me.

My name is Rachel. I am a gorilla. I sit in the third car with a coconut flask of fermented sap and I watch the back gardens go by, and a few branches past my stop — past the troop-tree where I used to live, where Tom still lives, with Anna and the baby — there is a tree I have given a whole life to.

Number fifteen, Blenheim Bough. A gibbon couple lives there. I call them Scott and Megan. They duet in the mornings, heads thrown back, in love, glossy as two chestnuts. Their terrace has passionflowers on it. Scott grills figs. Megan dances barefoot on the lawn, twirling.

They are perfect. They are proof that everything I lost still exists somewhere, in somebody's tree.

I may have been drinking a little. It helps the mornings blur.

Anna writes a blog called Perfectly Planted — how to ripen green fruit in a paper bag, how to keep second fur glossy, how to be happier with your second husband than you ever were with your first. I have read every post. I am the only reader she has blocked twice.

Saturdays are the worst, because on Saturdays I always see something that belongs to my old life. This particular Saturday I saw Megan on her lawn, and she wasn't dancing. She was standing very still, staring at her own tree the way you stare at a lump you hope is nothing. Then the gondola dipped into the long dark tunnel that runs beneath the old strangler fig, and the tunnel took things from me, as it does, and I don't remember coming home.

Monday, the tree at fifteen Blenheim Bough was covered in posters. HAVE YOU SEEN MEGAN.

I got off at the wrong stop. I know I did. I stood on their terrace saying, "I saw her. I see her every day, she's — she was —" while Scott's face went from hope to something worse than grief, and a police macaw named Detective Riley wrote down my name twice, and looked at my flask, and wrote something else.

Riley had me back the next morning. "Where were you that Saturday night, between eight and ten?"

"The tunnel took it," I said.

"The tunnel has a lot to answer for, Ms. Rachel." He showed me a picture from the station camera: me at 9:20, at the tunnel mouth, waving down a gondola that wasn't there. I looked like a gorilla hailing a ghost. I didn't remember it. That was worse.

The trouble with what I told them — that Megan looked troubled, that there was trouble in that tree — is that a week later it all came back on me. Because I had been seen that Saturday night, in the tunnel, wailing at strangers. Because I had woken up that Sunday with a bruised brow and dried blood in my fur and a hole in the middle of the night shaped like a scream.

Tom said, when I went to him shaking: "You did it to yourself, Rachel. You fell. Fermented bananas. You know how you get. You're embarrassing." Anna stood behind him at the door of my old tree, holding my old baby blanket against her shoulder, the way she holds it in the blog photographs.

But the hole in the night kept leaking. It started with a scent — fig-blossom shampoo, at the police station, on a constable's fur — and then the pieces came back the way figs fall: a laugh in the dark tunnel. Megan's face, wet. A silverback's silhouette in the leaves. Megan and Tom. Together, in the tunnel, where nobody was supposed to see.

Megan had babysat for them. Megan had sat for the baby on the very blanket Anna blogs about. And Megan had told Tom she was expecting, and that she was done being a secret. That is what I remembered, standing in my kitchen at 3 a.m., stone sober for the first time in a year.

I told Scott, because he deserved it before the rangers did. He sat down on his own terrace step under the posters of his wife. "She was pregnant," he said, in a small voice. "The rangers told me at the — I thought it was mine. I've been grieving one gorilla and there were two." He looked at me then without any hope left in him at all, which is how I knew the last thing he had was the truth.

They organized searches on the weekends — leaflets on every trunk, lines of monkeys holding vines, moving through the understorey like a comb through wet fur. I joined every one. It must have looked like grief. It was closer to digging: as if walking that tunnel's length enough times would let me find the night itself and shake it until the missing hours fell out of its pocket. Once the search line passed the mouth of the tunnel itself, and the marshal read out the drill — walk, look, call her name — and I stood under the old strangler fig calling Megan's name into the dark, and it was like pouring water into a hole in a canoe.

So I went to my old tree, and I asked my ex-husband about Megan, and I watched his face do the thing I had spent eleven years refusing to see.

"You were ape," Tom said softly, "out of your head, Rachel. Who'd believe you?"

"Detective Riley," I said. "For a start."

I won't pretend the rest was tidy. He came at me by the passionflower bed, and I went down, and my paw closed on the screw-thorn from the fruit crate, and I did what two years of being nobody had been quietly teaching me to do. He fell and did not get up. And under the passionflowers, where the ground was new, Riley's constables found what Tom had buried there in the dark.

Anna gave evidence at the inquest. She never mentioned the scratches on his arms, or the argument the neighbors heard on the Friday, or the way he'd re-sodded that bed in November. I don't know why. Maybe she'd been riding her own 8:04 all those seasons, watching her own windows, keeping her own secrets about what her perfect life was planted in.

Her next post was one line. Grateful. The comments asked what for. She didn't answer.

I ride the 8:04 now with a flask of plain water. I look at the trees as they pass and I see what is actually in them — ordinary monkeys, ordinary mornings, no stories but the true ones.

Cathy rides the 8:04 with me on Fridays now — two gorillas in the third car, two flasks of water, watching the gardens go by and judging them honestly.

My name is Rachel. I remember everything now.
