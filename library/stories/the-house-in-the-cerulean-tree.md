# The House in the Cerulean Tree

*A parody of "The House in the Cerulean Sea"*

Linus Bakervine was a mandrill of exactly forty years, exactly one cat, and exactly four thousand three hundred and twelve rules, all of them laminated. He worked for the Department in Charge of Curious Orphaned Monkeys and Youth — DICOMY, to those who enjoyed being audited — and he was very good at his job, the way a woodpecker is good at trees: thoroughly, loudly, and to the dismay of everyone involved.

Then, one grey Tuesday, Linus was summoned to the top floor, where Extremely Upper Management lived — the highest branches of the tallest fig tree in the city, where the fruit was rumored to be legendary and the silence absolute. He had never been asked to climb that high before. His hands shook on the ladder.

"Mr. Bakervine," said a voice from the leaves, vast and dry as old bark, "you will go to Monkeyshines Island. There is an orphanage there. Six dangerous magical children. One guardian. You will observe for one month and file a report. Do not deviate from protocol."

"Rule two hundred and six," Linus began automatically, "states that no case worker shall —"

"We know what the rules state, Mr. Bakervine. We wrote them. Go."

The ferry to Monkeyshines Island was piloted by a lemur named Zoe Chapelwhite, who was not, Linus noted in his notebook, entirely a lemur. Her eyes were the exact color of deep water, and she moved like a tide that had opinions.

"You're the auditor," she said. "The last one left after four days. One of them left his shoes."

"The shoes were a safety hazard," said Linus, who had read that file.

The island rose out of a sea so blue it looked like a rumor. In the middle of it stood a tree the size of a cathedral, its trunk wrapped in stairs, and halfway up, a house painted the exact impossible blue of the water below.

This was the home of Arthur Parnassus — a broad, silver-backed gorilla with reading glasses, a cardigan, and a laugh like distant, friendly thunder.

"Mr. Bakervine!" he boomed, descending to meet him. "Welcome. We've been expecting you for hours. The children found your ferry schedule and have been tracking you like weather."

And then the children came, and Linus's clipboard nearly went into the sea.

There was Lucy, a tiny macaque of six with a lopsided grin and a phonograph he dragged everywhere, who announced, "When I grow up, I'm going to destroy the world and then rebuild it out of pudding," and then, more quietly, "but not today. Today I have a popsicle."

There was Talia, a dwarf mongoose gnome with a garden of topiary shaped like famous primates, who took one look at Linus and said, "You'd fit in a hole I dug. Just saying. No pressure. But you'd fit."

There was Theodore, a small flying lemur who wore a green button pinned to his chest like a knighthood and pressed a spare button into Linus's paw within minutes: "For you. It's the good one. Don't lose it or I'll cry, and when I cry, I glide in circles."

There was Chauncey, an ambulatory pile of green goo in a tiny bellhop's cap, who had no legs and yet radiated the dream of luggage. "Bellhop!" he gurgled, saluting with a pseudopod. "Someday! Grand hotel! I carry the bags!"

There was Sal, a howler monkey of eleven who had stopped howling entirely, which was somehow worse; he hid in the linen closet when strangers came and spoke through the door in a library whisper.

And there was Phee, a bushbaby sprite with enormous lantern eyes, through whose paws the tree itself seemed to grow, who informed Linus that the tree liked him, "probably," and that this was provisional.

"They're not dangerous," Arthur said mildly, as they climbed to the house for dinner. "They're children."

"With respect," said Linus, consulting his file, "the Department classifies them as Level Four Threats."

"The Department," said Arthur, "once classified laughter as a Level Two."

That first night, Linus wrote in his report: The children appear to be loved. This is almost certainly a violation of something.

The weeks unpeeled like fruit. Linus observed morning swims, during which Arthur taught Lucy to float ("The world can't be destroyed if you're floating; that's just science"). He observed school lessons in the canopy, conducted under an actual apple-throwing threat, which Talia administered gleefully. He observed Theodore's button museum, all one hundred and forty-one buttons catalogued by shine. He observed Chauncey practicing bellhop lifts with a suitcase full of pebbles, falling over, getting up glowing.

He observed himself, slowly, going off-script.

When Lucy's phonograph broke and the little macaque wept — because the deep dark voice on the record was the only parent who had ever called him by name — Linus stayed up all night with a screwdriver and fixed it, and told no one, and wrote nothing down.

When Sal finally spoke — three words through the linen-closet door: "Are they gone?" — Linus sat down against the doorframe and said, "No. I'm still here. I'm not going anywhere," and Sal sat on the other side, and the tree grew one new leaf.

And one evening, when the sunset turned the whole sea to hammered copper and Arthur laughed at something Linus said — really laughed, head back, thunder and all — Linus discovered his heart doing something wildly unauthorized. He wrote in his notebook: Note: investigate. Then, later, in the dark, in ink he almost couldn't read: Never mind.

Zoe caught him watching Arthur and the children on the beach. "You know what I am," she said quietly. "The island's sprite. Its water. I've kept this place hidden for a long time. But water can't hide anything from someone who actually looks."

"I look at everything," said Linus. "It's the problem with me."

"It's never been the problem," said Zoe. "It's the point."

The month ended. And on the last night, with a storm coming in over the sea like DICOMY itself, Arthur told Linus the truth beside the fire, while six ostensibly sleeping children eavesdropped with the subtlety of freight trains.

"I was a DICOMY child myself," Arthur said. "Aged out at eighteen. Do you know what they do with a magical orphan nobody adopts? I'll tell you what they did with me." And then he changed — not into a monster, but into fire itself, an ape of flame standing calmly in the living tree, burning and burning and never consuming anything but the old rules. "I am a phoenix, Mr. Bakervine. Every time they burned me down, I came back. And I built this place out of the ashes so that no child — not one, of any species, of any level — would ever grow up as I did."

The children watched from the stairs, awed. "Do it again," Lucy whispered.

"Would you file that in your report?" Arthur asked softly, the fire settling back into cardigan and glasses. "Or would you file us away?"

Linus went home. He sat in his small grey flat with Calliope on his lap and four thousand three hundred and twelve laminated rules on the shelf, and he wrote the truest report of his life: These children are not threats. They are loved. The danger was never on the island; the danger is what happens to magical children when nobody loves them. Recommend: leaving them exactly where they are.

Then he climbed — all the way up, hands shaking, past branches he had never dared — to face Extremely Upper Management.

The leaves parted. And sitting in the topmost bough, vast as an old volcano, radiating heat like a forge, was a mandrill whose fur was the red of endings, whose eyes were patient as geology. Lucy's eyes, Linus realized. Exactly Lucy's eyes.

"You have met my son," said the Devil.

"I have," said Linus, whose knees had filed for early retirement. "He's six. He likes popsicles and phonograph records and he is going to be raised with love and topiary and buttons and a bellhop, and I will fight you on it. I don't know how, exactly. I assume there's a form."

The silence went on for a geological age. Then the Devil said: "The last caseworker begged. The one before offered to relocate him somewhere secure. You are the first to offer me paperwork."

"He's my family," said Linus. "Well. He will be. I'm retiring. It's all very sudden and I've been a fool."

"Yes," said the Devil. "You have." And then, the smallest crack of a smile, like lava cooling: "He is happy?"

"He floats," said Linus. "The world can't be destroyed if you're floating. That's just science."

Extremely Upper Management waved one vast paw. "The island is protected. The rules will be rewritten. You are, I believe the phrase goes, going bananas. You have my blessing."

So Linus Bakervine, mandrill, retired — or rather, relocated — to the house in the cerulean tree, where Talia buried him up to his neck in the garden ("It's composting! It's a compliment!"), Theodore gave him the other good button, Chauncey greeted every ferry wearing his cap, Sal howled — joyfully now, whole choirs of it — Phee upgraded the tree's opinion of him from "provisional" to "permanent," and Lucy, the future destroyer of worlds, fell asleep on his shoulder to a phonograph record, safe in the highest branches anyone had ever bothered to climb.

Arthur held his hand as the sun went down over the improbable blue.

"Facts are facts," said Linus, leaning against him.

"Family is family," said Arthur.
