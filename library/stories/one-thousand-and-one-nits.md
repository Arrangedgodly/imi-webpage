# One Thousand and One Nits

*A parody of "One Thousand and One Nights"*

In the great fig-forest capital there ruled a silverback named Shahriyar — but before his story, his brother's, because in this forest every story has a story behind it.

Shah Zaman, king of the neighboring grove, set out to visit his brother, and midway called for a forgotten talisman at his own palace — where he found his prime mate swinging through the high canopy with a smooth-cheeked lesser ape, both of them laughing at the whole institution of kings. Shah Zaman travelled on to Shahriyar's forest grey as ash, and would not say why. To cheer him, Shahriyar took him hunting; and while both kings were gone, they passed a pavilion by the sea, locked and guarded, wherein a Djinn — a great smoked-air genie with horns like a fig-branch — kept his captured lady while he slept his enchanted sleep. And the lady, seeing the two kings hiding, called them down and made them swing with her, and afterward showed them a string of five hundred and seventy rings. "Each ring," she said, "is from an ape who outwitted the Djinn while he slept. He thinks I am guarded. Nobody," she said, yawning, "is guarded."

The two kings looked at each other. "If this happens to a Djinn," said Shah Zaman, "what chance has a mere king?" And Shahriyar's blood ran cold — for he thought of his own queen. He went home early, unannounced, and found what his brother had found. Grief had him by the tail.

He banished them both and issued the cruelest decree a troop has ever suffered: each evening he would take a new bride, and each dawn she would be sent away forever, so that no ape could ever again make a fool of him. The mothers of the forest hid their daughters in hollow logs. The troopers stopped singing in the evenings.

Now the Grand Vizier had a daughter named Scheherazade, who had read every scratched record in the bark-archive and knew more tales than there are figs in August. "Father," she said, "give me to the Sultan tonight."

"He will send you away at dawn."

"Then I shall need a very good story," said Scheherazade, "and luckily, I have a thousand."

Her little sister Dinarzade came too, for the plan required her; and when the Sultan, sullen on his branch, had closed his eyes, Dinarzade spoke into the dark, exactly as coached: "Sister, if you be not sleepy, tell us one of your delightful tales, to pass away the night."

Scheherazade moved close and began, gently, to groom the Sultan's shoulder, as one does for family. She found a nit, held it up to the moonlight, and said, "A nit, sire, for a tale."

The Sultan grunted something that in silverback means *go on, then.*

"**The Tale of Ali Baboon.** There was once a poor woodcutter-ape named Ali Baboon, who saw forty thieves ride into a hillside, where their captain cried at the rock: 'Open, Sesbanana!' — and the rock opened, and inside was gold enough to buy the whole canopy. When the thieves rode off, Ali Baboon crept down and said, 'Open, Sesbanana!' and helped himself to one modest sack. But his greedy brother Kasim went after the whole hoard, and at the rock he remembered only 'Open, Parsnip! Open, Plantain! Open, Prunes!' — and the rock stayed shut, and the thieves came home at dusk to find him there, and —"

"Dawn is breaking," said Scheherazade, suddenly stopping. "What a pity. The tale must wait."

The Sultan's eyes flew open. "You cannot stop there. What did the thieves do?"

"Tomorrow night, sire," she said, "if I am still here."

And that — the Sultan found, to his own surprise — was a condition he rather wanted.

So it went, night after night. Kasim met the fate of all greedy apes with the wrong password; the thieves tracked Ali Baboon down and hid themselves in oil-jars in his courtyard, waiting for night — all but the jar the clever slave-girl Morgiana filled with boiling fig-wax, sealing each thief into his jar like a preserve. When the captain, disguised as a merchant, reached for his knife at supper, Morgiana danced with cymbals and, whirling, plunged a dagger neatly through his silk. "Morgiana," said Ali Baboon, "you are henceforth free, and also my niece-in-law, because this family clearly needs the genes."

Then Sinbad the Swinger told of seven voyages: an island that was, regrettably, a sleeping river-horse; an old aye-aye who clung to Sinbad's shoulders for a week and would not be groomed off. Then came the fisherman who found a sealed jar in his net and opened it — and out smoked a Djinn the size of a thundercloud, who said, graciously: "Choose the manner of your death. I have been in that jar so long that I have decided, on reflection, to kill you."

"Faith, brother," said the fisherman, "you were in THIS jar? A jar that size could not hold your elbow."

"It was roomier in those days," snarled the Djinn.

"I merely ask," said the fisherman, "because no ape alive would believe it. Show me. Then kill me."

The Djinn, who could resist anything but a dare, swirled back into the jar — and the fisherman stoppered it, sat on it, and preached him a short sermon about gratitude. ("And that," Scheherazade would say, "is why you never argue size with an ape holding the stopper.") There was even the Tale of the Lamp, in which a street-monkey became a prince on the strength of one polished lamp and a good Djinni — which is every monkey's retirement plan, and no ape has ever managed it since.

There was the Tale of the Ox and the Donkey, in which a farmer who understood beast-speech heard his ox begging a day off and his donkey advising him to play sick — and the donkey's reward was to be given the ox's plow. ("Meaning, sire," said Scheherazade, "that free advice is the most expensive thing in the forest.")

And every night, a nit for a tale. The executions were never mentioned; the Sultan's shoulders, unknotting under her fingers, began to shine like polished ebony; the troopers started singing again, quietly, to see if it was allowed.

Sometimes he tested her. "Are any of these tales true?"

"As true as grooming, sire," said Scheherazade. "The good of a story is not in the facts; it is in the picking-through."

On the thousand and first night, she had told one thousand and one tales and picked one thousand and one nits, and there were three infants tumbling over the Sultan's belly while he pretended to be above such things. That night she stopped in the middle of a tale and would not finish it, and when he pressed her, she said: "Sire, the only tale I have left unfinished is my own. I have told you a thousand nights of others. Shall it end by your decree — or by the telling?"

The Sultan wept, which silverbacks will deny to their last breath. In the morning he lifted her up before the whole assembled troop. "I was mad," he said. "Grief had me by the tail, and she groomed it out of me — one nit, one night, one tale at a time. Let no ape in this forest again be condemned at dawn. First, hear the story."

And Scheherazade told her last tale — her own — and the telling of it is not finished yet, for it is told still, night after night, wherever there is a branch, a listener, and something small to pick out of a loved one's fur.
