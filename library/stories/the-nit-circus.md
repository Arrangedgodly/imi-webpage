# The Nit Circus

*A parody of "The Night Circus"*

The circus arrives without warning. No announcements precede it. No paper posters, no barking baboons on corners crying its coming. It is simply there, when yesterday it was not, and the monkeys of the town wake itching with wonder.

Within the black-and-white striped gates there is no color at all, save the red of the performers' scarves and the gold of the fire. They call it Le Cirque des Nits — the Nit Circus — because, as the ringmaster says, it gets under your fur, and no matter how you scratch, you cannot get it out.

It began, as most terrible games do, with two old apes and a grudge.

Prospero the Enchimpanter — a theatrical old baboon whose magic was real but whose manners were not — received a visitor in his dilapidated theater: a grey bonobo in a grey suit, calling himself Mr. A.H.

"A new venue for our little contest," said Mr. A.H. "A fresh board. Fresh pieces."

"My daughter is five," said Prospero.

"All the better. Long games are the sweetest."

Prospero took little Celia's paw, and where he pricked it, Mr. A.H. bound the wound with a ring, and the binding sank into her blood like a nit into warm fur. In an orphanage across the ocean, Mr. A.H. chose a sad-eyed boy named Marco and gave him a matching ring. Neither child was told the rules. Neither child was told there were no rules. Neither child was told that the game could only end when one of them died.

The venue, when it came, was the mad dream of Chandresh LeFevre, a flamboyant lemur with too much money and a vision of a circus that would be less a circus than a love letter written in canvas and moonlight: open only at night, black and white, and absolutely no monkeys crashing cymbals on their heads. Some things, Chandresh felt, were beneath the species.

His assistant — a quiet young ape named Marco Marmoset, hired to answer letters — quietly built the place around himself, tent by tent, spell by spell, the way a spider spins: apparently at random, actually with terrifying intent. And in the lobby of a theater, a young bonobo named Celia auditioned to be the circus's Illusionist by making the panel's chairs vanish and reappear upside down on the ceiling. They gave her the job before their tails untucked.

The circus bloomed like a night flower, and the monkeys came. They came for the Carousel of Branches, whose carved macaques leapt from perch to perch as it turned. They came for the Ice Jungle, where snow fell upward. They came for the Wishing Tree, where you tied your wish to a banana peel and let it float into the black leaves. Most of all they came for the Illusionist, Celia Bonobon, who appeared in a burst of starlight and tore the fur of reality open like a ripe fig.

What the audience never guessed was that her illusions were not illusions, and that every tent was a move in the long game. Marco, hidden in Chandresh's shadow, would conjure a garden of impossible blossoms; Celia would answer with a labyrinth of mirrors that showed you your own soul grooming itself. Each wonder was a chess piece. They were trying to drown each other in beauty, and neither had yet noticed that they were holding hands under the surface of the water.

Some guests could not stay away. They began to recognize one another at train stations and dockside fruit stalls — apes in grey, with a flash of red at throat or wrist — and they called themselves the Rêveries. They followed the circus across the continents like migrating gibbons, passing maps and passwords and, occasionally, each other's fleas.

The oldest of the Rêveries' correspondents was Herr Friedrick Thissen-Nibble, a marmoset clockmaker who had built the circus's great clock, where tiny silver monkeys swung from the numerals on the hour. He fell, in the fullness of time, from his clocktower balcony while reaching for the moon — or possibly a banana he had hung there as a snack — and the Rêveries wore their red scarves at half-mast for a year.

Meanwhile the game rotted from the inside, as games do when the players fall in love.

"How am I supposed to destroy you now?" Marco asked Celia once, in the shadow of the Wishing Tree.

"You could start by holding me," said Celia, "and we'll improvise the apocalypse from there."

But the binding, engineered by two old apes who had never once considered the consequences of anything, demanded a winner, and the only way to win was for the loser to stop existing. The circus had by now become a living thing, held together by Celia's hands and Marco's spells and the quiet protections laid by Isobel the tarot-reading capuchin, who had loved Marco first. When she saw exactly where this was going, she packed her cards and her dignity and walked out past the ticket-taker, taking her wards with her.

The circus began to come apart at the seams. Tents sagged. The Ice Jungle thawed into a puddle of slush and crushed dreams. And through the flapping canvas tore a wild-eyed creature in a nightshirt: Chandresh himself, whose memory had been pruned so often by his "friends" that he no longer knew which of his lives was the real one, screaming about sabotage and where had all his elephants gone. There were no elephants. There had never been elephants. Chandresh had a letter opener and a grievance, and when he lunged, Mr. A.H. stepped calmly into the blade's path and let it pass through him like a memo he had already filed. Chandresh collapsed in tears. The grey bonobo could not be killed by anything so small as a letter opener. He was only ever killed by endings.

At the center of the dying circus, the bonfire guttered in its great cauldron. Celia stood at its edge and understood at last the only move that saved both the circus and the monkey she loved.

Marco arrived at a run, tails of torn canvas streaming behind him. "You are not going anywhere without me. I have spent fifteen years making you tents. I am hardly going to let you have an apocalypse solo."

They joined hands, the two rings clicked together like the last tick of Herr Thissen-Nibble's clock, and they stepped into the bonfire as one — and did not burn. The flames folded around them, and the binding, which could not tell which of them it was supposed to kill, snapped. Its two prisoners dissolved into the circus itself, into the smell of caramel and sawdust, into the light in the mirrors, into every last stripe of every last tent.

But a circus cannot hold itself up on its own. It needs a keeper, bound by choice rather than cruelty.

Widget and his sister Poppet — twins born on the circus's opening night, she who could see the future in the stars, he who could read your past off your fur — went to find a boy named Bailey.

Bailey was a spider monkey from a farm in Concord, an ordinary lad with extraordinary red gloves, given to him years ago by Poppet as a ticket, redeemable for one favor. The circus had found him a decade ago when he snuck in on a dare, and it had never really let him go. He had been groomed for this, in every sense.

"The circus is dying," said Poppet. "It needs a new owner. It has decided it's you. Well — Widget decided, and the circus agreed, which amounts to the same thing."

"What happens," said Bailey, whose mouth was very dry, "if I say no?"

"Then you spend the rest of your life wondering," said Widget, "and the rest of your life is long, and the circus's is not."

Bailey thought of the farm, the fences, the forty years of milking goats that waited for him like a sentence. Then he thought of the Ice Jungle, and the wishing tree, and the way the whole impossible place smelled like midnight.

He took the longest run of his life and leapt clean through the flames.

He came out the other side changed — not burned, but bound, his heartbeat and the circus's now one and the same clock. Around him, tent by tent, the circus pulled itself upright like a troop of monkeys shaking off rain. And the Rêveries, red-scarved and grey-furred, went on following it across the world, telling anyone who would listen its only secret: it arrives without warning, it opens at nightfall, and it will get under your fur. The circus is open when it is open.

Mr. A.H. tipped his grey hat, satisfied at last, and vanished between one blink and the next. Chandresh, his furious memories mercifully unraveled, lived out his days as the circus's dotty old patron, convinced he had invented the whole thing in a dream he was rather proud of. Tsukiko the contortionist capuchin went on folding herself into impossible knots, telling anyone who asked that she had once played the game herself, and won, and that winning was the worst thing that had ever happened to her.

As for Celia and Marco — the Illusionist and her prime mate — they never left. They are still there, if you know where to look: in the tent with no name at the heart of the maze, two silhouettes in the firelight, playing their game with no board and no rules and no ending at all.
