# The Wolf in Chimp's Clothing

*A parody of "The Wolf in Sheep's Clothing"*

The rains had been long and the hunting had been terrible, and Wolfgang the wolf was down to his last ribs when he found, snagged on a fence at the edge of the nature reserve, a wonder: a full chimpanzee costume, left over from the zoo's Chimpy Day celebration. Faux fur. Opposable thumb mittens. Even a rubber bottom.

"Now," he said slowly, "there's a troop of chimps beyond that ridge with a fig grove the size of the moon, and every chimp in it. But they'd smell a wolf at a hundred paces." He held up the costume. "A wolf in chimp's clothing, though..."

He rolled in a patch of crushed fig leaves to fix the smell, shimmied in, zipped up, and looked at his reflection in the rain barrel. He practiced walking on his knuckles and fell over twice. He practiced a chimp face.

"Ook," said Wolfgang.

It was, frankly, the worst ook in recorded history. But hunger is a great optimist.

The Gombe Grooming Circle met at sundown in the big fig tree, and the stranger arrived just as the greetings began.

"New fellow!" boomed Brutus, the big alpha, extending an arm. "Welcome! You're just in time for the groom-in. Give us your wrist."

Wolfgang extended a paw. Wrong. Chimps gave wrists. He corrected it with a lurch that carried him into the branch, off the branch, and briefly under the branch, from which he emerged smiling.

"Tired arm," he rumbled. "Long migration. Very long. Ook."

"Ho! A quiet one!" Brutus laughed, and the troop settled in.

The quiet one then attempted to sit, and sat *on* the branch rather than in it, like a suitcase. He attempted to climb higher for a better view and got exactly one body-length before his mittens slid off the bark; he came down slowly, in the manner of furniture, and claimed to be "stretching." When the troop swung hand-over-hand to the fruiting limbs, he walked beneath them on the ground, calling up that he was "more of a ground-floor monkey, traditionally."

Nobody had ever met a ground-floor monkey. But Gombe was famously welcoming, and it is rude to stare.

Then came the grooming. Wolfgang had never been to grooming school. When little Nia presented her back for a gentle nit-pick, he stared at her fur with a strategist's eye — and *bit* her shoulder like a chicken leg.

"OW! He BIT me!"

"Gnawing helps the gums," said Wolfgang quickly. "Old grooming technique. From the south."

"Whereabouts in the south?" asked Nia, rubbing her shoulder.

"Deep south," said Wolfgang. "Past the maps."

Then figs were passed around. Wolfgang held the beautiful golden fruit, sniffed its perfume, and turned up his snout.

"Not hungry," he said.

The whole troop froze mid-fig.

"Not... hungry," repeated a youngster, as though the words were foreign.

"He's on a cleanse," volunteered Wolfgang.

"A what?"

"A... fig fast."

Nine hundred years of chimp law held that no fig, freely offered, had ever been refused. The silence that followed had teeth in it. And through it all, from her throne of roots at the base of the tree, the oldest chimp in the troop — Granny Greentooth, small and bent and sharper than a macaw's gossip — watched him with narrowed eyes.

She watched him sit on the branch like a suitcase. She watched him flinch at a kiss-squeak and answer a pant-hoot with a howl, hastily claimed as "a northern accent." She noticed that the fruit he declined had been quietly, unconsciously sniffed — by a nose that worked three times harder than any nose in Gombe. She had lived too long to trust anything that didn't eat figs. Even the leopards ate figs, in a pinch.

And when he fell asleep at moonrise, his rubber tail wagging in a dream of running, Granny Greentooth rose, crossed the circle on her knuckles — slow, silent, deliberate — and peeled back the fur at his neck with one finger.

There, small and silver, was a zipper.

"WAKE UP," said Granny Greentooth, pleasantly.

The troop woke with her. So did Wolfgang, to find three dozen chimps standing over him, arms crossed, and one ancient grandmother holding his zipper like a doorbell she was very much enjoying the option of ringing.

"So," said Granny. "You're a wolf."

"...Ook," said Wolfgang, with no conviction whatsoever.

"He's a wolf and he BIT Nia and he DOESN'T EAT FIGS," gasped Brutus, torn between fury and fascination. "Why the costume? Speak!"

And Wolfgang, unzipped and out of tricks — half in the suit and half out of it, which is the most honest posture there is — told the truth: the long rains, the empty snares, the pack moved north, the empty belly, the fence, the fur. When he finished, his ears lay flat and even the rubber bottom looked ashamed.

The troop conferred in urgent hoots. Some wanted him driven off. Nia, still rubbing her shoulder, pointed out that he had bitten her exactly once, and gently, and that her uncle bit harder at weddings. And Granny Greentooth held up one ancient hand.

"Had you come as a wolf," said Granny Greentooth, "and asked, we'd have fed you at the fence, because that is the law of the forest and we are not monsters. But you came as a lie, and lies bite deeper than wolves. A wolf at the fence is hungry. A chimp who is secretly a wolf is a *plan*. Nobody sleeps well inside somebody's plan."

The troop conferred again, and ruled. The costume was confiscated and burned in the fire pit, where it smelled so awful that even the mosquitoes left. Wolfgang was banned from the Gombe Grooming Circle for a year and a day, and made to apologize to Nia's shoulder formally, with witnesses. But the troop had noticed one thing during the whole humiliating affair: no leopard, jaguar, or ghost had come anywhere near the fig tree that night, because a *wolf had been sitting in it*.

They tested this before they trusted it. The very next week, a leopard came slinking at dusk toward the grove — and Wolfgang, on probation at the fence line, threw back his head and howled. The leopard, who had never once heard the reserve's alarm system work in her life, sat down hard, rethought her evening, and left at a speed the troop found educational.

So they offered him the night watch, paid nightly in figs — a wolf's nose, Granny pointed out, was worth ten guards, and his howl beat their alarm system, which consisted of everybody screaming.

Wolfgang took the job. He learned to eat figs, slowly, grudgingly, and then — he denied it under direct questioning — gloriously. He learned the wrist, and then the kiss-squeak, and then, in time, a passable ook. He never wore false fur again. And on a warm night near the end of his year and a day, he groomed Nia's shoulder so gently she fell asleep mid-sentence, and the whole Circle pretended not to see the alpha wolf crying, because that is also the law of the forest.

But stitched above the Gombe fire pit, where every young chimp learns to read, hangs the moral of that night:

**False fur may fool the eye, but true nature always sheds through — so never trust a chimp who won't share a fig.**
