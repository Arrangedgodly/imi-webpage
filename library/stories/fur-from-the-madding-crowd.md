# Fur from the Madding Crowd

*A parody of "Far from the Madding Crowd"*

Far from the madding crowd's ignoble strife, in the green hills of Weatherbury Jungle, a young gorilla shepherd named Gabriel Oak tended his grove of two hundred banana trees with a heart steady as the seasons.

One evening a new keeper arrived at the neighboring plantation: Bathsheba Evergreen, a young bonobo with startling eyes and more vanity than a peacock in a puddle, who had just inherited her uncle's grove.

"Good evening, madam," said Gabriel. "Your trees came in well."

"Did they?" she said. "I know it already. I have a looking-glass, you know."

Gabriel loved her anyway, because love is not a reasoning creature. Within a month he proposed. Bathsheba laughed — actually laughed, then apologized for it.

"I want a husband who can tame me," she said. "You'd only worship me, Gabriel Oak, and I should grow insufferable. I shall always be beautiful, and I shall always be somebody's trouble."

Fate answered promptly. Gabriel's young herding dog chased his whole flock over the cliffs — two hundred trees uprooted in the ravine, and Gabriel ruined in one afternoon.

"A little dog has thrown a big gorilla," he said, and went to seek work.

He found it at Bathsheba's own grove — she had just caught her foreman stealing fruit — and when her ripening shed caught fire that very night, Gabriel saved the crop and was made bailiff. Thus the shepherd became the served. He served so well, and told her so few pleasant things, that when half her flock broke into the young fermenting-fig field one greedy morning and lay down bloated and groaning like a troop of land whales, Bathsheba ran to Gabriel in an unbuttoned panic.

"I have heard that a thorn, stuck in the right place below the ribs, will save them," she gasped. "Gabriel, do it, or I am ruined — and stay with me always, for I never knew what you were worth till this minute."

Gabriel pricked forty fig-bloated trees with a thorn, calm as a cobbler, and saved all but a handful. He did not remark that this was her second proposal scene and that his first had gone better for neither of them. A gorilla can wait. It is mostly what gorillas are for.

But madding crowds gather where beauty goes. Bathsheba, teasing her neighbors on a whim, sent a valentine to Farmer Boldwood, a solemn old orangutan of forty-one, writing on the seal: "MARRY ME."

It was a joke. Boldwood did not receive it as one. Nobody had joked with him in twenty years. He brooded until the words rang in his fur, then came courting in his Sunday grooming, stiff as a fallen log.

"I cannot marry you, Farmer Boldwood," said Bathsheba. "I may not marry anyone — yet."

"Ask me at harvest," he begged. "Only do not dismiss me." And he began, quietly and terribly, to count the weeks to harvest, the way a miser counts and a lover counts, which is faster.

She might have come to love him, gravely, as one loves a mountain. But into Weatherbury marched Sergeant Troy — a dashing chimp in a scarlet uniform with a bamboo sword-cane and a tongue quicker than a striking snake. He met Bathsheba in the ferns, twisted a lock of her fur round his finger, and practised upon her undisciplined heart as only a soldier can. One evening he gave her an exhibition of the sword exercise — cuts, guards, points, the cane hissing past her ears so close it trimmed the wind of it — until she stood trembling in the ferns with her fur blown in shapes no honest breeze makes.

"Ah, Miss Evergreen," he said, sheathing it, "you are loved by a monkey who can fight for you. It is a finer thing than to be loved by a monkey who can farm for you."

They married in secret, and the jungle learned it too late. "It is a mistake, I fear," Gabriel told her once, plainly, and was nearly dismissed for his honesty.

The harvest that followed was saved by the two of them alone. On the last night of threshing, with the fruit in ricks and the sky going the color of an old bruise, the storm came up the valley. Gabriel saw it first, as Gabriel saw everything second and understood everything first, and covered the ricks in the lightning while Troy — who had promised the pickers a supper of fig-wine and given them a flood of it — lay sleeping off his own hospitality in the barn. Down the wind came the rain like a charge of cavalry. Gabriel thatched, and hauled, and cursed softly; and Bathsheba, soaked to the skin in a coat she never noticed, held the ropes and handed the straw and obeyed the bailiff's orders as meekly as any hired fur, and the corn was saved by morning.

"You and I are the only sober monkeys on this plantation," said Gabriel, at dawn.

"Then we are the plantation," said Bathsheba, which was the first sensible thing marriage had gotten out of either of them.

Troy squandered her money and mocked her pride. Then his old sweetheart, poor Fanny Rhesus, died on the workhouse step, and the coffin that came to Weatherbury was two coffins in one, for the lid said FANNY RHESUS AND CHILD, and Bathsheba, who had come jealous in the night to see a dead rival, found a live one in Troy's grief.

"She was worth more than all the world," said Troy, "and I killed her with my neglect," and he went clean out of his mind with remorse, bought her a tombstone, planted flowers on the grave by moonlight — and the church gargoyle above, dripping storm-water as it had dripped for two centuries, washed every flower into the gravel by morning, which is the sort of review stone gives to regret. Then Troy walked into the sea, they said, off the dreaded Cat-and-Weather cliff, and did not come back.

Bathsheba mourned him fiercely, and Boldwood's hope — a plant that should never have been watered — flowered again. He begged at Christmas, at a party of his own giving, and she promised, at last, six years hence, which in Boldwood's fur was tomorrow.

Into that party, through the vines, a strange figure climbed: Troy — alive, sea-tossed, and insolent as ever.

"My wife," he announced, "is come to fetch her husband home."

What happened next happened fast. Boldwood seized a coconut from the table and hurled it with terrible purpose. Troy fell. Bathsheba screamed. The barn emptied like a nest of startled starlings, and Boldwood stood swaying, saying over and over: "I am a gorilla who meant no harm," which was true in the way that it is true of weather.

He was given over to the Great Ape Asylum, spared by petition; Troy was buried beside Fanny under the trees — one gravedigger remarking that here, at last, was a wedding.

The seasons turned. Bathsheba grew quiet and wise, and discovered that all along she had known where steadiness lived. On a soft evening she walked out to the hill where Gabriel was counting his herd and asked, roundabout, whether he meant to leave for California.

"I have been thinking of moving," Gabriel admitted, "for your sake — because you wanted me gone, I thought."

"O Gabriel," she said, "I am too vain to say it plainly! You have kept my grove and kept my heart, and you might have known me better. Do not go."

Gabriel took her paw. "Bathsheba Evergreen," he said, "if you can love a plain gorilla, I have been yours since the first evening, when you showed me your looking-glass."

They married so quietly that Weatherbury only learned of it when Gabriel walked to market arm in arm with his wife. And everyone agreed that fur from the madding crowd was best — and best of all when the crowd was two, and one of them was Gabriel Oak.
