# The Scratcher in the Rye

*A parody of "The Catcher in the Rye"*

If you really want to hear about it, the first thing you'll probably want to know is where I was born and what my lousy childhood was like, and all that David Copperpeel kind of crap, but I don't feel like going into it, if you want to know the truth. I'll just tell you about this one December when I went completely bananas, and how it all started with me getting the axe again.

They gave me the axe at Pencey Prep — that hoity-toity boarding school for young chimps up in the canopy — on account of me flunking four out of five subjects. Ancient Grooming was the one I passed. I'm quite illiterate, but I read a lot.

The night before I left, my roommate Stradlater — a handsome, secret slob of a chimp who shaved with a rusty blade and borrowed everybody's fur oil — came back from a date with Jane Gallagher. Jane used to keep her kings in the back row when we played checkers, because she liked them where they couldn't get hurt. I'd told him that. I'd told him to keep his paws respectful, and he'd winked at me, and I sat there getting sicker and sicker.

"Did you keep your paws respectful?" I asked.

"What's it to you, anyway?"

I tried to smash his jaw, being illiterate that way. He pinned me down and rubbed my face in the floorboards till I saw figs, and old Ackley just watched from the next bed, being Ackley. So I packed, yelled down the corridor at the whole sleeping dormitory, "Sleep tight, ya morons!" — and got the old hoof out of there about three days early. I decided I'd hole up in New York Jungle by myself till Wednesday, then go home good and rested, and break the news to my mother and father gently.

The thing is, everything in New York Jungle made me depressed. All those apes at Ernie's Banana Bar, clapping their paws and going "swell, Ernie, swell" when this hotshot piano-playing orang did his number. Phony. Everything was phony. The Lollipop Gorillas in the lavender room. The movies. The whole goddam jungle, if you want to know the truth. I even took a room in a crumby hotel, and when the elevator ape asked if I wanted company, I said sure like a madman, and then regretted it about one second after Sunny showed up — a young chimp about my age in a green coat who mostly seemed tired. I just wanted to talk, if you want to know the truth. She took the bananas anyway, and then her boss Maurice came back with her and said it cost five more, and when I said no thank you, he introduced my stomach to his thumb. Robbed and slugged in my own hotel room. That killed me.

I bought a record for my little sister Phoebe — "Little Shirley Beans" — and dropped it in the park, and it broke into about fifty pieces. That killed me too. Then I started thinking about Allie.

Allie was my kid brother. He died about three years before — the kindest little chimp that ever lived. He wrote poems in green ink all over his baseball glove, so he'd have something to read out in the field. Stradlater had asked me to write his English composition about anything, and the only thing I could see when I shut my eyes was Allie's mitt, so that's what I wrote it about, and Stradlater said it was supposed to be about a room, the moron. I can still see the cemetery in the rain. Boy, I hated that.

I got pretty drunk that night — on fermented fruit juice — and I sat in the park freezing, watching the ducks in the lagoon, wondering where they go when the water freezes over. You'd think a monkey could figure out where the ducks go, but I couldn't, and it worried me.

The next morning I felt like giving old Jane a buzz, and instead I arranged to meet old Sally Hayes at the theater. Sally is quite good-looking, but I have to be in a very particular mood for Sally, the mood where her whole life is one big phony favor. We went ice skating at Radio Canopy Music Hall, mostly because she looked good in the little skirt, and halfway through a hot chocolate I got this wild idea.

"Look," I said. "What we could do is, you and me could get the hell out of here. I've got around a hundred and eighty bananas. Tomorrow morning we could drive up to Massachusetts or Vermont and get a little cabin near a brook — I'd chop wood and all, and we could ice-skate and I'd pretend I was a deaf-mute ape, and we'd never have to talk to another phony gorilla again. How about it? Do you want to? Say yes or no."

"You can't just do a thing like that," Sally said, and she was right, I admit it, but I called her a pain in the ascus anyway, and she threw her cocoa at me more or less, and that was that.

Then I got this crazy idea again, the West. I'd hop a bus out West where nobody ever saw me, get a job at a filling station, pretending to be a deaf-mute ape — I'd build me a little cabin in the woods, and if any phonies came around wanting to visit I'd tell them to go chase their tails. Before I left, though, I had to say goodbye to Phoebe.

I sneaked into the family apartment while the 'rents were out, and Phoebe — she's only about ten, with red fur sticking out in the back — hugged me, and then read me the riot act.

"Dad's gonna kill you," she said. Then something that knocked me flat.

"Name one thing you really like. One thing."

And I couldn't do it. Then it hit me.

"Anyway, I keep picturing this big field of rye, way out in the jungle where nobody's around. Thousands of little chimps swinging and playing in the rye, and nobody's around — no big apes, nobody bigger, nothing but little chimps playing games. And I'm standing on the edge of some crazy cliff, and my job is to catch them if they start to go over the cliff. That's all I'd do all day. I'd just be the catcher in the rye and all. I know it's crazy, but that's the only thing I'd really like to be. I know it's insane."

Old Phoebe didn't say anything for a while. Then she said, "It's 'if a body meet a body.' Not 'catch.' Daddy said so."

Boy, she kills me.

I walked her to school the next morning, and on the wall by the stairs, in scratchy little charcoal, somebody had written a word that made me sicker than the flu. I rubbed it off with my paw, but I couldn't rub it off everywhere — that's the trouble, you can't ever rub a thing off everywhere. Then I went to the Museum of Natural History, my old museum, and stood at the glass with the Eskimo monkeys frozen forever in their little exhibit, catching their fish forever, and I felt so happy all of a sudden, and then not happy. Certain things they should stay the way they are. You ought to be able to stick them in one of those big glass cases and just leave them alone.

When I got home, there was Phoebe with a suitcase — she'd packed to come West WITH me, the little madwoman, and she wasn't taking no. "I'm going with you. I can go anywhere." And when I wouldn't let her, she wouldn't speak to me, and that was the worst hour of my whole lousy December.

Then I said the only thing that would fix it.

"You can't go with me. But I'm not going either. I'll stay. Tell you what — we'll go to the park."

And I took her to the park, and it started coming on rainy, and she went around and around on the carousel, this old rickety thing that plays "Smoke Gets in Your Eyes," reaching for the brass banana ring, and she nearly fell off trying to grab it, but I didn't say anything. The thing with kids is, if they want to grab for the banana ring, you have to let them do it, and if they fall off, they fall off.

I was damn near bawling, I felt so damn happy, if you want to know the truth. It was just that she looked so damn nice, going around and around in her blue coat and all.

I didn't go West. I got sick — really sick — and I'm here now, telling you about it, and I'm not even supposed to tell anybody anything. I just wish I knew where the ducks go when the lagoon freezes over, that's all.

Don't ever tell anybody anything. If you do, you start missing every chimp in the world.
