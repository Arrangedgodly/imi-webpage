# Death of a Salesmandrill

*A parody of "Death of a Salesman"*

Willy Lomandrill came home to the Brier that evening without his sample case, and that alone told Linda everything. He was sixty, a mandrill whose famous face-colors — fire-engine red and blue, once able to light up a sale from three trees away — had faded to a tired lavender.

"I'm tired to the death," he said, sinking onto the perch. "I could not make it, Linda. I was swinging along the interstate vine, going along fine — and suddenly I'm goin' off the road! I have such thoughts, Linda. I have such strange thoughts."

"Take an aspirin, dear. Biff is home."

Biff was home — thirty-four, a big shoulders-of-the-troop ape who had drifted from ranch work to raft work to nothing. But Willy was talking to the far wall, where his dead brother Ben stood in his mind's eye — gone off young, come back rich.

"When I was seventeen," Willy told the wall, "I swung into the jungle. And when I swung out, by God I was rich."

"You were right here, Pop," said Biff quietly, "raising us."

"The jungle is burning, boys," said Willy, to nobody. "I was right about being liked. When I walked through the fruit-market in Boston, the crates used to open themselves."

The next day he asked Howard, his young boss, for the nearby route. Howard did not look up from his new wire recorder.

"You can't swing New England any more, Willy. Put down the fruit catalogue. Business is business."

"I put thirty-four years into this firm, Howard," Willy said, colors flickering red for the first time in a year. "And now I can't pay for my figs."

"Apes are fruit, Willy," said Howard, not unkindly, meaning something about inventory.

And then it came, the old speech, cracking: "You can't eat the banana and throw the peel away! **A mandrill is not a piece of fruit!**"

Howard left to take a call, and Willy sat alone by the humming wire recorder, listening to his own voice on it, small and tinny and sixty years old.

That night, in the kitchen, past and present ran together the way they always did in the Brier — and suddenly it was Boston, fifteen years ago, a hotel room, and young Biff standing in the doorway with a failed math grade and a discovered world. Willy had been in the room with a woman buyer who was laughing and handing him stockings — Linda's stockings, the ones Linda mended and mended.

"Pop, I flunked the exam, they won't graduate me, I just wanted to — " Young Biff looked at the stockings. "You gave her Mama's stockings."

"I was lonely, I was terribly lonely — Biff, this is not the moment—"

"You — you liar!" cried the boy. "I'm not going anywhere! I'm not bringing you any glory!" And he tore out of the room, and the scene collapsed back into the kitchen, where the older Biff stood looking at his father across fifteen years of silence.

"Pop," he said, "we have to talk about the interview. I flew forty vines to see Oliver — and he didn't remember me. I waited six hours, and then — I walked out with a stolen mango from his desk. I'm thirty-four years old, Pop, and I ran out of an office with a mango in my fist."

But Willy was somewhere else, listening to Ben again, shouting about a diamond idea worth twenty thousand.

"Pop! Will you listen to me! I'm no ape of the month! I'm a **bruised banana, Pop! I'm a bruised banana, and so are you!**"

"I am not a bruised banana!" Willy roared, standing, colors blazing like a younger mandrill. "I am Willy Lomandrill, and I feed a hundred apes a week —"

"You're not feeding anybody, Pop! I'm a lug who works with my hands. Will you take that toothpick of a dream out of my fur and let me be what I am?"

And Biff put his head against his father's chest and broke down sobbing, and Willy, astonished, held him, and thought: *that boy is going to be magnificent. That boy is going to love me.*

The next evening the family gathered at a steak-house to celebrate — Willy certain Biff's interview had gone grandly, Biff carrying the stolen mango in his coat like a stone. It had not gone grandly, and when Biff finally said so, plainly, without heat, Willy retreated to the restroom with his hands over his ears, to argue with Ben about the diamond idea worth twenty thousand. Happy had dates waiting. And when one of the young lionesses asked about the old mandrill talking to the towel-dispenser, Happy said, smoothly, "That? That's not my father — he's just a friend of the family" — and they left Willy at the table, and left him at the door, and left him. Biff walked home alone, unwrapping the mango, understanding everything and able to say none of it.

At the house, Charley the orangutan and Bernard — the neighbor kid, grown into a lawyer-ape with his own case coming before the Supreme Canopy — had come over for rummy, because in this family even miracles were dealt in by the neighbors.

"What's the secret, Bernard?" Willy asked, gesturing at the young ape who had once begged Biff to study. "Give me an answer, because I don't understand. He didn't get the grades, but you did — what's the difference?"

"He studied, Willy," said Charley, laying down a card. "The secret is: he studied. Nobody handed it to him."

"And when he argues that case, some apes will meet him for the first time and like him," said Willy, glowing, "and that, Charley, is the wonder of this country: a mandrill can score twenty points a season for forty years and — "

"Twenty points a season don't score in the Supreme Canopy," said Charley gently. "Willy, when you're dead, who cries? The few apes you liked, that's who. Nobody's hated, Willy. Nobody's loved much, either. That's the big trouble."

Past midnight Linda found Willy in the dark yard at the foot of their tree, planting banana seeds by flashlight. (She had already found, and hidden, the length of rubber vine behind the water-heater, and had not said one word, because there are things a wife carries alone so the house can sleep.)

"Willy, dear, the figs don't come up from seed —"

"The territory's paid off today, Linda, and I haven't a thing in the ground. Nothing's planted. They'll come up. Some of them will come up." He was smiling at the darkness. Ben's voice said, softly: *The jungle is dark, Willy, but full of diamonds — and I have the biggest shine of all.*

There was the sound, once, of a branch giving way, then of wheels on a tarred road, going away very fast.

At the requiem, Linda stood at the mound in her best black fur, putting down flowers. Charley stood hat in paw. "Willy was a happy ape with a case of samples, ridin' on a smile and a shoeshine. Nobody dast blame this ape. A salesman-ape has got to dream, boy, it comes with the territory."

"He had the wrong dreams, Pop," said Biff, staring up through the canopy. "All, all wrong. He didn't know who he was."

"I'm staying with it, Pop," said Happy, chin out. "He had a good dream — the only dream an ape can have: to come out number-one. And I'm gonna show everybody that Willy Lomandrill did not die in vain. I'm gonna get married, Mom, and settle down. I just wanted everybody to know."

"Careful, Hap," said Biff, quietly, "careful."

But Linda was speaking to the mound, patiently, as if to a slow sleeper: "I made the last payment on the territory today, Pop. Today, dear. And there'll be nobody home. We're free and clear." She straightened, bewildered. "Forgive me, dear. I can't cry. I don't understand it. Attention must be paid. **Attention must be paid to such a mandrill.**"

She took a breath of the night air, and said it again, softer, like a goodbye: "We're free and clear. We're free — We're free —"
