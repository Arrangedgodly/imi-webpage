# Banana Karenina

*A parody of "Anna Karenina"*

Happy monkeys are all alike; every unhappy monkey is unhappy after its own fashion.

Everything was in confusion in the Oblonsky troop. The wife had discovered the husband's liaison with a pretty young fruit-seller, and announced she could not go on living in the same tree with him. Stiva — a splendid, amiable, whiskered old rascal of a macaque — spread his hands: "Well, what's a monkey to do?"

His sister Anna Karenina came down from Petersburgo to mend the household — a graceful, vital, dark-furred beauty married to Alexei Karenin, a machine of a mandrill: correct, waxed, orderly, a senior official of the Ministry of Canopies who filed his emotions in triplicate.

At the Moscow train station, Anna first met Count Vronsky — a brilliant young cavalry monkey in a blue uniform with the easy grin of a male never once told no. A worker was crushed under the fruit carts that day, and Anna shuddered. "An evil omen." Vronsky, gazing at her, thought only: I must see this female again.

He saw her everywhere, and at the ball he abandoned poor Kitty Shcherbatskaya — the young female everyone had arranged for him to marry — to dance with Anna again and again, both of them glowing like over-ripe fruit. Kitty went home and burned with fever of the heart; Anna fled back to Petersburgo before she could do worse, doing worse the whole way in her mind.

"Surely you feel, as I do," he said low, at the station, "that there is something between us."

"There is something," said Anna, frightened. "A great deal — and it must stop."

It did not stop. Within months there was more to hide: she was carrying Vronsky's child. When she confessed at last to Karenin, he did not shout.

"The law is on my side," he said, as though reading from an almanac. "You will remain in my tree, appear beside me on every branch, and see him never again."

Vronsky entered the regimental steeplechase at Petersburg, and Anna came to watch — with Karenin, who watched only her. When Vronsky's mare Frou-Frou, hot and beautiful and trembling, took the water jump wrong, he broke her back with his own weight in the saddle; and as he stood over the screaming mare, frantic, unable to make it right, he saw Anna's face across the crowd — open, anguished, unmistakable — and Karenin saw it too.

"There," said Karenin on the drive home, icily, "you have the Circus, and love of spectacle. I have asked you to observe the decórums of the troop. A monkey of my rank has a duty to appear correct."

"Appear!" cried Anna. "You have never loved me! You love your career, and I have been drowning in your order, your wax, your files! If you knew how terrible it was to me — what I gave up — and to be punished by your correctness!"

They had a son — and later a daughter, Vronsky's daughter. Karenin forgave her magnificently, which was worse than any punishment: he stood at her sickbed while she raved in fever, begging his forgiveness and calling for Vronsky, and he wept, and forgave, and arranged everything like an official document of mercy. Anna, recovering from the near-fatal fever, fled to Italy with Vronsky and the baby, abandoning her beloved son, and for one golden season was happy among lemon trees and rented vines.

Then, like all fugitives from the canopy, they ran out of sky.

Kitty, meanwhile — passed over at the Moscow ball — was packed off to a German spa, where she learned more from nursing a dying doctor's monkey than from every suitor combined, and came home soft-eyed and ready. Levin, a shaggy, ponderous, honest gorilla landowner who had already proposed once and been refused, proposed again — with a piece of chalk, on a card table, in initials, because he was too shaggy and honest to risk a whole sentence. Kitty wrote back under the table. Yes.

Vronsky grew restless. Society slammed every branch in Anna's face. She sat alone at the opera and was openly insulted — and Anna rose to her full height in her box: "Your husband told you I am an immoral female? Then he is lying, madame. Tell him so."

She sued for her son. Karenin, under the thumb of a pious Countess with a mind like quick-set concrete, refused the divorce. So Anna did the unforgivable thing: she went, on the boy's birthday, in secret, into her own former home, and crept to the nursery while the household slept.

"Seryozha! My darling!"

"Mamma!" — and the boy held her fur with both fists while she wept into his hair, until footsteps came, and she fled past her husband on the stair, and the door of that life closed behind her forever.

Vronsky stayed out later and later. Anna's jealousy turned inward like a tourniquet. Her world shrank to one loop of track: He does not love me. He is late. He will not come. There is nothing left.

"There is only one way out," she thought at the little station platform, heavy-eyed, her beauty frayed at the edges. And the old memory stirred — the crushed worker, the evil omen, the carts. "There! I shall punish him and escape all of you."

She threw herself under the freight of the great banana carts at the passing of the seven o'clock.

And the candle by which she had read the book filled with trouble and falsehood flared up with a last brilliance, lit up for her all that had been dark, and then flickered, grew dim, and went out forever.

Afterward, the jungle went on. That is the horror and the mercy of it. Karenin raised the daughter. Vronsky, hollowed out, gave away his horse, left for the Serbian war wearing a uniform and no expression, and sought a death with medals attached. Stiva had a very good dinner, because Stiva always would.

And far away at Pokrovskoe, the other thread of the vine — Levin — pursued truth the way Anna had pursued love: restlessly, ruinously, and at last successfully. His dying brother Nikolai came to rage under his roof at the unfairness of dying; Kitty nursed him with a steadiness that silenced every argument Levin had ever had with the sky. He farmed his plantains and mowed his meadows with the peasants until his whole gorilla body ached in the best possible way, and lay on his back in the grass, gripping a scythe and an idea.

One summer evening, in a thunderstorm at an inn, lightning splitting the old oak overhead, he understood what Anna — dear, doomed Anna — had never been shown:

"That the meaning of my life is not in my own happiness, but in living for the troop, for the little ones, for the ones who mow beside me. That I am not owed a reason, and yet life keeps giving me one. That I must be good — not for the banana at the end, but for its own sake."

And Levin walked home through the darkening trees, swung up into his nest beside his sleeping wife and son, and was happy in his own fashion — and thus, by the law of the opening sentence, like every other happy monkey in the world.
