# Hairy Potter and the Half-Peeled Prince

*A parody of "Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince"*

The summer Hairy Potter turned sixteen, something in the wizarding world of the jungle went quietly rotten. Bananas were disappearing from ministry storehouses. Voldechimp — He-Who-Must-Not-Be-Groomed — was back, and monkeys who said his name tended to be found the next morning, unpeeled.

And in a dark drawing room, on her knees before Professor Snipe, Narcissa Malfoy was begging a favor: her son Dracco had been given a task by Voldechimp himself, and she wanted Snipe's protection sworn on it. Snipe agreed, then agreed further — when Bellabananatrix Lestrange pressed her paw between his paws, and three golden threads bound them, and the Unbreakable Vow tightened: to watch over Dracco, to carry out the deed if Dracco could not. Which vow he then kept for a year, a book, and a half, in a manner nobody understood until far too late.

Professor Dumbledoo, silver-maned and twinkling, collected Hairy personally from the Dursleys' treehouse, swinging branch to branch like a monkey a third his considerable age, for one last mission first: to recruit old Professor Slugmonkey, who kept a tank of prized giant snails and prized favorite students in equal condition.

"Ah, Hairy Potter," Slugmonkey purred, pressing a candied fig on him. "The Chimp Who Lived. You must come to my little supper club. It's not everyone, you understand. Only the best connected young primates in the troop."

At Hogwarts School of Monkeycraft, Dumbledoo gave Hairy private lessons — which were really history lessons, tours through a magical memory-vat of a young, already-slippery Voldechimp, who had begun asking how to split his soul.

"A Horcrux," Dumbledoo said gravely, "is when a monkey hides a piece of his soul inside an object, so death cannot fully catch him. Voldechimp split his like a banana bunch — seven pieces, flung to the far corners. To beat him, each piece must be destroyed. No soul-paste survives."

Meanwhile, Hairy had come into possession of a used Potions textbook, annotated in a cramped, brilliant hand. *For fur-shine, stir clockwise, not counter — the book lies, the author wins.* In faded gold on the spine: the property of the Half-Peeled Prince.

With the Prince's marginalia, Hairy topped Potions for the first time in his life, winning a tiny vial of Felix Bananis, liquid luck, which glowed like molten sunshine and smelled of every good thing that had ever happened to anyone. He used his dose that year — or rather, used the *idea* of it. Before the biggest match of the season he let Rondald see him tip a droplet of Felix into his morning juice. Rondald then played out of his fur: fifty saves, one goal, a swagger visible from the staffroom. Hermonkee held up the bottle afterward, still full to the cork. "You didn't put anything in his juice." "No," said Hairy. "But he didn't know that." It was, Dumbledoo might have said, the entire subject of confidence, taught in one breakfast. The match itself was called by Luna Lovegibbon, who commentated the way a poet reports a hailstorm — "Rondald Weasel has the quaffle, or possibly a peach, and everyone seems very emotional about fruit" — and was never once asked back, and never once minded.

He also won the Prince's enemies. Among the marginalia was a curse labelled, with an author's honesty, *for enemies* — and Hairy, ambushed in a flooded bathroom by a crying, cursing Dracco, used it without thinking. *Sectumsempra.* The spell opened Dracco up like an overripe mango, and only Snipe's arrival, and Snipe's murmured counter-charm, sewed him back together in time. Hairy hid the book and lied about it; Snipe knew; and a detention, a lifetime of one, passed between them.

His suspicions ran elsewhere all the same: on Dracco, pale and thinner-furred than ever, sneaking off with the troop's map, whispering to somebody through cabinets. Hermonkee insisted there was no evidence. Rondald insisted on eating. Everybody insisted Hairy was obsessed.

"I know what Dracco's doing," Hairy said flatly.

"You don't know anything," said Hermonkee.

"That's the wrong series," said Rondald — who was, at the time, dating a girl who called him "Won-Won" at volume, having first poisoned himself, romantically speaking, with a box of love-laced chocolate cauldrons meant for Hairy. It was that kind of year.

The year turned terrible exactly as fast as fruit ripens. Katie Bell, hexed by a cursed collar. Rondald, poisoned by mead meant for Dumbledoo — saved by a bezoar-stone from a fig, applied at the last second, by Hairy, who remembered a Prince's scribbled note and a first-year lecture: *bezoars are counter to most poisons, and hold your hair on in the meantime.* Slugmonster — Slugmonkey — cornered at last by Hairy under Felix Bananis's warm certainty, confessing the memory Hairy needed: the memory of telling young Voldechimp about Horcruxes at all.

Then Dumbledoo came for him. "Tonight, Hairy, we go to a cave."

The cave was beyond the sea, past a lake of the dead — and the dead still lived there. At its center, on a fig-altar, sat a locket-shaped bunch of bananas. Dumbledoo, half-dead with thirst, drank the potion guarding it while Hairy, bound by promise, forced him cup by cup.

"It's all right, boy," Dumbledoo whispered, gray around the muzzle. "I am not worried — I am with you."

And when the drinking was done, the surface of the lake stirred, and the pale inferi — dead apes, preserved and patient, the world's worst bath toys — rose up around the island. Dumbledoo, half-conscious, produced a ring of fire that wheeled them back like a lasso of sunrise, and they crawled out through the storm to the tower top of Hogwarts, to home, to safety.

Where the Dark Mark hung in the sky, and Dumbledoo froze Hairy under the invisibility-fleece, faced the tower door alone, and stood there, wandless, as Death Chimps apparated onto the ramparts with Dracco among them.

Dracco's wand shook. He couldn't.

But Professor Snipe came through the door, black fur sleek and unreadable, looked his master's fate in the face, and said, in a voice Hairy would hear all his life:

"Avocado Kedavra."

Green light filled the tower. Dumbledoo, greatest silverback of his age, tipped slowly over the battlements and fell, and the whole jungle below seemed to rise up in grief and war.

Hairy chased Snipe across the grounds, screaming curses — the Prince's own curses — and Snipe blocked every one, and Snipe — bleeding, furious — shouted back: "Don't call me a coward, Potter! You dare use my own spells against me? Yes — I'm the Half-Peeled Prince!"

And then he was gone, and the locket was opened, and inside was nothing but a note: *I, R.A.B., have taken the real Horcrux ahead of you, and I pray to the troop it is found by hands worthier than mine.* Regulus. Somebody's brother. There would be one more year, and a forest, and a final battle over the fate of the troop. But that came later.

They buried Dumbledoo by the lake: merpeople singing from the black water, centaurs at the tree line shooting their arrows in salute — a salute, from centaurs, being the nearest thing to an apology their calendar allows — and a white tomb rising quiet in the sun. Hairy hardly heard the eulogies. He spent the service doing the arithmetic of who was left, and deciding, the way decisions are actually made: not at all, and then all at once. He told Rondald and Hermonkee there would be no seventh year, no Hogwarts for him. They did not argue. "We're with you," said Hermonkee, like a footnote confirming a text. "Whatever it turns out to be," said Rondald, through a sandwich.

Much later. And it came for the Prince's reasons, which were, in the end, the most loyal in the whole jungle.
