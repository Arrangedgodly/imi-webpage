# Furtastic Mr. Macaque

*A parody of "Fantastic Mr Fox"*

Down at the edge of the jungle there were three farms. The owners of these farms had done well. They were rich men. They were also nasty men.

All three of them were about as cussed and mean as it was possible for men to be. The local jungle had a rhyme about them. It went like this:

*Bogus and Bunch and Bean,*
*One fat, one short, one lean,*
*These horrible crooks,*
*So different in looks,*
*Were nonetheless equally mean.*

Farmer Bogus owned the vast banana plantations that ran down to the river. He was enormously fat, because every day he ate a whole frying pan of banana fritters smothered in condensed milk for breakfast, lunch, and supper, and snacks between.

Farmer Bunch kept a thousand free-range hens on doughnut meal. He was a pot-bellied dwarf. His diet was doughnuts and goose livers, which made him foul-tempered.

Farmer Bean was a turkey-and-coconut man. He was thin as a stick and never ate a bite, exercising instead his single remarkable talent: he drank gallons and gallons of his own strong coconut cider.

And up the hill above the farms, in the roots of an enormous banyan tree, there lived a most unusual golden monkey.

His name was Macaque — Furtastic Mr. Macaque — and every night after the moon came up, Mr. Macaque would slink down the hill through the fern shadows to the farms. He took bananas from Bogus, eggs and the odd plump hen from Bunch, and coconuts — and, when he was feeling bold, a jug of cider — from Bean.

"Last night the golden one was in my banana sheds!" roared Bogus.

"He got three of my fat hens!" shrieked Bunch.

"The dirty rascal drank my CIDER!" wailed Bean, mournfully hugging his jug.

"There's only one thing to do," said Bogus, and the three farmers shook hands on it, one fat paw, one short paw, one lean paw. "We'll shoot the scoundrel."

That night the farmers crouched in the ferns with guns aimed at the small dark hole under the banyan. The moon rose. A handsome golden shape stepped out, tail curled like a signature.

"BANG! BANG!"

Mr. Macaque jumped back into his hole, unhurt — but his beautiful tail was not so lucky. When he reached the inner chamber where Mrs. Macaque and the four Small Macaques waited, his tail ended in a ragged little pom-pom.

"Poor darling," said Mrs. Macaque, binding it in a leaf. "It'll grow back fluffy."

"It'll match my ears," said Mr. Macaque. But he knew the farmers would be back. And he was right.

At dawn the farmers returned with shovels. "Dig him out!" cried Bogus. They dug till noon and found nothing but roots. So they sent for machines — five huge tractors and two bulldozers with mechanical arms — and they gouged and tore at the hillside until the great banyan itself leaned like a sleepy giant.

Down below, in the dark, the Macaque family dug for their lives. Monkeys can dig like the wind when they must, and Mr. Macaque, digging first with his hands and then with his pom-pom of a tail flicking earth behind him, dug faster than any monkey in the jungle's memory.

"He's escaping!" howled Bean.

"He'll die of starvation down there if we wait!" cackled Bunch, and the farmers settled in around the hole with tents and sandwiches and a thousand crates of cider, to wait forever.

Three days and three nights the tractors stood guard. And three days and three nights, in the deep dark tunnels, the Small Macaques grew thin and gray around the muzzle.

"Daddy," whispered the Smallest Macaque, "I'd rather be shot than starve."

"No you wouldn't," said Mr. Macaque. And he smiled his golden smile in the dark. "I've had an idea. Nobody in the history of animals has ever dug as fast as a frightened monkey, and there is one thing those three farmers will never, ever learn about us."

"What's that, darling?" asked Mrs. Macaque.

"Monkeys dig *better* than machines." And Mr. Macaque turned his family's paws downhill — toward the farms.

They dug and dug and dug, and suddenly Mr. Macaque's paw broke through into a hollow space that smelled — oh, it smelled like heaven warmed over. Mr. Macaque climbed up into the dark above him, and his hand touched something smooth and long and cool.

He pulled. It was a banana. Then another. Then another.

"Glorious!" cried Mr. Macaque. "We are exactly where I hoped! We're in Banana Storehouse Number One!" And the whole family swarmed up into Bogus's secret ripe-stash, a cavern of hanging bunches like golden chandeliers. "Eat, my darlings, eat! But mind — quiet as little ghosts, and we take only what we can carry. A thief with manners is a host's worst nightmare."

And as they stuffed their bellies, they heard a voice. "Who — who's there?" Out of the shadows crept an old Binturong with a shovel, and behind him a whole crowd of diggers — Tree Shrew, Bamboo Rat, Mother Loris and her babies, and dozens of rabbits with no homes since the tractors came.

"We're done for," moaned the Binturong. "The farmers gassed and gouged us all out. We're starving in the tunnels, every digging family on this hill. And now they'll catch you too, Mr. Macaque, and then they'll catch us."

"Caught!" cried Mr. Macaque. "My dear old Binturong, you are looking at the happiest monkey alive!" And he told them what he had realized: that every farm lay within digging distance of every other farm, and that monkeys, underground, are faster than any farmer on any tractor on any day. "Tonight, my friends, we shall go to Bunch's Doughnut Coop. And then — Bean's Cider Cellar!"

They dug to the poultry house, where fat hens roosted over sacks of doughnut feed and the bacon smokehouse breathed its perfume through the floor. Then they dug still farther, down to the cellar where the cider barrels stood in silent rows like sleeping moons.

"The cellars of Bean," said Mr. Macaque softly. "The nectar of the gods." He drew a jug, and passed it round, and everyone drank — even the smallest Loris baby, who hiccupped a tiny hiccup and fell asleep smiling. "A toast!" cried Mr. Macaque, raising the jug. "To Bogus! And to Bunch! And to Bean! Gentlemen, we thank you for your generous donations to the Furtastic Feast!"

Then they cooked. In a vast underground hall between the three tunnels, the Macaque feast assembled: fried bananas from Bogus, plantains and mango chutney, doughnut-fed hen and eggs, Bunch's smoked goose, Bean's cider — enough for a hundred and forty hungry diggers, every rabbit, shrew, loris, binturong and macaque on the hill, all seated at one long table of roots.

The Small Macaques were so full they had to lie on their backs and fan themselves with their own tails.

"Daddy," said the Smallest Macaque, "could we do this every night?"

Mr. Macaque stood, golden in the cider-light, and made the finest speech of his life.

"Why not?" he said. "We shall build a great underground village! Diggers' houses for all! And a market, straight down the middle — bananas from Bogus on the right, doughnuts and hen's eggs from Bunch on the left, and at the very end of the street, a sweet little cider shop. No monkey need ever show his face above ground again. We shall eat like kings and sleep like sloths, and let those three fussy farmers sit in their tents in the rain forever."

"Hurrah for Mr. Macaque!" they cheered. "Furtastic Mr. Macaque!"

Up above, in the rain, the farmers sat. They sat all night, and all the next day, and all the next. Bogus ate banana fritters and grew fatter. Bunch ate doughnuts and grew shorter-tempered. Bean drank cider and grew leaner, and none of them had yet noticed that down below them a whole civilization was stocking its pantries.

They were still sitting there when the rains came, waiting for the golden macaque to come out.

And down in the deep dark, at a long table of roots, the Smallest Macaque raised a jug of cider and asked, "Papa — what will the farmers do when they find out?"

And Furtastic Mr. Macaque smiled, and helped himself to another banana.

"They won't," he said.
