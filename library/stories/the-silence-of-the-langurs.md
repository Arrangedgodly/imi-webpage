# The Silence of the Langurs

*A parody of "The Silence of the Lambs"*

Clarice Sifaka had been at the Primate Bureau of Investigation for three months — Behavioral Science, the branch that studies how monkeys fall — when Chief Crawford sent her down the long branch to the Canopy Asylum for the Criminally Chimp.

"You'll talk to Dr. Hannibal Langur," said Crawford, a grizzled silverback who smelled of old case files. "He's smart, he's polite, and he knows the Banana Skinner. Get him to talk. And Clarice — don't let him inside your head."

"He's a monkey who eats what isn't his," sniffed Dr. Chimp-ton, the asylum director, jogging to keep up. "He'll study you, my dear, the way you'd study a banana. And what does the monkey do with the banana?"

"Eats it," said Clarice.

"Flatters it first," said Chimp-ton.

Down eight branches and one burrow, past the urping, poo-flinging residents, sat Dr. Langur in a cell of glass and polished bamboo. He stood when he saw her, which no one had taught him.

"Good morning, Clarice."

"Hello, Dr. Langur." She showed her credentials. "I'm here to ask you about Baboon Bill."

"Ah. The one who shaves his victims." The langur's eyes were maroon and amused. "He wants to be something he isn't, Clarice. He wants to be a langur. Now. First principles. What does he do, this Baboon Bill? He covets. That is his nature. And how do we begin to covet? We begin by coveting what we see every day. Don't you feel eyes moving over your fur every day, Clarice? Am I close? Tell me — do you have a prime mate?"

"That's — quid pro quo, Doctor. I answer, you answer."

"Quid pro quo, yes. Begin."

She told him about the case: five young female monkeys, shaved bald, sunk in dry moats across three provinces. In exchange, in careful slivers, she told him about herself. It came out before she could stop it: the ranch where she grew up, the spring langurs lined up at the slaughter shed.

"I tried to save one," she said. "I carried her and ran. I thought if I could save one, the screaming would stop. It didn't."

"You still wake up sometimes, don't you," Dr. Langur said gently. "You wake up in the dark and hear the langurs screaming."

"Yes."

"Silence the screaming, Clarice, and you will stop hearing it. I will help you catch him. Not because I like him — he is rude, and ugly, and a bad artist's copy of a langur — but because I like you, and I never lie to a monkey I like. Now: he is keeping one girl alive right now — the senator's kid, in the well of an old dry moat — and he is grooming her patience thin. He will offer her a trade. *It rubs the aloe on its fur, or else it gets the misting bottle again. It puts the aloe in the basket.*"

"Put the aloe in the basket," Clarice whispered, somewhere far away.

"Run along now. I dislike being kept from my book." (He was reading an old zookeeping manual, annotated in a beautiful hand.)

The aloe basket reached the Bureau in an evidence bag, and coiled inside it, like a small brown question, a chrysalis. Clarice carried it to the Museum of Natural Canopy, where two graduate simians fought over it with tweezers.

"Acherontia banana," said the first. "The Death's-Head Howlermoth."

"It's called that," said the second, "because of the marking. See? A little skull on the back."

"It's called that," said the first, "because it screams when it wants to be noticed. They breed on aloe and on misery, and this one was raised in a basement, by hand, by someone patient."

"How do you spell that?" asked Clarice.

"We usually look it up," they said together. Then the first one asked her to the pictures, the second called him a poacher, and Clarice left them to their moths.

Meanwhile the Bureau cut its bargain. Senator Martin came down the branch in person, a female howler with a voice like a falling tree. "Whatever he wants," she told Crawford. "A park of his own. Sun. Books. One book a week. I'll sign anything with my own teeth. He knows what my daughter is to me."

Dr. Langur heard the offer through the glass and pronounced it "adequate, in the way a fruit basket is adequate." He gave them a name in his beautiful hand: SOOI FRIEND. The raid team arrested a mild fruit-stand cashier in Zoohio and held him eleven hours, until a chemist monkey in the lab giggled helplessly and pointed out the letters also spelled FOOLS' DINER.

"I lied to them, Clarice," Dr. Langur said, maroon and serene. "Lying to bureaucrats is a custom of mine, like flattery. But I have never lied to you. You want him? Then stop chasing the latest body and look at the first one. He buried himself in his beginnings. Even a monster comes from somewhere — which is more than I can say for most bureaucrats."

He was transferred to Memphis to be studied like a rare beetle, and there he did what clever monkeys do: he waited for dinner. Two keepers went into the courthouse cage with his tray. One langur came out wearing a keeper's cap and the keeper's pleasant expression. Both keepers turned up the next morning — unharmed, well fed, and curiously well read — and Dr. Langur was gone west in a truck full of freight bananas. The Bureau did not hold a press conference.

Clarice, officially taken off the case, unofficially drove to Belvedere, following the one clue the langur had left her like a bone thrown from a train. His first victim's name: Miss Hester Mofet. She said it aloud in the car until it fell apart — *the rest of me* — and understood that Hester Mofet was the rest of somebody. The rest of Baboon Bill.

At Your Self-Storage, unit 31, paid ten years ahead by an estate, she found it: shelves of preserving jars, a dress form wearing a half-finished coat of grey langur fur, and a lightbox with a pin-up of a langur starlet, a paper moth pasted neatly over her mouth. Clarice stood very still, then did what the PBI trains you to do last: she breathed.

"What do you see, Clarice?" Crawford demanded over the bad line. "I've got a team wheels-up—"

"He's making a coat, Chief. He's making himself. Fredrica Bimmel — his first — she was the seamstress's daughter, a big clever girl who took in alterations, and Bill apprenticed himself to what he loved. He didn't want her coat. He wanted her *pattern*."

The trail of thread led to the Belvedere Home of Rest, a funeral parlor with lace curtains and, in the back room, a window full of dress forms wearing monkey coats in every stage of completion.

"Geez, lady," said Baboon Bill, stepping out from behind a curtain of pelts, soft-faced, cocking his head. "Don't you knock?"

Clarice couldn't see. He'd broken the lamps. She heard him breathing through his muzzle, getting closer, and felt her way along the closet branches, hands out, tail stiff — nine fingers, one trigger.

"PBI!" she called, and the world went green and thundering.

In the cellar, down a ladder and behind a wall of potting moss, the senator's daughter was still in the well of the old dry moat, having spent her captivity trying to bribe the guard — a teacup tamarin called Precious — with one raisin. Precious was a tamarin of principle, by which I mean none, but she was also loyal. When Clarice pulled the girl up over the lip, the tamarin rode her shoulder like a very small admiral, and the raisin went with them.

Afterward there were medals and photographs. Clarice stood in her little graduation sash, holding the fruit cup, and the phone rang.

"Clarice," said the maroon-eyed voice, from a beach cabana somewhere with no extradition treaty. "I'm having an old friend's banana stash for dinner. I do hope you'll forgive me. The world is more interesting with you in it — and with fewer customs inspectors. I have no plans to call on you, Clarice. The world being what it is, courtesy is the best I can offer. Ta-ta."

The line went dead. Somewhere far off, the langurs had stopped screaming, and Clarice — who knew now that you cannot save every monkey, only the ones in front of you — finished her fruit cup and went back to work.

That night, on her window screen, a moth landed with a little skull on its back. She let it be. It seemed only polite to flatter it first.
