# Pet Simiantary

*A parody of "Pet Sematary"*

Louis Creed took the troop-vet job in Ludlow because the fig trees were tall and the rent was small and he did not yet know about the path behind his new treehouse. Nobody knows about the path at first. The path introduces itself.

Jud Crandall, seventy-two, an orangutan with eyebrows like draft excluders, came over the first evening with a basket of fruit. "Mind the road," he said. "The Orinbanana trucks run it night and day, and they don't stop for anything smaller than a hay wagon."

That first semester, a young macaque named Victor Paskow ran out on the road chasing a runaway unicycle, and the trucks did not stop for him either. Louis held him as he died on the clinic floor, and the last thing Victor Paskow said, clear as a bell, was: "It's not real, is it, doc? The barrier is not crossed. Tell them. Tell them the barrier is not crossed." Then he was gone, and Louis, who had never believed in ghosts, spent the rest of the night being introduced to one.

One warm October afternoon, Jud walked Louis and the children up the path — a path that seemed to lean uphill even as it went down — to a little clearing ringed with dead-nettle, where a handmade sign bore letters carved careful and crooked into bark: PET SIMIANTARY.

"The kids who made it spelled it wrong," said Jud. "Back in the old days, and every generation since. Dead is dead, however you spell it." And Louis read the graves by dying light: BISSKET. A GOOD MARMOSET. 1968–1975. SMUCKY, HE WAS A GOOD BOY. Little animals, little graves. Ellie, six, held her capuchin, Winston — Church for short — a little tighter all the way home.

That night Ellie dreamed aloud in her sleep. "Paskow says the ground behind the simiantary is sour," she said, in a small voice not entirely hers. "Paskow says the barrier is not crossed, but it can be."

Louis did not tell Rachel about that, because Rachel had her own dead. Her sister Zelda had spent years dying badly in the back room — Rachel, fourteen, left to tend her — and had become, in family memory, something with a twisted neck and a laugh like a rusty hinge: Oz the Gweat and Tewwible. Rachel could not hear the word *dead* around children without going somewhere else. Rachel had gone somewhere else and had never fully come back.

Church was the first to meet the trucks.

Louis buried him in the ordinary ground, and Ellie cried for two days, and Jud, watching, made a decision that old apes should not make for young ones. "Come with me," he said. "Sometimes dead is better, Louis. But there's a place beyond — old ground, up past the simiantary. The bonobos buried their dead there, and the ground has gone sour, and I've no business taking you, and I'm going to."

They carried Church up past the dead-nettle, over a wall of stones that seemed mounded rather than built, to where the mist hung wrong, and buried him a second time; Louis laughed at himself all the way down — until Church came home the next morning, walking stiff-legged like a windup toy, smelling of the swamp.

Church was not evil, exactly. He was *off*. A banana left too long in the sun: same shape, wrong everything. He stole every banana in the treehouse and hid them, ripening, inside the walls. He bit the postman's shadow — only the shadow, but he meant the rest. He would sit for hours facing the corner, grooming the wall the wrong direction, and once, looking at Louis, made a sound like the rusty hinge out of Rachel's worst year.

"Sometimes dead is better," Jud repeated, until Louis told him to stop, because it was not better, and a male cannot un-know what he knows.

"You want a story, then," Jud said grimly, "and I'll give you the one I got. Timmy Baterman, back in '58. His father buried Timmy up there, and Timmy came home too. Sat in the kitchen three days. Knew things — things only the dead should've known, and said them out loud, cheerful as you please, at supper. The town went up that path with lanterns, Louis, and what they did I'll not describe, except that nobody came back down carrying anything." He looked up the path without turning his head. "That ground never was meant for our kind. The bonobos knew. They knew something else too, and they left."

Louis nodded and heard nothing, because by then he was listening to the trucks.

Then Rachel took her mother to Boston for a week, and Louis said the road was safe, and the kite was red, and Gage — two years old, a macaque toddler with a whoop of fur and a whoop of a laugh — saw his kite land on the far shoulder, and the Orinbanana truck saw nothing at all.

*The soil of a monkey's heart is stonier,* Louis had read once, and understood now, carrying his son up the leaning path by moonlight, past the misspelled sign, past the wall of mounded stones, to the sour ground. *A monkey grows what he can, and he tends it.* He buried his boy twice, and waited in the mist.

Gage came back wrong. Cold-furred, giggling at nothing, grooming his father's arm backwards, against the grain, the way you never groom anything you love.

"Oz the Gweat and Tewwible," said Gage, in a small, pleased, wrong voice — and no one in that treehouse had ever said it in front of him. "Zelda says hi, Daddy. She says you brought me to her playground."

Before dawn, wrong-Gage slipped out and padded up the lane to Jud's, and Jud, who was expecting him — having made his peace and put on his good coat — opened the door. What happened in that kitchen was short, and quiet, and old; and when Louis got there, Jud was sitting by the stove with his coat buttoned and his eyes closed, unhurt and un-haunted, and on the table lay a note in an orangutan's careful hand: *Buried nothing up there tonight. Told him the ground was full. Believed me — the little ones always believe an old ape. Check the clock, Louis.*

The grandfather clock said ten minutes to four. It had said ten minutes to four for sixty years.

Louis did not run from what came home with him. He took his son in his arms — the cold little body that was not his son and was, horribly, exactly his son's shape — and he carried him down the leaning path, past the stones and the dead-nettle and the misspelled sign, to the ordinary sweet ground below, where things that die stay put.

"Gone is gone, little man," Louis said. "The jungle has a circle, and I tried to bite my own tail off it. Forgive me."

He buried Gage a third time, in the good ground, and sat under the figs until dawn, doing the hardest thing a primate can do, which is nothing.

"Louis?" Rachel's voice, dawn-soft, behind him. "You're freezing. Come inside."

A small paw tugged his other hand. Ellie, wide awake, unafraid. "Daddy. Church ran away in the night."

"Yes," said Louis, looking up the leaning path, where mist was lifting off the sour ground. "I expect he did. Some things know when they're not wanted."

He took his family inside, and he never climbed that path again, and every October, when the dead-nettle bloomed, he checked the latch on the gate.

Just in case. Because sometimes gone is better, and the brave thing, the thing that takes every muscle in a monkey's stony heart, is letting it be.
