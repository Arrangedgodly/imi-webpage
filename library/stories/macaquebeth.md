# Macaquebeth

*A parody of "Macbeth"*

Thunder rolled over the canopy, and on a storm-bent branch three aye-aye sisters hunched together, tapping their long eerie fingers in the lightning flash.

"When shall we three meet again?" they crooned to one another. "In thunder, lightning, or in rain?"

"Fair is foul, and foul is fair. Hover through the fog and filthy lair!"

Below marched two generals of the Troop of the Tall Fig: Macaquebeth, golden-furred, golden-hearted, and Baboonquo, brave and grizzled. The sisters shrieked with laughter and hailed them from the storm.

"All hail, Macaquebeth, Thane of the Lianas! All hail, Macaquebeth, King of the Canopy hereafter!"

"And Baboonquo! Thou shalt get monkeys, though thou be none!"

"What monkey nonsense is this?" muttered Baboonquo. "Fewer than three monkeys have ever gotten monkeys, and I intend to keep it that way." But Macaquebeth said nothing, because inside his chest a small banana-shaped seed of ambition had just been planted.

When King Duncoconut named him Thane of the Lianas — just as the sisters promised — Macaquebeth wrote at once to his wife. Lady Macaquebeth read it by lantern-fruit light and frowned: her husband was too soft for the second half of the prophecy.

"Yet do I fear thy nature," she murmured. "It is too full o' the milk of monkey kindness. Come, you spirits that tend on murderous thoughts! Unsing me here! Take my banana milk for gall!" She rose and shook her fist at the storm.

When King Duncoconut came to stay in their great fig-hollow ("This tree hath a pleasant seat! The air smells of mango and honesty!"), Lady Macaquebeth simply observed that the old monkey was hogging the top branch — and the top branch, by rights, was Macaquebeth's. She drugged the fig juice of the two guarding baboons herself, humming a lullaby.

"Look like the innocent banana blossom, dear husband," she whispered, "but be the serpent underneath it."

That night Macaquebeth stood frozen in the dark, staring at the fruit knife in his paw. "Is this a fig-knife which I see before me, its handle toward my paw? Thou marshall'st me the way that I was going..."

"Are you coming or not?" hissed his wife, who was very practical.

The deed was done quietly, and the guards slept through it in every sense. But Macaquebeth came back carrying the king's favorite golden fruit, unable to put it down.

"I have done the deed. But I could not say 'Amen' when I most needed blessing! Macaquebeth has murdered sleep — I shall nap no more!"

"Consider it a retirement plan," said Lady Macaquebeth. "A little sap clears us of this deed. Go wash your sticky paws."

Down at the gate, meanwhile, a drunken old porter macaque banged on the trunk, answering himself for sport: "Knock, knock! Who's there, i' the name of Beelzebub? A fig-farmer, who hanged himself on the expectation of plenty! Knock, knock! Who's there? A grooming-sponge, that turned the whole troop itchy and cold!" — and it was through this nonsense that the thane MacDuff entered in the morning, went to wake the King, and came back white to the ears.

"Confusion now hath made his masterpiece!" cried MacDuff. "Most sacrilegious murder! The King's fruit — I mean, the King — is DEAD!"

King Duncoconut was buried with figs and honors; the princes, smelling a plot, fled at once; and Macaquebeth was crowned King of the Canopy, where he sat smiling tightly at his feast, trusting nobody, because a stolen branch is a lonely branch.

He remembered the sisters' other prophecy — *thou shalt get monkeys, though thou be none* — and decided Baboonquo's descendants were a problem. Ambushers fell upon Baboonquo on the dark path home; but in the scuffle Baboonquo's little son Fleabane wriggled free and escaped into the night. Remember him. The prophecy did.

That very night, at the great banquet, Baboonquo's ghost slid into the seat of honor, dusty and drooping, a coconut-shell crown on his see-through head.

"Avaunt!" screamed Macaquebeth. "Quit my sight! Let the earth hide thee! Thy bones are marrowless, thy blood is cold!"

"Sit down," hissed Lady Macaquebeth, tugging his tail, "and stop yelling at the empty stool."

"The empty STOOL!" shrieked the King, pointing at precisely the wrong branch. The guests stared at their king, who was apparently berating the furniture. "The feast is sold," muttered one thane, "when the king himself sits not," and the whole court dispersed early, which no monkey feast had ever done before.

Desperate, Macaquebeth sought out the aye-aye sisters, stirring a fruit-bowl cauldron: "Double, double, toil and trouble — mango mash and mango bubble!"

They raised him apparitions. A helmeted coconut warned: "Beware MacDuff, the Thane of Fife!" A sticky baby monkey giggled: "None of monkey born shall harm Macaquebeth!" And a sapling-crowned chimp sang: "Never vanquished be, until Great Banyan Wood climbs to Dunsinane Hollow!"

"I am invincible!" laughed Macaquebeth. "No monkey born of monkey can touch me — and what monkey can make a forest climb?" But then a fourth vision passed before him: a show of monkeys, eight and eight and eight, each wearing Duncoconut's crown — and at the head of them, golden-grizzled, grinning, came Baboonquo's unbroken line.

"Horrors!" howled Macaquebeth. "Thou other gold-and-grizzled one! AVAUNT!"

"Show," said the sisters, and vanished.

He should have paid closer attention to everything. MacDuff had already fled to the lowlands, howling for justice, and Macaquebeth's raiders sacked his nest while he was gone. MacDuff's family escaped in time — but the fig stores were ash, and MacDuff's grief was a terrible thing to hear across the valley. An army gathered.

At Dunsinane Hollow, meanwhile, Lady Macaquebeth began to sleepwalk, grooming her own arms over and over by lantern-fruit light while her doctor and her gentlewoman watched from the door.

"Out, damned spot!" she scrubbed. "Out, I say! Who would have thought the old monkey to have had so much sap in him? The Thane of Fife had a wife. All the perfumes of the mango-lands will not sweeten this little paw! Wash your paws. Put on your nightcap. To bed, to bed, to bed."

"This disease," said the doctor, quietly packing his leaves, "is beyond my medicine. She needs a healer of the heart, and I do prescriptions."

Macaquebeth alone in his watchtower had no heart left to heal. "Tomorrow," he said to nobody, "and tomorrow, and tomorrow, creeps in this petty pace from day to day, to the last syllable of recorded time. Out, out, brief banana! Life's but a walking shadow, a poor player that struts and frets his hour upon the branch — a tale told by an aye-aye, full of screech and fury, signifying nothing."

"The queen, my lord, is dead," a servant whispered.

"She should have died hereafter," said Macaquebeth, who could no longer feel it, and that was the worst of it.

Then the watchman came running. "My lord — as I did stand my watch upon the hill, I looked toward Great Banyan, and methought the WOOD BEGAN TO MOVE!"

"Liar and slave!" Macaquebeth spat. "If thou speak'st false, thou shalt hang alive upon the next tree till famine cling thee!"

But it was true. MacDuff's army of wronged monkeys had each cut a bough of Great Banyan Wood to march behind, and from Dunsinane it looked exactly like a forest climbing. When the gates broke, Macaquebeth fought like a mad thing, still chanting his charm.

"Beware MacDuff!" he cried, swinging. "But none of monkey born shall harm me!"

"I'll show you a footnote," said MacDuff, stepping forward. "I was born in a zoo, delivered early by the keepers' own hands — from my incubator was I timely plucked! No monkey born of monkey, Macaquebeth. Me."

"Accursed be the tongue that tells me so!" howled Macaquebeth, and swung anyway. They fought branch to branch until the false king fell, and his golden crown rolled down the hill like a runaway banana.

"Hail, Malchimp!" the army roared to Duncoconut's son, "King of the Canopy! The time is free!" And MacDuff, holding the crown aloft, added the moral that the whole troop would teach their grandchildren: "Beware the prophecy that tells you what you WANT to hear — for that one always keeps a little something back."

And far away, three aye-aye sisters tapped their long fingers in the lightning and agreed that fair was foul, and foul was fair, and that ambition unhitched from conscience is the fastest way down a tall tree — and, they cackled, grizzled golden Baboonquo's line was doing very nicely, thank you, and would get monkeys indeed, forever and ever, crown after crown, all the way down the ages.
