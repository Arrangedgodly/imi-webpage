# Monkey Screech

*A parody of "Monkey Beach"*

The week we buried my grandmother's boat hook, I started sleeping with the windows open, because that's when the little man comes — if he comes — singing up off the water in a language older than the village. My grandmother heard him before her sister died. I heard him three days before Uncle Mick. And I heard him, this spring, and I did not tell anyone, because I was not ready to know which of us he had come for.

My name is Lisa. I'm a Haislangur girl from Kitamaat, on the coast, and if you've never heard of us, that's fine; we've heard of you. We are the monkeys of the inlet, the beach people, the fish smell and the fog. Ma-ma-oo used to say we've been here since the ice, and the ice remembers.

Here is what I knew in the spring, though I wouldn't say it out loud: my brother Jimmy was on a fishing boat out of Prince Rupert, and the boat had gone down off Banks Island, and they had found the skipper and they had found the hull, and they had not found Jimmy.

The official word was missing. Missing is a word like a door left open. Wind comes through it for years.

Jimmy was the good one. I don't mean good like obedient — I mean good like the center of a tree. He was two years older, quiet the way deep water is quiet, and he was the only one who never looked at me funny when I said I saw things. When I was six I told him there was a man with no eyes standing at the end of the dock, and Jimmy said, "Tell him the herring aren't running yet." That was Jimmy. He treated the spirit world like weather: real, inconvenient, no reason to panic.

Ma-ma-oo treated it like a toolbox. My grandmother could read the water, the crows, the dreams. She taught me to bake 'nannock — banana bannock, golden on the outside, our one great improvement on the old recipe — and she taught me the rules. If you hear the little man singing, someone is going to die, or someone already has, or you do. If you see the B'gwus, the old man of the woods, and he isn't angry, it's a gift, and if he is angry, you run, and either way you don't put it on Facebook.

"The world is more than the world," Ma-ma-oo said, rolling dough with her strong hands. "That's all anybody's trying to tell you with the stories. The rest is decoration."

She died in a fire the winter I was thirteen. I stood on the snow in my pajamas and couldn't move, and afterward I went a little wild. There's no story in that; every girl in Kitamaat has a version. Mine involved a lot of fighting, a certain amount of going ape at parties, one very bad boyfriend named Josh, and a night on the beach with my friend Pooch when we screamed at the ocean until the ocean screamed back. It was getting dark, and Jimmy carried me home on his back, most of the way, humming, and he never once said, you're embarrassing us. He said, "You scream like the old ones, Laees. Save it for something that matters."

Then he took the fishing job, because there was money in it, and because he wanted to see the coast, and because you can't keep the good ones on the beach forever. Nobody keeps anybody anywhere, that's the first thing the inlet teaches you.

So: the spring I turned nineteen, me and Pooch and Frank — big Frank, gentle Frank, who had known Jimmy since they were in diapers together — took Ma-ma-oo's old boat and ran it north, up the inside passage, to Monkey Screech.

Monkey Screech is nothing much: a curve of white sand under a wall of dark trees, a place the old people wouldn't camp. Even the gulls avoid it at dusk, when the cliffs give back every sound you make one beat late, like a stranger imitating your voice. My name for it when I was small was the crying beach, because I thought the screech was crying, and Ma-ma-oo said, "No, Laees. It's singing. That's worse."

We came in at slack water and anchored. Frank set the kettle on the little stove. Pooch looked at the trees and said nothing, which for Pooch was a eulogy.

I told them why we'd really come. Not fishing. I wanted to find the B'gwus. I wanted to ask the old man of the woods whether my brother was alive on the other side of those trees, or alive some other way, or just — I said the word out loud for the first time since the phone call — gone.

Frank said, "Okay." Pooch said, "Okay." That's what friends are: the ones who say okay.

I went ashore alone in the grey rowboat, and I walked the beach, and I did the thing Ma-ma-oo taught me never to do on purpose. I listened.

The screech came up out of the cliffs at dusk, and under it, the way a bass runs under a melody, was the little man's song. I can't give you the words. There aren't words; it's the sound a person makes in a dream when they're trying to tell you something through glass. I stood in the cold sand with the fog coming in like a tide of ash, and I saw him at the edge of the trees: small, green-dark, hair like wet kelp, tilted head, patient eyes. Not the B'gwus. The other one. The one who comes for people.

Behind him, sitting on a driftwood log in the last light, was Jimmy.

He looked the way he always looked, like the inside of a quiet morning. He didn't get up. On the other side you don't hurry; there's no point, is what I understood, without him saying anything.

"Laees," he said — and the screech carried his voice the way water carries a boat, from a long way, and gently. "You can't come yet."

"I can," I said. "I'm not doing anything over there."

"You're doing everything over there," Jimmy said. "You just don't like any of it yet." And he smiled at me the way he used to when I was small and furious, and he said the thing I've decided to organize my life around: "Save it for something that matters."

The little man turned, and the fog turned with him, and my brother went into the trees, and I did the hardest thing I have ever done, which was not to follow. The water was right there — grey, calm, deep, open-armed. I want you to know that. I stood at the hem of it with my feet in the cold, and the song pulled like an undertow, and I turned around.

Pooch was waiting in the rowboat. She'd been watching the beach for an hour without moving.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay," I said.

We drank tea out of enamel cups in the cockpit, all three of us, and Frank told a story about Jimmy and a seagull and a sandwich, and I laughed until I made a sound like the cliffs make, and Pooch held my hand in both of hers, hard, the way you hold something you're not done with.

In the morning we ran south with the ebb, home to Kitamaat, where the village was stringing the smokehouse racks and my mother would cry and make too much food and my father would say nothing beautifully. The inlet was flat and silver, and the trees stood along the shore like all the people who ever lived there, watching us pass. That's one thing you can say about this coast: it looks back.

I still leave my window open. I still listen. Sometimes, on the worst nights, I hear him — or I decide I do, and Ma-ma-oo never explained the difference, because there may not be one. The little man sings. Somebody somewhere is dying, or has died, or will.

Not tonight, he doesn't sing tonight. Tonight the ocean just breathes, and I put out my hand in the dark and touch the warm fur of my own life, going on, and I save it for something that matters.
