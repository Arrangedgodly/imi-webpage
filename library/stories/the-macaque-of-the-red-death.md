# The Macaque of the Red Death

*A parody of "The Masque of the Red Death"*

The "Red Rump" had long devastated the jungle. No pestilence had ever been so fatal, or so hideous, or so embarrassing. Redness was its avatar and its seal — the redness and the horror of a blushing, itching rump. There were sharp pains, sudden dizziness, and then profuse staining upon the fur about the tail — and none who wore it could sit, or stand, or be comfortable in any position whatsoever.

But Prince Prospero was happy, and dauntless, and sagacious. He was a golden lion tamarin of enormous wealth and enormously larger opinion of himself, and when his dominions were half depopulated, he summoned a thousand hale and lighthearted nobles of his troop and retired with them to the deep seclusion of one of his fortified kapok lodges: seven levels of platform along one great branch, east to west, linked by vines so hung that none could cross without passing all the way through. The provisions were plentiful — bananas baked, fried, and flambéed at table — and there were buffoons, ballet-dancers, grooms to groom, and gibbons. Without was the Red Rump.

When the last of his troop had come up, the prince had the vine-ladders drawn in after them and the trunk sealed with gum and proclamations. The proclamations said that the Red Rump was a matter for the outer jungle, and that the outer jungle, in the prince's considered opinion, could look after itself. It was widely agreed, below, that this was the finest thing anyone had ever said about them.

It was toward the close of the fifth or sixth month of his seclusion that the prince entertained his thousand friends at a masked ball of the most unusual magnificence.

There were buffoons, there were improvisatori, there were ballet-dancers, there were musicians, there was a gibbon on a unicycle whom nobody pressed for explanations. There was much of the beautiful, much of the wanton, much of the bizarre, something of the terrible, and not a little of that which might have excited disgust — which, in a troop with no mirrors, went entirely unnoticed. The prince's own taste was for the bizarre. His dreams wore the same costume, and those of his guests followed the decor as dreams will: dreams of falling fruit; dreams of reaching for a liana and grasping a snake; dreams in which one sat all night upon a cactus, in formal dress, being applauded.

Each of the seven platforms was given its own color: the easternmost hung violet; the second, blue; the third, green; the fourth, orange; the fifth, white; the sixth, violet again. But the seventh, at the western end, was hung in black — black palm-cloth upon black walls — and the panes here were scarlet, a deep blood color, so that anyone who walked through took on, in the western light, a hue so unsettling that no lady monkey would set her tail in it. The platforms were so disposed, with vines and sudden turns, that no reveler could pass from the first to the last without winding through all the rest — a design the prince found uproariously funny and his architect found prophetic. In this western chamber stood also the great coconut clock: a huge clock of polished shell, whose pendulum swung with a dull, heavy, monotonous clang; and when the minute-hand made its circuit, there came from the coconut throat a sound clear and loud and deep — bong — bong — bong.

And every hour, when the chime struck, the orchestra was constrained to pause; the waltzers stilled; the gibbons gasped mid-aria; and a strange discomfort spread from reveler to reveler like a bad smell at a picnic, while every masked face turned, in spite of itself, westward, toward the black. Even the prince grew pale at each striking, and covered it, after, with louder orders for music. Then the chime died, and the band lifted again, and the laughter came back like a monkey who has forgotten why he fell. But it came back a little thinner each hour, as laughter does when it has been wound up too long.

The gongs sounded midnight; and then, with the last stroke, the attention of the company was seized by a figure among them which none had observed before.

A buzz went along the platforms, from mask to mask: who had invited it? Nobody had invited it. It had no invitation, no branch, and no name; and every monkey in the lodge knew, in the way one knows a ripe fruit by its smell, that it had been the first to arrive at the ball and would be the last to leave.

It was a new guest, tall and gaunt, shrouded from head to foot in the habiliments of a red-rumped macaque. The mask concealed every feature; the costume, red from brow to haunch, was stiff with red tissue-paper; and where the rump should have been bulged a great crimson cushion that swayed, horribly, in time with the music. With the Red Rump raging without, such a masquerade was a matter of extremely poor taste. Even the unicycle gibbon got off and walked.

Prince Prospero convulsed — first in terror, then in outrage. "Who dares?" he demanded hoarsely, brandishing a coconut-husking spike. "Who dares insult us with this blasphemous mockery? Seize him and unmask him — that we may know whom we have to hang at sunrise from the highest branch, by his own tail!"

The figure turned, with slow and stately movement, and retreated westward through all seven platforms, the terrible cushion swaying all the while; and no paw was laid upon it, for a nameless terror had seized all who looked. Through the violet it went, where the cushions were; through the blue, where the dancers scattered like startled butterflies; through the green, where the parrots fainted in their perches; through the orange, where every flushed face was shown at its worst; through the white, where the company could bear to follow no farther — and so, at last, into the black, where the figure halted in the shadow of the great coconut clock, and turned, and stood.

Then the prince, maddened with rage and shame, rushed after him alone; but as the spike fell from his grasp, clattering upon the black palm-cloth, the prince felt a sudden warmth about his own haunches, and his fur flushed scarlet from hip to hip, and he fell all his length in a swoon, glowing gently in the dark like a rearview sunset.

Then the company, in the resolution of a thousand nobles, rushed into the black chamber and seized the figure where it stood — and they gasped and let go, for there was nothing to seize. The red tissue-paper fell to the floor. The crimson cushion deflated gently, with a sound like a sigh. There was no monkey inside — not a hair, not a bone, not even a flea. The mask stared, empty. And the Red Rump, having come like a thief in the night, held illimitable dominion: one by one the revelers dropped in the despairing posture of their fall — each clutching, unanimously, at his own rump. And the life of the coconut clock went out with that of the last of the gay.

And Darkness and Decay and the Red Rump held illimitable dominion over all.

(The jungle doctor later recorded the outbreak as stress-related. But nobody in the lodge sits on that western platform after dark, and the unicycle gibbon, who lives, will only say that he has retired.)
