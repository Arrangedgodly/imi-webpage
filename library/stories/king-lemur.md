# King Lemur

*A parody of "King Lear"*

King Lemur of the Bristlewood was old and tired, with a magnificent ringed tail and an even more magnificent opinion of himself. One evening he called his three daughters before him on the Grand Branch.

"Give me the map there," he declared, unrolling a leaf with great ceremony. "I have decided to divide my canopy among you — so I can crawl off to a sunny fork and take my ease. But first: which of you shall we say doth love us most? The richest third goes to the finest speech."

Gonerilla laid a paw upon her heart. "Sir, I love you more than words can wield the matter — dearer than ripe figs, warm grooming, and liberty itself! I love you beyond the reach of any tail!"

"That's my girl. A third of my canopy!"

Reganja curtsied. "I am made of the self-same metal as my sister, and she comes up a fig short. I find she names my very deeds — but I love you beyond beyond!"

"Beyond beyond! Another third!"

Then he turned to his youngest and best-beloved. "Now, my pretty Coralia, what can you say to draw a third more opulent than theirs?"

"Nothing, my lord."

The Grand Branch went very quiet.

"Nothing? Nothing will come of nothing — not even a banana peel! Speak again!"

"I love your majesty according to my bond; no more, no less," said Coralia gently. "You are my father, and I honor you. But my sisters said they love you above all things — why have you kept their husbands, then, if they love you above all things? Speak plainly, and love plainly. I cannot heave my heart into my mouth."

"So young, and so untender!"

"So young, and true."

"TRUENESS!" howled King Lemur, tail lashing like a whip. "How sharper than a serpent's tooth it is to have a thankless child! Out of my sight! Banished! — A third more opulent than THEY, is it? We'll see how much an empty speech warms you at night!"

His old friend Kentry the macaque stepped forward, quaking but stubborn. "See better, Lemur," he said, "and let me still remain the true blank of thine eye. That youngest daughter does not love you least, and those empty speeches are not worth the breath they rode in on."

"What, sir, do you bandy words with your king? Out of my sight! Banished, on pain of death — GO!"

So Kentry smeared himself with mud and returned within the week as a servant named Caius, loyal monkeys being resourceful monkeys. And Coralia left without her third and married the King of the Lowland Gibbons, who thought her the only honest primate in the canopy.

King Lemur, meanwhile, learned about calendars: two daughters with branches, one father with a visiting schedule. First he stayed with Gonerilla, whose troop grumbled at his fifty followers — fifty monkeys who gobbled, hooted, and groomed at all hours. Her steward tipped a bowl of figs on the ground on purpose, to make the old king's monkeys look like pigs. Then Reganja's household did the same, and worse: when his retainers screeched at her servants, she locked the fig stores.

"MY retinue reduced?" raged the old king. "Darkness and devils! Saddle my monkeys! I will have it ALL: my full fifty!"

"Why, Father," said Reganja sweetly, "you are old. Naps and soft grooming become you. What need you troop-mates? O, reason not the need!"

"O, reason not the need!" cried King Lemur. "Our basest beggars are in the poorest thing superfluous! Allow not nature more than nature needs, and a monkey's life is cheap as a beast's! You will take away my fifty? Then — then — I shall go mad!"

And go he did, into the worst monsoon in memory, with only his Fool, a bushbaby with a bell, at his heels.

"Blow, monsoon, and crack your boughs!" howled the old king on the open hillside, while the rain fell in ropes. "You cataracts and coco-nuts, spout till you have drenched our treetops! You think I'll weep? No, I will NOT weep! I am a monkey more sinned against than sinning — though I have done some sinning, mind you, and I know where the ledger is kept!"

"Thou shouldst not have been old," observed the Fool, from inside a dry hollow, "till thou hadst been wise. Nuncle, here's a riddle: what has a crown at one end and a fool at the other? Thy tail? No — thy canopy, divided, with thee sitting at neither end."

"That's not a riddle, that's an audit," said King Lemur, and laughed, and wept, and did not know which.

They sheltered in the hollow with a mad beggar named Poor Tom — young Edgar-gibbon in disguise, fleeing a leaf-scroll forged by his rotten half-brother Edmound to turn their father, old Gloster the galago, against him. ("A forged scroll! 'Dear Father, when I have the canopy, you shall have the sunny fork' — as if I would WRITE that! I can barely write!") King Lemur studied the shivering figure and pronounced the finest thing he ever said:

"Is man no more than this? Consider him well. Thou art the thing itself — unaccommodated monkey, no more but such a poor, bare, forked-tailed animal as thou art. Off, off, you lendings!" And he tore off his crown of leaves and gave it to Poor Tom, one mad beggar crowning another.

The old king then held a court. "Let us arraign the false daughters!" he declared. "Thou, robed man of justice, take thy place — Fool, thou shalt be judge; here's a gourd for thy gavel. Arraign Gonerilla first! What's the crime? Nothing, you say? Then she shall have — nothing! JUSTICE. Where are his daughters? Bring them before the gourd!" The Fool banged it solemnly and acquitted everybody of everything, which helped no one, but the old king slept afterwards.

And it was in those weeks that Poor Gloster was caught helping King Lemur. For this, Edmound and Reganja's troopers gummed his eyes shut with fig-sap. "See't shalt thou never," they told him. But Gloster, stumbling, only said: "O, my follies! Then the lesson begins. So now I see it feelingly." And he stumbled off toward Dover-vine, guided by his disguised son, who let him believe he had leapt the great drop there and survived. "Thy life's a miracle," Edgar whispered, and gave him his arm, and added the only comfort the jungle affords: "Monkeys must endure their going hence even as their coming in. Ripeness is all." Blind Gloster nodded. "As flies to wanton monkeys are we to the gods," he sighed. "They pluck our tails for their sport."

And far away, Coralia heard the whole sorry tale, and raised an army of gibbons.

By the time she landed, Gonerilla and Reganja had fallen out over Edmound — that rotten banana — who had made each of them the same promises, in the same pawwriting, which neither had thought to compare until the trophies did. Gonerilla mixed Reganja a smoothie of the wrong berries entirely; Reganja, fading, drank Gonerilla's smoothie by mistake; and the sisters poisoned each other's health with matching straws. Meanwhile Edgar-gibbon, risen from his mad beggary, sounded a conch before the camp and challenged his half-brother: "Let the gourd decide." They fought, branch to branch, and Edmound fell — and falling, confessed: "The wheel is come full circle. I am caught in my own netting. Listen — I sent the falconers. I ordered the nets. It was for Lemur and Coralia both. Despite of mine own nature, some good I mean to do."

It was indeed too late — but not in the way he meant. The rescue came too late for the old king's liberty: zoo-keepers had already netted Lemur and Coralia and shipped them to the Sanctuary by the Sea. The Fool, who had stayed behind at the holly tree to mind the kettle, was never caught at all, and is said to be minding it still.

King Lemur woke in the enclosure and found a familiar small face watching him through the vines.

"Is this the Sanctuary? Am I captured?"

"And so I am, well met," said Coralia, kneeling by him. "Do not laugh at me — I am a very foolish fond old monkey."

"Mend when thou saw'st me last," said Lemur, touching her face very carefully, as if she might evaporate. "Pray you now, forget and forgive. I am old and foolish. I know you do not love me, for your sisters have done me wrong. You have some cause — they have not."

"No cause, no cause," said Coralia.

Lemur looked around — the feeders, the ropes, the keeper rolling a barrel of fruit up the hill, the sea shining beyond. "Come, let's away to this cage," he said, taking her paw, and for once in his whole ringed-tailed life meant it. "We two alone will sing like monkeys in the cage. Upon such sacrifices, my Coralia, the gods themselves throw bananas."

And they did sing, ringing the bars like bells, in an enclosure with fig trees, warm ropes, feeding times regular as tides, and no speeches required of anybody — while Edgar-gibbon, at the Sanctuary gates, spoke the epitaph for the whole mad troop:

"Speak what we feel, not what we ought to say. The oldest hath borne most. We that are young shall never climb so high, nor groom so long, nor love so plainly — as this."
