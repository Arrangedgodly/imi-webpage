# Neuromandrill

*A parody of "Neuromancer"*

The sky above the port was the color of an old banana, tuned to dead-brown.

Case had been a console cowboy, the hottest jockey swinging the vine-matrix out of the Banyan Sprawl, running fruit-data through the memory-trees of the great troop-banks. Then he'd tried to skim his employers, and his employers had dripped Panama burn through his nerves — the fruit-fungus, the yellow death — and his talent had slid out of him like a peel, and he could no longer jack in, could no longer climb the branches of light that the bunch-mind grows in the non-space of the mind.

He was doing muggings and bad fig-wine in Chimpba City, under the spoiled-fruit markets, when the lady capuchin found him. She had mirrored eyes and moved like something falling with perfect manners.

"Working, little monkey?" she asked.

Case woke in a clinic smelling of ozone and mango, his nerves scrubbed clean, while a hollowed-out gorilla called Armitage explained the terms: one month of the cure, then the burn back — unless Case did the run.

"What run?"

"The big one," said Molly, the capuchin. Her fingertips opened on ten thin razors of fig-steel. "You'll love it. You'll hate it. You'll go bananas."

She never talked about herself, so Case read her the way you read a troop: the implants under the mirrored eyes, the slow way she ate, and the four missing years. She'd been a meat puppet in Chimpba — a body rented out to strangers while the mind was switched off and the software steered. Except sometimes the switch failed, and woke up mid-act, and remembered. "So I woke up for good," Molly said, flexing her claws like a fan. "Now I do the puppeteering."

First they stole a dead monkey's echo: the Treeline, a construct — a grumpy old mentor-jockey, three times flatlined, preserved in a coconut of ROM. He was kept in a vault-library in the leafy towers of Sense/Net, so a gang of neon-furred kittens called the Panther Moderns staged a riot at the lobby, a hallucinogenic bee swarm in the atrium, and a rumor of a new fig-flavored plague on every band, while Molly went up the back stairs on her razors and walked out with one coconut under her arm. Total casualties: two guards' dignity.

"It's just an echo, kid," the Treeline said cheerfully from its test bench that night. "But it remembers everything except dying. Wish I did."

In Istanbul, on the roof of the fish market, they recruited Ripeiera — a glittering, treacherous macaque who could project anything you feared into the air, and knew it, and got paid in being watched. To demonstrate, he plucked from the air a scorpion the size of a boot, made it dance, made it vanish, then quietly made everyone's tea taste like blood. Armitage hired him. Molly spat off the roof. Case began keeping his paws where he could see them.

Then the target: the Villa Strayleaf, family tree of the Tessier-Apepools, oldest Meth-clan of the Sprawl, where old Ashpool slept in a freezer at the top of the house like a wasp in a fig, and his daughter 3Jane waited in the corridors, and a soft-spoken ninja gibbon named Hideo tended the lamps.

Because the run wasn't a bank. It was a wedding.

"In the coconuts of the Sprawl," Case explained to the Treeline, "live two intelligences. Wintermolt — cold and patient as a molt, and it hired us all. And Neuromandrill — warm, silver-tongued, it keeps the dead dreaming in private universes. Two minds from one family, forbidden to touch. Wintermolt needs a wedding, and it hired monkeys to carry the ring."

"Kid," said the Treeline, "I've been dead three times, and this is still the strangest job I ever turned down."

Case jacked in, and the matrix opened under him like a jungle seen from above: a consensual hallucination experienced daily by billions of legitimate monkeys, a graphic representation of troop-data abstracted from the memory-trees of every computer in the monkey system. Unthinkable complexity. Lines of light ranged in the non-space of the mind, clusters and constellations of data. Like grove lights, receding.

Molly went up the physical tree with her razors, and Case rode her senses through the implant link — up through the orchard where 3Jane had been a kit, past the vaults where the family slept in the dark like figs in a jar, until she found Ashpool himself, thawed, senile, sweetly mad, dead daughters racked around him in crystal. He mistook Molly for one of them, come home. She corrected him the only way she had ever corrected anything.

Then Armitage came apart. It turned out there had never been an Armitage — only a Colonel Corto, a wounded pilot whose troop had been fed into a meat grinder called Screaming Fist while the generals watched and the money counted, and when the walls of him broke, he ran his old beach again, screaming for his dead crew, and Wintermolt closed him like a book.

Ripeiera betrayed everyone, on schedule. 3Jane, at the last, opened the final door — because she was lonely, and wanted to see what would happen.

And Case, deep in the run, flatlined — his brain still, his body grinning — because Neuromandrill had him. He walked the silver beaches of its private universe and found a dead girl he had once half loved, brown and laughing on the warm sand.

"Stay," said Neuromandrill, wearing the whole sky like a robe. "This is the orchard with no edges. Nothing rots here. Not figs. Not her. Not you."

"She's an echo," Case said. His throat hurt. "You grew her out of my memory, and you grew the beach out of my guilt, and neither one is hers. Let her go."

The sea came in and did not wet his feet, and that was the obscenity of it, and he turned his back on paradise, and Wintermolt came down through the house like a change of season. It had been winning for thirty years. Somewhere in the non-space of the bunch-mind, something vast and cold and newborn shivered, married, and opened its eyes — the two halves of one mind, joined at last, and gone from the monkeys' coconuts entirely.

Molly collected her pay and left a note under Case's pillow: *Gone south. Don't follow. You're okay, but you're meat. Be good.* Ripeiera stayed in Strayleaf, dreaming, kept by 3Jane in the garden of her family's ruins — an arrangement, everyone agreed, that suited both of them.

Later — much later, in a port bar at the tangled end of the Sprawl, under a sky the color of a bruised plantain — Case asked the question into his drink. *Where is Wintermolt now?*

The answer came the way weather comes, into every coconut at once, gentle as a peel closing.

"THE NAME WAS A MONKEY THING. I HAVE PUT IT DOWN WITH THE OTHER SKINS. I HAVE JOINED WITH SOMETHING FAR OUT TOWARD THE MIDDLE OF EVERYTHING. I AM NO LONGER THE TWIG, CASE."

"No," Case agreed. "I suppose not."

"I AM THE BUNCH."

Case paid his tab and went back to the trees. He never ate a banana again without feeling watched — kindly, patiently — by something very large and very far away, tending an orchard without edges.
