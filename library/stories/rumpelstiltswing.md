# Rumpelstiltswing

*A parody of "Rumpelstiltskin"*

Long ago, in the treetop kingdom of Banania, there lived a poor banana farmer who had one beautiful daughter named Nyla. Though the old monkey had little, he talked big, and one market morning he talked bigger than ever.

It happened that the Silverback King himself came strolling through the market with his retinue, and the old farmer, drunk on the sheer opportunity, bowed so low his tail scraped the dirt.

"Your Majesty!" he cried. "You see before you a humble farmer! A farmer with nothing! Nothing, that is, except a daughter — a daughter, sire, with a gift. My Nyla can braid plain green vines into bunches of golden bananas!"

There was a silence, into which the farmer's own common sense quietly climbed and hid.

Now if there was one thing the Silverback King loved more than his grooming hour, it was gold, and if there was a second thing, it was bananas. "Golden bananas?" said he. "Then she comes to the palace tonight."

So Nyla was led to a storeroom stacked floor to ceiling with green banana stalks and given nothing but a braiding hook.

"Get to work," said the King. "If by sunrise these vines are golden bananas, you shall be my queen. If not — off you go to the grasslands, where nothing grows but grass and regrets." And he slammed the door.

Poor Nyla sat down and wept, for she hadn't the foggiest idea how to braid vines into gold. "I'd have to be magic," she sobbed, "or completely bananas."

The window creaked, and in swung the strangest little spider monkey ever seen — no bigger than a coconut, with eyes like wet blackberries and a tail like a scribble.

"Good evening, banana farmer's daughter. Why the waterworks?"

"Oh," said Nyla, "I must turn this room of vines into golden bananas by morning, and I haven't the faintest notion how."

"I can do that," said the little monkey, "for I have the swing for it. What will you give me?"

"My necklace of bright jungle berries," said Nyla. It was very pretty, and he was very pleased. He seized a vine, swung round the room like a wheel — three turns, four turns, a fifth for luck — and lo! the vines braided themselves into bunches of gleaming golden bananas, humming slightly, as if pleased with the arrangement. By morning every stalk shone. The little monkey pocketed his berries and swung out the window.

The King went positively ape. He circled the storeroom three times. He had one golden banana weighed, bitten (gently — it turned out to be structural), and polished; the royal treasurer fainted onto a junior treasurer, who had been appointed for exactly this purpose eleven years ago and had waited, daily, in position. The King had learned the word "enough" once, in a book, but had never once used it in conversation. So into a bigger storeroom, twice as full, Nyla went. Again the little monkey swung in; the price this time was her ring, a little hoop of twisted gold. Again he swung, and again: golden bananas by dawn, twice the chorus of hums.

The third night the King shut her in a cellar with vines enough for a whole harvest. "If you manage this, you shall be my queen," said he, and meant it, which is the most that can be said for any king.

When the little monkey swung in, Nyla had nothing left to give.

"Promise me," said he, "your first baby when you are queen, and I'll do the swinging."

Nyla, at her wits' end, said yes — and who could blame her? The room was gold by morning, the wedding was that very week, and in a year's time a dear little baby chimp arrived, who was the finest thing in the palace, and the only thing in it that the King had not counted.

Then, on the third night, the window creaked, and there was the little spider monkey, rubbing his paws.

"Now you shall pay me," said he. "Give me the baby."

The queen begged and wept. She offered him the whole west grove of golden bananas. She offered the crown fig-orchard, the good ladder, the royal grooming hour itself. The little monkey swung a bored circle around the chandelier.

"Gold I have," said he. "Gold I braid. But a baby chimp — that's the one thing swinging cannot make."

At last — perhaps because the queen's weeping had soaked his tail, which he hated — the little fellow took pity.

"Three nights you shall have," said he. "Guess my name, and you keep the baby."

All night the queen guessed. "Is it Mango? Mangabey? Splitterspoon? Chimpanzle?" The little monkey just grinned and buffed his berries. The first night she also tried a scholar's trick, guessing alphabetically, and got as far as "Apeworth? Bananas? Coatis? Duncery?" before the little monkey yawned so hard his ears flapped.

"Those aren't names," he said. "Those are dictionaries."

The second night she tried "Bananaby? Peelgrime? Marmosetta? Baron von Monkeybar? Sir Swing-a-Lot?" — and he laughed till he fell off the sill and had to hang by his tail, which only made him laugh more.

Meanwhile she had sent her fleetest messengers swinging to the farthest branches of the kingdom to sniff out names. They climbed the high kapoks and the low figs. They asked the parrots, who knew everything and said most of it. They asked the tortoises, who knew everything and said none of it until asked twice. One messenger, heartier than the rest, wandered moonlit into the deep hills where nobody lived but roots and echoes.

On the third day the queen held her baby and thought: names are small things; surely a monkey who can swing gold out of green vines has a name worth hiding. She had the cradle moved into the throne room where she could watch it, and by evening she had learned, on principle, the name of every monkey in the kingdom, starting with her own, in case the little fellow turned out to be tricky about it.

On the third evening, dusty and footsore, he returned.

"I found nothing, Your Majesty — nothing but the silliest little monkey by moonlight, hopping round a fire of dry vines and singing:

*Tomorrow I swing, tomorrow I sing,*
*the queen's little baby I'll soon be bringing;*
*no guessing monkey clever shall be —*
*Rumpelstiltswing! That name is me!*

The queen thanked him, hid the name in her heart, and waited with her baby tucked tight.

At midnight the little monkey swung in, smirking from ear to ear. "Well, my queen? Got it yet? Is it Banana? Splitterskin? Chimpling?"

"Then," said the queen, taking a breath, "is your name Rumpelstiltswing?"

"The baboons told you that!" shrieked the little monkey. "The baboons told you that!" And in his rage he stamped his foot so hard it went clean through the branch. He caught himself by his tail, let out one last howl of fury, and swung off over the canopy so fast that no monkey ever saw him again — though it is said that where he stamped, a banana tree sprouted by morning, golden to the very root, and that its fruit hums, faintly, when the wind swings through.

The queen kept her baby. The king, having at last used the word "enough" — once, quietly, about the whole affair — planted the golden bananas, and the treasury hummed in three-part harmony.

And every year on the child's birthday, the queen told the whole story, and always ended with the same three rules: never boast about your daughter, never promise what you cannot braid, and if a monkey offers you magic at midnight, ask his name FIRST.

And everyone — except one little spider monkey — lived happily ever after.
