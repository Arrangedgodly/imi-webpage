# A Series of Unfortunate Descents

*A parody of "A Series of Unfortunate Events"*

If you are interested in stories with happy endings, you would be better off swinging toward some other tree.

The Banaudelaire chimps — Violet, fourteen, who tied her fur back with a vine whenever she invented something; Klaus, twelve, who had read every book in his parents' fig-leaf library; and Sunny, an infant whose four teeth could bite through a coconut — received terrible news at Briny Beach, delivered by Mr. Poe, a proboscis monkey whose enormous nose arrived several seconds before the rest of him.

"Your treehouse has burned to the ground," said Mr. Poe. "Your parents, I am sorry to say, were in it. Cough. You will stay with my family until we find you a new guardian."

And so began the descents. Their first stop was the branch-house of Count Olaf, a baboon with an eyebrow like a dead caterpillar and a tattoo of a staring eye on his left rump. His home had bricks where the windows should be, a tower the children were forbidden to climb, and a troupe of actors who dressed like bats and smelled worse.

"I am your guardian now," Olaf said, baring his teeth in something that was technically a smile. "You will cook dinner for my theatrical friends. Nine courses. And if anything goes wrong, remember —" he glanced up at the tower — "I have the little one."

He meant Sunny. That night Sunny slept in a birdcage dangling from the tower window, and Violet and Klaus scrubbed pots until their paws cracked.

Their only comfort was the neighbor: Justice Strauss, a kindly gibbon with long silver arms and a private law library. While Klaus read everything about trusts and inheritances, Violet rummaged through Justice Strauss's shed for parts. And Olaf, meanwhile, unveiled his plan with all the subtlety of a falling branch.

"I have cast you, Violet, as the bride in my new play," he announced. "The Marvelous Marriage. A real minister. Real vows. A real document to sign. Just theater, of course."

Klaus read all night. In the morning his glasses were gray with dust and his voice shook. "It isn't theater. If Violet signs that document before a real judge, the play is a legal wedding. And then, if anything happens to you, your husband controls the fortune."

On the night of the performance, Olaf locked Sunny in the tower cage and stationed his henchman — a capuchin with a hook where his tail should be — at the door. But Violet had spent the evening in the shed, and what she made there was a grappling-vine: a claw of bent forks tied to forty feet of braided rope, which she hurled over the tower's lowest branch and climbed, hand over hand, in the dark.

By the time the curtain rose, Sunny was safe under Justice Strauss's arm — for the gibbon had come, at Violet's request, to play the minister.

"You may kiss the bride," said Justice Strauss.

"May I?" Olaf cried. "I MAY!" He turned to the audience and howled with laughter. "The document is signed! The fortune is MINE!"

"Is it?" said Violet, stepping forward. "You asked me to sign with my right paw, and I did — except that I am left-pawed, which my brother learned from my birth certificate, which he read last night. A signature isn't valid unless it's the paw you truly use. Ask your lawyer."

Olaf's lawyer looked very hard at the ceiling. Somewhere in the dark, the hook-tailed capuchin quietly let himself out a window.

"That's not — she can't —" Olaf sputtered, and then, seeing Mr. Poe's enormous nose swing his way, he seized his cape. "You haven't seen the last of me, Banaudelaires!"

And he descended — down the tree, into the ferns, and away — while the three children held on to one another, which is what Banaudelaire chimps did best.

"Surely now things will improve," said Justice Strauss gently.

They would not. I am sorry to tell you that Olaf was only the first guardian, or rather the first disguise, in a series of unfortunate descents, and that Mr. Poe's nose, for all its grandeur, could not smell a villain in a costume.

There was Uncle Monty, a kindly mandrill herpetologist with a roomful of snakes, including one he had proudly named the Incredibly Deadly Viper, on the theory that a terrifying name keeps burglars away. Uncle Monty planned to take the children to Peru and let Sunny handle the reptiles, which was the happiest fortnight of their lives. Then his new assistant arrived — a pale baboon with a pepper-dusted beard who called himself Stephano and could not answer simple questions about snakes. Klaus asked them anyway. By the time the children proved Stephano was Olaf, the baboon had fled into the ferns, and Uncle Monty had gone somewhere from which no snake charmer returns. The children inherited his notebooks, and a lifelong wariness of assistants.

There was Aunt Josephine, a widow langur so frightened of the world that she had removed the doorknobs (dangerous), the radiators (terrifying), and the telephone (unthinkably rude), and lived on cold banana blancmange above a lake famous for its leeches, which could smell a single drop of banana at forty yards. When a cheerful stranger called Captain Sham offered to take the children off her paws, Aunt Josephine signed so fast the pen smoked. Violet, Klaus, and Sunny sailed the lake themselves to intercept her, baited the leeches with blancmange, and unmasked Captain Sham as — need I say it — but the boat was leaking, the storm was rising, and by dawn they had lost the house, the aunt, and the argument, though not, crucially, each other.

There was the lumber camp, where the face in the reception window — a "young typist" named Shirley with one visible tattoo of an eye and an eyebrow like a dead caterpillar — turned out to be, according to the foreman, "a perfectly nice girl, and I'll hear no more about it." The foreman heard more about it. The children escaped on a log flume.

Along the way they collected what the adults around them refused to see: that Olaf always wore a disguise, that the hook-tailed capuchin was always near, and that each of their guardians' homes hid, scratched into the woodwork, the same three letters: V.F.D. Klaus found them in Monty's snake cages, Violet in Josephine's window frame, Sunny — with her four teeth — in the rungs of a ladder. What the letters meant, no adult would say, and the children were just beginning to understand that the not-saying was the whole point.

In a dreadful boarding school they met the Quagmire triplets, who were two — a brother and sister whose brother and parents had been lost in a fire that the Banaudelaires found far too familiar. The Quagmires had notebooks too, and a theory, and the letters V.F.D. underlined twice, and then one gray morning they were gone — bundled into a net, dragged over a hedge, and carried off in a red truck driven by, I regret to report, an eyebrow with a baboon attached.

The Banaudelaires chased the truck on a rolling backpack of Violet's invention. They lost it at the toll fig.

And here is the thing, the terrible, useful thing: Mr. Poe coughed. The police yawned. Justice Strauss was outvoted, the foreman was paid, and the boarding school lost their file. Not one adult in the whole descending world would do the one simple thing, which was believe two children and an infant with excellent teeth.

So they stopped asking.

"If nobody will be our guardian," said Violet, tying her fur back with a fresh vine, "then we shall have to be each other's."

"That's not how it works," said Klaus, who had read the relevant books.

"No," agreed Violet. "That's how WE work."

They set off down the road with the notebooks in a bundle, the baby on Klaus's back, and the letters V.F.D. in their pockets like a lit firefly — three orphans looking for two, hunted by a baboon, employed by nobody, and armed with a vine, a memory for books, and four teeth.

And that is why this account is called a series of unfortunate descents — because it kept going down and kept going on — and why, if it's a happy ending you're after, you really should keep swinging. Perhaps there was one at the bottom. There was, at least, a next branch, and on it, three Banaudelaires, together.
