# Chimpnapped

*A parody of "Kidnapped" by Robert Louis Stevenson*

I will begin the story of my adventures with a certain June morning when I took the key for the last time to the door of my father's nest in the Lowland figs. My name is Davie Balfour; I am a chimp of seventeen, lately orphaned, with no notion of the monkey business ahead of me.

My father's last word had sent me to the House of Shaws, a great treehouse, where I found my uncle Ebenezer — a hard old chimp who lived alone with one candle, and gave me porridge from a wooden bowl, counting every spoonful as he gave it.

"Did ye ever climb the tower stair, nephew?" he asked that night, sly as a tick. "Mind your feet."

I climbed, in the dark, and found the stair wanting its whole last flight; only my grip on a rung saved me. My uncle, I saw, had peeled the step out over my head — and would happily peel me out of my inheritance next. I knew then that the house, and the nut-harvest, and the whole estate, had somehow been mine, or my father's, before ever it was his.

Two days later he walked me to the shore to see a brig take cargo — the Covenant, a log-built ship of the leaf-sail trade, captained by one Hoseason, a scarred drill-baboon with a face like a cliff in rain. I was admiring the rigging when the world went dark about me: a sack of woven vines over my head, a rope at my elbows, and my uncle's voice on the quay, counting out nuts.

"Twenty," he was saying, "and cheap at the price. Bind for the coconut colonies, Captain, and sell him there."

So I was chimpnapped, and carried out to sea, to be sold in the colonies as a planter's monkey — with no word to any ape, and no friend aboard but the rats in the hold. Yet even a bad ship carries one decent ape: the mate, Mr. Riach, who slipped me water and a dry corner out of the rain when Hoseason wasn't looking. Sailors, I learned, are two apes in one skin, and you never know which one you have until the weather changes.

Aboard the Covenant I met Alan Breck Stewart, a small, bonnie bonobo with a coat of red seed-pods and silver buttons, and a hickory stick he handled like a lightning bolt. Hoseason meant to rob him; Alan knew it; and that night he made his stand in the roundhouse, with me for his second.

"David," said he, "ye're slow but ye're leal. Stand at the door, and let me see the scoundrels that dare put a paw upon a gentleman."

They came fifteen strong, and Alan fought like a dancing master of death, singing the while in the howling fashion of Appin, until the deck was strewn with groaning macaques and the captain cried quarter. I loaded his sticks for him (he threw them like a gibbon flings figs), took a slash across the knuckles, and was, for one night, a soldier.

"Ye fight well for a small ape, Alan," said I after, when we shared the last of the plantains.

"There are two things a bonobo never refuses," said Alan, "a ripe plantain and a fair quarrel. And now we'll drink the king's health — the King over the water, and the little that's good in his kingdom."

The Covenant went down at last on the mangrove bars, and Alan and I were cast away — I upon a tidal isle of mussels and midges, where I starved among oysters for three days, calling it a desert island, until Alan found me laughing from a hilltop on the mainland. "Are ye there, Davie?" he called. For the isle and the shore were joined at low water — a fact I might have learned by walking in any direction, and did not, being seventeen.

Then we set out across the heather for Appin, Alan's country, with Alan wanted by the law and me wanting only a lawyer. In Appin we saw the Red Squirrel, Colin of the Copses — the Crown's rent-collector, who took the harvest of every glen and left the clans in rags. We had barely passed when some hidden hand pelted him across the head with a moldy chestnut, and he fell into the fern.

"Alan!" I cried, in horror. "They'll say it was you!"

"The man will be blamed," said Alan grimly, "that has the gift of being blamed. The world thinks Alan Breck threw every chestnut since the Flood. Run, Davie!" (And indeed, though the jungle argued about it for a hundred years, the paw that threw that chestnut was never proved to this day.)

We ran through the bracken with the Red-rump soldiers on the hill, lying in the heather till the flies were mad, drinking from burns by night and sleeping in hollows by day. We were held one night at the cage of Cluny — a chief's hiding-nest hung in a great chestnut, where Cluny, keeping royal state in exile, diced us at cards until he had won all my nuts, my spare shirt, and the loan of my comb, and Alan thought it the finest evening of the summer.

And in the wood at last we quarreled — over my foolish tongue and his wild cause — and for days we climbed side by side without a word, two prouder apes never sulking under one canopy. Then, one evening, Alan played a lament on his bone whistle for three hours — a mournful, penitent, thoroughly manipulative air, "I am a poor wee ape and I am sorry, Davie, Davie, Davie" — until I laughed, and we were friends again before the last note.

At last, in the town, we found Mr. Rankeillor, an old gibbon advocate, who heard my story of the stair, the sack, and the twenty nuts, and nodded as though he were hearing a very old song with new words. The estate, he told me, had been my father's before ever it was Ebenezer's: two brothers, one nut-grove, and one she-chimp between them; the clever brother got the grove, the better brother got the wife — and the quarrel over it had outlived them both.

"Then we shall call upon your uncle," said he — and then he removed his spectacles and rubbed them slowly. "For the walk, I find I have quite forgotten my glasses. A lawyer must be careful what he sees."

We caught Ebenezer in his nest, the lawyer hidden below in the bushes, and I called up out of the dark that I had come about the estate — and about a certain tower stair, and a certain twenty nuts paid on a quay.

"Wha — what'll ye want?" quavered Ebenezer, peering into the night with his one candle.

"Either your nephew in the heather," said I, "or your nephew in the courts. Mr. Rankeillor will be glad to advise which is dearer."

"Twenty nuts a year to the lad, and — and the courts will ruin us all!"

We settled for two-thirds of the yearly nut-harvest, and no courts at all; and Mr. Rankeillor put his spectacles back on, having seen nothing the whole while.

Then came the parting. Alan took the outer isles for France; I took the low road for the lawyers, to speak for poor James of the Glens, who was blamed for the chestnut as Alan was. We stood a moment, hands clasped, two friends made by one shipwreck.

"Well, goodbye," said Alan, and turned up the brae whistling; and the whistling died away into the wind.
