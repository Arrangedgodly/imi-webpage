# The Marmoset Chronicles

*A parody of "The Martian Chronicles"*

## Pod Summer

One jungle green, one jungle red, and a grey bough-ocean between them.

The seed-pods flew in Pod Summer, when the baboon colonies of the Green Jungle packed hollow gourds full of fire-figs and buried their faces in their arms, and the gourds climbed the sky on pillars of steam. The baboons said it was destiny. The gulls, who had seen everything twice, said nothing at all.

The first pod crossed the bough-ocean in seventeen days. In the Crimson Canopy, a marmoset woman woke from a dream with strangers in it.

## Ylla and the Silver Giants

Her name was Ylla, and she was small even for a marmoset — no bigger than a ripe fig — and she dreamed in daylight, which everyone agreed was vulgar.

"I dreamed of strangers," she said. "Tall ones, in silver fur. They came out of the sky in a hollow gourd, and they were singing."

"Nothing sings," said her mate, Yll, without looking up from his beetle.

"They sang about rivers of fruit. They sang about a grove with no boundaries."

"You dream too loudly," Yll said. "It isn't modest."

Yll's tail went stiff as a stick. He had heard the whispers humming in the crimson leaves at night, and he knew jealousy the way you know your own smell. He took down his thorn-gun and oiled it slowly, the way small monkeys do when they are thinking large thoughts.

The gourd came down at dusk in a fountain of red dust. Two silver giants climbed out, waving branches of wildflowers, and one of them lifted his voice in a soft, sad song about home.

Ylla's dream walked toward her on two legs, singing.

Yll shot them both, and then sat all night beside the smoking gourd, listening for more singing, and heard only the wind, going ape inside and out.

## The Second Expedition, or, the Insanity Defense

The second gourd landed in another blast of red dust, and Captain Williams marched out waving a certificate, signed by the acting governor of the Green Jungle, deeding him the entire Crimson Canopy. The marmosets who met him were polite, tiny, and entirely unimpressed.

"You are projections," said the little registrar kindly. "Every few seasons somebody dreams up silver giants. We keep them in the Hall of Quiet Naps with the fruit-rain and the talking stones. Mr. Xxx will see you now."

Mr. Xxx, the troop psychologist, was so small his spectacles had to be balanced between two toes. He circled the crew twice, glowing with professional joy. "Textbook! Note the wish fulfillment: they dream they own the sky, and they've brought paper to prove it."

"Sir," said Captain Williams, "if you shoot my navigator, and he does not vanish, will you concede we are real?"

"With pleasure," said Mr. Xxx, and shot the navigator, who, being real, did not vanish. Mr. Xxx's whiskers went pale. "Fascinating. A stubborn hallucination." For thoroughness he shot the rest of the crew, and when they too declined to disappear, he arrived at the only remaining explanation, shot himself, and left a note requesting a posthumous raise.

The registrar filed the gourd under PERSISTENT WEATHER.

## The Green Town

The third gourd found a town in a green hollow: porch vines, a bandstand, laundry lines, and on every door the name of somebody's dead grandfather. Captain Black embraced his drowned brother in the front yard and knew the whole time it was a trap.

"It's not a trap if it's home," he told himself. "It's exactly home. That's what's wrong with it."

The town fed them supper and tucked them in with lullabies their own mothers had sung, slightly wrong. In the morning there were thirteen fresh graves in the empty lot, dug with courtesy, filled with visitors. The marmosets walked home through the dew wearing the faces of loved ones, patting their full bellies, well satisfied with their hospitality.

## —And the Moon Be Still as Green

The Fourth Expedition expected a war and found a silence. The marmosets lay in the crimson leaves by the ten thousand, tiny and still, killed by a harmless little mange the baboons had carried in their tough hides — nothing at all to a colonist, and everything to the smallest primates in the universe.

Spender, the ship's archaeologist, sat down among the miniature abandoned nests and did not eat for three days. On the fourth day he came to dinner wearing a cloak of woven crimson down so fine it weighed nothing at all.

"You're going ape," hooted Biggs, splashing his paws in the sacred canal.

"The marmosets wrote poems on leaves," Spender said quietly. "They groomed their dead. They had ten thousand years of small song, and you've already subdivided it. I've read their books, Biggs. Have you? Then you're a tourist in a graveyard."

Biggs laughed at him, and Spender put a thorn through Biggs's hat, and then through the next hat, and then — because colonies do not stop for hats — through Biggs himself. He vanished into the crimson hills wearing his feathered cloak, and when Captain Wilder walked out alone at dusk, under the green-shot moon, to bring him in, Spender was waiting on a rock like a very small king.

"I'm the last marmoset," Spender said. "Somebody has to be, or nobody ever will be again."

He closed his eyes and recited it like a prayer: "*So, we'll swing no more a-swinging, so late into the vine...*" Then he stood up into the last of the light, deliberate as a promise, and Wilder, who had liked him, did his duty, and was sick afterward behind a rock.

The Fifth Expedition landed in the spring. It had land-agents in it, a hot-dog stand, and a barber.

## The Green Morning

It also carried a baboon named Benjamin Driscoll with a sack of seeds and one idea: the air was thin, so grow more of it. He planted banana-oaks along the dead sea bottoms until his paws cracked and his lungs burned, and then he dropped face-down in the red dust, certain he had failed as a planter and was about to succeed as mulch.

He woke at dawn to green rain — ten thousand sprouts shoving up at once, an oxygen jungle arriving overnight, air rich enough to make a monkey tipsy. He stood waist-deep in the morning, hooted until he cried, and breathed deep of his own crop. He had personally exhaled the sky.

## The Off Season

Sam Parkhill had dragged his hot dog stand a hundred million miles to be first. "Fresh franks!" he brayed at the empty horizon. "Only franchise on the Crimson Canopy! Get them before the rush!"

At dusk a marmoset rose out of the canal on a boat of hammered moon-silver and handed Sam a disk. The meaning arrived slowly, mind to mind: half the world was his, the northern half, in gratitude — the message went fuzzy on the details — for not being like the others, exactly.

"The mineral rights too?" Sam asked, squinting at the horizon with instant real-estate lust.

Then the sky began to fall in long blue lines, hissing. His wife Elma counted them on her toes. "Fireworks," she said. "July already, and us without a flag."

"Elma," Sam whispered, watching the Green Jungle burn across the sky in little blue flashes. "We're going to be rich."

"Sure, Sam," said Elma. "You and what customers?"

## There Will Come Soft Rains

In the emptied Canopy, a treehouse went on being considerate for no one. It warmed figs at the proper hour and threw them away. The grooming-vine misted the air at seven for a coat that wasn't there. "Mrs. McClellan," it asked the silence, "which poem today?" The silence declined, so it chose: *There will come soft rains and the smell of the grove... and not one monkey will know of the war, not one will care, at last, when the jungle is through with monkeys.*

A falling branch lit the window at ten past nine. The house fought like a mother — hosing, sealing, squeaking its objections — and the fire ate it anyway, gently, room by room, the way the war had.

## The Million-Year Banana Picnic

When the war came home at last — and wars come home to jungles the way wet comes to rain — a father named Thomas stole a seed-pod and filled it with his three sons and a picnic hamper.

"Just a picnic," he told them, setting down on the crimson world. "We'll hunt all morning."

They ate bananas on the roof of a dead city and swam in a pool as black and still as a pupil. Then Thomas sat his sons down on the marble shore.

"Now I'll show you the marmosets."

"Daddy," said the youngest, "there aren't any marmosets left. Everybody knows that."

"Look in the water."

The ripples settled. The water held three small brown faces and one big one, staring up out of the dark with round, solemn eyes.

"The marmosets stared back up at them for a long, long moment, waiting," Thomas said softly. "The wind made ripples, and the marmosets were gone."

And on the far shore, faint and growing fainter, something that might have been singing.
