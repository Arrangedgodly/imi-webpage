# The Climb Machine

*A parody of "The Time Machine"*

The Time Chimp had gathered the Troop Club around the parlour fig tree, passing fermented fig-juice and honeyed bark, when he made his announcement.

"Four dimensions. Length, breadth, thickness — and *duration*," he said, tapping his own grey chest. "A monkey moves through time exactly as he moves through the canopy. We are always climbing out of the past into the future. I have merely built a saddle."

"Speak plainly," said the Psychologist, a mandrill of creeping habits and creeping fingers.

"Very well. I can climb time." He pressed a lever, and a small glittering model — nickel frame, brass vine-work, a seat no bigger than a walnut — flickered, wobbled, and vanished. The Psychologist, who had pushed the lever himself, swore he felt nothing and saw everything. The Club roared with laughter anyway and called it a trick. The Medical Marmoset advised sea air.

The following week they came to dinner and found the parlour empty, the great Climb Machine gone, and the Time Chimp himself staggering in from the garden, fur scorched and wild, limping on one bleeding foot.

"Bring me the fig-juice," he croaked, "and I'll tell you where I've been."

He had pushed the lever, he said, and the laboratory walls had dissolved like fog. Days flickered past like startled bats. He watched his housekeeper macaque cross the garden at impossible speed, a streak of agitation. He watched his house decay into green ruin; he watched the jungle swallow the city; he watched the sun itself swell. Then the machine slammed to a halt in the year 802,701, in a garden of enormous flowers, under the shadow of a colossal white gorilla of stone — wings folded at its sides, eyes blank as old bone — and there his troubles began.

"Before I had even eaten, I went back to where I had left the machine," he said. "Gone. Vanished into the pedestal of the stone gorilla as if the stone had swallowed it. I confess, gentlemen, I went a little mad. I ran in circles. I shouted at flowers. It was days before I remembered that I had pocketed the two little control levers before stepping out — so that whoever held my machine held a paperweight. And then I stopped panicking and started thinking, which is the harder climb."

The jungle, he said, was one enormous cultivated fig-garden. He couldn't believe his luck. He wasn't alone.

Small, delicate monkeys came down the lianas — round-eyed, sweet-faced, red-furred creatures in purple weaves, graceful as falling leaves and about as energetic. They did not climb. That was the first wrong thing. They ambled. They lounged. They fed him fruit from a great stone trough in some ruined hall, and they giggled, and they had no more curiosity than a peeled banana. He asked them where his machine had gone and they laughed as if he had asked where the wind slept.

"Among them I made a friend," the Time Chimp said softly. "A little female. I called her Weena. She had fallen into the shallow river — in plain view of twenty of her own troop, floating, going down, and not one of them moved. Not from cruelty. It simply did not *occur* to them that anything should be done. I fished her out, and after that she gave me a garland of flowers, and she followed me everywhere, and she was the only one of her whole troop who could be *troubled* about anything. Being troubled, I was learning, is the whole of civilization."

At night, he learned the second wrong thing. Down below the garden earth, in the shafts of a vast underworld, lived a second troop — pale, gray, big-eyed apes who worked clanking machines by lantern-light and kept the troughs full. The canopy-folk called them, in their silly little singsong, the Root Folk.

"I had supposed the jungle-folk were the masters and the Root Folk their servants," the Time Chimp said. "Balderdash. It was the other way, and worse. The Root Folk were farmers. Weena's people were the *crop*. I descended one of the wells alone, like a fool, by matchlight, and saw their halls: tables, machinery, and joints of meat. There are no cattle in that future, gentlemen. There are only the fed and the feeding."

He escaped up the shaft by a hair and spent a feverish day at the Palace of Green Porcelain — the far future's museum, a vast derelict of rusted monsters and shattered glass cases, chemical galleries, an armory of decayed weapons, and, in a mineral hall that had kept out the damp for eight hundred millennia, two things of price: a case of matches, firesticks to these soft people, and a metal bar to serve as a lever and crowbar. He loaded Weena's arms with flowers and set out to pry open the pedestal of the stone gorilla and go home.

"And then they came for us in the dark."

The Club had stopped drinking. The Time Chimp's voice went flat and quiet.

"Root Folk, all around, in the black of the forest. Gray eyes in the dark, like mice watching from wainscoting. I had matches. I struck a whole box at once and set the undergrowth alight, and the ring of fire became a wall, and the wall became a forest fire, and in the light of it — I got the pedestal open. I got the levers in. And Weena—" He stopped. He looked at his hands. "I lost her. She was behind me. Then she was nowhere. The fires I started — she would have been terrified of them from the beginning, if her people could still feel terror long enough to remember it."

He went on, because the Club was dead silent now. He had pushed on into the far future, to a grey shore under a swollen, dying red sun, where giant coconut crabs the size of oxen clicked across the freezing mud, and a vast dark thing like a football with tentacles heaved itself along the tideline. No monkeys. No birds. Only silence, the crash of waves, and a solar eclipse that turned the world the color of an old bruise. In that light, dying, he had understood the whole audit of creation: life, spreading outward and upward, and then the tide going out. He had fled back to the lever, and to now, and to fig-juice.

"Two troops," he said. "One soft and fed above, one hard and hungry below, each turning the other into furniture and food. It is the law of the jungle we are always becoming, my friends: comfort without work is just husbandry with extra steps. Weena's people were not defeated by the Root Folk. They were *harvested* by their own ease, one nap at a time."

The Medical Marmoset opened his mouth, and shut it again. On the table between them, the Time Chimp laid out, of all things, two withered flowers — strange white blooms, pressed flat, monkey-made paper showing more art than anything he had described — and would only say that Weena had put them into his pocket when he was not noticing.

The Time Chimp then took the fig-juice, three white bananas, and his little camera into the garden, where the Climb Machine stood glinting under the fig leaves. The Club waited.

He has not come back. That was three years ago. The fig tree still grows. The machine has not returned. Sometimes, in the stillness after sunset, you can see the narrator — I — out there with a lamp, grooming the lawn where he vanished, and waiting. I have two flowers pressed in a book, and I find I cannot quite believe he is dead, and I cannot quite believe he is alive, and so, like every monkey who has ever lost someone to time, I wait for both at once.
