# Macaque on the Shore

*A parody of "Kafka on the Shore"*

## 1.

On his fifteenth birthday, Kafka Tamurin ran away from home.

The night before, the boy called Caw had talked to him out of the back shelf of his skull, the way he always did. "Somewhere ahead of you a storm of sand is waiting," Caw said. "Not the kind you watch from the veranda. The kind you're inside of. You'll come out the other side as a different monkey, and you won't get to approve the design."

"My father cursed me," Kafka said. "You know the curse. Someday I will kill my father. And hold my mother in my arms, and my sister too."

"A curse from him is a monkey stamping his feet at the sea. But the storm doesn't care whose fault it is."

Kafka took his savings and his backpack and boarded a night bus to the south, intending to become the world's toughest fifteen-year-old macaque.

## 2.

Some hundreds of miles north, in a quiet shopping district, an old monkey named Nakata was talking to a cat.

"I'm looking for a tortoiseshell called Goma," Nakata told the cat. "She's been missing three days. Her owner is very worried."

"Nakata's not very bright," Nakata added, as he always did. "I'm not good with words. But I can talk to cats."

"So I gather," said the cat, a plump tabby named Kawamura. "You want the smart one. Otsuka, by the shrine. The Siamese says he's insufferable, but informed."

Otsuka, an old cat with white whiskers, listened to Nakata's trouble with his eyes shut.

"Goma is close," Otsuka said at last. "But not in a good way. There's a man in the empty lot at the end of the lane. He comes at dusk in a red coat and top hat, struts like a trademark. Calls himself Johnnie Branchwalker. He has Goma in a basket. Nakata — he is not a monkey to trust."

"I don't trust anybody," Nakata said politely. "It saves time."

"Then go carefully. And if you must do what I think you'll have to do — do it all at once. Half measures are worse with his kind."

## 3.

In Takamatsu, Kafka found the Komura Library, a beautiful old building of dark wood, built by a silk-fortune family who had since made a larger one in bananas. High ceilings, deep chairs, old paper and floor wax: the smell of a place where nothing bad can happen, which is the smell of a place where everything bad is already over.

He met Oshimpan first: a slim, elegant black-furred librarian of devastating intelligence and zero patience for nonsense, who confiscated his backpack, observed that Hemingway was not a lifestyle, and then looked at him — really looked — until something in the young macaque's face closed one conversation and opened another. "Talk to Miss Saki," Oshimpan said.

Miss Saki ran the office upstairs: fifty-ish, silver to the shoulders, beautiful the way a shoreline is beautiful after rain. She poured Kafka barley tea, and when he asked about the old 45 turning softly in her office, she smiled.

"I wrote that song, once. It was called 'Macaque on the Shore.'"

"Was it a hit?"

"For one summer. Then the summer ended, and so did everything else." She gazed at nothing Kafka could see. "The singer died young. It's just a record."

That night Kafka woke at two in the morning and saw a girl of about fifteen at the desk by the window, long hair, summer dress, staring at a painting on the far wall — a monkey sitting on a shore, gazing at the water. When he blinked, the chair was empty, and the room smelled of sea salt, though the sea was twenty miles away.

## 4.

In the empty lot, at dusk, Nakata found Johnnie Branchwalker: red coat, top hat, cane, a basket at his feet from which a small unhappy miaowing came.

"Ah, the famous Nakata," the man said, with a bow that managed to be an insult. "Do you know what I'm building? A flute. From cat skulls. When it's finished it will play a note that makes every cat in the world walk into my collection. Everything I do, I do to complete the collection." He tilted his head. "You want the tortoiseshell. Come take her. Or — watch."

And he showed Nakata what he was.

Nakata, who had never in his long slow life struck anything, took out the little knife he used for paring fruit and did what Otsuka had told him to do, all at once.

Then he walked home through the dark with the basket under his arm and his hands shaking, and behind him the sky over the empty lot began, impossibly, to rain fish: sardines, hundreds, flickering down through the streetlights and slapping the asphalt, while dogs howled and the man in the red coat did not get up.

By the time Nakata reached his little apartment, the clouds had squeezed out one last time, and leeches fell softly on the whole district, hissing on the summer pavement like fat drops of rain in a nightmare.

Nakata swept his walkway and put out the garbage and made himself a rice ball. "Nakata is tired," he told the empty room, accurately. That night, far to the south, Kafka Tamurin woke in the guest room of the Komura Library with blood on the chest of his shirt and no memory at all of how it got there.

## 5.

Nakata could not read, but he recognized weather changing, and the morning after the fish and the leeches he closed his apartment door forever and set out walking, because his legs knew something his head did not. On the road he met a young truck-driver macaque named Hoshino, in a Chunichi Dragons cap, who had never met anyone like Nakata.

"Dude," Hoshino said, "you're going the long way around. Let me give you a ride. Where are we headed?"

"Nakata doesn't know," Nakata said brightly. "But we'll know the stone when we see it."

The stone was waiting in the overgrown grounds of a shrine two hundred miles on: a black, fatly oval boulder with a pale seam around its middle, like something designed to open.

At a crossroads that had no business having traffic, they got directions from a tall white-suited gentleman with a goatee, grinning with total commercial sincerity.

"Colonel Sanders," Hoshino said flatly.

"I'm a concept," the gentleman corrected. "A pure idea wearing a suit — which makes me the only honest being you'll meet this week, since I never pretend to be anything but vending." He sold Hoshino, at extortionate rates, a map to the shrine and a comment on Kierkegaard.

Hoshino opened the stone. It took two days of cussing and wooden wedges, and at dusk on the second day the pale seam widened with a groan like a ship changing its mind, and the air that came out smelled of every library, every hospital, and every beach at once.

"Okay," said Hoshino, who understood nothing and accepted it, which is its own genius. "Now what?"

"Now," said Nakata, "we close what was opened, return what was taken, and then Nakata will be very sleepy. Will you make sure I don't dream anything unfinished?"

## 6.

That week, Miss Saki began to fade. She'd been fading for decades, Oshimpan said; she had only stayed to wait for something, and no one had known what.

The girl in the summer dress came back twice more, always at two a.m., always staring at the painting, and Kafka understood, the way you understand in dreams, that she was Miss Saki at fifteen, still watching that shore for someone never coming out of it. One night she put her cool hand on his head as if he were hers, and it was the gentlest thing that had ever happened to him.

"The curse is a riddle," Caw said, on the last night. "Kill your father: your father's rage dies the day you walk out of his life — you did that with a bus ticket. Hold your mother: a memory laid her hand on you tonight. Little monkey, the prophecy was never going to be paid in blood. It was always going to be paid in monkeys forgiving. Which costs more, by the way."

Miss Saki died in her sleep by the window and left the record to the boy with the hat. Nakata died too, in an inn two hundred miles away, at about the same hour, leaving a tidy stack of documents, which Hoshino burned in the shrine brazier as promised. As the papers went up, something long, white, and slug-shaped slid toward the entrance stone, patient as a rumor, and Hoshino killed it with a bamboo rake — the bravest, stupidest-looking heroic act in history.

"You stay shut," he told the stone. "Fried chicken ghost. Talking rocks. I'm going back to trucking."

## 7.

Kafka Tamurin went home to Tokyo, older by a summer, which at fifteen is a geological age. On the bus he said goodbye to Caw — not forever; a storm, once survived, becomes a companion of the landscape.

In his backpack: one change of clothes, one cell phone, and one 45 rpm record called "Macaque on the Shore," which he could play nowhere and did not need to.

The rain, when it came, would fall. The fish, when they fell, would be remarkable. He was still fifteen — going back, as the world's toughest fifteen-year-old macaque, to find out what that was going to mean.
