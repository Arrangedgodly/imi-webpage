# The Runaway Furry

*A parody of "The Runaway Jury"*

The trial of the year opened on the fourth floor of the zoo courthouse, where Celeste Would — a widow in her Sunday fur — was suing Banynex, maker of Smooth Peels banana-leaf cigarettes. Her husband had smoked two packs a day for thirty years and died of banana-lung, and Celeste wanted the record to show it.

Banynex wanted the opposite. To arrange matters they had hired Rankin Flinch, jury consultant, war-room operator, a monkey who could smell a verdict at forty paces. Behind the courtroom Flinch kept a bunker full of dossiers, surveillance footage, and young associates trained to know everything about everybody, down to their grooming preferences. His staff had run forty mock trials before jury selection began and lost the plaintiff's case in thirty-nine of them, which Flinch considered not a warning but a margin.

Selection took a week. Flinch's team struck anybody who had lost kin to banana-lung, subscribed to anything, or wept audibly during orientation. They ran the questionnaires through a model that scored each prospect for spite, and the model gave its highest approval to a young chimp with an unpainted apartment and a paper route's worth of charm.

"We've never lost one," Flinch told Banynex's lawyers, and it was true. In the jungle, that is called the law of the jungle.

The plaintiff's lawyer was Wendall Roar, a silverback with a pulpit voice and a genuine outrage. The defense was Durwood Flimflam. The jury was sworn: twelve monkeys, sequestered at the Hooton, the finest hotel the zoo could offer — twelve nests, one fig bowl, and the Continuous Coconut News Network — in the care of a bailiff whose only instructions were "feed them, water them, and don't let them read anything."

By the second week, everyone wanted to know about Juror Four.

Nicholas Feaster was a young chimp with a manner as harmless as a banana in its pajamas. He organized the jury's lunch orders. He petitioned the judge for movie night — and got it, over Flinch's strangled objection, the feature being *Twelve Angry Mandrills*, which the jury watched with the attention of a troop studying a rival troop's war council. Flinch, watching them watch it, said a word the bunker did not bill.

Flinch's dossier on Juror Four said: *harmless*. His diggers said more, or less: two years of tax returns, a warehouse job, both parents dead, and an apartment that had burned to the studs the month before trial, taking every record in it. An apartment fire, one of the diggers admitted, is a very thorough reference letter.

Then the she-monkey appeared: sleek, black-crested, calling herself Marlee. She visited Wendall Roar with an offer — the jury, guaranteed, for a fee. Roar threw her out with a speech about justice that Flinch's wiretap later enjoyed twice. Then, to be thorough, she visited Rankin Flinch with the same offer. Flinch, who believed everything was for sale because he had never once seen otherwise, demanded proof she could deliver.

"Certainly," said Marlee. "At 3:15 this afternoon the jury will send the judge a note asking to rehear the testimony of Banynex's own chemist, page sixty-one, the one about the ammonia bath. Watch."

At 3:14, the note arrived. Flinch read it twice, felt his spine go ripe, and opened negotiations — which ended, over four nervous days, with millions of banana notes wired to her branch accounts in the Caymango Islands.

Celeste Would testified for one morning: how she had met Burt at a troop dance, how he had smoked since the army, how the leaf came cheaper than the fruit in their neighborhood. She never cried. "I am not here for sympathy," she said. "I am here because somebody wrote *smooth* on the pack, and there was nothing smooth about the last two years." Then she was done. Flinch's own pollster clocked her as the most persuasive monkey in the building, and she had not been on the payroll a single day of her life.

The trial itself was a war of experts. Banynex's chemist, a marmoset with impeccable cufflinks, spent two days explaining that Smooth Peels contained no more tar than a salad. It was Wendall Roar who got the jury's ears up. "Page sixty-one of your own study, sir. The ammonia bath. Why does Banynex soak its leaf in ammonia?" "For flavor," said the chemist. "For *freebase*," said Roar, and twelve monkeys leaned forward at once, the way a troop leans toward a fig tree in a windstorm.

Flinch fought back the only way he knew: pressure. An associate approached the nephew of Herman the Howler with a job offer; the nephew reported it to Herman, who reported it to the jury over breakfast, which was worth more to the plaintiff than any expert. A "wrong number" call offered Juror Nine's mother a cruise; the mother, who had raised nine jurors' worth of children, took the cruise and told her son everything. Every string Flinch pulled turned out, somehow, to be attached to something on his own end of the rope.

And still the jury swung. The ambitious capuchin who had campaigned hard to be foreman got himself bounced for concealing a lawsuit of his own. Herman the Howler slept through most of the expert testimony and all of Durwood Flimflam's closing. Feaster, meanwhile, kept the panel fed, amused, and — as Flinch's pollster confirmed with rising panic — swinging steadily toward the plaintiff. In the deliberation room he never argued. He only wondered, aloud, at exactly the right moments, why a company with four thousand documents about banana-lung needed a bunker, and what a jury was for, if not to ask. The others did the rest, each convinced it was their own idea, which is the only kind of idea a jury trusts.

On verdict day the jury filed in, hung from its tails for style, and delivered itself of the following: liable; one million figs compensatory; four hundred million punitive — enough to buy every banana on earth and throw them at the Banynex tower with both hands.

Flinch wired nothing further, having wired plenty already. And it was only that night, digging through Marlee's dissolved shell companies, that his people found the truth: the money had gone to the Itchy and Orphaned Monkey Fund within the hour, and the verdict had never been for sale. It had been honest all along. All Rankin Flinch had bought, at full retail, was the truth. A week later came a receipt in the morning mail, marked *Paid in full — M.*, which he filed under insults.

The rest emerged slowly, the way jungle news does. Years ago, a jury had been fixed in a case involving a poisoned grove and a dead monkey with a family. Flinch had fixed it — had picked that jury the way a butcher picks a loin — and the family had got nothing: no verdict, no damages, not the cost of the funeral. The family had included a daughter. She had grown up, changed her name twice, kept her father's death certificate in a drawer, and studied one subject the way other monkeys study fruit: how juries are chosen. Finding her took Flinch's people another month, and by then there was nothing left to do with the information, because there is no crime in the zoo's books called *stealing a verdict back*.

Feaster and Marlee met once more, at the ferry, two monkeys with no luggage.

"Swing vote," said Marlee, nodding at the newspaper.

"It was a whole swinging jury," said Nick. "I just greased the branch. Flinch built the ladder himself."

They went over the side, hand over hand, into the trees, and were never seen again — which, in the jungle, is the happiest ending anyone gets, and the only sure thing besides: never bet against a jury that can swing.
