# Shadow and Bonobo

*A parody of "Shadow and Bonobo"*

Alina Starkov was a runty bonobo with a cold and a clipboard, which is to say: a mapmaker. She had grown up in the Keramzin orphanage beside Mal Oretsev — tracker, best friend, better shot, worst speller of love letters — and she had spent every year since pretending that was a small thing. The two of them rode the canopy-skiff into the Unjungle with the rest of the conscripts, and the Unjungle was exactly what it sounded like — a gash a mile wide where the canopy had sealed itself shut with black vines, and no light came through, ever. Inside lived the volcra: giant screeching megabats with appetites.

"Stay in the middle of the skiff," said the sergeant, "and if you hear screaming, make a note of it."

"Scared, Starks?" Mal whispered, grinning.

"Terrified," said Alina. "I'm mapping it for the record."

"Scared of me?" said the Darkling himself, riding at the prow: the mysterious young general of the Second Army, black-furred, smoke-eyed, trailing shadow like a cloak even at noon. Nobody had noticed him board. Monkeys rarely noticed him doing anything, which was roughly his plan.

Then the volcra came — a storm of wings and shrieks — and one seized Mal in its claws and began to peel him away. And Alina, screaming his name, did the one thing no one in the history of Ravka's jungle had ever done.

She shone.

Light burst out of her chest like the sun cracking an egg. The volcra scattered, shrieking, and every monkey on that skiff stood frozen, blinking at the small bonobo glowing in the dark.

The Darkling turned. For the first time in a hundred years, he smiled.

"Like calls to like," he said softly. "Hello, little sun."

They took her to the Little Palace, home of the Grisha — the Tailed Saints, monkeys who could do things with wind and fire and flesh that had no decent explanation. Squallers whipped storms. Inferni breathed flame. Heartrender instructors occasionally made cadets' hearts stop "educationally."

Genya the Tailor, a scarlet-collared tamarin with a cosmetic case and a grudge, was assigned to dress Alina for court. "My gift," she explained, powdering Alina's nose while insulting half the court through it, "is making other monkeys look expensive. You, at the moment, are a candle in a kebab."

"You're a good friend."

"I'm a distraction with a mirror. It's the best kind."

An old macaque named Baghra with a whacking stick taught Alina to summon her light, mostly by whacking her.

"Again," Baghra croaked. "Worse. Again."

Alina couldn't do it. Not once, not on command. Baghra's method was pedagogy by whacking; her syllabus was abuse; her office hours were a rumor. Then one dawn the old macaque stopped mid-whack, which was somehow more frightening.

"Stop *summoning* it, fool," Baghra said. "You do not summon breath. You do not summon breakfast. It is already in you. Let it *out*."

And the light came — a shy candle at first, then a bonfire — simply because Alina stopped asking its permission. It came even better when the Darkling stood beside her in the training yard, shadows curling off his fur, like dawn answering a dare.

"I've been waiting for you," he told her. "All my life."

Which should have been romantic, and was in fact a warning label. So was the Apparat, the bald-pated colobus priest who began appearing in Alina's doorway with candles and a notebook, cooing "Sankta Alina" and sketching her halo. Saints, the Apparat explained, were very valuable dead. He said it in a friendly way. It was not friendly.

Even Zoya, the imperious Squaller the Darkling had discarded like a peel, stopped by to hiss a warning while pretending it was a social call: "He collects light the way other monkeys collect fruit flies, little one. Ask the last of us how that ended."

Because Baghra, in a rage of old grief, finally told the truth: "My boy is not called Darkling because of his fur, fool. He is the Black Heretic. He made the Unjungle. A hundred years ago he tore the light out of the jungle and left a mile of shadow — and he means to collar *you* and use your sun to make it a thousand miles wide."

Alina ran. But she ran to the white stag.

It was Morozova's great white stag, glowing between the black trunks like the jungle's last good idea — and Mal, who could track a whisper across a marsh, found it first, because he had crossed half of Ravka on the rumor his best friend was in trouble and a tracker never loses that trail. They found it together. It was found. It gave her its light willingly, a warm flood of it, and Mal knelt beside her, awed, and said the thing he'd been misspelling for years: "You were never small, Alina. I just wasn't looking."

Before she could kneel in thanks, an arrow dropped it. The Darkling walked out of the shadow, cut the antlers from the beautiful dead thing, and locked them around Alina's neck as a collar of pale bone. Her light obeyed him now.

At the winter fete, before all Ravka, he displayed her like a lantern: the Sun Summoner and her master. In the crowd, one young tracker's face did not applaud.

Mal came for her that night, of course, because he was Mal. They fled toward the Unjungle, and at its black edge the Darkling caught them both, patient as mold.

"Please," Alina said. "You could heal it. All of it."

"I could rule it," said the Darkling. "Fine, little sun. Make me your villain."

He forced her light up, out, wide — on a skiff above the Unjungle, with Mal lashed to the prow and an army watching — and the black vines began to crawl over the far village of Novokribirsk, where howler families were still asleep. The volcra poured out, shrieking, drawn to all that impossible brightness, and the Darkling stood at the rail in his neat black coat, directing a massacre like weather.

And Alina, burning, collared, and out of options — stopped fighting the collar and started fighting *through* it. She gathered her light into one white-hot point and severed the bone at her throat.

The flash was the brightest thing the jungle ever saw. It carved a stripe of sunlight across the Darkling's face, scattered the volcra like ash, tore the skiff's rigging loose, and cracked the black vines for a hundred yards in every direction. Mal's ropes burned away; Alina caught his arm; they went over the side into the dark water as the Unjungle screamed.

They came up on the far bank, out the far side, and climbed up into the honest daylight, hand in hand, while behind them a smoke-eyed monkey with a new white scar hauled himself out of the water and straightened his fur, because villains are never in a hurry.

"I'm not a saint," Alina said, as the sun rose over the broken Unjungle. "But I'm the only sun this jungle's got."

"Then we'd better keep you lit," said Mal. "Sandwich?"

They ate. It was, Alina thought, the first meal in a year that nobody had poisoned, enchanted, or served on a tray carried by a priest. Somewhere behind them, in the dark, plans were being made patiently, and in the Apparat's notebook, a sketch of a haloed bonobo waited under a heading that read *Volume Two.*

The shadow always gets a sequel.
