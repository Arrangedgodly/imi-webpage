# The Alchimpist

*A parody of "The Alchemist"*

The boy's name was Santiago. He was a young spider monkey, and he was a shepherd, and he was having, for the second time, the same dream.

He dreamed it in the ruin of an old chapel where he and his flock sheltered, under a great banana tree growing up through the broken roof, and in the dream a child took his hand and said: come to the Pyramids of Egypt. There is a treasure buried under your bananas there.

He was telling a gypsy about this in the market at Tarifa when an old marmoset, badly needing a haircut, sat down beside him and said, "You have come for the treasure."

"How do you know that?"

"Maktub," said the old monkey. "It is written. Also, you told the entire plaza." He introduced himself as Melchizedek, King of Salaam, and gave the boy two seeds from his pouch, one bright and one dark, called Urim and Tummim. "Worth more than gold. Mostly because one of them is, and I've forgotten which."

The gypsy, meanwhile, interpreted the dream for free, on one condition: a tenth of the treasure when he found it. "Dreams are the language of the Big Monkey Upstairs," she said. "When he speaks in the language of the jungle, even I can charge for the translation."

"Everybody," said the King of Salaam, "when they are young, knows their Personal Le Grand. But a mysterious force begins saying it's impossible, and slowly everyone believes it." He tapped the boy's chest. "When you want something, all the jungle conspires in helping you to achieve it. The jungle is pushy that way." He mentioned, as proof, the case of a baker he had known, who had wanted to travel since boyhood and had instead bought a bakery, keeping the dream in a jar on the top shelf, between the flour and the dust.

He took six bananas as payment. Santiago sold his sheep and crossed to Tangier, where a charming monkey with a reed flute stole every last coin from him in under an hour. This is also Maktub, though it feels worse.

Santiago spent a year with a crystal merchant, a cautious old gibbon who sold nothing to nobody on a hill nobody climbed, and learned the secrets of trade: put the display case at the top of the hill, and serve banana-juice, cold, in crystal. The merchant's own Le Grand was to cross the desert and see the Great Golden Tree, and he never went, and kept the wish like a coin he was afraid to spend.

"That will be our great difference," the merchant said. "I am afraid my dream will come true, and then I will have nothing to live for. You are afraid yours won't."

So Santiago joined a caravan heading for the Pyramids, and made two friends on the way. One was the desert, which he began, slowly, to hear. The other was an English mandrill with spectacles and a trunk of books, who had crossed the sea in search of a two-hundred-year-old alchemist said to live at the oasis of Al-Grove-youm.

"I have read everything about the Master Work," said the Englishman. "The Philosopher's Banana, which turns lead to gold, and the Elixir of Long Life, which cures everything except reading too much. It is all in here." He patted the trunk.

"And what does the desert say?" asked Santiago.

"Nothing," said the Englishman, "which is why I brought the books."

Each kept to his own school. Santiago looked at the desert and heard the Soul of the World in the wind; the Englishman stared at the desert, took notes on it, and got sunburn in the margins. When they reached the oasis — where the date palms stand in rows like an orchard of parrots — the Englishman built a furnace and tried for the Master Work, and produced lead; and Santiago, watching two hawks over the sands, saw an army.

The Englishman went off to bother the local chief with his book learning. And Santiago met Fatima at the well — a desert bonobo with kohl-rimmed eyes, from whose bucket, afterward, he could not recall drinking a drop — and Le Grand stopped being a treasure and became, complicatedly, two things at once.

"I am a desert woman," Fatima said. "We desert women do not get jealous of Le Grands. If you are part of my destiny, you will come back. If not, I will assume the desert ate you, and I will not wait — and then I will wait."

Then the omen came true. Raiders struck the oasis; and because Santiago had warned the chiefs, the oasis defended itself and won, and the tribal elders, who had been on the point of expicturing the whole caravan, let it go. It was at Al-Grove-youm, in the stir after the battle, that the Alchimpist arrived — a two-hundred-year-old howler in a black cloak, riding, absurdly, a large pale capybara.

"You saw the omen," the Alchimpist said. "Good. You also nearly got yourself killed listening to hawks. Sit down. Lesson one: the fear of suffering is worse than the suffering itself. Lesson two: your heart is in your treasure, and a heart in two places leaks like a gourd."

"Are you really two hundred years old?"

"I am really an alchimpist. I turn bananas into gold. Occasionally I turn the other way while an oasis is sacked, but let's not dwell. Now: the chiefs want proof of your power, and we have three days, and the desert is full of monkeys with spears who would like to watch. Any thoughts?"

"I could talk to the wind," said Santiago, who had been practicing.

"Excellent," said the Alchimpist. "The jungle will help you. Speak to the desert. The desert will speak to the wind."

So Santiago spoke to the desert, and the desert spoke of the figs it had once grown and would grow again; and the desert spoke to the wind, and the wind spoke of the far jungles it had combed; and the wind spoke to the sun, and the sun spoke to the Hand That Wrote All — and Santiago, a shepherd spider monkey who could barely read, became the wind. For one hour a simum bowed to him across the sand, the palms lay down flat as parrots pretending to be furniture, and the tribal chiefs never forgot it. The Alchimpist, satisfied, gave him his capybara's blessing and an escort as far as a Coptic monastery, where he cooked lead and a sliver of the Philosopher's Banana in a pan and cut the resulting gold into four parts: one for the monk, one for himself, one for the boy, and one left with the monk, for the boy to collect when he came back this way, "because I am old and like to be proven wrong."

At the Pyramids, at last, Santiago wept, and dug where his tears fell. And out of the dunes came robbers, who beat the treasure out of him and got nothing but sand.

"You're mad," their leader said at last, sitting on the boy's chest to catch his breath. "Two years ago, right here, I dreamed of a treasure under a banana tree in a ruined chapel in Andabananasia, where a boy sleeps with his sheep. Ha! But I am not stupid enough to cross a desert for a dream. Now go."

Santiago laughed until his sides ached, thanked the robber sincerely — the only creature in the whole adventure who had told him where the treasure was for free — and went home.

Under the banana tree of the ruined chapel, his shovel found a chest of old gold coins and gold bananas.

"The wind carried me here," Santiago told his sheep, who had new owners but old loyalty. "I am coming, Fatima."

And his first stop on the way was Tarifa, to pay the gypsy her tenth. He had learned, crossing two deserts, that the jungle conspires to help you — but it sends the bill in advance.
