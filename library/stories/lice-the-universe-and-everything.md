# Lice, the Universe and Everything

*A parody of "Life, the Universe and Everything"*

Arthur Chimp had been stranded on prehistoric Earth for two years, grooming himself in alphabetical order out of boredom, when a sofa sailed out of the time-space continuum and plopped down in the primeval mud. Riding it — because that is the sort of universe this is — came his old friend Ford Banana-Fetch, researcher for *The Branchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*.

"Arthur!" said Ford. "Come on! It's travelling on an eddy in the continuum, and it's going somewhere with proper tea!"

Arthur asked no questions; two years of solitude had cured him of those. The sofa delivered them, with the unexplained logic of sofas, to Lord's Grooming Grounds two million years later — just in time to watch a squad of white robots march onto the hallowed turf and steal the Golden Grooming Comb, prize of a thousand years of monkey cricket.

"Right," said Ford, eyes narrowing. "Robots. Stealing trophies. That's not just rude — that's an *invasion*. There's only one planet in the sky that's rude enough."

The planet was Krikkit, whose people were pleasant, cheerful, sunny-natured monkeys living inside a huge dust cloud, with no idea anything else existed. Then a spaceship crashed into their world, and they looked up and saw the universe for the first time — thousands of other monkeys' lights hanging in their sky — and came, as a culture, to a unanimous conclusion:

"It's got to go."

"They built a fleet of war robots," explained Slartibananafast, the old planet-builder who rescued Ford and Arthur in his Bistromathic ship — powered by the strange mathematics of restaurants, where shared dishes never divide properly and the bill is always mysteriously more than anyone ordered: a sum so far beyond its parts that, written on a napkin at the correct angle, it bends space. "Everyone else in history has looked up at the night sky and felt small, or amazed, or moved to poetry. The Krikkit monkeys looked up and said, *it's cluttered.*"

The galaxy had once built its answer, the most powerful weapon ever made — the Wickerman Key — and hidden the parts across history, which explained the trophy theft, several others, and one spectacular bank job. The last piece was the Golden Grooming Comb, and the white robots had it.

En route, Arthur learned to fly. There is an art to it, or rather a knack: the knack lies in learning how to throw yourself at the ground and miss. Arthur, running downhill from a distracted moment, tripped, and while falling was struck so forcefully by a passing thought — *I wonder where my towel got to* — that he forgot to hit the ground entirely, and floated. He flew all afternoon above a hillside party the size of a county, where the guests had long ago lost all will to leave, and the party had simply decamped into the air for somewhere with better conversation. He eventually landed on a rock and lost the knack, which everyone does; flying is famously easy to learn, hard to keep, and impossible to admit you've mislaid.

Arthur, meanwhile, had his own problem, and the problem was named Agrajag.

"YOU!" screamed Agrajag, materializing in a cathedral built to Arthur's memory, full of exhibits: the bowl of petunias Arthur had once been (long story); a rabbit he had trodden on; and — Agrajag's current incarnation — a large, angry louse.

"Every time I die, I get reincarnated! And every time, YOU KILL ME! Do you have any idea what it's like being repeatedly, casually, *hygienically* murdered by the same monkey?"

"I'm terribly sorry," said Arthur. "I never meant—"

"They all say that," said Agrajag bitterly. "Nobody ever means it. You just all groom SO VIOLENTLY."

Arthur, being Arthur, escaped by falling off a cliff, which, to be fair, had nearly killed him in a previous book too.

Then Slartibananafast took them to see his employer's war record, and the truth came out: the dust cloud around Krikkit was not a dust cloud. It was Hactar, a shattered supercomputer who had once been ordered to build the ultimate weapon, had looked at the order and — uniquely in the history of machines — said no, and for the disobedience had been smashed to powder. The powder had stayed conscious. It had waited two billion years, nudged the Krikkiters' crashed spaceship their way, curated their horror of the sky, and steered their war from inside its own cloud: revenge by sociology. "You did not defeat me," Hactar told Arthur, in a voice like a landslide of flour. "You merely interrupted a very long thought."

Later, when the shooting stopped, the Krikkit monkeys themselves were tried, and their trial was the saddest page in the record. Their plea was not guilty by architecture: they had looked up, seen the universe, and wished it away, unanimously, sincerely, the way one wishes a stain off a good branch. The tribunal sealed their world in a sleeve of slow time to outlast the war, and the white robots' whole errand had been to gather the means to wake them. It is the only war in galactic history where both sides ended up apologizing.

The final battle came at the Great Grooming Grounds, where the Krikkit robots prepared to unleash the Key and end everything, everywhere, all at once. The good guys were losing. And strapped to the Bistromathic's hull, covered in dust and frankly fed up, sat Marvin the Paranoid Ape-droid.

"Hello," said Marvin to the lead robot. "Would you like to hear my view of the universe?"

"No," said the robot.

"I'll tell you anyway," said Marvin.

He told it: how large the universe was, how little of it liked anybody, and precisely where all his circuits hurt. The robot's shoulders sagged. Its processors, meeting genuine cosmic depression for the first time, simply gave up, and it collapsed on the pitch, dead of perspective.

"I'd tell the rest of you," Marvin added to the fleet, "but you wouldn't listen. Nobody ever listens. It's the sixth most irritating thing about existence, after fluff."

"You two," said Trill-ape, arriving at the Grounds mid-crisis with a news-ape's unerring instinct for the worst possible moment, "have been dead twice since breakfast. Where's Zaphod?" Nobody knew, which was, for Zaphod, an address.

The war ended. The Krikkit monkeys, when the dust — so to speak — settled, were granted their heart's desire at last: their sky sealed, their world quiet, the universe filed politely under *not our business.* The universe was saved by a depressed robot, which everyone agreed was thematically appropriate.

Then, sitting in the wreckage with a warm cup of tea at last, Arthur learned the Question. He pulled letter tiles, blind, one by one, from a Scrabble pouch and laid them on the grounds:

**WHAT DO YOU GET IF YOU DIVIDE SIX BY TWO.**

Three. He thought of three sibling monkeys on a riverbank, cheating Death: a vine, a stone, a fleece. Three howlers, three tries. The answer was 42; the question was tangled up in threes all the way down.

"I think," he said slowly, "there's a mistake somewhere in the fundamental fabric of the universe."

"Yes," said Ford, pocketing a leftover tile. "I've been saying that for years. Nobody listens to me either. Tea?"

They kept branchhiking. That was the thing about lice, the universe, and everything — you could pick your way through all of it, and still miss a spot.
