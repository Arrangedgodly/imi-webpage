# Ramona Chimpy, Age 8

*A parody of "Ramona Quimby, Age 8"*

Ramona Chimpy was eight years old, and this year, she felt, everything was going to be different. Third grade at Glenwood Grove School was for big monkeys. Big monkeys rode the bus by themselves, wore bangs like her mother's, and did book reports. Nobody would treat Ramona like the littlest monkey in the branch anymore.

"Big monkey, big monkey," sang her sister Rhesus, who was twelve and insufferable.

"Brave and fearless," said Ramona, tossing her new bangs, because that was what she was determined to be on the first day of third grade.

The morning started beautifully. Ramona wore her new fur ribbons. She carried her new olive-green book bag. On the bus she saw a boy she knew from the old neighborhood, a burly young ape everyone called Yard Ape, because his family was ape-shaped and he had a big yard and an even bigger mouth.

"Well, if it isn Ramona," said Yard Ape, snatching the rubber eraser off her pencil and holding it out the window. "Want it? Come get it, furball."

Ramona, brave and fearless, snatched it back. This was the beginning of what she suspected might be a friendship, though she would not have admitted it under torture.

Her new teacher was Mrs. Mangabey, who was also vice principal, and Ramona liked her immediately. But on the very first morning — before printing, before silent reading, before anything — Ramona's stomach began to feel like a barrel of monkeys doing acrobatics. It flipped. It lurched. It climbed.

"Mrs. Mangabey," said Ramona faintly, "I think I'm going to —"

She was.

The good news: she made it to the wastebasket. The bad news: the wastebasket was in the middle of the rug, in front of everybody, and third-grade monkeys are not known for their mercy. The room went up in a chorus of "EWWW" like a troop of howlers at sunrise. Ramona was whisked home in disgrace by her mother, who had been called from her new job at Dr. Hobbs's office, where she had just started working full time now that Ramona's father was clerking at the Banana & Save market.

So much for brave and fearless.

The flu passed. School resumed. And then came the coconut.

It began as a point of pride. Ramona's father had shown the family, one breakfast, how a real jungle monkey opens a young coconut: whack, one smart knock against the table edge, and the top came off clean for drinking. He had done it with his head? No — with the table. But Ramona, packing her lunch the night before school, got to thinking about lunchtime attention, and book reports, and how a person might impress a whole classroom at once.

Her mother had packed her a young drinking coconut.

At lunch, in front of everyone, Ramona held the coconut high, wound up like a big-league chimp, and smacked it against her own forehead.

Two things happened. First, the coconut did not open. Second, Ramona's forehead made a sound like a ripe melon, and she sat down very suddenly, and the whole table roared with laughter, and Yard Ape laughed loudest of all — and then, seeing her face, stopped laughing and made everyone else stop too, which Ramona filed away in her heart without examining it.

But that was not the worst of the coconut. In class the next day, showing off was still in Ramona's system like an itch. When Mrs. Mangabey asked who could demonstrate jungle math, Ramona waved both arms, stood on her chair, and hollered "OOH! OOH! ME!" with such enthusiasm that she overbalanced into the class guinea pig cage.

The class shrieked with joy. Mrs. Mangabey did not shriek. She said, quietly, "Ramona. Sit down and settle down, please."

Ramona sat. But she had seen something flicker across her teacher's face, and it stung worse than the coconut.

Then came the library day that Ramona would remember all year.

The class was in the school library, and Ramona was hunkered down in the low stacks, hunting for a book report book, when she heard two teachers talking by the encyclopedias. One of the voices was Mrs. Mangabey's.

"—book reports, yes," said the other teacher. "How's your class this year? I heard you had a bit of a time with that Chimpy girl."

"Ramona," said Mrs. Mangabey, with a small laugh that was not unkind, but was not kind either. "Yes. A show-off and a nuisance. She's creative, though."

A show-off and a nuisance. Ramona sat frozen behind the low shelf, a book about volcanoes going blurry in her hands.

She had been called Ramona the Pest before, and it hadn't felt like this. Third grade was supposed to be different. She was a big monkey now. And here was her own teacher, telling the whole faculty that Ramona Chimpy was a show-off and a nuisance. Ramona crept out of the library without checking out a single book, and for days she went around school like a monkey with a stone in her stomach, doing everything exactly, painfully right. She did not raise her hand. She did not offer to demonstrate anything. She was so quiet that Yard Ape asked her if she was sick.

"I'm fine," said Ramona, in a voice the size of a mouse monkey's.

"You don't seem fine," said Yard Ape. "You seem like somebody stole your banana."

"Nobody stole my banana," said Ramona, and smiled a tiny bit, because of course Yard Ape had given her eraser back weeks ago and then started saving her a seat on the bus, which was Yard Ape for friendship.

At home that autumn, things were not much better. Mr. Chimpy came home from the Banana & Save with aching feet and short temper. Mrs. Chimpy came home from Dr. Hobbs's office tired and short-tempered too. One evening everything went wrong at once: the dinner burned, Rhesus slammed a door, Ramona's book report note got lost, and Mr. Chimpy said "Beats me" in the voice of a monkey who had been beaten.

"I don't have to put up with this," announced Rhesus.

"Neither do I," said Ramona, because if her sister was leaving the troop, she was too.

"Sit down, both of you," said Mrs. Chimby. And then her parents looked at each other over the wreckage of the dinner, and something passed between them, and Mr. Chimpy started to laugh.

"Look at us," he said. "The Chimpys in a sorry state. Cranky as crocodiles. Well — we're a family. We're in this together, like it or not. Might as well like it."

"We could eat the burned parts and pretend they're charcoal cookies," suggested Ramona.

"Ramona," said her father, "you are strange and wonderful, and dinner is served."

That night Ramona lay in the nest thinking. A show-off and a nuisance. Well. Maybe she had shown off. But she was not going to be one forever, and she was not going to let one overheard sentence run her whole third grade.

Her book report was due Friday. Her book, which she had finally chosen in defiance of everything, was about a little witch monkey. And Ramona had a plan so daring that even her own stomach did a barrel of monkeys when she thought about it.

On Friday morning, Ramona put on her old Halloween costume under her coat — the black witch dress, the paper hat — and painted her face in the mirror until she looked like a proper little jungle witch, whiskers and all. Then she stood up in front of Room Three and gave her book report as the witch herself, cackling, stirring an imaginary cauldron, telling the story instead of reciting it, ending with the witch's spell: "Double, double, coconut trouble!"

For one terrible heartbeat, the room was silent.

Then it exploded. Third graders howled, clapped, stomped, and begged her to do the cackle again. Even Mrs. Mangabey was laughing — really laughing, with her whole face — and when the noise died down she said, "Ramona, that was the most original book report I have seen in fifteen years of teaching. I had no idea what was under that coat."

After school, Mrs. Mangabey stopped Ramona at the door. "Ramona," she said, "I understand you heard me in the library. I want you to know I was answering a question about that particular day — and I chose my words badly. Every monkey shows off sometimes. Every monkey is a nuisance sometimes. It's the creative part I remember at the end of the day. Don't you?"

"Yes," said Ramona, who had lately decided something about herself: that being eight was going to mean being brave and fearless after all, mostly, on most days.

That evening the Chimpys ate pancakes for dinner, just because, and Ramona stood on her chair without being told.

"To the whole troop!" she announced, raising her glass of juice. "We're in this together!"

"Like it or not," agreed her father.

"Might as well like it," said her mother, and clinked.

And Ramona Chimpy, age eight, sat down feeling exactly like what she was: one monkey in a troop of five, all swinging from the same branch, and none of them letting go.
