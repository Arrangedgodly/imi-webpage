# The Vine Assassin

*A parody of "The Blind Assassin"

by Laura Chimp, posthumously (it says here)*

Ten days after the Great Troop War ended, my sister Laura swung off the Jubilee Vine and let go.

The papers called it an accident — brakes, rain, a slick of wet bark. This was easier for everyone, and I let them have it, the way I let them have everything else, including the only thing I ever did that was worth doing. I am an old monkey now, with lumps in my fur the size of grapes, and I have decided that before the last leaf drops I will set down the truth, however crooked. Time is a blind carpenter; it builds whatever it builds, and calls it a life.

* * *

**From the Port Ticonderoga Nut-News, May 1945:** GIRL GIBBON, 25, DAUGHTER OF LATE NUT-AND-BOLT KING, DIES IN VINE-SPAN COLLAPSE. Survived by sister, Mrs. Iris Griffon, wife of R. Griffon, Griffon Nuts & Munitions.

* * *

We were the Chimp sisters, of the Chimp Nut & Bolt Works, in a mill town that smelled of machine oil and grapes. Laura was the beautiful one — she saw through things the way some monkeys see through vines at night. I was the sensible one, which is what the guilty ones get called.

After our mother died, our father drank the factory and then himself, and a young capuchin named Alex Thomasson hid in our attic — a wanted monkey, an anarchist, blamed for a fire he hadn't set, who wore his charm like a bandage over a wound. Laura hid him. Laura worshipped him. And I — the sensible one — crept up those attic stairs one night when the hunters were in the town, and spent the night, and told myself it was to protect him.

That is the hinge my whole life swung on. Laura never knew. Or she knew the way she knew everything: silently, as an article of faith, like a religion she'd decided to practice alone.

Richard Griffon proposed to me that winter — Richard of the great shoulders and the hollow chest, the golden gazelle of Griffon Nuts, who wanted a Chimp for his collection the way he wanted a Riviera estate. It would have been a joke to refuse him, in that economy. So Laura and I were shipped to his glass cage in the capital like two imported fig trees, and there we stayed through the lean years, the strikes, the war, fading on our stems.

Laura never forgave him, and later she forgave him even less, for reasons she folded up small and kept in her pockets like sugar stolen from a funeral.

* * *

**From the capital papers, 1946:** R. GRIFFON, NUTS & MUNITIONS MAGNATE, DIES IN BALCONY FALL. No inquest. Survived by wife.

* * *

And then there is the other story — the one the world knows. *The Vine Assassin*, by Laura Chimp, published after her death: a scandalous little pulp novel, banned in three cities by monkeys who bought it in bulk. Laura, the beautiful dead girl, its author. It won prizes, later, after prizes stopped meaning anything.

In the novel, a rich spoiled young lady meets a low-born fugitive in shabby rooms above fruit stalls, and between their quarrels he tells her a tale of another world.

* * *

On the planet Sakiel-Nut, he tells her, the children are put to the vine-loom at five. They weave the sun-vine carpets that are the wealth of the world, and by twelve their eyes are burnt out by the work. The blinded boys are sold to the Guild of the Vine Assassins, for a blind assassin cannot see the face of what he kills and so kills anything. The little girls with the cleverest fingers are worse off: their tongues are cut, so that the patterns of the carpets — which are the sacred words of the Sun-God — may never be spoken aloud. They call them the silent seamstresses. They live and die weaving, and their tongues are kept in the God-House, in jars, against the Day of Speaking.

In the story, a blind assassin scales the God-House on the night of the Dark Moon to cut a silent seamstress loose from the loom and run with her over the roofs of the world. Why? she asks him, in signs, with her clever fingers. Because, he signs back, I was told a tongue in a jar would speak my name, and I want to be somewhere when it does. Where are we going? she asks. Out, he signs. Out of the tale, into the next one.

The rich girl in the shabby room always falls asleep before the end. The fugitive never gets to finish it. In the book, that's how it ends — mid-tale, unfinished, like a life.

* * *

Now, the truth, which is the part I have circled all these pages like a suspicious old monkey circling a mirror.

Alex Thomasson came back, in the last spring of the war, fugitive and feverish, and it was not Laura who hid him in the boathouse. It was I. And it was I who went to him in the dark, again and again, while Laura — who had believed all this time that Alex was dead at sea — slept thirty feet away, faithful to a ghost.

Then the war ended, and Alex was taken, in the way of such monkeys, quietly, with no body and no grave. And Laura, who had found the boathouse, who had put everything together the way she put everything together, wordlessly, got into our father's old nut-cart ten days after the peace bells, drove it to the Jubilee Vine at dusk, and let go.

She left her gloves on the seat. Folded. She was the only one of us who was ever neat.

And after the inquest, when Richard Griffon — smiling his gazelle smile at the sympathy — took my arm at the top of his marble staircase and said, "Well, Iris, that's the last of the Chimp surprises, I hope," I told him what Laura had written in the notebook she left me. What he had done to her, in that house, when she was seventeen and I was in the nut-hospital losing his child. I let him read it. Then I watched him go over the railing — and whether he jumped or whether I unhooked his paw, I will leave for the carpenter to decide. Time builds what it builds.

* * *

The novel was not Laura's. I wrote it. Every word. In the shabby rooms of my own widowhood, I wrote down the rooms I had actually been in, and the fugitive I had actually loved, and the tale he had actually told me, once, half asleep, about Sakiel-Nut — the vine-loom, the jars of tongues, the blind assassin who is blind only because the world burnt out his eyes with work and then sold him the knife.

I put Laura's name on it because it was hers. Everything I ever had that mattered was hers first — our father, Alex, even Richard. I had only borrowed it all. The book was my repayment.

The reviewers said: such violence, such tenderness, what a strange gift the dead girl had. And I held my tongue, which is what us silent seamstresses are trained to do.

* * *

So who was the blind assassin? Not Alex, though I dressed him in it. Not Laura, though she wore the name. It was me — I killed with a pen, from behind, without ever having to look at what I was stabbing. I cut out tongues — Laura's, above all — by letting her speak through me, forever silently.

The end of the tale, the part the rich girl never heard: on the far border of Sakiel-Nut there is a door woven into the edge of the finest carpet, too small for any monkey but a seamstress to see. The blind assassin and the silent girl go out through it, into the next tale, carrying their jars.

That is where I've sent them, anyway. It's the only place I could.

What is a life? A vine, mostly — one that grows whichever way it's bent, and holds up whatever it has to, and shows its flowers late, if at all. I hold up this one. I leave it, now, in your hands. What choice do I have?

*Iris Chimp-Griffon*
*Port Ticonderoga, some autumns after*
