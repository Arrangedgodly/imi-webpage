# Destination Ungroomed

*A parody of "Destination Unknown"*

In a hotel in Casabanana, under a ceiling fan that stirred the heat like soup, a young chimpanzee named Hilary Craven sat composing her suicide. She was practical about it, as she was practical about everything: a husband gone, a small daughter dead of fever, a bottle of sleeping tablets in the suitcase, and a life with no branches left to swing to. She had written the note. She was, she told herself, simply waiting for the right evening.

The evening that came instead belonged to a solemn grey gorilla named Jessop, who sat down uninvited, ordered mint tea, and said, "Madame Craven, forgive me. My bureau has been watching you for a week."

"Then your bureau," said Hilary, "has been extremely bored."

"On the contrary." Jessop turned his cup a quarter-turn. "In the past year, eleven of the world's finest young physicists have vanished. The latest is Thomas Betternut — you will have seen it — the man who solved the fig-splitting problem; the secret of the little atom, the one that makes it give up its energy. Betternut defected, says the newspaper. We believe something else. Betternut's wife is in a hospital here, dying of a jungle fever, and trying with her last strength to travel to him. We require a healthy young she-monkey of her age and colouring to make the journey in her place. It occurs to us, madame, that you have no reason at all to value your life. We are offering to spend it for you."

Hilary looked at the fan for a while.

"If you are spending my life," she said, "I should like a receipt."

They dyed her fur, taught her Olive Betternut's voice and habits, and put her on a series of aircraft beside a museum of the vanished: a chatty American macaque named Mrs. Calvin Baker with a stars-and-stripes bow; a stiff British gorilla dame called Janet Hetherington; a melancholy French langur, Henri Laurier; a moody young American chemist, Andrew Peters, who argued physics with Hilary across three time zones; a Norse langur with glacier eyes; and a famous talkative doctor named Barron. Each of them, Hilary understood, was bound where she was bound. Each had been chosen by somebody.

Destination unknown. The name of it, when they finally got there, was a modern concrete research station sunk into the Misted Mountains, presented to the world as a leproserium — a colony for the study of the mangabey sickness — and it ran like a hospital and felt like a cage. There were laboratories, seminars, a dining hall with excellent fruit. There were no locks, and no exits either; the jungle below the cliffs was wall, and the mountains were wall, and the smile of the deputy director, Dr. Nielson, was wall.

And at dinner the first night, a hollow-eyed young monkey was presented to Hilary as Thomas Betternut, and he looked at her fur, and her eyes, and said in a voice full of cracks, "You are not Olive."

"She died," Hilary said, as arranged. "I am sorry."

"Did she?" said Betternut. "I am sorry too. Whoever you are — whatever you are — help me. I have been trying to get out of this place for a year."

He had not defected, he told her, in snatches, in corridors. He had been *collected*. And in the inner rooms of the station — the rooms no visitor saw — were the monkeys collected before him, the ones who had stopped resisting: brilliant minds, de-fanged, working serenely on problems set by others, all curiosity intact and all will removed. The station's owner was the patientest billionaire alive, an ancient loris called Aristides, who had concluded that governments were unworthy of genius and that genius, properly kept, was an investment. He let the free world hunt defectors; and quietly he had simply *bought* the century's best brains, and meant to lease them back to the survivors at whatever price he named.

"I am not a defector," said Betternut. "I am inventory."

Hilary thought about her bottle of tablets, five thousand miles behind her. It had not been hope that brought her here, but it might as well have been; despair, usefully spent, curdles into something with a spine.

There was one more thing. Andrew Peters, the moody chemist, stopped arguing physics one evening and began arguing *her* — her safety, her cover, her habit of walking at night on the cliff terraces — and it was on the terraces that he said, low, "Madame, whoever you are: I am one of Jessop's. I have been inside this operation nine months, and I cannot get a message out, and I am going to need a partner with a better nerve than mine. That is you. Don't argue; it's in your file."

"Jessop doesn't keep files on despairing wives."

"No," said Peters. "He keeps them on the courageous ones. I've read yours twice."

Together they did what Jessop's bureau had been unable to do. Hilary — "Olive" to the end — fed the route out: a dropped phrase in the tourist office, a pattern of fruit-pit marks on a map, a word to a pilot, each detail placed where she had learned to place things while being nobody in particular. Until one windless dawn there were aircraft over the Misted Mountains that did not belong to Aristides, and men in the corridors who did not smile like Dr. Nielson, and the walls, which had never had locks, simply stopped having any authority at all.

Aristides was taken at breakfast, finishing his papaya, entirely unafraid. "I am old," he observed, to no one in particular, "and I was curious. Governments! Governments are merely monkeys with flags. I merely *collected* better ones." They led him out through the laboratories, past his de-fanged geniuses, and none of the geniuses looked up, which was the saddest thing Hilary saw in the whole affair.

Betternut wept on the airstrip and asked after Olive; and it was Peters who told him the truth in private: that Betternut's first wife, Elsa, Peters's own cousin, had not died as reported — that Betternut himself had killed her, years before, to free himself for a cleverer marriage, and that the murder was the loose thread Jessop had pulled to unravel the whole winding-sheet of Betternut's disappearance. Peters had not come into the mountains for his country. He had come for Elsa.

The rescuers flew them out over the green floor of the world. Hilary looked down at the cloud cover, destination unknown again, and found she had never in her life wanted so badly to arrive somewhere.

At the airfield in Casabanana Jessop was waiting, solemn as ever.

"No bureau," said Hilary, "ever says thank you."

"No," agreed Jessop. "It says: your return ticket, madame, is first class, and there is a young chemist aboard who has refused to travel unless you are on the aircraft. The bureau considered detaining him for the peace of mind of the service, and decided against it."

Hilary Craven lived, which is the whole point of spending your life instead of ending it. She married Andrew Peters in the spring. On the subject of her salvation she was always exact, and once said it aloud at a dinner party, to a silence that lasted three courses:

"Nobody groomed me, in that place — not once, not a single monkey laid a finger to another's fur. That is how you know a paradise is a cage, you know. Not the walls. The *fur*. No monkey builds a society without grooming; only a monkey who has decided his neighbours are equipment." She looked around the table, at everyone's sleek, social, slightly-mussed coats. "So when I got home, the first thing I did was sit down in an ordinary chair, in an ordinary garden, and let an ordinary friend pick through my shoulder-fur for an hour, complaining about her sister-in-law.

"Best hour of my life," said Hilary Craven, "and I include the aircraft."

Somewhere far off, in the mountains, a concrete building stood empty in the wind, ungroomed, unmourned, destination — at last and forever — unknown.
