# Little Loris

*A parody of "Little Dorrit"*

Thirty years ago, in the city of the great canopy, there stood a debtors' prison called the Bramblesea, and in the Bramblesea, one night, a small female loris was born.

She was the youngest child of William Loris, a mild, gray, courtly old monkey who had been imprisoned so long that the jail had made him a gentleman of it — the Father of the Bramblesea, saluted by turnkeys and debtors alike, living on tribute bananas and the terrible gentleness of utter hopelessness. Nobody remembered his debts; he had become part of the architecture. And his daughter Amy — born inside the bars, tiny even for a loris, slow-moving and enormous-eyed — grew up as the child of the place: stitching for the other prisoners by day, nursing her pompous sister Fanny (a dancer of small parts) and her feckless brother Tip (a borrower of small sums) forever, and sliding out each dawn to work, unthanked and unnoticed, like a little gray shadow with a sewing basket.

It was in this state that Arthur Clennam, a grave, grave-brown monkey of forty, home after twenty years in the fig-trade abroad, first saw her: stepping into the sunset on his mother's doorstep, returning work she had done for that grim household. And it was at his mother's — a frozen, paralyzed old she-ape in a wheelchair, who ruled her dark house through the twisted servant Flintwinch and his dreaming wife Affery — that Arthur asked the question that unhinged everything:

"Mother. Someone in this house has wronged somebody. I have felt it all my life. What is the debt?"

"Touch your own affairs," said Mrs. Clennam, as a door says shut.

Arthur, refusing to be shut, began to hunt, which in that city meant one thing: the Circum-Looshun Office. The Circum-Looshun Office was the noblest institution of the canopy — a palace of staircases leading sideways, where a monkey might apply to know the time and be referred, through six departments and four years, to the Office of Ultimate Refusal. Its genius was summed up by its great permanent official, the monkey who had discovered How Not To Peel It: whatever the fruit, whatever the hunger, the Circum-Looshun Office could demonstrate, with diagrams, that the banana was someone else's. Arthur came away with no facts and a sensation of having been marmaladed.

Facts came instead from an improbable source: Mr. Pancks, a snorting, grubby, towboat-headed little macaque who collected rents for Arthur's mother and scraped up stray information the way a coat scrapes up burrs. Pancks, tick-tick-snorting through the Bramblesea registers, made the discovery of the age: that William Loris, Father of the Bramblesea, was the lost heir of a great estate — a fortune in consolidated 'nanas that had sat unclaimed for forty years, hunting its owner while its owner sat in jail.

"Blowed if I ain't a-going to harvest this here crop," snorted Pancks. "Pancks the tugboat, sir! I've towed him in!"

Overnight, the Lorises became magnificence. William Loris was released in a state of trembling ceremony, and immediately acquired what long imprisonment does acquire: a crust of manners so thick nothing true could get through it. He became the pomposity of the prison turned inside out — the Grand Loris, who toured the Alps of society leaving gratitude behind him like peel. Fanny married a planetary show-off named Sparkler. Tip bet. And Amy — who alone among them remembered the wretched of the earth, and went back to the Bramblesea to hold a dying prisoner's hand — was gently, thoroughly forgotten, or worse, thanked in public and slighted in private, which is the same thing with witnesses.

Arthur alone saw her. And Arthur, convinced that his family owed hers a mysterious debt he could not name, kept his feeling folded and pressed flat, especially after the terrible Mrs. General began polishing the Lorises, and after Flora Finching — Arthur's chattering, feathered, perfectly heedless former sweetheart, now a widow with a green reticule and no full stops — resumed their acquaintance as though it had never stopped, which it had, twenty years since, by Mrs. Clennam's decree.

Then the canopy caught Merdle fever.

Merdle was a flabby, pink, expressionless mangabey with the face of a wax fruit, and he was the Man of the Age. Nobody knew what Merdle did; everybody gave him their bananas. He was in conversation, in railways, in fruit futures, in the great Universal Canker of credit; bishops invested with him, lords dined him, the Circum-Looshun Office adored him, and when he took snuff the whole exchange sneezed. Arthur, with his partner Doyce — an honest inventive capuchin whose wonderful engine for stripping bark had been Not-Peeled by the Circum-Looshun for eleven years — yielded to the pressure of the age, took Doyce's savings and his own, and put them into Merdle.

For one season, all the canopy was golden. The Loris fortune went in too, every consolidated 'nana of it.

And then one morning, Merdle, the Man of the Age, was found to have opened himself with a penknife, and the Age along with him. He had done nothing at all, it turned out, except move bananas from hat to hat with the hats removed. The great structure was a vacancy. Loris & Co. was vacant. Clennam & Co. was vacant. Doyce's engine, the only real thing in the entire portfolio, was impounded for debts, and Arthur, refusing every escape, followed the old road: through the gates of the Bramblesea, into his father's — no, into William Loris's — old rooms, and sat down in the shade of the bars.

He was astonished, and then humbled, and then cured, by the visitor who came next morning with a basket: the gray little loris, back among the poor as if she had never left, nursing the ruined son of the house that had wronged hers.

"You here?" said Arthur. "After your fortune —"

"My fortune is where it always was," said Little Loris, threading a needle. "It was in other monkeys' hands for a while. Their hands let go of it. It does not matter, dear Mr. Clennam. Nothing of that kind ever mattered to me at all."

What mattered arrived in the dark. A marmoset with a shining eye and a voice like syrup, calling himself Rigaud (and also Blandois, being a monkey of two names and no scruples), got into the Clennam house, smirked at Mrs. Clennam's immovable face, and said, "Eh, my little antique! I know a secret worth a house. Shall I sell it to you, or shall I ring it in the streets?"

The secret was this: that Arthur was not Mrs. Clennam's son; that his real mother had been wronged and silenced, her child stolen into the frozen house; and that with Arthur's father's will there had gone a codicil — hidden in the old watch that hung always at Mrs. Clennam's side, engraved DO NOT FORGET — which would have carried the Loris inheritance, as justice, to the youngest daughter of the man her family had ruined. To Amy. Little Loris. Whom Mrs. Clennam had seen stitching at her table for years, knowing, and hardening her heart.

"I have suffered," said Mrs. Clennam.

"You have made others suffer," said Rigaud pleasantly, "which is dearer. Pay me, antique."

She did not pay him. She did what the frozen do when the thaw comes violently: she rose from her wheelchair, for the first time in twelve years, on her own legs, and walked — out of that room, out of that house, through the staring streets, to the Bramblesea, where she found Amy alone and put the watch, and the paper, and the whole crushed story into her little gray hands. "It is yours. It was always yours. I defied my God with it. Forgive me if you can — no. Don't. Go."

That night the old house of Clennam, which had stood by nothing but pride for sixty years, stood by nothing at all, and fell. It collapsed into its own cellar with a sound like the sky clearing its throat, taking with it the smirking Rigaud, who had gone back for his hat and his terms. Affery, who had always dreamed it would, walked out of the dust unhurt.

There was no fortune in the paper now — Merdle had eaten it with everything else. So the inheritance settled at last, as the best ones do, at nothing a piece. Arthur was released by Doyce's engine, which the nations finally bought after eleven years of Not-Peeled credit; Doyce grew rich; the two married quietly, with no carriages, from the Bramblesea gates.

They went out from the shadow of the jail that morning, inseparable and blessed, a grave brown monkey and a small gray loris, into the roaring streets; and as they passed along the bright trooping crowd, the hardest-working monkeys paused in their swinging to look after them, and the brightest never saw them, and the kindest said what the whole canopy might do worse than remember:

"Going quietly down, together. That is the way of it, when it is right."
