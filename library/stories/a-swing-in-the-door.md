# A Swing in the Door

*A parody of "A Wind in the Door"*

Meg Murry was going ape with worry, and she had been for weeks.

The trouble was Charles Wallace — her small, odd, wonderful baby brother, a runt of a chimp with a vocabulary like a professor and eyes like pools you could fall into. Lately his eyes had gone dull. He was tired all the time. He said, in his matter-of-fact way, "My mito-chimp-dria are sad, Meg. The little ones inside my cells won't dance anymore."

Their mother, a brilliant scientist gorilla with a typewriter and a frown, ran tests deep into the night. Something was failing inside Charles Wallace, something smaller than small, and no lab in the jungle could find it.

School did not help. Mr. Jankins, the ancient silverback principal, summoned Meg to his office and rumbled, "Your brother is not like the other young chimps, Margaret. You must accept that he is — peculiar. There is something wrong with that child."

Meg wanted to fling a fig at him. Charles Wallace, waiting outside, only smiled his small smile. "Mr. Jankins is sad inside," he said. "He's been empty so long he thinks empty is what he is."

That afternoon, walking home through the branches, they were ambushed by a gang of bigger chimps, led by a bruiser called Chuckles, who threw stones and insults with equal aim. Charles Wallace stood his ground, small and unafraid, and said something quiet and true about Chuckles's father that made the whole gang slink away, ashamed and dangerous.

And it was that evening that Meg saw the dragons.

They came whirling around the knot-hole of the family tree-house like a swarm of leaves — except leaves do not have eyes. Wings, a great boiling many of them, and eyes, dozens, blinking gold and silver in the dusk. Meg screamed and grabbed a branch.

"Dragons," said Charles Wallace calmly, "are exactly what a cherubim looks like if you expect cherubim to be harmless. Hello. We've been waiting for you."

The wings settled, and folded, and the eyes arranged themselves into something like a face, and a voice like a brass wind said: **"I am a cherubim. My name is Proginoskes. You may call me Progo. And you, Margaret, have been recruited."**

Then a giant blue orangutan stepped out of nowhere — a Teacher, he said, named Blajeny — and he explained it the way Teachers explain things, which is to say, barely.

Something was attacking Charles Wallace from inside. Not sickness. Something worse: the Echthroi, the un-Namers, the hollow ones, who X out the world. They had already X-ed their way into Charles Wallace's inner jungle, down where the little dancing banandolae lived in his mito-chimp-dria. A banandola that would not take root, would not put down its deep taproot and become a tree, went wild and hollow, and the Echthroi rode the wildness like a fire rides wind.

"There will be three of us Teachers," Blajeny rumbled. "Proginoskes. Louise the Larger" — he indicated a wise old boa sunning herself on the rail — "and, Meg, you."

"Me? I'm not a Teacher. I flunked patience."

"That," said Blajeny, "is the curriculum."

For days they trained, because there is an art to fighting what isn't there. Meg learned kything — speaking heart to heart without any lips at all, a useful skill in a family of screamers. Progo learned to tolerate Meg's temper. ("You are a Namer, or you are nothing," he told her. "To Name a thing is to love it into being. The Echthroi un-Name. That is all the evil in the jungle, Meg, and all the good is the opposite.")

Then came the first test, and it was the worst one possible: Mr. Jankins.

Three identical silverbacks sat on the school fence one gray morning — three Mr. Jankinses, exact as three figs off one branch. Two of them were Echthroi wearing the principal like a coat, and hollow. One was real. Meg had to pick the true one, and every time she guessed wrong, Charles Wallace dimmed a little more, like a firefly in a jar.

"Look at their fur," urged Charles Wallace, fading in her arms.

"I'm looking! They're identical!"

"No. Look. With the other part."

So Meg stopped using her eyes, which had never been her strong suit anyway, and kythed — and there. The two false ones were smooth inside, like gourds. The real one had a wound in him, an old grief the size of an empty nest, and — she saw it with a shock that nearly knocked her off the fence — a small, stubborn, secret hope.

The real Mr. Jankins was lonely. The Echthroi had not made him hollow; they had only found him hollow, years ago, and moved in.

Meg, who knew all about being the odd monkey in the troop, did the hardest thing a Namer can do. She reached into that wound and loved him.

"Hello, Mr. Jankins," she said. "You're real. I Name you, and you're real."

The two hollow copies shrieked and scattered like torn leaves. The real silverback blinked, and for one instant looked like what he might have been, forty years and one kindness ago.

**"Go or no go?"** boomed Progo.

"Go," said Meg.

Then they went small. Smaller than small. Down through Charles Wallace's skin, through the dark red rivers of him, into a single cell of his faithful heart, and landed — with Progo somehow still enormous, wings and eyes folded up like a parachute in a thimble — in a glowing green jungle where it was always dusk.

The inner jungle of Charles Wallace was dying. The farandolae — banandolae, little bright dancing monkey-shapes of light, each with a tiny tail — were racing and whirling in wild orbits, refusing to root. And there at the dying heart-tree stood the vainest of them all: Sporos, who shimmered and preened.

"Root?" scoffed Sporos. "Roots are for trees. Roots are stillness, stillness is death. I intend to dance forever, at the top of everything, in the wind and the light!"

"But if you don't root," Meg said, "you go wild. And the wild ones hollow out, and then the Echthroi come in, and then there's nothing at all. Not even dancing."

"I have seen the Echthroi," said Sporos, loftily. "They offered me the wind."

It was then that the X-ing began. The green dusk went gray. Patches of the inner jungle simply stopped — not destroyed, but canceled, un-Named — and through the blankness came the Echthroi: nothing in the shape of monkeys, voids that wore faces like masks on hooks. Progo spread himself between them and the heart-tree, wings and eyes blazing, one cherubim against the cancellation of a world, and he began to Name — every leaf, every river, every dancing spark — faster and faster, wearing himself thin.

Meg held Sporos by his bright little tail.

"Listen to me. My brother is dying because everyone thinks being small means being nothing. You're a speck inside a runt chimp inside an ordinary troop in an ordinary jungle. So am I. So is Progo, next to the stars. And the Echthroi lose, Sporos, every single time, because one rooted speck is real — and they never, ever are."

Sporos looked at the blankness. Then he looked at the heart-tree. Then, with the expression of a small god deciding to become a garden, he stopped dancing, and went down, and put his roots into the dark.

The heart-tree flowered like a fire. Light poured up the trunk and out along every branch of the inner jungle, and the wild banandolae felt it, and rooted one by one, like rain falling upward — and the X-ed gray places bloomed green again, and the Echthroi, who cannot abide a real thing, fled shrieking into the small of the small.

Somewhere enormously far away and enormously near, Charles Wallace sat up in bed and said, "Oh. That's better."

They came back the long way. Blajeny bowed. Louise the Larger went off to teach a class of hatchlings the difference between hunger and appetite. And Progo — patched, shining, and rather pleased with himself — began to fold away.

**"Will I see you again?"** Meg asked.

**"I expect so. I am generally about. And Meg — you Named a principal today. That was champion work."** The wings turned into wind. **"Chimp-ion,"** he added, unable to resist, and was gone.

Mr. Jankins retired that spring and took up beekeeping. Nobody knew why. Nobody but a young chimp who sometimes stopped by his fence, and looked at the hives with their small stubborn realness, and grinned.

And Meg — who still went ape with worry, because she was Meg — worried now with Charles Wallace swinging beside her, all the way home, through a jungle far, far larger than it looked from the outside. The swing in the door had opened, just a crack, and she understood that it could never entirely close again.
