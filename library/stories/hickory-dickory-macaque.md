# Hickory Dickory Macaque

*A parody of "Hickory Dickory Dock"*

Hercule Poirot's secretary was a lemur named Miss Lemur, and her typing was famous for its errors — a famousness she had personally and deliberately engineered, since there is no better way to make an employer read a document carefully. One morning she typed *bananas* where *banners* should have been, and Poirot, brushing crumbs of breakfast fig from his moustaches, looked up with interest.

"You have made a mistake, Miss Lemur."

"Have I?" said Miss Lemur, who had made three that week, all in the same direction, all pointing at a story she wanted him to notice. "My sister, Monsieur Poirot, runs the student hostel at twenty-six Hickory Lane. She is at her wits' end. Things have been — *taken*."

Poirot's orange face went pink with pleasure. "Taken! What things?"

The list, when it arrived the next day, was a poem of pettiness: one climbing shoe, one stethoscope, a bracelet, a powder compact, a banana-bread cookbook, a box of chocolates, lightbulbs, a tube of itch-paste, a rucksack, a silk scarf, and a diamond ring that had later been discovered, to universal shrieking, in a bowl of soup.

"Magnifique," breathed Poirot. "A problem most *ripe*. I congratulate your sister. One does not often meet a crime so beautifully ridiculous."

Mrs. Hubbard, the warden — a motherly mandrill with a face like a concerned sunset — showed Poirot round the hostel herself. It smelled of toast, fur oil, and seventy kinds of ambition, and it contained, among others: Celia Austin, a homesick little capuchin; Colin MacNab, the psychology student she adored; Nigel Chimpman, brilliant and silk-voiced, who wore green ink the way other monkeys wore cologne; Patricia Langur, who was fond of Nigel to the point of blindness; Valerie Hobhouse, who worked in a dress shop and gambled; Len Bateson, hearty and stethoscoped; and Mr. Akibombo, a West African vervet exchange student of great gravity and small appetite.

Within a week, Celia Austin confessed to the thefts.

"I did it for Colin," she wept, glowing even through her tears. "I read it in his own textbook. A female who appears to be in *distress* attracts protective attention. So I stole things. It was like being a bandit! Only small. A very small bandit."

Colin proposed to her on the spot, so pleased was he with her command of the literature. The hostel threw a party. The missing ring was returned, its diamond mysteriously dimmer than anyone remembered, and harmony was restored.

At three o'clock the next morning, harmony stopped. Celia Austin was found dead in her bed, a spilled sleeping draught beside her, a note in her own hand. *Suicide*, said the note. *Morphia*, said the doctor. *Murder*, said the little grey fur follicles of Hercule Poirot.

"Look at her paws," he told Inspector Sharpe. "No monkey who has taken morphia by choice grips her blanket like *that*. That is the grip of somebody who changed her mind and was not permitted to. Somebody at this hostel is not a student of anything except other monkeys."

The thefts, Poirot now saw, had been two crimes wearing one coat. Celia had stolen her trinkets for love. But the *nastier* items — the lightbulbs unscrewed, the notes defaced in green ink, Len Bateson's rucksack slashed — those belonged to somebody else. Somebody who wanted a general fog of wickedness in which any specific crime might hide.

Nigel Chimpman admitted, at last, to the stethoscope. "A wager," he said lightly. "I dressed as a doctor and raided a hospital dispensary to win a bet — morphine, digitalin, hyoscine, the whole medicine cabinet. Boys' monkey business."

"You are thirty-one years old," said Poirot, "and no boy's monkey business is conducted in a hospital dispensary. What did you *do* with the drugs, monsieur?"

"Lost them, probably," said Nigel, and smiled like a silk scarf sliding off a table.

Then Mrs. Nutcoletis, the hostel's shrewd old macaque landlady, who had grown nervous and talkative in the same week, drank poisoned brandy and died. And Patricia Langur, who had telephoned the inspector to say she had discovered *something further*, was found bludgeoned on the stairs. She had, it emerged, meant to write to Nigel's estranged father. She never finished the address.

Poirot sat in the hostel common room with the slashed rucksack in his lap, feeling the base of it with his thumbs, over and over, like a baboon testing fruit.

"Corrugated," he announced at last. "Double-bottomed. Messieurs, mesdames — these rucksacks have been sold to you, innocent students, and sent home to distant countries by you, innocent travelers, and every one has trotted round the world carrying jewels and drugs under its lining. The rucksacks were the *point* of the whole enterprise. This hostel was never a den of thieves. It was a *post office* for a smuggling ring — with Mrs. Nutcoletis supplying the money and her dress-shop connection laundering the takings."

The police raided the dress shop. Valerie Hobhouse, white-faced, confessed her part: it was she who had suggested the theft scheme to silly Celia in the first place, planning only that worthless things should vanish — and when Celia accidentally stole a real diamond, Valerie had swapped it for a chip of glass and staged the return in the soup. "I had debts," she said flatly. "The shop was a hole I kept falling into. I am not sorry for the ring. I am sorry for Celia. She was the only one of us who stole for a *nice* reason."

"But you did not kill her," said Poirot, "and you did not kill your — aha. Yes, mademoiselle. I have said a word too many, and your face has said several. Mrs. Nutcoletis was your *mother*."

The common room went silent as a tree at moonset. Valerie's chin came up. "She never acknowledged me. She made me live in her house and be a stranger in it. But when he poisoned her—" her voice cracked like a green branch, "—yes, monsieur. I will tell you everything, and gladly."

She had, it came out, telephoned the inspector that last night in imitation of Patricia's voice, to give Nigel an alibi. She had been loyal to the ring. She was loyal to no one now.

"There remains the formality," said Poirot, "of the *who*."

He gathered the hostel — students, warden, inspector — beneath the big clock on the common-room wall, and he told them a story. Of a young monkey who poisoned his own mother years ago when she threatened to prosecute him for forging cheques in her name. Of a father who discovered it, and, to avoid scandal, made his son sign a full confession — change your name, he said, begin again, and know that this paper goes to the police the day you offend again. Of a father recently dead. Of a solicitor's office, visited that very morning by one Hercule Poirot, warrant in pocket.

"Hickory dickory dock," said Poirot softly. "The macaque ran up the clock. But the hands go round, monsieur. They always go round. The clock has struck for you."

Nigel Chimpman lunged — for the door, for the poker, for dignity, history does not agree — and was sat upon by Len Bateson, heartily, with the whole rugby-trained weight of the hostel's most stethoscoped resident. Even then, Nigel had one weapon left.

"He was going to ruin everything," Nigel said, in a voice of pure silk. "Patricia. The little fool meant to write to my father. One does not let sentiment interfere with *business*."

"No," said Poirot. "One does not let murder interfere with breakfast, either, and yet you did, twice, and breakfast here is very good. I do not approve of you, monsieur. You are a bad primate, and worse, a *tedious* one — you have made seventy young monkeys frightened in their own tree. Take him away."

They did. The hostel, in time, recovered its appetite. Mr. Akibombo was elected president of the students' society by a landslide, mostly for having been right about the soup. Celia's diamond was recovered and given, by Valerie from prison, to Colin — who married it, in a sense, publishing in due course a paper on criminal attention-seeking that became a classic, dedicated *to C.A., who understood the theory better than the practice, and paid for my career, and did not deserve any of it.*

And Mrs. Hubbard took down the clock to dust it, and found, jammed behind it, one missing lightbulb.

"He unscrewed them," she told her sister wonderingly, "so we'd all go about in the dark."

"That," said Miss Lemur, typing it out neatly with no errors at all, "is what he thought monkeys were for."
