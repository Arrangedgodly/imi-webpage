# Swinger in a Strange Land

*A parody of "Stranger in a Strange Land"*

The expedition ship *Envoy* had reached Mars a generation ago and vanished, and the world had filed it under tragedies and moved on. So when the recovery mission *Champion* finally landed and found one survivor, the news vines went insane — because the survivor was not one of the crew. He was their son, born on the outbound voyage and orphaned in the landing, a small macaque with grave golden eyes who had been raised, alone among his kind, by the Old Ones of Mars.

His name was Michael Valentine Smith, and he was quietly the most legally important monkey alive: under the old frontier precedent, the sole owner of an entire planet.

The government stashed him in a hospital suite and told the world nothing, on the theory that a monkey who owns Mars is simpler as a rumor than as a patient. Mike, for his part, did not mind the room. Mars had been rooms — vast, patient, waterless rooms — and he did not yet understand doors, or money, or weather, or grief, all of which are hard subjects for a monkey who had never seen rain.

What broke the stalemate broke it by accident. Gillian Boardman — Jill, a bonobo nurse with a rule-breaking streak and the bonobo conviction that every problem is ultimately a grooming problem — got into the suite on a stolen badge, sat with the small lost monkey from another world, and, when he stared at her glass of water with a hunger that had nothing to do with thirst, taught him without thinking the oldest gesture of her kind.

She gave him half her banana.

In Martian terms, this was not a snack. Water — and its soft yellow cousin — was the rarest thing on Mars, and sharing it was the deepest bond two minds could make. Mike received the gift the way a drowning monkey receives a vine, and from that hour, in his own accounting, Jill was not a nurse or a stranger. She was his 'nana-brother, and he would move worlds for her, which is more literal than it sounds, since he owned one.

"'Nana-brother," he pronounced, carefully, in a voice like leaves. "Now we are one troop. I will gibbon you."

"What does that mean, sweetheart — gibbon?"

Mike considered with the terrible patience the Old Ones had taught him. "It is when you drink a thing so completely that you fill it and it fills you, and you can never be a stranger to it again. A stone gibbons rain. A troop gibbons its tree. I have not yet gibbed Earth. I do not understand monkeys. Earth is very wet and very loud."

He would come to understand monkeys. It would cost him everything, and he would pay it gladly.

Jill and the muckraking journalist Ben Caxton — a chimp with a scandal in every pocket — smuggled Mike out of the hospital in a nurse's coat when the government's doctors began talking about him in the third person while he was in the room, and carried him to the one monkey in the country loud enough, rich enough, and ornery enough to hide a planet's owner in plain sight: Jubal Harshaw, ancient orangutan, doctor, lawyer, novelist, composer of filthy songs, and owner of the most comfortable estate in the hemisphere.

"What you have brought me," Jubal said, peering over his spectacles at the small macaque drinking in the sight of the swimming pool like scripture, "is either the biggest lawsuit since the Big Bang or the end of the world. Possibly both. He stays."

Under Jubal's roof, Mike learned Earth at orangutan speed. He learned English and six other languages, and taught the household Martian in exchange — Duke the handyman and Miriam the secretary and Dorcas and Anne the Fair Witness, who saw everything and testified to exactly what she saw and not one fig more, a profession Mike studied for a year before he gibbed it. He learned laughter last and hardest. Martians, he explained, did not laugh, because they did not hurt; and then one afternoon, watching the circus monkeys of Earth bump and brawl and misunderstand and love each other anyway — all of it wrong, all of it at once, the whole species trying its enormous best with its comical small hands — Mike suddenly understood the joke that human beings are, and laughed until he had to hold a tree, and wept, and laughed again.

"I gibbon it," he said at last, astonished. "Monkeys hurt, and know they hurt, and go on. It is funny because it is sad, and sad because it is funny, and I laugh because the other choice is to go home. Thou art all so *brave*," he said, and every monkey in the room had to walk into the garden for a while.

Mike toured the churches, as an anthropologist tithing. The biggest was the Church of the Rapturous Hoot — a cathedral-and-broadcast empire where a monkey in a gold jumpsuit healed the faithful by the score live on the hour, and the collection figs came in by the truckload. Mike listened with his whole body, the Martian way, and reported to Jubal: "It is a grove that gibbons money. There is no 'nana in it. They share nothing." And Jubal said: "Congratulations, son. You've just founded a theology. Now don't do anything with it."

He did something with it.

It began as a nest — a house where 'nana-brothers came and went, where water and fruit and blankets and love were shared on sight, where Mike taught what Mars had taught him: that every monkey is a festival in progress; that anger is just grief wearing armor; that if two monkeys truly gibbon each other, there is no room between them for fear. They called it the Church of All Trees, and the trees in the name were not decoration. Every faith on Earth, Mike said, was a grove of one troop: "All the trees are one tree. All the troops are one troop. Thou art Ape. I am Ape. The beggar at the gate and the banker in the tower: Ape. All of it, grokking — *gibbing* — itself. How can there be strangers, if we are all one troop?"

The young came in their hundreds. The old institutions took note, as old institutions do, with lawyers and then with mobs. Mike, who had learned the Old Ones' way of unmaking matter — who had quietly made two troublesome photographers simply *not be*, and grieved for a season over the wrongness of it, and never did it again — was begged by his nest to make the danger not be. He refused.

"You do not gibbon it," he told them, gently, at the last sharing. "I could make the hurt stop, and then you would be safe, and safety is a cage with soft walls. A monkey must be free to go ape, even at me. Especially at me. I came to Earth to learn monkeys, and this —" he gestured at the torches coming up the hill, almost fondly — "this is the last lesson. Wish me luck. I am only a sprout."

The mob broke into the nest an hour later. What happened inside, nobody could testify with a straight face: the lights died, there was a sound like ten thousand figs falling at once, and when the torches came back up there was no angry mob left standing and, on the floor of the nest, no more Michael — only a faint sweetness of bananas, and a warmth in every monkey who had been present, and, in the rafters, the sound of laughter — full, free, unafraid — getting fainter and further away, like a swing released at the top of an arc.

His 'nana-brothers took what remained in the Martian fashion, that nothing be wasted and no one be parted, and each of them carried him out into the world forever after, in the way you carry anyone you have completely gibbed.

At the estate, Jubal Harshaw — who had hidden a planet, raised a messiah, and outlived them both — poured two glasses, drank one, and addressed the empty chair in the finest ORANGUTAN TRADITION: "Well, son. You always said monkeys were the joke and the punchline both. Go home, tell the Old Ones we're trying. And if they know what's good for them —" he raised the second glass — "they'll count you a full grove."

And far out past the orbit of the moon, in the vast dry rooms where the Old Ones keep the memories of worlds, a small macaque with grave golden eyes opened his eyes on all of it at once — every troop, every tree, every brave small hand — and began, at last, to laugh like a Martian.
