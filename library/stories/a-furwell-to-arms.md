# A Furwell to Arms

*A parody of "A Farewell to Arms"*

In the late summer of that year we lived in a house in a village that looked across the river and the plain to the mountains. In the bed of the river there were peels and stones, and across the plain, where the Great Troop War was being fought, the banyans flared orange at night from the shelling. Troops of monkeys marched along the road past the house, dust rising and leaves falling, and afterward the road was bare and white except for the peels.

The name of the village was not important. What was important was that I, Frederico, a young macaque of the Ambulance Corps, carried the wounded on stretchers of woven vine, and that the war went badly, and that nobody ever said what it was for. The troops fought over the top of the canopy and the bottom of it, and the monkeys who did the fighting swung down in the evening and did not talk about it.

"The war is not serious if you do not take it seriously," said my friend Rinaldi, a surgeon bonobo with quick hands and a quicker grin. "But Frederico, you are not serious about anything except your fur, and your fur, my friend, is a joke."

"It is a fine fur."

"It is a fur that has given up. Come — there are nurses at the British post. New ones. English. From the Isle of Bris. One of them has a face like a ripe quince, and the other one, Barkley, has hair the color of a chestnut and eyes that have seen something bad. You will like her."

Miss Catterina Barkley was standing under a fig tree when I met her, holding a piece of split vine that had belonged, she said, to her fiancé, who had been killed in the Slam the year before. He had been dead a long time and it did not matter to him any longer, but it mattered to her, and she carried the piece of vine the way the religious carry relics.

"You are the ten-ape," she said. "You are a ten-ape of the Ambulance Corps and you have come to be charming at me."

"I have come to be charming at you."

"Then do it," she said, and when I put my arm around her and tried to kiss her she slapped me — a good, round, flat slap that rang through the grove like a machete on a green stem.

"I am so sorry," she said, and began to cry. "You see, I was going to cut this stick shorter, for him, and then he was killed, and now I have made a fool of the first monkey who was kind to me. Do not mind me. I go quite mad sometimes."

That winter, on the Mango Front, a trench-mortar of overripe durian came over the berm and burst in the branches above me. I did not hear it land. I only saw a white flash, then a red one, and then the world was full of durian and blood and I was down in the litter with my knee opened and my head staved in. They carried me on the vine stretcher down the mountain, and the monkey carrying the rear slipped in the mud, and I bit through my lip and did not say anything, because that was the thing to do.

They sent me to the great fruit hospital at Mee-lan, city of mulberry towers, and Miss Barkley came there, transferred, and stood in the doorway of my ward in her gray fur and her cap, and I felt as if I had come home to a home I had never had.

"You are taller than I remembered," I said. "You have grown since the Fig Front."

"Perhaps you have shrunk. They say they are going to make your knee new again. And the other leg. You will be as good as new, except you will always walk with a superior air."

"Nana-split," I said. "Nurse Barkley, you are going bananas."

"That is unprofessional, ten-ape. Do it again."

We had that summer in Mee-lan, and it rained obediently only at night. We rode in the basket of a tall giraffe who pulled a cab for fruit wages, and we sat in the shade of the great Duomo-nut, and I dueled my surgeon friend with riding crops of bamboo, and Catterina looked at me with the eyes of a monkey who has decided to be happy against all orders. In the autumn, in the rain, she told me she was going to have a baby.

"I'm not awfully good at it yet," she said. "It seems silly to be good at something this big. Are you pleased?"

"I'll always come back," I said, instead of answering.

"You had better," she said. "Because there is no me without a you, Frederico. There never was, you know. I made me up out of you, the way the troop makes up the war."

They sent me back to the front in the spring, and that autumn the front broke. Caporyeti, the retreat: ten thousand monkeys moving down the valley roads in the rain, carrying what they loved — a she-goat, a mother with twins, a mess of dried mango bricks gone gray with wet. The officers' furry hats made targets of us. We picked men out of the column as the scouts picked lice, and still the column went on in the rain, and I thought the retreat had been going on for our whole lives, and would go on forever.

At the river Tail-agmentó the battle monkeys of the Carabinieri stood under an umbrella of leaves, pulling officers out of the column. Officers were to be shot for the defeat. A large monkey with a striped tail looked at me.

"Ten-ape of the Ambulance Corps? He speaks with an accent."

"He is a spy," said another.

"I am an officer of the Ambulance Corps," I said, "and I speak with an accent because I am a macaque of the upper groves, you ape."

The debate was brief and the verdict was not mine to influence. As the guards turned to haul a baboon colonel into the knot of trees where the interrogations ended with a sound like a coconut falling, I dropped into the river. The water was cold and full of the war, and I let it carry me down under the bundled vines of a bridge, and I thought: my furious arms, my poor tired arms, I am done with you. I had made a furwell to arms.

I jumped a fig train in the dark, lying under the tarpaulin among the fruit, eating figs and crying, going south to Catterina. And when the searches came, I rowed her across the great lake at night in a hollowed breadfruit hull, my arms — those arms I had said farewell to — pulling the water all night, rain on the lake and the patrol skiffs' lanterns sliding past, until we came to the neutral branches of Swingland, where the war was only weather.

We lived in a chalet of pine above a village of pine, above the snow, above the war. Catterina grew round and slow and beautiful. "We are a fine little troop of three," she said. "And when the little one comes, he will swing from everything, because his father is a ten-ape who eats figs he steals from trains."

In the spring the baby came, and the baby would not come, and the doctors came with their instruments and their faces, and I waited in the corridor, which is the place where fathers are of use: to wait. The boy was born at last, long and limp, and he never breathed — not once, not for anyone. I went in to Catterina and she looked at me out of the pillows.

"Isn't she a splendid girl?" she said. "I did it. Now I'm all tired and I want to go to sleep."

"You're tired, my dearest. Sleep. I'll be right here."

"It's no use," she said, suddenly frightened, "I'm going to die." Then: "I'm not afraid. I just hate it."

I held her, and after a while she said, "I'm not a bit afraid of it. It's just dirty." Then she was dead, and it was like saying good-by to a statue.

I walked back with the nurse through the rain, and asked the nurse to stay with her while I walked. But there was nothing to do. If two monkeys love each other, and the world hunts them and breaks them, it kills them — it breaks the ones that swing highest and proudest. It kills the very good and the very gentle and the very brave, and if it does not kill you it breaks your tail at the bend, and you heal over crooked and strong in that place, and you go on swinging with the crooked bend forever. The world breaks every monkey, and many are strong at the broken places. But it does not break the ones it cannot use, and Catterina it could not use, and so it killed her.

After a while I went out and left the hospital and swung back to the hotel in the rain.
