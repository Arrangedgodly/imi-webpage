# Love in the Time of Gorillas

*A parody of "Love in the Time of Cholera"*

It was inevitable: the scent of overripe bananas reminded Florentino Ariza of the fate of unrequited love. Gorillas, strictly speaking, have no scent — but a young spider monkey in love believes everything he is told.

It began, as these things do, with a death. Jeremiah de Saint-Amour, a photographer of one-eyed capuchins and the finest chess-player on the coast, had poisoned himself against the pain of old age, leaving instructions with his great friend Dr. Juvenal Urbino. It was the Sunday of Pentecost, and Dr. Urbino, sixty-one and elegant, went to identify the body and came home meditating on death, which is a monkey's only unavoidable appointment. That same afternoon he climbed the mango tree in his own courtyard to catch the parrot that would not come down, fell from the lowest branch, and was unconscious before he hit the ground. His last breath was for his wife: "Only the Great Ape in the Sky knows how much I loved you."

But the love had come first, and so had the other one, and the reader will forgive a jungle for telling it out of order.

Florentino Ariza, the shyest vine-climber on the telegraph lianas of Puerto Plátano, fell in love at first sight with Fermina Daza, a young howler monkey with doeskin eyes and, when she chose to use it, a voice that could be heard two canopies away. He wrote her letters — seventy pages, then a hundred — perfumed with crushed orchid, and delivered them by the electric liana until her father, a banana smuggler with a mule train and a whip, discovered the correspondence and marched her into the mountains to forget the boy.

She nearly did. But her Aunt Escolástica, keeper of the letters and secret ally of love, had taught her to read between the lianas, and for four years the courtship continued in secret. Then, one afternoon back in the city, she saw Florentino waiting in the arcade, cold and aloof and trembling, and in an instant, with the resolve of a free and independent monkey, she felt the spell break.

"No, please," she said. "Forget it."

And she went on fanning herself without looking at him again, and the abyss of despair opened at her feet. Florentino swore eternal fidelity on the spot, to the arcade, to himself, and to the paving stones — a vow he would spend fifty-one years, nine months, and four days finding ways to keep.

Some years later she married Dr. Juvenal Urbino — a gorilla, the most elegant and thoroughly modern physician in the jungle, who courted her with a genuine African parrot and the most persuasive arguments of medical science, and carried her off to a house with mirrors, a piano, and an imported mahogany examination tree. He was correct, punctual, linen-suited, and an inch short of passionate; on their wedding trip to Europe she wept with homesickness over the ruins of Castile, and he consoled her with architecture. On their return they had one legendary fight, which lasted four months and was about soap: she said the sheets smelled of the soap he chose; he said he had never smelled any soap; both waited for the other to crack, in a silence like two troops at war over one mango, until he — magnificent, defeated — surrendered. "The smell stays," he said. "But it was always the soap." Marriage, Fermina concluded, was an absurd invention: two monkeys solving with tears what they could have solved with a washcloth.

It was the era the historians called the Time of Gorillas. The great apes had come down from the mountains a century before and built the port, and the opera house, and the hill-climbing trolley lines; and with the gorillas, the howlers said, had come the fever. Gorilla fever, they called it, though the gorillas called it slander: the patients turned gray, stopped grooming, and died sitting upright. Dr. Urbino had given his whole life to sanitary reform, aqueducts, and the draining of the fever swamp. Fermina was, for fifty years, the doctor's prime mate.

Florentino Ariza never married. He waited.

He was not, in the meantime, idle. His mother, Tránsito, a cheerful launderer of other monkeys' secrets, told him the only cure was to take his mind off it; his uncle Leo XII, director of the Caribbean River Banana Company, gave him a desk and a career. He rose to be president of the company, dressed like a funeral, wrote beauty-shop ditties for the whole coast, and kept his liaisons recorded in notebooks — six hundred and twenty-two long-term loves, besides countless fleeting adventures of the vines — and each one, he told himself, was only a way of bearing the unbearable absence of Fermina. There was the widow Nazaret, who was glad of him, and Ausencia Santander, who left him with a feeling of flight, and countless others whose names he underlined in ink. "Monkeys," he wrote, "are not born once and for all on the day their mothers give birth to them. Life obliges them, over and over again, to give birth to themselves."

At the funeral, Florentino Ariza — gaunt and gray and trembling — approached the widow, held his hat over his chest, and said:

"Fermina: I have waited for this opportunity for more than half a century, to repeat to you once again my vow of eternal fidelity and everlasting love."

She was seized by a fury of the living. "Go away," she said. "And don't ever let me see you again. I beg you."

He went. He was seventy-six years old, and he began again.

This time he wrote no orchid letters. He typed her letters about dead heroes and old monkeys and the strange dignity of the banana, meditations on life and grief, and signed no name. For years they came, one a week, and slowly the widow — who in fifty years of marriage had never once felt anything but comfortable — began to look forward to the mail. Her daughter-of-the-heart, OFELIA, ordered her to stop: "Love at our age is a monkey business." Fermina answered by banning the daughter instead. She invited Florentino aboard the riverboat New Fidelity for a banana soda. He was eighty-one. She was seventy-two.

The first evening on deck, he said, "I have kept my virginity for you." She did not speak to him for a week. But he was patient the way rivers are patient, and by the return trip they were sharing guava paste under the awning while the banks slid by, two old monkeys amazed to discover that the heart does not wear out — it only changes address.

"And how long do you think we can keep up this coming and going?" the captain asked, when the two old monkeys asked him to keep the gossiping passengers and the parrots away from them.

Florentino Ariza kept the answer ready, as he had kept it ready all his life.

"Forever," he said.

The captain looked at the couple, and at the brown jungle river sliding past, and gave the order: up with the yellow flag of gorilla fever, that no ship might come alongside and interrupt the love of two old monkeys adrift on the current of time. The port authorities radioed in alarm — a fever ship, quarantine — and the New Fidelity was free at last of every monkey alive. Down the river they went, and turned, and went up again, past the burnt ghost of the old banana compounds, past the drowned villages of the flood, and the howlers on the banks never learned that it was not the fever that would not let them land — it was the cure, at last, after fifty-one years, nine months, and four days.
