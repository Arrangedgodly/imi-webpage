# A Chimpmas Carol

*A parody of "A Christmas Carol"*

Marley was dead, to begin with. There was no doubt whatever about that. Old Marley had been Scrooge's partner for many years, and his name was now as dead as a doornail — or, as the jungle apes said, as a dried-up banana peel.

And Scrooge — Ebenezer Scrooge, the cold-hearted old baboon — was worse. The frost of the jungle was in his fur, his chin, his pointed nose; he carried his own low temperature about with him, and iced the grooming pool in hot weather. In his counting-house, his clerk Bob Cratchit — a humble little chimp in a comfort scarf — tried to warm his paws at a candle.

"A Chimpmas Carol, uncle! God save you!" cried his nephew Fred, a warm young chimp with a smile that could have ripened fruit. "Chimpmas a humbug, uncle? Come dine with us tomorrow!"

"Nonsense," said Scrooge. "Chimpmas is a time for finding yourself a year older and not an hour richer. Keep Chimpmas in your way, and let me keep it in mine."

"Keeping it!" said Fred. "But you don't keep it!"

"Then let me leave it alone, then," said Scrooge. "Much good may it do you."

"There are many things from which I have never profited, I dare say," returned the nephew. "Chimpmas among the rest. But I have always thought of it as a good time; a kind, forgiving, charitable, pleasant time; the only time I know of when monkeys and apes seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely. God bless it, I say!"

Two gentlemen apes came collecting for the poor. Were there no prisons, Scrooge asked? No workhouses? Then the poor were all provided for. And when told that many would rather die than go there — "They had better do it, then, and decrease the surplus population." Bob Cratchit asked meekly for Chimpmas Day off. Scrooge granted it, sourly, "but be here all the earlier the next morning."

That night, in his gloomy chambers, Scrooge heard the clanking of chains — and there, through the heavy door, came Marley's Ghost: a translucent baboon dragging a heavy chain of cash-boxes, keys, ledgers, and — this being the jungle — several thousand banana stems.

"How now!" said Scrooge. "What do you want with me?"

"Much. You don't believe in me."

"I don't," said Scrooge. "A little thing affects the senses. A slight disorder of the stomach makes them cheats. You may be an undigested bit of banana, a blot of mustard, a fragment of an underdone potato."

"Woe upon you, Ebenezer! I wear the chain I forged in life, link by link and yard by yard. And you have labored on one of your own! Seven years dead, and I have wandered ceaseless — for charity, mercy, and benevolence were all my business, and I never did them! Monkeykind was my business!"

"Speak comfort to me, Jacob!"

"I have none to give. Hear me: you will be haunted by three Spirits. Expect the first tomorrow, when the bell tolls one. Without their visits, you cannot hope to shun the path I tread!"

Marley vanished, wringing his ghostly hands, and Scrooge went to bed — and was woken by the Ghost of Chimpmas Past, a strange young ape glowing like a flame.

"Rise, and walk with me."

Scrooge watched himself as a lonely schoolboy, abandoned at the boarding-tree at Chimpmas — and wept to see it.

Then, across the snow of the vision, came little Fan, his sister, leaning out of a litter-sled. "I have come to bring you home, dear brother! Father is kinder than he used to be. You're never to go back to that school! We're to be together all Chimpmas long!" And Scrooge, watching, whispered: "Always a delicate cub, Fan. She had a large heart. She died a young ape — and her son is my nephew Fred, whose dinner I have never once attended."

He watched the merry Fezziwig Ball, where old Fezziwig the gorilla fiddled, and Fezziwig's wife laughed, and every clerk's heart was light with dancing and great seething bowls of banana punch. "He had the power to render us happy or unhappy," Scrooge whispered. "A power easily abused — and mine, and never used."

And then Belle, his sweetheart, in a room where a young Scrooge sat counting by a fire that was going out. "Another idol has displaced me," said Belle. "You fear the world too much. All your other hopes have merged into the hope of being rich. I release you with a whole heart." And the Spirit showed him Belle in after-years, a mother with grandchildren, happy without him — and Scrooge begged to see no more, seized the great extinguisher cap, and plunged it down over the shining head.

The Ghost of Chimpmas Present — a jolly giant gorilla upon a throne of bananas, with a glowing torch — showed him the Cratchit home, where Bob came in with Tiny Tim, a small crippled chimp with a tiny crutch, riding on his shoulder. They had a goose and a pudding (both small, but "the greatest success since the invention of bananas"). Bob proposed the toast: "Mr. Scrooge, the Founder of the Feast!"

"The Founder of the Feast, indeed!" said Mrs. Cratchit, reddening. "I wish I had him here. I'd give him a piece of my mind!"

"My dear," said Bob, "it is Chimpmas Day."

"I'll drink his health for your sake and the day's," said Mrs. Cratchit. "Not for his."

And Tiny Tim declared, "God bless us every one!" — and the ghost warned that Tim would die if these shadows remained unaltered, and Scrooge hung his head, remembering surplus populations.

Then to Fred's house, where the guests played the game of Yes and No, and Fred, thinking of something live and savage and growling in London, got the answer "It's your uncle Scro-o-o-ooge!" — and they laughed until their ribs ached, and Fred drank his uncle's health, promising to give him the same chance every year, whether he liked it or not.

And then, from the great gorilla's robes, crept two wretched, wolfish chimpanzee cubs — a boy and a girl — yellow, meager, ragged, scowling.

"Spirit, are they yours?" Scrooge asked, appalled.

"They are Man's," said the Spirit. "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Beware them both — but most of all beware this boy, for on his brow I see written: Doom. Deny it!" cried the Spirit, stretching a hand toward the city. "Admit it for your factious purposes, and make it worse, and abide the end!"

"Have they no refuge or resource?" cried Scrooge.

"Are there no prisons?" said the Spirit, turning on him for the first time. "Are there no workhouses?"

Last came the Ghost of Chimpmas Yet to Come, a hooded phantom that pointed only. Scrooge saw merchants in the jungle exchange dividing a dead ape's bed curtains and shirt buttons — "He frightened every ape away from him when he was alive, to profit us when he was dead!" — and Scrooge, not yet understanding, said, "The case of this unhappy monkey might be my own." He saw the Cratchit house, hushed, Tiny Tim's crutch beside an empty chair. And he saw a neglected grave — with his own name upon the stone: EBENEZER SCROOGE.

"Assure me that I may yet change these shadows!" Scrooge cried, clutching the phantom's spectral fur. "I will honour Chimpmas in my heart and try to keep it all the year! I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future! The Spirits of all Three shall strive within me!"

He woke in his own bed. Chimpmas morning! Sunlight! And he was alive as a monkey can be.

"I am as light as a feather, I am as happy as an angel, I am as merry as a schoolboy — I am as giddy as a drunken orang!"

He dressed in his best, went to church, patted children on the head — and in the afternoon went straight to his nephew's and astonished Fred nearly out of his fur: "It's I, your uncle Scrooge! I have come to dinner. Will you let me in, Fred?"

He caught a passing urchin by the collar: "An intelligent boy! A remarkable boy! Do you know whether they've sold the prize banana at the corner stand this morning — the big one? Go and buy it!"

He sent the Cratchits the biggest banana in all the jungle — so big that Bob Cratchit, opening the door, nearly fainted twice.

The next morning Bob was late — eighteen minutes and a half late.

"Hello!" growled Scrooge, in his old inimitable way. "What do you mean by coming here at this time of day?"

"I'm very sorry, sir," said Bob. "I am behind my time."

"You are?" repeated Scrooge. "Yes, I think you are. Step this way, if you please. Because, Bob Cratchit — I am about to raise your salary. A Merry Chimpmas, Bob! I'll raise your salary, and endeavor to assist your struggling family, and we will discuss your affairs this very afternoon over a bowl of banana punch!"

And Scrooge was better than his word: he became a second father to Tiny Tim (who did NOT die), and it was always said that he knew how to keep Chimpmas well, if any ape alive possessed the knowledge.

And as Tiny Tim observed: "God bless us — every single monkey of us!"
