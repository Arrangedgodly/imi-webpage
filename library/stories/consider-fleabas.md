# Consider Fleabas

*A parody of "Consider Phlebas"*

Consider Fleabas, who was once called the finest groomer of his troop, and who floated dead in the long canal of the Vavatch Orbital with his fur combed out around him like a halo, going nowhere at exactly the speed of regret. Was he lucky, that he missed what came after? This story does not answer.

The war was between the Gorridans — three-ton gorilla zealots of the triune god, who believed every bough in the universe belonged properly under one troop — and the Culture: a scattershot paradise of bonobos and langurs gone pleasantly soft, their everything run by the Minds, ship-brained intelligences as clever as a warehouse of genius capuchins.

In the middle: Horza the Pelt-Changer, a lone langur whose gift was shifting his fur, his face, his very scent, into any monkey's shape at all. He hated the Culture's easy bananas. So when a crippled Culture Mind came down in hiding on Snar's World — the forbidden Planet of the Dead, an old quarantine ground guarded by a dormant machine deity — the Gorridans hired Horza to go in and get it. With him, as prisoner and proof the war was real, they sent Balveda: a Culture agent, a bonobo of unbearable calm, who spent the voyage studying Horza the way a patient anthropologist studies a bad-tempered chimp.

"You'll lose," she told him, over the ration fruit. "Not because you aren't clever. Because you're clever *alone*, and we're clever in orchestras."

To reach Snar's World he needed a crew, and the nearest crew was Kraiklyn's Free Company aboard the *Ripe Air Turbulence*, mercenary monkeys of flexible morals. To reach *them*, Horza had to survive the Overripe: a cult on Vavatch who met in white tents to eat fruit that had gone black and bubbling, so that, as they put it, "no sweetness is wasted, and no monkey escapes fermentation either."

"Do you accept the Ripe?" the cult's matron asked Horza that last evening, offering him a banana that had achieved a color bananas should be arrested for.

"I accept that everything ends," Horza said.

"Wrong answer," said the matron, fondly, and that night, while the Orbital was being evacuated ahead of the war's appetite, Horza escaped the casserole by out-changering the guard — taking the shape of the matron's own dead first groomer. A cruel trick, but he was in a hurry.

Balveda, watching the evacuation from their hold, said nothing for a long time. Vavatch, the carnival Orbital, was being emptied monkey by monkey — and then, by arrangement, it would be eaten. The Culture had judged the place indefensible, and what could not be defended would not be left as a trophy: after the last shuttle left, the war would send its appetite down the spinways, and a world that had taken ten thousand years to grow would be digested in an afternoon.

"You could stop it," Horza said. "Your orchestras."

"We are stopping it," said Balveda. "That's what stopping it looks like, from the inside. Remember that, Pelt-Changer, when you're deciding who the monsters are: everyone in this war is somebody's idea of the fire brigade."

On the way out he paused, one hour, to watch the great game.

They played Peel in a circle of torchlight while the sky shuttles roared and the last monkeys of Vavatch wept their goodbyes: each player stripping a browning fruit, layer by layer, betting with grooming-debts, then whisker-length, then the willingness of their own memories. The house took everything eventually. The game did not care who won; it only wanted everyone to admit, before the world ended, what everything is worth. Horza played three hands, won a young courier named Waha, lost him again, and left lighter than he arrived.

The *Ripe Air Turbulence* took him as gunner, along with Balveda in a cabin-cell, a sulking drone the company had won at dice — a brass coconut named Unaha-Clops, all sensor and sarcasm — and Yalson, a scarred macaque with a rifle and a low opinion of mystics, who changed that opinion slowly, over a season of shared watches, into something neither of them named.

"I've seen your kind before," Yalson told him. "Monkeys who can be anybody. They're nobody for so long they forget which one's the spare."

"That's why I like you," said Horza, who was, at that moment, wearing his own face.

Kraiklyn, pink-eared and cheerful, sold their next raid to a temple: the Temple of Light on Marjoin, said to hold relics of the war's first age. "It's a legend," Horza said. "Legends are where treasure *lives*," said Kraiklyn, and led them in. The temple's wards did not care about treasure or legend, only about trespass, and the raid bought the company three graves and no relics. Horza voted, afterward, for mutiny by attrition: he simply stopped disagreeing, and let Kraiklyn's judgment do the rest.

They ran for Snar's World with the war behind them, ship bleeding atmosphere, and set down on the ice above the black tunnels of the Command System, where the crippled Mind lay hidden — a little silver sphere, the cleverest thing in the sector, minding its own business in the dark like a dropped fruit under a bench. The landing wrecked the ship; for shelter there was only the Olmedreca — a dead leviathan half-buried in the ice shelf, whale-vast, and, crucially, hollow. The company wintered in its ribcage, burning blubber, while Unaha-Clops hovered by the entrance narrating the weather with murderous politeness.

"Ceiling of meat," the drone observed. "Walls of meat. I have served many poor commands, but this is the first with an ambiance."

It did not stay hidden. The tunnels awoke: guardian machines, dead gods shifting in their sleep, ice coming down. Kraiklyn, deep in the dark, saw the silver sphere and saw, at last, the payday that would refit the ship and the legend, and turned his rifle on Horza to claim salvage rights. Horza was faster. He did not gloat and he did not hesitate, and he did not wear his own face when he did it, which he would think about, later, at length. The Free Company died around that betrayal — Yalson last of all, going down firing in a side-tunnel, leaving the future she'd been saving up for on the ice where nobody would find it — and Waha, betrayed and redeeming himself in the same ten seconds, hauling Horza clear of a fall that took two machines with it.

Horza ran the last stretch with the Mind cradled in his arms, the two of them, killer and cargo, traitor and treasure, all the way to the surface and the light of the rescue ships.

He did not make it. A collapsing bore took him at the threshold — not dramatically, not poetically; a threshold, and a falling shelf, and then the galaxy's finest Pelt-Changer was just a still shape with a borrowed face he never got to change back.

The Mind — rescued by the Culture's people, with Balveda and one surviving brass coconut at the wreck's edge — looked down at him for a long moment.

"He came all this way," Balveda said.

"He came all this way," the Mind agreed, "to keep me from going home."

They flew him out anyway, and buried him warm.

Consider Fleabas, then, floating in the canal with his fine combed fur. Consider Waha, who was won and lost in an hour and gave the hour back. Consider Yalson, and Kraiklyn, who died defending a legend from his own greed, and every monkey on both sides who ripened early for the war. The Gorridans won some of it; the Culture won the rest; and the war ended the way a banana peel ends, somewhere off to the side of history, slippery, forgotten, still faintly sweet.

Consider the cost. The game of Peel always collects.
