# The Bonoborrowers

*A parody of "The Borrowers"*

*May the fourteenth. Pod has just gone up to borrow. Mama is mending the hammock. I am fourteen years old today, and I have never once been borrowing. — A.*

Under the roots of the Great Fig Tree, in a cozy burrow furnished with a beetleshell bathtub and a postage-stamp portrait of Great-Aunt Sophy, lived three tiny bonobos: Pod, his wife Homily, and their daughter Arrietty. Above them, in the high branches, lived the Big Troop — enormous gorillas who stomped and dropped things and never once looked down.

"Don't be bitter, Homily," Pod always said. "Their dropping is our living."

For the Bonoborrowers were borrowers. What a gorilla lost between the floorboards of the world, they borrowed. A crumb of banana bread fed them for a week. One dropped peanut was a feast and a hat.

"Pod," said Homily that night, wringing her paws, "she wants to go borrowing. She's too young. She'll be SEEN."

"She has to learn sometime," said Pod.

"Seen by a gorilla!" whispered Homily. "Gorilla-beans are dangerous, Pod. My mother always said so, and my mother was right about everything except the weather."

So it was that Arrietty, on her fourteenth birthday, climbed the root-ladder after her father and saw, for the first time, the whole enormous ceiling of the world: the bark floor, the giant leaves, a banana peel lying across it like a fallen golden bridge.

"Breathe," said Pod. "Walk softly. Never run. If you run, you'll be seen."

"Borrowing," said Pod, as they climbed, "is not taking. Taking is what gorillas do to each other at birthday parties. Borrowing is quiet, and honest, and mostly invisible. You borrow what won't be missed. A pin. A crumb. A drop of honey from the rim of the jar, never the middle, the middle is noticed. And you NEVER," he said, stopping the ladder with one foot, "never borrow twice from the same place in the same moon. A missing crumb is an accident. Two missing crumbs is a ghost, and gorillas set traps for ghosts."

"What do they set for borrowers?" asked Arrietty.

"Nothing," said Pod. "Because they don't believe in us."

He said it proudly. But Arrietty, looking up the long dark of the ladder, thought it was the saddest sentence she had ever heard.

They had come up for a hatpin. But Arrietty stood too long in a beam of moonlight, drinking the whole enormous world in — the leaves like green sails, the fireflies like a fair — and that is exactly where a young gorilla saw her.

He was a small gorilla, as gorillas go — the troop's youngest, lonely, up past his bedtime, come down to look for a lost marble. He sat down very slowly, the way you sit when you don't want to frighten a butterfly, and he said, "Hello."

Arrietty's whole body said RUN. But she remembered her father's rule, and besides, there was nowhere to run to, and besides, nobody had ever said hello to her before. So she lifted her chin and said, "We're borrowers. We borrow."

"Borrow what?"

"Crumbs. Buttons. A drop of honey. Things you gorillas never miss."

The young gorilla thought about this with tremendous seriousness. "I have six peanuts," he said at last. "You may borrow four." And he set them down beside her, and asked her a hundred questions, and Arrietty — who had only ever spoken to Pod and Homily and Great-Aunt Sophy's portrait — found she had a hundred answers.

Then she asked one of her own, which she had been saving her whole life: "Are there others? Other borrowers. Other families."

The boy gorilla was quiet for a moment. "There's a story," he said. "My grandmother told it. About little people under the old fig tree, who came out at night. But she said the story was finished."

"Finished how?"

"She said the little people were all gone."

"Does your grandmother believe the story?" asked Arrietty.

"She says believing a finished story only makes it sadder."

"Then she is wrong," said Arrietty, with all fourteen of her years behind it, "because here I am."

After that, he left things beside the root-fence every few nights. A thimble cup. A matchbox dresser. Once, a slice of banana bread so large they had to saw it in two. And once, a book — a whole stamp-sized book of poems — and Arrietty read it aloud in the evenings until Homily cried, which she called the highest compliment.

But gifts are loud things in a quiet burrow. Homily grew fat on pride, hung stamps on the walls, said "really, we're almost like gorilla-beans ourselves" — and stopped carrying the borrowed things quietly, and began leaving them OUT, on the shelf, where visitors could admire.

"Homily," said Pod, low, "put the stamps away."

"It's only dear Arrietty's friend," said Homily. "He's practically family."

"He is a gorilla," said Pod, "and family is exactly what he must never be allowed to become."

And one night the troop's cook, a great grumpy she-gorilla named Mrs. Dryer (she was always drying banana chips, and her heart had dried along with them), noticed a peanut missing, and a button, and a crumb — and this time, a stamp, on the floor, in front of the root-hole, where Homily had dropped it while showing off.

"They've GOT something down there," she snorted, to old Crampfurl the gardener. "Little thieves. I'm calling the snake-catcher."

"Rats," said Crampfurl, who did not like to be bothered while mulching. "You've got rats."

"Rats," said Mrs. Dryer, "do not use postage stamps."

The boy gorilla overheard them from the veranda. He could not climb down to warn them — the root-hole was being watched — so he did the only thing he could think of: he sat by the hole and read aloud, slowly, from Arrietty's favorite of the poems, and hoped she knew his voice.

Deep below, they knew.

They had one night. Pod, Homily, and Arrietty packed by snail-light: the beetleshell bathtub (too big — abandoned, with honors), the stamp collection (Homily sat on it), and Arrietty's journal. Mrs. Dryer poured smoke down the root-holes at dawn and poked at the burrow with a broom handle, and the snake-catcher came with lanterns and jars and a long dreadful smile. "Come out, little moochers," he sang. "No biting, no borrowing."

"Out the back root!" cried Pod. "To the leaf-boat!"

They ran — Arrietty carrying nothing but her journal, Homily carrying the stamp collection and her shrieking — down through the damp dark earth to the stream that ran beneath the world, where a curled brown leaf sat waiting in the shallows. Behind them, faint and far away, they heard Mrs. Dryer crying "THERE! In the bracken! TINY FOOTPRINTS!" and the snake-catcher's jars clinking like a sad music box.

They climbed in. The leaf bobbed, turned, and slid away downstream toward the bamboo groves.

"Everything," wailed Homily, "everything is lost!"

"Nothing is lost," said Pod. "We are borrowed ourselves, a little, by the stream."

Arrietty looked back once. High above, on the Great Fig Tree, she saw a young gorilla waving. She could not hear him through the water and the wind, but she knew what he was mouthing, because she was mouthing it too.

And she thought: a story is only finished when nobody in it is still telling it.

*June the first. The bamboo is green and kind. Mama hates it, which means it is safe. I have made a pen from a bamboo splinter and ink from a squashed berry, and I am writing this by snail-light.*

*Papa says there may be other borrowers, in other roots, under other trees. I have started a second book, and I am leaving room in it for them.*

*If a boy gorilla ever leaves a peanut by a certain root, he will find it borrowed, and beside it, in its place, a note.*

*I will write: TAKEN WITH THANKS. — A.*
