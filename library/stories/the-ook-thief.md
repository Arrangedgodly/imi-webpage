# The Ook Thief

*A parody of "The Book Thief"*

Here is a small fact: you are going to die.

I only mention it because I was there, nearly all the while, collecting souls in the tops of the trees. I am Death, and I have a great deal of work among monkeys, who die of leopards and snares and each other, and who insist, to the very last, on telling jokes. Of all the souls I have ever gathered, the one that haunts me most belonged to a small brown macaque who stole books. Ooks, she called them, because she could not yet read the word, only want it.

Her name was Liesel Meminger, and I first noticed her on a train, where her little brother died beside her with a cough like a snapped twig. At the burial, in the scrabble of dirt by the tracks, the grave-digger dropped his handbook in the snow, and Liesel picked it up. *The Grove-Digger's Handbook*: her first theft, with her brother barely cold, and I was intrigued.

She was delivered to a troop on Himmel Vine. Mama was a squat, bristling chimp who spat insults like cherry pits — *"Saumensch! Sau-monkey!"* — and Papa was Hans Hubermann, a silver-shouldered chimp with kind eyes and a leaf-accordion that he played until the whole vine sounded alive. When Liesel woke screaming from dreams of the train, Papa sat with her in the dark and said, "Tell me. What did you see?"

"The snow. The shovel. My brother."

"Mmm." He turned a page of the stolen handbook. "Then let's make the basement a schoolroom."

He painted the alphabet on the basement wall in charcoal, one smudged letter at a time — *B, for Banana, Liesel, the bunch you're holding* — and soon she was reading, and after reading came wanting, and after wanting came stealing.

She stole her second ook from a bonfire. The Leader's birthday had been celebrated with a mountain of forbidden books in the square, set alight while grown monkeys saluted and young ones howled. Liesel waited until the flames were low, snatched a smoldering book from the embers — *The Scruff Shrug*, history does not record why anyone had banned it — and hid it, smoking, against her chest. Papa saw, and said nothing, and played the accordion that night a little more softly — because he did not love the Leader, and because he had promised a dead friend a favor that would come due wearing a coat.

Her accomplice in all crimes was Rudy Steiner, fur the color of ripe banana, a boy so fast the gym teacher despaired and so doomed I have never gotten over it. He had covered himself in charcoal from head to tail one famous afternoon, to run the hundred branches as his hero, the great Jesse Ooh-Owens, and the neighbors talked for a year, and his father washed him in the river and asked him quietly if he wanted to be taken away, and Rudy said no, he only wanted to be that fast, and fast he was — fast enough to be missed by everything life threw at him, until he wasn't. He wanted a kiss from Liesel for years, and always teased instead of asking.

The mayor's wife, Ilsa Hermonkey, had gray eyes and a library like a cave of quiet. She had seen Liesel take the book from the fire — she was the only one who watched the crowd instead of the flames — and instead of telling, she opened the library. Once a week Liesel sat on the polished floor and read to her, and thought it was friendship, until the day the laundry job ended and the note came: no more washing, no more visits. Liesel shouted through the tall window that the gray ape should be ashamed, her fur, her library, her cold silence — and Ilsa Hermonkey took it the way stone takes weather, and years later gave Liesel a small black book with a note: *write it yourself.* That black book is the reason I was late collecting one small soul in a certain basement. Remember the black book.

It came at midnight before that: Max Vandenburg, a young bonobo with restless fists, whose father had saved Papa in the last war. He arrived carrying the Leader's own book over his face like a mask, hidden in plain sight by the very pages written against his kind. They put him in the basement, and Liesel brought him weather reports — *today it is gray above Munich and the fog comes up the vine* — and the two of them traded nightmares like trading fruit. He painted over the hateful pages and wrote her a picture-story, *The Standover Monkey*, about a little ape who learned that "the bestubbed monkey stands over the small monkey, and the small monkey is not afraid." Later, in the cold months, he made her a second one, *The Word Shaker*, about a troop that grew a forest of words, and one small ape who climbed the tallest word-tree and shook it, and the right words fell like ripe fruit on the right heads, and the whole wrong forest came down.

They were brave, all of them, and stupidly so — I include Papa, who one winter morning handed a crust of bread to a line of shuffling prisoners and was whipped for it, which meant Max had to run. Liesel watched every marching column after that, hunting one tired face, hating herself for the hope.

By then the sirens had become the troop's weather. On raid nights the neighbors packed down into the Fiedlers' basement, and the small ones cried, and the old ones prayed at the wrong volume, and Liesel — because Papa asked her to, and then because it worked — stood on a crate and read aloud from whatever ook she had. She read the scruff-shrugging one, and the dictionary, and the picture-stories Max had made, and I watched the shelter do what shelters cannot: the shaking stopped being fear and started being only the sky. Her voice held the troop the way Papa's accordion used to. Words, that small brown thief had learned, are the one thing that gets bigger every time you give them away.

Then came the night the sky fell in. I have seen leopards kinder than what dropped on Himmel Vine. The houses folded like termite hills. Liesel was in the basement, writing her own life in that little black ook, and that is the only reason I did not collect her beside the others.

I carried Papa with the accordion broken under him, and Mama, and Rudy. She kissed him then, in the rubble, soft and true on his lips, and told him she loved him, and I looked away, as I always do when the living do the dead's work for them.

The ook thief lived. The ook thief grew old, on the far side of the world, with a family and a garden, and when I came for her at last — gently, I do get to choose, occasionally — she asked if I had read it, the black book she had left on the ground of Himmel Vine so long ago. I had. I told her so, and she wanted to know what I had learned from it, and there in the kitchen of her small warm house I leaned close and told her the only thing I have ever wanted to tell anybody.

Max came back after the war, from the far camps, and the two of them stood in the ash of Himmel Vine holding each other up. That, too, is in the book.

People ask me what I know. I know that a monkey's heart is a line, whereas mine is a circle, endless, and I am always in the right treetop at the wrong moment. I know there was one small brown macaque who stole words and gave them away.

I am haunted by monkeys.
