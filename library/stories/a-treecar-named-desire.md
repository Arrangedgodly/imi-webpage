# A Treecar Named Desire

*A parody of "A Streetcar Named Desire"*

Blanche DuBough arrived in the Fig-Quarter of the old jungle city wearing silks ten seasons out of fashion and a manner two centuries out, fanning herself with a banana leaf as though the heat were a rumor she declined to repeat.

"They told me to take a treecar named Desire," she said to the neighbor ape who directed her, "and then transfer to one called Cemetrees, and ride six boughs, and get off at — Elysian Boughs!" She laughed, a silver little laugh. "What an appropriate address for my sister to have chosen! Poetry in a zip-line."

Elysian Boughs turned out to be a crumbling fig-branch tenement — two rooms, a curtain for a door, a poker game roaring in the kitchen. In the middle of the poker game, dealing with terrible confidence, stood Stella's husband: Stanley Kolobus, a thick-necked silverback with a chest like a kettledrum and a laugh that startled hornbills.

"Stella's sister?" he said, looking her over the way he counted change. "Well, take a seat. Poker's only for the boys."

"Is it now," said Blanche, and drank his liquor.

She had lost the family grove, she told Stella that first night — Belle Reve, the beautiful dream, gone to lawyers and funerals and the long parade of dying aunts. Stanley, ear cocked, did the arithmetic. "In this state we have the Troop Code," he said, shuffling through her trunk like a customs ape. "What belongs to the wife belongs to the husband, and vice versa. So where are the papers? A grove of that size and not one leaf of paper? I don't want to get anywhere with anybody, but I go through the trunk, see, because if I didn't, the trunk would go through me."

"Stanley!" cried Stella. "She was born there!"

"Listen, baby," said Stanley, level. "There such a thing in this grove as gratitude."

That same night, boiled on whiskey and a losing hand, he swept the plates off the shelf, and when the radio kept playing, snatched the whole set and hurled it out the window, and struck Stella — only once, and once was plenty. Stella fled upstairs with Blanche; and then, out of the rain, Stanley came back and stood beneath their branch and bellowed up at the shuttered window, and it is possible they heard him across the river:

"STELL-AAAAAAH! HEY, STELLAAAAAAH!"

A silence; a shutter opened a crack. Stella — quiet, rounded, expectant Stella — came down the stairs with slow, sullen grace, her eyes going to Stanley with something like fascination; and they went inside together, and every ape in the tenement obligingly looked the other way.

"Apes of the branch," said Blanche to the rain, shaking. "Stone apes. What such people are too simple to feel, they make up for in noise."

"I'm not in anything I have a desire to get out of," said Stella softly, later, from the doorway.

Blanche, for her part, made the place bearable with craft. She bought a paper lantern at the corner and fitted it over the naked bulb — "I can't stand a naked light bulb, any more than I can a rude remark or a vulgar action" — and soaked in the bath for hours, singing, while the poker-night scandal soaked out of her.

Through that spring she courted Mitch, a gentle, lonely poker-friend with a sick mother, who carried a cigarette case with an inscription: "And if the Great Ape had made anybody sweeter than you, he'd have kept the recipe for himself." Blanche read it and knew she had him. She told him her history in the dark. She had married appallingly young; her husband was a beautiful boy who wrote poetry on fig leaves; one night she told him he disgusted her, and he swung off the high falls into the river, and she heard the splash in her dreams ever since. After that she was asked to leave one troop after another: too many romances, too much perfume, too many stories that did not match.

"I don't tell the truth, Mitch," she told him, "I tell what *ought* to be the truth. **I don't want realism — I want treemagic!**" And when the bare evening sun came cutting through the leaves she flinched. "Put the shade on the light! A ape's attraction is her mystery, and I will not be looked at under a bare bulb."

While she soaked and sang upstairs — "It's a fig-leaf and banana world, just as phony as it can be — but it wouldn't be make-believe if you believed in me" — Stanley told Stella, quietly, what he had swung to her old grove to find: the departures, the dismissals, the card-party that had got out of hand, the lies. "In the Fig-Quarter they call her Banana-Season Blanche — because she came and went so often."

Then he handed what he knew around the poker table like a bad deck, and Mitch stood before her in the sunset and tore the paper lantern off, so that she stood in one cruel golden shaft.

"Lies, lies, all conceit!" he said, near tears. "Grooming-school teacher! You're not clean enough to groom my mother's fur!"

"I never lied in my heart," said Blanche, and after that nobody in the house could get a true sentence out of anyone.

Her birthday passed without Mitch. They sat at the little table with the cake going stale between them, and Stanley, cheerful as a filing cabinet, presented his gift: a bus ticket back to Laurel. Blanche laughed and laughed, because if she had not laughed she would have shattered. "That ticket! Oh, what a clever, clever ape — one little slip of leaf, and I'm shipped!"

Stanley, home from the hospital corridor where Stella had gone into labor, found Blanche packing her silks, and the two of them quarreled over her illusions the way other apes quarrel over territory, until Blanche's treemagic had been torn off like a shade, and something in her went with it. When Stella returned with the baby, Blanche was already somewhere else — taking sponge baths in polite decades that no longer existed, expecting a telegram from a sap-grove millionaire, planning a sea-trip with Shep Huntleigh, who did not exist.

The matron and the doctor came on a late spring afternoon, while the poker game ran in the kitchen and Stanley dealt and grinned. As they led her toward the door, an old flower-woman passed along the vine below, crying her wares — "Frutas! Frutas para los muertos!" — fruit for the dead — and Blanche turned at the sound and said, gently, "No. Not now. Not flowers, not fruit. I have been to the funerals of the heart enough for one spring."

Blanche fought, then swayed, then gathered her silks with a dignity that silenced the room.

Then she saw the doctor, a stranger, kindly, holding out his arm — and she took it.

"Whoever you are," she said, sailing grandly into the sunlight, "**I have always depended on the kindness of strangers.**"

They led her off down the vine. Stella, sobbing, clutching the baby, let the neighbors console her — "Don't you believe it! Don't you believe her story!" — but Stella could not both believe her sister and keep on living with Stanley, and so she held the baby tighter and cried. Eunice pressed the bundle into her arms: "No, honey, now don't you cry. Life has got to go on. No matter what happens, you've got to keep on going."

In the kitchen, Stanley tore open a fresh deck.

"Now, now, honey," he said. "We've had this date with each other from the beginning." He shuffled. "This game is seven-card stud. Now deal."
