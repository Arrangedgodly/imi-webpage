# The Louse on the Strand

*A parody of "The House on the Strand"*

The invitation came from Magnus Lane, who had been my tutor at Oxford and was the only professor mandrill in England who kept a chemistry set more dangerous than his sherry cellar. He had taken a house in Cornwall — Kilmarth, on the headland above Tywardreath — and he wanted a guinea chimp.

"Not a medical experiment," he said, port in both nostrils. "A *temporal* one. You drink one measure of this, Dick, and you find yourself six hundred years away. Not in body. In attention. The body stays on my sofa being an inconvenience. The mind goes walking in the fourteenth century, and can watch, and listen, and follow, and cannot touch, cannot speak, cannot be seen. You become, for want of a better term, a louse on the pelt of time."

"A louse," I said.

"Cling as close," said Magnus cheerfully, "and matter as little."

My wife Vita was sailing to America with her sons for six weeks, and I was between books, which is the condition in which monkeys agree to anything. I drank the measure. It tasted of copper and overripe banana.

And I fell out of the twentieth century like a monkey out of a tree.

I was standing in the manor hall of Tywardreath in the year of grace 1320 — could smell the wet rushes, the dogs, the smoke — and none of it could see me. Not Sir Roger Kylmerth, the lord, a hard-riding black baboon with a scar through one brow; not his household; not, thank heaven, the fleas, though I itched for an hour afterwards on principle. And down the stair came Lady Isolda.

I will not describe her. She was a langur, silver-grey and still, with a face like a closed book, and she was unhappily married to Sir Oliver Carminowe, and within three of my "trips," as Magnus insisted on calling them, I would have walked through the fourteenth century on my knuckles to follow her. This is the fact at the bottom of everything that happened, and I put it first for honesty.

Each dose lasted perhaps an hour; each return dumped me back on Magnus's sofa with my heart going like a drum troupe and my legs gone to rope. The drug was a brute to the body. The past was kinder. I came to know the whole cast of that corner of Cornwall: fat Sir Otto Bodrugan, a gorilla knight who roared oaths and wept at mass; the Prior of Tywardreath, timid as a titi; the herd-boy Chimm, my favorite, who talked to his cows all day for company.

And Isolda, walking the strand alone. Roger Kylmerth loved her — hopelessly, hopefully, every way but openly — and she loved him, and in that century that meant the two of them stood a yard apart at the manor gate saying courteous things about the weather while their eyes did the actual marrying. Six hundred years away, on a sofa, I ached for them. I, Dick, whose own wife was crossing an ocean and would cable and cable, and be answered late.

That was the vice of it. You must understand: the drug gave nothing but watching, and watching, it turns out, is the purest appetite there is. I took to ringing Magnus at dawn to beg the next measure. When Vita cabled that the boys had won a sailing race, I read it twice and thought: *tonight I shall see whether Roger rides to the ferry.*

The fourteenth century, meanwhile, went from comedy to plague. The Great Sickness came over the water in the summer. I watched it empty the parish house by house — gently, at a distance, the way you watch weather — with Chimm the herd-boy surviving on cow's milk and luck, and fat Otto locked in his tower with a barrel of wine and his sins. The Prior buried forty of his flock in a fortnight and grew a white muzzle in a month. And Lady Isolda, who had nursed the sick without stopping, was rewarded in the autumn by an accusation from a neighbor with a grudge: that the sickness was her doing, that she was a witch, that she had charmed the cows and blighted the corn.

They tried her by water at the pool below the manor. I stood invisible among them while the mob — Otto's tenants, primed by Otto, who had coveted the Carminowe lands for years — pushed her under, and the water, which is honest, floated her, which by their lights proved nothing except that they were ready to be satisfied either way. Sir Roger came through that crowd like a bull through a hedge, hauled her out dripping, and carried her up the strand wrapped in his own cloak, and if the whole county did not know their secret before, they knew it then.

Her husband stood to lose everything by scandal. Her church stood to lose everything by mob. She was, as Roger said to her on the strand that last evening — I was ten feet away; I have never in my life listened so hard — "shut in a tower of other monkeys' making."

"I am very tired, Roger," she said, in a voice like the tide going out. And then: "There is a draft the leech-ape left me. It is only sleep. You will tell them I drank it by the sea to still a fever, and the tide — the tide will do the rest, and no one will be blamed, and you will be free of it, and I of everything."

I will not write what he answered. Some things belong to the century that said them. But the next morning I stood on the strand — invisible, irrelevant, a louse — and watched Sir Roger Kylmerth dig a grave above the tideline with his own paws, and lay his lady in it, and stand there one long hour without moving, and I understood that I had spent the whole summer six hundred years too late to help anybody at all.

The dose wore off. I came back weeping on Magnus's sofa, and Magnus, appalled, sympathetic, and (I know now) jealous as fire, gave me brandy and said, "Ah. So you reached the burial. My dear Dick — I've been three hundred years further."

I should have noticed the state of him. I was too deep in my own affair. Two days later Vita's cable said she was home early, and Magnus was found dead in his laboratory with the second vial — the strong dose, the one that takes a monkey *further* and keeps him *longer* — empty on the floor, and on his face an expression I can only call arrival.

The inquest said heart failure. I say: heart *departure*.

Vita came down to fetch me, and found what the drug had left me — a monkey who stumbled, whose paw went numb on the gear-lever, who stared out at the Cornish sea as though reading mail. She was wonderful to me, and I lied to her, which is the one thing I cannot put right and won't pretend to. She has taken the boys home to her mother's. The doctors say the damage is to the conductivity of the nerves, that it may arrest, that it will not improve. I walk with a stick. Some mornings the stick is ambitious.

Kilmarth is shut up. But I still have the key, and I am writing this at Magnus's laboratory table, and I will tell you why.

On the shelf above the sink, behind the flasks, there is a third vial. Magnus's notes call it the completed tincture: not the measure that trips a monkey into the past, but the one that keeps him there. No return. No sofa. No louse on the pelt of time — but a monkey *in* the pelt, permanently, at last, where the tide can't send him home.

Isolda is buried above the tideline. Chimm the herd-boy lived to be old, and his cows after him, and the parish records say the mound on the strand was never ploughed. It is still there. I checked, in this century, before my legs gave out, and I stood over it like a paying guest.

You will say: the drug shows the past, Dick, but it cannot put you *in* it; you know it can only ever be watching.

I know what I know. The vial is small. The strand is six hundred years away and one swallow off, and down there the light is fourteenth-century gold, and Sir Roger walks that shore every summer of it, forever, a yard from his lady.

I have taken up the vial twice today just to hold it.

Forgive me, Vita. It is only sleep.
