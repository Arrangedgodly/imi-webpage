# The Ugly Chimpling

*A parody of "The Ugly Duckling"*

It was a beautiful morning in the fig forest, and Mrs. Colobus looked proudly over the five babies asleep in her nest of leaves high above the river.

Four were slim and elegant, black-and-white as ink on paper, with magnificent whiskers already promising greatness. The fifth was... different. Big, grey and pot-bellied, with a flat little face, fur like old moss, and not one stylish whisker.

"Goodness," said Aunt Colobus. "What an odd chimpling. Did he get into the mud?"

"That's just how he came," said Mrs. Colobus stoutly. "I'm sure he'll grow into his fur."

He grew into more fur.

The other babies learned to leap almost before they walked — long, glorious, floating bounds, tails streaming like ribbons. The ugly chimpling tried a leap exactly once. He launched, he wheeled, he made a shape best described as a dropped papaya, and landed in the river mud like a boulder falling into porridge.

The whole troop went ape.

"Monkey see, monkey do — but what are *we* supposed to do with *him*?"

At grooming time — the most important time in any monkey's day — nobody picked through his dull grey fur. The grooming clique swept past him like a breeze that couldn't be bothered. "We don't groom *that*," said the young colobuses, flipping their magnificent tails.

"He is so ugly," said the parrots, who were themselves mostly bald, "that even the fleas won't live on him."

He took to sitting by himself on the lowest branch at the edge of the troop. He would have groomed every one of them, ears to tail, and asked for nothing back. But nobody asked.

One evening his own mother sighed gently. "I wish you'd try to be graceful, dear. Leap *lightly*."

So he tried. And one day, chased from the best fig tree by his own brothers, he light-leaped, missed, took out a fern and his own dignity, and decided he had had enough. Before the sun rose he swung away on his thick arms, further from home than any colobus had ever bothered to go.

In the next valley he fell in with two young gibbons, who looked him up and down for a long time.

"You are remarkably odd-looking," said the first gibbon.

"Shockingly," agreed the second. "No offense."

"None taken," said the ugly chimpling, though some was.

"As long as you don't marry into our family," said the gibbons cheerfully, "we shall get along splendidly. We're off to the tall figs. There are hunters in this wood, so keep your head down, and don't stand under the nets."

There were hunters in that wood, and their nets came sweeping through the canopy that very afternoon. The gibbons vanished like thrown shadows. The ugly chimpling was too slow; the net came down; a great hunting dog stood over him, hot-breathed and yellow-eyed — sniffed him, snorted, and trotted away after the gibbons.

"I am too ugly even for the dog to bite," said the ugly chimpling to himself, and that was the bitterest hour of the whole bitter year.

He escaped the net under a riverbank, and dragged himself at dusk to a little hut where an old auntie macaque lived with her cat and her parrot. She took him in because he could reach the high shelf, and for a while it was warm. But the cat ruled the sunny cushion and the parrot ruled the conversation.

"Can you purr?" the cat asked him.

"No."

"Can you talk philosophy?" asked the parrot.

"No."

"Then you have no right to an opinion," they said together, and the auntie macaque nodded, because the shelf was dusted now.

So the ugly chimpling sat in the corner and thought about fresh air and sunshine, which is the beginning of every departure. One morning, before the parrot was awake, he slipped out of the window and went off to be somewhere else.

Autumn came on. The fig leaves fell and fell, like the forest undressing for bed. He wandered lonely through the cold, and by the first frost he had tucked himself into a hollow log, rolled into a ball, living on beetles and stubbornness, while frost strung silver through the forest.

One dawn a kindly old orangutan found him half-numb by the frozen stream and carried him home in his hat. The orangutan's children meant well, but they meant loudly; they wanted to see the strange grey monkey, and poked, and squealed, until the ugly chimpling panicked and tumbled backwards into the milk pail, and then the butter crock, and thrashed around in both, and came up wearing the breakfast. Out of the house he scrambled, while the children laughed till they fell over, and the orangutan shouted kindly, "Come back when you're bigger!" — and for the first time, that sounded less like a threat than a promise. All that winter, the orangutan left breakfast out on the windowsill, so kindness is sometimes loud, but it can learn to be quiet.

But cold seasons end. That is their one good quality.

One bright morning he woke to a sound like the forest breathing out, and came into the warm green spring — and there, on the flat rocks by the river, he saw them.

Three enormous creatures rose out of the mist, huge and calm and magnificent, black as storm clouds, with great broad chests like the fronts of temples. They moved slowly and surely along the ground — not leaping at all, not caring to — and the biggest carried silver across his back like armour.

His heart thumped. He knew, without knowing how, that these were the most beautiful creatures in the world, that beside them he was grey and graceless, and that they would drive him away.

He marched out and bowed his flat grey head, expecting to be driven off.

A great warm hand came down and groomed him gently, once, between the ears.

"Well," rumbled the biggest, in a voice like the river talking in its sleep. "And where did *you* come from?"

He looked up — and in the still water he saw his own reflection for the first time. Looking back was a young gorilla, black as a storm cloud, taller than any colobus who had mocked him, with a chest like the front of a temple and eyes that were kind.

"I'm not ugly at all," he whispered. "I was never a bad colobus. I was a good gorilla all along."

"Come along," said the silverback, gathering him in with one arm. "You look like you haven't been groomed in three seasons. Unforgivable. We'll fix it directly."

They fixed it directly. That evening he sat in the biggest grooming circle he had ever seen, picked clean by great careful fingers — the young gorillas groomed carefully and the old ones carelessly, and both ways felt like home — while the mist came up off the river and the fig trees stood about being beautiful.

Far above, two young colobuses looked down, flipped their magnificent tails, and said to each other, "I say — the new one. Isn't he the most splendid creature you ever saw? I wouldn't mind being grey and enormous like *that*."

And the young gorilla, out of their hearing, groomed and warm and home, thought:

"I never dreamed of so much happiness, back when I was the ugly chimpling."
