# Neverhair

*A parody of "Neverwhere"*

Richard Macaque had the most ordinary life in the Canopy. He had a good branch in a good tree in Old London Wood, a fiancée called Jessica with strong opinions about dinner parties, and a career sorting fig futures. His fur was tidy. His future was tidy. He was, in every way, a monkey going quietly up in the world.

Then, on the night of an important dinner party, he found a girl bleeding on the pavement of the high branches.

She was small, wild-eyed, and grey as ash — grey as the fur nobody in the Canopy ever grew. Her name was Door.

"They're after me," she whispered. "Please. I need to get off the main branch. I can open things. Any lock, any knot, any cage. But I can't run much farther."

Richard should have called the zookeepers. Instead he carried her home, ruined Jessica's dinner party, and lost his fiancée. That was the easy part.

Because the next morning, nobody could see him. The troop walked straight past him. His branch key no longer fit. His own mother groomed the empty air where he stood. The girl called Door had fallen out of the Canopy, and now, somehow, so had he.

"She took you Below," said a voice, "and now you're one of us. Welcome to Neverhair."

The speaker was the Marquis de Caraboon, a macaque in a coat of forty different furs, who took ruffled bows. "The Understory, Neverhair — call it what you like. Everything the Canopy drops, we catch. Lost socks. Lost monkeys. That's why we're grey, my boy. Down here, nothing keeps its colour. Not the fur. Certainly not the people."

Door's whole family had been slaughtered in one night, and she meant to find out why. The Marquis knew everyone worth bribing. And so they went to the Floating Market, held that week in the ivy vaults of Hairyods, where a toothless proboscis monkey sold second-hand thunderstorms, a stall did a brisk trade in umbrellas of various fates, and nobody made eye contact with anybody, as a matter of manners and survival.

Door stood on the Earl of Orangutan's travelling log and asked, in a clear small voice, who had killed her family, and the whole market became very interested in its own boots. The Earl offered them tea, protection, and the loan of Hunter — the greatest huntress of Neverhair, a scarred silverback with a belt of teeth, one for every legendary beast she had killed, all but one. Door paid for the passage by opening the market's strongroom, which had been jammed shut for a decade, as a demonstration; the trader tested the lock, wept briefly with gratitude, and doubled the price out of respect.

But two shadows followed them through the dark: Mr. Croup and Mr. Vandelouse, a matched pair of baboons in old-fashioned coats, who conversed like academics while they dismantled their victims.

"Do you know, Mr. Vandelouse," said Mr. Croup, "that I have an insatiable appetite for ancient civilizations? I find them… tender."

"And I, Mr. Croup, favour the slow approach. The stalk. The pounce. And then — the grooming. Very personal, grooming. Intimate."

"Tell you what," said Mr. Vandelouse pleasantly, to a trussed-up informant, "we'll ask you one more time, and then Mr. Croup does the talking."

"Do you ever doubt, Mr. Croup?"

"Never, Mr. Vandelouse. Doubt is the rust on the blade. You may polish a monkey's opinions out of him, but doubt keeps growing back."

They were hunting Door on behalf of an employer whose name they would not say. They were also, though Richard did not know it, hunting the Marquis, who had made them an offer: Door, in exchange for his own coat and continued wearing of it. The Marquis had assumed, as he later admitted, that he would have time to be nobly double-crossed afterwards. He was double-crossed immediately.

Richard came back from scouting the night bridge to find a note in the Marquis's elegant scrawl — *Forgive me. I had no choice* — and Door white-faced and steadier than Richard had yet seen her.

"He sold us," she said. "Everyone does, down here. That's how it works." She said it like someone reciting a lesson, and then added, in a smaller voice, "I didn't want it to be him."

The Marquis, meanwhile, was lying broken on a rooftop in the rain, being politely looted by Mr. Croup, when he was found by Old Bailey, an ancient roof-talker who lived among the pigeons and owed the Marquis a debt. Old Bailey owned one treasure — a feather from the Bird of the Long Sleep, which heals anything, up to and including opinions — and, trembling with terror at his own boldness, he used it.

So it was that the name, when Richard finally learned it, came with footsteps behind him: the Howler Islington, a being of white fur and vast wings who lived alone in a hall of candles beneath the roots of the world, where the walls were wallpapered with the labels of doors. Islington had wept when Door asked about her family.

"I will find who did this terrible thing," it said, luminous and sad. "Go to the angel at the Blackfriars' Bough. Open the way for me, and I will give you the name of your family's killer."

They went. There were trials — a test of the black-furred friars, a riddle of the night bridge — and then the old zoo tunnels and the Beast of the Understory, an ancient boa the length of a District Line train. Hunter had been promised this hunt since she was young; it was, she confessed at the tunnel mouth, what she had sold Door's route to Islington to obtain. "Everyone sells something," she said. "I sold it for the only thing I ever wanted. Now watch me pay for it." The Beast killed her; Richard, carrying her spear, killed the Beast; that is the way of hunts. Richard stood over it, shaking, no longer a monkey going quietly up in anything.

At last Door opened the final door, the one only she could open — and Islington stepped through, radiant, smiling, with Mr. Croup and Mr. Vandelouse at its heels like luggage.

"You asked who killed your family," said the Howler. "You were speaking to it. Look up, little monkey. Angel. Over the door. I have been here the whole time."

It had wanted Door's gift. Her family had refused. Now it would take the way she had opened, and rise, and howl the world grey. But Door, weeping and furious, had opened not a way out, but a way *away* — a door to the farthest dark, and through it Islington fell, screaming light, and Mr. Croup and Mr. Vandelouse went with it, arguing elegantly about whose fault it was, all the way down.

The Marquis arrived, fashionably late and dramatically bandaged, in time to cut the last vine and take a bow. "I did sell you out," he told Door. "And then I got better. In Neverhair, that is practically a reference."

And then there was Richard, standing at the edge of the Canopy's light, offered his old tidy life back. The troop could see him again. The fig futures needed sorting. Jessica had left a message, for old times' sake.

He went up. He lasted eleven days. The troop invited him to parties as the fascinating monkey who had been away. Jessica had left a message for old times' sake, and it took him four days to work out that he no longer had any idea what she was talking about, or why he had ever sorted fig futures, or how he had ever slept at night without the sound of something moving underneath the world. Every night he stood at the top of the tallest tree, looking down into the green dark where nothing keeps its colour, and heard the market calling.

He went back down. Nobody in Neverhair ever climbs by accident twice.

"Mind the gap between branches," said the Marquis, appearing at his shoulder with a flourish. "It minds you."
