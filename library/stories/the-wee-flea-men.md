# The Wee Flea Men

*A parody of "The Wee Free Men"*

Tiffape Aching was nine years old the day she hit the river monster with a frying pan.

Her little brother Wentworth had toddled too close to the shallows, yelling his only word — "Wanna nannee! Wanna nannee!" — when a long green arm came out of the water and wrapped around him. Tiffape didn't think. She picked up the big 'nana-split pan and brought it down on the monster's head with a clang like the troop's dinner gong.

The monster sank, sulking. Wentworth sat in the mud and said, "Wanna nannee."

Because for one second, before the pan, Tiffape had *seen* the thing properly: green fur, weed teeth, a crocodile's idea of a smile. Jenny Greenteeth was a story. Stories did not climb out of the water hole — and certainly not on the Chalk.

The Chalk was sheep country, one white hill after another like a green thought interrupted, and it had belonged to the Aching family for a thousand years. Tiffape's grandmother had been Granny Aaching, the shepherdess — a big silent woman in a huge brown coat and a hat like a cottage, who could stop a dog fight with a look and read the weather off a sheep's ear. She'd been the Chalk in a headscarf, people said. When she died, two summers back, the sheep had walked up the hill in a long grey line, of their own accord, and stood there. Nobody had ever explained that, and nobody had ever tried.

A week after the monster, a thin lemur in a pointy hat arrived, with a tick in a tiny wig riding her shoulder ("my familiar; he has a law degree"). She called herself Miss Nit, witch-finder.

"Two worlds are rubbing together, girl," said Miss Nit, "like two tomcats in one basket. The barriers are thin."

The Feral Folk were worse than monsters. They were *beautiful*, and cruel as a peeled banana in the sun.

That night, six inches of red fur and bad intentions climbed in through Tiffape's window: tattooed, blue about the ears, wearing a stolen 'nana skin as a helmet.

"Crivens! Ye're awake!"

"I'm awake," said Tiffape, "and you're six inches tall."

"Six inches of trouble, missy. Rob Anybody, Big Man o' the Nac Mac Fleagle, at yer service." Behind him came Daft Wullie, Slightly-Bitten Jock, No'-as-Hairy-as-Medium-Sized-Jock-but-Bigger-than-Wee-Jock-Jock, and the gonnagle William, of the weeping ratpipes.

The Fleagles feared nothing, respected nothing, and stole everything that wasn't nailed down, plus the nails. Their kelda — the big gold-haired mother of the clan — looked Tiffape over the way you look at weather coming.

"Ye have First Sight, girl," said the kelda, "an' Second Thoughts. First Sight is seein' what's there. Second Thoughts is thinkin' about what ye're thinkin'. Yer granny had baith, and it's in the bones o' the Chalk that a hag stands in the gap. For the Queen o' the Feral Folk has come back, and she'll tak' the wee ones."

The Queen came for Wentworth on a moonless night, with her grimhounds baying like empty stomachs, and the only sound in the world after was a voice on the wind, sweet as overripe fruit: *Come and take him, if you can dream your way in.*

Tiffape took the frying pan. A troop of Fleagles took the whiskey. And together they went into the old mound under the hill.

Inside was the Queen's country, where dreams come true and true things go to die. The Dromes sat in the doorways of it — fat grey dream-keepers with big sad clever eyes, asking everyone the same question: *Wot's yer dream, doll?* — and whatever you answered, they made it, and wrapped it around you like fog, and while you lived in it they had you.

The dreams found you, and fed you exactly what you wanted.

Tiffape walked into a warm kitchen where her mother was baking 'nana pies, endless 'nana pies, and her mother said, *Stay, love. Stay forever.* It smelled like Sunday.

*This is a dream,* said her Second Thoughts. *And I am the one who is awake.*

"Coom on, ye gowk!" roared Rob Anybody, fighting his own dream with an empty bottle — in his dream he was in a great hall with a thousand barrels and no taps, which for a Fleagle is the worst thing there is. "It's no' real! Ye cannae eat a dream pie!"

The kitchen tore like wet leaf. Something rode past them on a road that wasn't there, holding its head under one arm and complaining about the toll; the Fleagles jeered at it on principle, headless horsemen being, in their view, showing off. Beyond it lay the Queen's winter garden, white and silent, and there stood the Queen, lovely as frostbite, with Wentworth — sticky with dream-sweets — and a boy called Roland who had been napping for a hundred years.

"You are nothing," said the Queen, gently. "A big lump of a farm girl. Go home, child."

"I'll be going home," said Tiffape. "With my brother. And him. And I'm telling you this for your own good: you're not the biggest monkey in this jungle."

The Queen laughed — and that was her mistake, because Tiffape was angry now, and the anger ran down through her bare feet, and the Chalk answered. The white hill that had carried the Achings for a thousand years — that had watched a big silent woman in a brown coat walk its slopes in every weather — *warmed*, under all that snow. Grass came up through the frost like a green opinion. It remembered Granny Aching, and it knew her granddaughter.

"I can see you," said Tiffape, stepping forward with the pan. "That's the whole trick. I can see you *properly*. And behind all that beautiful fur you're nothing but a cold little mean thing that has to steal children, because nothing real will ever love you."

The swing of the pan rang out across the dream like a dinner gong, and the winter cracked.

They came out of the mound at dawn — Tiffape, Wentworth ("Wanna nannee," he said, and got one), Roland, and the Fleagles stealing the celebratory fruit. Roland, it turned out, could remember almost nothing except that he'd been promised there would be rescuing, and by the time they reached the Baron's hall his memory had improved enormously: in his version, he fought the Queen single-handed while a local girl happened to be present with a kitchen implement. Tiffape let him run on. She knew what she'd done, and what she'd do again, and there is a kind of power in being the one who doesn't need the story.

Miss Nit watched them come down the hill. "You're a hag now, Tiffape Aching."

"I'm a chimp with a frying pan," said Tiffape.

"Aye," said Rob Anybody, saluting her with the whiskey. "Same thing, missy. Crivens!"

"And I'll be back in the spring," said Miss Nit, "with a proper teacher, and the first thing you'll learn is boots. Then First Sight, then Second Thoughts, then law — the tick can help with that — and then, if you're very lucky, how to know a Drome's dream from your own kitchen."

"I can do that one already," said Tiffape. "Mine has dirty dishes in it."

The Fleagles carried off the whiskey, the fruit, and — by accident, probably — the gate. And on the hill above the farm, in the long grass where the sheep had once stood in a grey line, the Chalk lay warm in the morning sun, keeping, as it always had, the things that were its own.
