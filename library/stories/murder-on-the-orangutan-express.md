# Murder on the Orangutan Express

*A parody of "Murder on the Orient Express"*

It was five o'clock on a winter morning in the jungle, and Hercule Pongo — the celebrated orangutan detective, small, precise, and magnificent of whisker — was cold, which he considered an outrage against both nature and himself.

He had boarded the Orangutan Express for home after resolving the Little Bananas case ("nine stolen bunches and one dishonest ambassador"). The great bamboo train hissed through the vines on its fermented-fruit furnace, its sleeping cars run by the Banana-Lit Company.

Waiting on the platform at Leafy St. Bough, he had noticed two things. First, Mangosteen Bouc, director of the line and an old friend, who found him a berth. Second, two monkeys saying goodbye in English — a tall lady macaque with neat grey fur and a large colonel of a baboon — and the lady saying, very quietly, "Not now. Not here. When it's over."

"Now, madame," said the colonel, "when it's done."

Pongo filed this where he filed everything, in the enormous orange cabinet behind his eyes, and went to lunch.

Also aboard: Dr. Constanvine; and, in berth No. 1, a repulsive ape with false whiskers calling himself Mr. Ratchimp.

"Monsieur Pongo," Ratchimp drawled, "I have enemies. I am a man of wealth — protect me."

"I do not like your face, monsieur," said Pongo politely, and went to bed.

The train did not reach morning. A drift of dead banana leaves, five feet deep, buried the line, and the Orangutan Express stopped in the middle of nowhere, cut off from the world.

And Mr. Ratchimp died in berth No. 1, door locked and chained from within, stabbed twelve times — some wounds deep and savage, some feeble, almost gentle — as if performed by several monkeys of very different strengths.

In the wreck of his cabin Pongo found a burnt scrap reading "...ITTLE DAISY CHIMPSTRONG..."; a handkerchief initialled H.; a woman's tiny red banana-silk dressing gown; a pipe-cleaner Pongo was sure he had seen no passenger use; and, in the washstand, a crushed grape — stepped on deliberately, pointing.

"Ah," said Pongo softly. "Then it is that."

He interviewed the carriage, and the carriage lied to him beautifully. Everyone had an alibi, and every alibi was another passenger. The young secretary MacKing had been talking all night with Colonel Arbufig — sworn by both. The German lady, Hildegarde, had been dosing Mrs. Hubbaboon with aspirin. The princess had slept. The Italian, Foscarini, talked so continuously that he disproved himself and two others. Greta, the gentle Swedish nurse, went white as milk at the name Chimpstrong and said she had heard nothing. The conductor, Pierre Michel, a soft old mangabey with three daughters, had seen a small dark woman in a red dressing gown who did not exist. And a hard-eyed American called Hardman — supposedly a detective hired to protect Ratchimp from a gangster enemy — could not produce a client.

"The one passenger," Pongo observed to Bouc, "whose story stands alone is the one I cannot crack, and the one whose story stands with everyone else is the whole carriage. And this handkerchief, monsieur — the Countess Andremonkey's — a grand lady launders her initials in three languages. Curious."

He told the story over cold figs. Little Daisy Chimpstrong, daughter of a famous colonel and his actress wife, had been kidnapped for a crate of golden bananas. The ransom was paid; the child came home dead. Her mother died of grief; her father took his own life; the nursery maid, suspected out of spite, destroyed herself, though innocent. The kidnapper, caught at last under another name, escaped justice on a legal vine-loop and vanished.

"Ratchimp," said Pongo, "was that kidnapper. And in this carriage, messieurs and mesdemoiselles, rode every monkey his crime ever touched."

He laid it out like a fruiterer laying out trays. Mrs. Hubbaboon, loud and motherly, was in truth the grandmother, once the jungle's greatest tragic actress. Young MacKing, the secretary — his father prosecuted the case. Old Masterman, the valet — the Chimpstrongs' butler, dying slowly and in no hurry. Hilda the maid — the dead nursery maid's sister. The Countess Andremonkey — the child's nurse; her handkerchief had simply, humanly, dropped. Colonel Arbufig — the father's brother-in-arms, who had quizzed MacKing all night in a uniform of loyalty. The tall English macaque, Mary Debenham — the governess. Princess Dragonfruit-off, godmother, so old and yellowed she resembled a shriveled plantain. Dr. Constanvine, the family surgeon. Greta, the nurse who had held the child. Hardman — a policeman in love with the dead nursery maid. Foscarini, the chauffeur. Pierre Michel, the conductor — father of the third daughter, who had been the maid's friend. And the count, who had lent his title. Twelve in all — twelve passengers, twelve wounds — gathered by the grandmother across two continents of canopy, booking themselves aboard the one train their quarry could not leave.

"Each was to strike once," said Pongo. "A self-appointed jury of twelve, passing sentence in the one courtroom the law had abandoned. The locked door was theater. The chain, drawn tight and dropped with a pair of pincers. The dressing gown, worn to invent a small dark woman. The pipe-cleaner, planted — forgive me, monsieur le comte — to confuse the track. Even the grape —" he bowed — "the grape was left for me, by a jury that wished, in its heart, to be understood."

Then Pongo did what no detective should do and every great one does: he rehearsed the crime aloud, night by night, knock by knock, and as he spoke the masks came off one by one, like skins off a fig. MacKing wept. The colonel stood at attention. The princess nodded, a plantain accepting ripeness. Mrs. Hubbaboon began to rock.

"Little grey cells," said Pongo, tapping his enormous orange head, "have never failed me, and they insist upon the truth. But I confess a second truth: my grey cells are not my heart, and my heart was on that train the day Daisy Chimpstrong came home in a crate."

Silence, except for the jungle birds and one marmoset quietly weeping.

"There are two solutions," Pongo announced. "The first: a stranger — a jaguar gangster, shall we say — boarded the stalled train in the night, stabbed the prisoner twelve times with twelve different arms, and vanished into the vines. The second: the impossible truth, that twelve honorable monkeys did, together, what the jungle failed to do apart."

He turned to Mangosteen Bouc, who must decide what the world would be told.

"Monsieur, you are the director. Choose."

Bouc mopped his brow. He looked at the twelve monkeys, sitting like a jury trying themselves. He looked at little Mrs. Hubbaboon, rocking. He looked, last, at the crushed grape.

"I choose," said Bouc, "the jaguar."

"Naturally," murmured Dr. Constanvine. "The jaguar did it. Notorious escape artist, the jaguar. Doubtless he leapt off into the vines."

"Then," said Hercule Pongo, rising and straightening his whiskers with both hands, "we are all agreed, and I have the good fortune to be neither judge nor jury today. I am merely a passenger on the Orangutan Express — and I say, messieurs, the jungle is well rid of a monkey who stole bananas from a child."

The train whistled. The leaves were cleared. And the Express went on through the jungle — carrying one secret more, and twelve monkeys who slept, for the first time in years, the whole night through.
