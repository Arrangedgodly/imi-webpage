# World War Chimpanzee

*A parody of "World War Z"*

I was hired by the United Troops Postwar Commission to write the official history of the Great Chimpanzee War. I turned in four hundred pages: dates, claw-marks, troop movements. What they cut was the apes. These are the apes.

**[Chikankawa District. The village vet, who saw the first case.]** "He walked in calm. A young male, gray about the muzzle, a bite on his shoulder three days old. He didn't cry, didn't groom, didn't reach for the fruit. He asked for a banana. I gave it to him. He didn't eat it. He just held it, and asked for another one. And then he asked with his teeth. — Everyone wants a patient zero. Fine. Patient zero is the male who made me stop trusting a quiet chimp."

**[The Blight.]** "It wasn't a curse. It was farming. Three generations of banana monoculture — one breed, one valley, one food — and a mold jumped. The chimps ate the gray bananas, and the gray came up into them. The papers called it going bananas. Accurate, and it helped nobody."

**[Jurgen Warmbrunn, gorilla, intelligence.]** "We built the wall before any other troop believed in the Grayfolk. Our neighbors called us paranoid. We said: paranoia is the oldest tool we have. You are a primate alone in a jungle that has been trying to eat you for fifty million years. The day you stop checking the canopy is the day something drops on your head."

**[The Great Panic.]** "The panic was everyone swinging at once. Whole troops abandoning the tall figs for the ground, where we are slow, because up high suddenly felt exposed — upside-down logic, but panic is a troop running through itself. I saw a silverback try to fight a river. And the whole time the Grayfolk didn't run at all. That was the difference. They just walked."

**[The celebrity compound.]** "Yeah, that was us. Nine famous apes, one mansion, forty thousand followers watching the wall cams. We had a film crew, a yogi, and a rapper with a machete in each hand and a third one in his hair for later. We broadcast every night: *they'll never take the canopy.* The Grayfolk never even tried. They just stood at the gate, moaning, while our viewership went up. You know what killed the compound? Not them. Us. The followers outside, begging to be let in; the guards arguing; the yogi opening the gate because 'they're still monkeys, man.' We lost the house, the yogi lost an arm, and the rapper — credit where due — swung eleven civilians out on a rope with a machete in his teeth. The livestream of that saved more troops than my whole ministry ever did."

**[The Redeeker Plan.]** "Paul Redeeker, a bonobo so logical his own troop called him cold-blooded. His doctrine: you cannot save every branch. You choose, in advance, out loud, which branches to cut so the troop survives — you write off whole groves and say so before the screaming starts, because the alternative is deciding mid-scream. Everyone hated him. Every troop used his plan. He never forgave himself either, which I always thought was the most human—" [The interviewee said *primate*. The interviewee was right the first time.]

**[Lieutenant A. D. Winters, artillery. The Battle of Fig Creek.]** "They gave us every toy: coconut cannon, screech-boxes, ziplines, flash-groom mirrors. Toys. The Grayfolk had one sound with no words — the scream — and they came up the creek bed, knuckles down, eyes like fog, and our toys did not matter. At Fig Creek we learned the war was not going to be quick. It was going to be *standing still*."

**[The painters.]** "Nobody talks about the painters. Chimps who painted themselves gray to be left alone. Some were cowards, some were worse — opportunists herding other chimps to the Grayfolk to buy themselves a week. You cannot imagine the noise a real troop makes when it finds a painted one. And here is the part I still dream about: for one heartbeat you couldn't tell them from the real ones either. The Grayfolk just walked. A painted chimp screams *please*. The war lives in that difference."

**[The general they called a whacko, because he wept in public and told the truth.]** "Stop trying to be fast. Speed is a solo sport, and this was a troop war. So we went back to the oldest way, the one we forgot when we got clever: the line. Shoulder to shoulder, shields of fig-bark, spears of greenheart. No hero swinging out. No one breaking. At Hope, in the high orchards of New Mexico, we stood a square on good ground with the sun behind us, and we let them come, and they broke against us like fog on a wall. A monkey alone is a snack. A troop in a line is a fortress. That sentence won the war, and I had it carved on my stump."

**[The feral-children therapist.]** "The war orphans didn't die; they went wild. A whole generation raised by no one, in the deep canopy, on raw shoots and silence. People ask what you do with a child who has never been spoken to. You sit. You offer fruit on a flat palm. You do not grab. Weeks, sometimes months, and then one day a small hand touches yours and grips, and you go home and weep in the pantry where nobody sees. Ten years on, most of them groom again. One of them is my son. He still won't eat bananas — the gray ones frightened him, and, well, who can argue with that?"

**[The astronaut.]** "I watched it all from orbit. Every night the canopy went dark, country by country, and the smoke plumes stood up off the dark like the jungle was growing gray flowers. They sent me up to watch for missiles. I watched for you instead. When they finally brought me down, there was one question everybody asked: what did Earth look like from up there during the war? Small, I said. Blue. Worth it. That's the whole report."

**[Ten years later. The vine-warden.]** "Every troop has a warden now. Every schoolchild can form a square before breakfast, and does, grumbling, which is how you know it's working. There are still apes who say the war was staged by the banana cartels. They were not at Fig Creek. — I took the recorder to the last cured Grayfolk, one of the few, late, and I asked whether it had been worth it. He looked at me a long time with his new brown eyes and said: *ask the figs.*"

The figs of Fig Creek came back the following spring, which surprised everyone, figs being figs. So did the birds. So did the noise — real noise, troop noise, politics and grooming disputes and the wonderful ordinary racket of the canopy.

I filed my report. They filed it away. But I keep this last note in my own hand, unauthorized:

I stood at the edge of the replanted valley on a quiet evening with the recorder running, and the wind moved through the high branches —

— and for the first time in a decade, nothing moved back.
