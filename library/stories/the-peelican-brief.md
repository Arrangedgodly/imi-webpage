# The Peelican Brief

*A parody of "The Pelican Brief" by John Grisham*

In one night, in the swamp city of Orleans-au-Bois, somebody killed two justices of the Supreme Jungle.

Justice Raisenberg, ninety-one, ancient and irreplaceable, was taken in his own roost. Justice Jennison, who had never met a fig-tavern he could pass, was taken outside his favorite one. The jungle went ape. The newspapers went bananas. The President, who appointed nobody anything without a poll, held a press conference and said several hundred words, among them, "peelicans."

He did not mean the bird. He meant the case.

Because six hundred miles south, a rich and terrible monkey named Victor Bananeese wanted to strip-mine the last great mangrove rookery of the rufous peelican — the finest peeling bird in creation, the bird that skins a banana in one pass and leaves the jungle floor tidy — and his case was on its way to the Supreme Jungle, where Raisenberg had been voting against him for thirty years. Bananeese had also, as it happened, contributed generously to the President's last campaign, which was why the White House press office said "peelicans" in the tone of a monkey saying "spiders."

Which brings us to Darby Macaque, twenty-four, second-year law student at Two-Cane Jungle Law, handsome in the way that young lawyers never notice until the deposition, and cursed with a working imagination.

For Professor Vireo's constitutional seminar she wrote a brief as a game. *Who benefits?* it asked, and answered, in forty careful pages: Bananeese. Motive, means, temperament, and a paper trail through a front group called the Coalition for the Coast. She called it the Peelican Brief, because she was young, and because the joke seemed harmless. Vireo gave her an A-minus, told her it was brilliant, told her it was impossible, and took her out to celebrate the impossibility, he being fifty, brilliant, and fond of her in the way professors should not be and increasingly were.

He read the brief once more over dinner, laughing at its audacity. Then somebody at the Bureau, where Vireo had an old friend and old friends have new assistants, talked to somebody at the Coalition, who talked to somebody in the swamp, and the world's most expensive assassin — a silent macaque known only as Khameleon, a quiet professional who had killed on four continents and never once raised his voice — bought a ticket to Orleans-au-Bois.

The nice thing about Khameleon was his thoroughness. He had studied the law school. He had studied the parking lot. So when the little red roadster exploded outside the fig-tavern where Vireo stopped every night at eleven for a last cognac-banana, the explosion was flawless, and Vireo, who had been talking about the brief again, went with it. Darby was one minute late coming out of the library — one minute, for once in her punctual life — and from the corner, two hundred paces off, she understood two things at once. Her brief had been right. And being right was now fatal.

Khameleon saw her run. He made a note of it, the way he made a note of everything.

She ran for four days. She cut her fur short, bleached it with lime juice, swapped trees three times a night, and read her own description in the papers with the sick interest of a monkey studying her own wanted poster. In the Quarter she passed a gray cap standing very still by a fountain — still the way nothing alive stands still — and walked four more blocks before she let herself run, and by then Khameleon had her hotel, and by then she had already checked out of it, and the game went on like that for a week: the assassin thorough, the law student imaginative, and imagination, that quarter, winning by a paw.

In the capital, the White House debated saying "peelicans" again. Fletcher Coal-Smoke, the President's press ape, argued that the word had become a problem, and solved it by leaking that the President had never said it, which created three new questions per press conference. The Director of the Bureau, an old polecat named Voles, suspected everybody equally and the President slightly more, which was the most anyone had ever trusted him. Voles put a quiet watcher on Khameleon's hotel. The watcher reported that somebody else was watching Khameleon too — a small gray passenger with an English accent who had checked in across the hall and did not seem to sleep.

Darby finally put a call through to the one monkey in the capital who owed her nothing and printed the truth for a living: Gray Gibbon, metro desk of the Washington Howler.

"I'm a reporter," he said, when they met in a mangrove hideout. "I print what I can prove."

"Then let's make him provable."

They worked it backward with a drunk named Sarge, who tended the tape recorders at the Coalition and sang fig-brandy ballads about his boss's phone calls. A deposit here, a roster there, an October meeting at the rookery itself. Sarge wanted to do the right thing, on the strict condition that it not involve doing anything, and he had just decided, over the third ballad, to sign a statement, when his heart declined the honor. He left the tapes in a fig crate addressed to the Howler, with a note in an unsteady paw: "For whoever's asking. Don't print my name. Print everything else."

By the time the marshals quietly moved Bananeese's pilots into custody, Gray Gibbon had sixteen notebooks and a grin like a zoo at feeding time.

"Where'd a law student learn to investigate like this?" he asked.

"I read your paper. Somebody has to. You can't spell."

Khameleon, meanwhile, had grown frustrated for the first time in his career, and frustrated professionals take chances, and he took one in a hotel corridor. The English passenger, whatever his errand, was not taking any. The Bureau's watcher reported the outcome laconically: one assassin, checked out. The English passenger's bill was paid through Sunday. Nobody ever established for whom, and Voles logged it as "inconclusive," a word he was that year using a great deal.

The Howler ran it across two Sundays. PEELICAN BARON BOUGHT THE COURT, SAID THE TAPES. The President was heard to say "peelicans" eleven more times, each with less conviction, and finally without the article, as if it were a place he had once visited and did not care for. The grand jury did what grand juries do when tape recorders learn to sing, and by midwinter Victor Bananeese was finished, along with his coalition, his jetski fleet, and two of his senators.

Darby Macaque spent the spring recovering on a small warm island, incognito, at considerable expense to the Washington Howler, which did not mind, and in the company of Gray Gibbon, who pretended to mind.

"What now?" he asked her on the beach. "Chief counsel to some senator?"

"I thought I'd grow bananas. Or write a book. Something safe." She frowned at the horizon. "Maybe both. Peelican farming."

"You," said Gray Gibbon, "are the only lawyer I ever met who should have been a reporter."

"It was an A-minus brief," said Darby, and went back to her tan.
