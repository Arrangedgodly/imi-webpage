# The Great Tree Robbery

*A parody of "The Great Train Robbery"*

In the year 1855, in the great Fig Empire, the monthly payroll of the troops stationed on the distant thorn front — a quarter-ton of tree-gold amber, the fossil resin of ten thousand springs, every nugget of it the Crown's — traveled from the Royal Fig Exchange in the Upper Bough down to the harbor in two iron-banded gourd-safes aboard the Great Tree Express. The Express was the marvel of the age: a chain of connected baskets that ran the trunk-line on greased runners, gravity-fed down the great bole, whooshing through the bough-stations with the roar of a troop of howlers, the fastest freight in the canopy.

Every monkey in the Empire knew the schedule, and every monkey in the Empire knew the safes were locked with four carved nut-keys: two kept by the terminal master at the Top Bough, one by the bank's own vice-warden, and the fourth locked in the office of the Tree-line's directors, never to be removed.

Every monkey knew it could not be robbed. Which is why Edward Pierce — a chimp of excellent grooming, easy fortune, and no visible profession — had been studying the problem for two years with the contentment of a monkey who has found the one ripe fig at the top of the tree.

"It cannot be done," his acquaintances told him.

"Yes," Pierce agreed pleasantly. "It cannot. That's the reason it will be."

A crime of this scale requires specialists. Pierce recruited Robert Agar, a young capuchin "screwsman" — a picker of locks so gifted he could open a nut-key with a quill, a prayer, and four seconds — and Barlow, a squat macaque who drove a leaf-cab and whose loyalty was purchased at a fair rate and renewed quarterly. His mistress, the actress Miriam, learned the schedules of half the aristochimps of the Bough without ever once asking why. And when word came down the vines that Clean Willy — the finest escape artist of the criminal troop, a louse-picking specialist who could wriggle through a knothole wearing a top hat — was being held in the New Gate Cage awaiting transport to the penal islands, Pierce set about springing him, on the principle that a monkey who could escape New Gate could escape anything, especially gratitude.

The keys were the campaign. Pierce courted Miss Trent, the terminal master's daughter, with the patience of a spider, learning the layout of her father's locked drawer; then arranged, on separate evenings, for Barlow's cab to break an axle, a beehive to be misplaced in the household, and a chimney fire to alarm the Exchange itself — after which the vice-warden Fowler was observed, by the monkeys Pierce had stationed to observe, to carry his precious key to the strong-room and lock it in a little drawer with a lock whose tumblers Agar now knew like his own whiskers. Each night, a wax impression. Each impression, a carved duplicate. Two months of theater, and Pierce held in his paw four keys to two safes full of tree-gold.

They almost lost it all at New Gate. Clean Willy, sprung and paid, was re-arrested in a gambling den within the fortnight — and this time, offered the choice of the penal islands or the witness vine, Willy talked. The constables did not believe him. The robbery of the Express was, every official monkey agreed, impossible; Willy's story was entered in the blotter as the fantasy of a louse-picker, and only a young sergeant wrote it up in full, because he was new, and thorough, and nobody read his report until long after it would have mattered.

On shipment night, fog sat in the canopy like a held breath. Pierce and Agar boarded the Express at the Top Bough in the guise of fruit inspectors, with passes Agar had tied into the manifest-vines himself. In the basket-van, the guard — a comfortable old baboon with a flask of fermented sap — accepted a share of the strangers' "medicinal fig brandy," and shortly afterward accepted sleep. They cuffed his ankle to the bench, took his lantern, and opened his own official key-ring with Agar's quill.

Then, over the roar of the runners, in a swaying iron box lit by one swinging lamp, Robert Agar — the finest screwsman of his day, and this is the moment his whole life had swung toward — bent to the first gourd-safe, inserted the duplicate nut-keys, felt for the false turns, and turned them in the order he had rehearsed on wax for half a year.

The lock surrendered. The gourd opened. Amber fire: a quarter-ton of tree-gold, glowing in the lantern light like frozen honey.

They packed river stones into the safes to keep the weight honest, relocked them, and at the Brown Bend crossing, where the trunk-line ran slow around the great scar of the lightning strike, they dropped the loaded net-bags down onto the flat roof of the way-station — where Barlow, waiting with a hand-cart and a strong opinion about gravity, caught the fortune of the Fig Empire in eleven sacks.

The whole thing had taken nine minutes. Pierce stepped back aboard the Express, straightened his fur, and rode calmly down to the harbor terminal as a fruit inspector should, past the way-station where a night clerk had heard eleven odd thumps on the roof and decided, in the way of night clerks everywhere, that it was owls.

Pierce was arrested a week later, in Barlow's own cab, on the word of Clean Willy — at last believed, too late, by an inspector who had finally read the sergeant's report. It was the scandal of the age. The Crown demanded blood. And on the first day of the trial, at the Old Bark Court, the Empire discovered the most delicious thing about a victim nobody can embarrass: the Royal Fig Exchange, the Tree-line, and the Treasury — each fearing that the public learning a quarter-ton of Crown gold had been lost through four borrowed locks would end the confidence of every depositor from the Upper Bough to the sea — rose, one after another, and swore that no gold had ever been aboard the Express that night, that the manifest said fruit, that no robbery had occurred, that nothing was missing, and that in any case the defendant could not have robbed what did not exist.

The jury was out eleven minutes, and most of those were spent finding the door.

Edward Pierce walked free into a cheering crowd of five hundred monkeys who had read between every line of the transcripts, and who understood, as crowds always do, exactly which crime had actually been committed that night, and by whom. He lifted his hat. He said nothing at all — the first rule of the profession. Miriam was seen that season wearing an amber brooch the size of a walnut. Barlow bought his own cab and never drove another monkey's errand again. Agar emigrated to the penal islands, where by coincidence he had excellent connections. Clean Willy was found in New Gate with his cage door locked and his pockets full, and never learned who had paid the warden.

As for the amber, it was never recovered, and never officially missed, and the whole affair passed into the speech of the canopy: when a monkey pulls off something enormous and walks away clean while the victims themselves swear nothing happened, you nod toward the old trunk-line and say, *"There goes the Great Tree Robbery."*

The Great Tree Express ran for another sixty years. It was never robbed again. As far as anyone can prove.
