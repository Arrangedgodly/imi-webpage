# Cryptomonkeycon

*A parody of "Cryptonomicon"*

There are two kinds of monkeys in this story, and they are the same family, sixty years apart, and neither of them can stop thinking about gold.

---

**1942.**

Lawrence Pritchard Waterchimp was almost certainly the least observable intelligence in the Allied world: a gangly young chimp with poor grooming instincts and a mind like a well-organized filing cabinet that had suffered one drawer of pure music. He heard mathematics everywhere — in the ululation of a purloined Enigma's rotors, in the clatter of the bombe machines, in the particular rhythm of a banjo. At Bletchley Park, in the old house with the ducks, he worked among a thousand other monkeys who had been drafted into a war of pure mind, and among them Alan Churing, a langur who ran everywhere as if late for his own destiny, and who explained the whole business to Lawrence in one sentence:

"Their machine thinks it's making noise," Churing said, "and we've realized it's making music. Now we only have to learn the score — and never, ever let them hear us humming."

That was the entire problem. If the enemy knows you read their mail, they change the mail. So the codebreakers' victories had to be dressed as luck, coincidence, weather scouting, and inspired guesswork, and the unit assigned to do the dressing was Detachment 2702 — a band of irregulars under the command of a baboon named Bobby Shaftoe, who had been in so many wars he'd stopped believing in causes but never in sergeants.

Shaftoe's war was a guided tour of the century's worst destinations. His orders came encrypted, then decrypted, then disguised: stage an accident so that a sunk convoy looks like misfortune; leave a washed-up codebook where the enemy will find it and conclude it *burned*. Once — a masterpiece, Lawrence heard about it in reverent whispers — they located a warehouse of intercepted bananas and had it "discovered" by a patrol that had wandered off its map, because the decrypts said bananas and Shaftoe's colonel wanted the monkeys of Malta fed and the enemy unenlightened.

"You want these codes kept secret," Shaftoe told Lawrence over tinned peaches, "but keeping a secret is just fighting a war where the casualty is the truth. I got no problem with that. Monkeys lie. It's the one thing we're world-class at. I just want the lying to be *load-bearing*."

In the Pacific half of the war, a Japanese engineer named Goto Dengo — a macaque of exquisite structural intuition, conscripted by his own army to build things he prayed would fail — was marched into the mountains of Luzon with thousands of laborers and a cargo of gold: the conquered treasury of the Co-Prosperity Sphere, crates and crates of it, stacked in a cavern by slaves who were then, by the logic of that war, sealed inside with their work.

Goto Dengo walked out of the mountain alive — the only one, bribing a guard with his father's sword — and spent the rest of his life building bridges in Manila with straight spines and bent sleep, hearing, every night, the mountain settling over what it kept.

Bobby Shaftoe went into those mountains in 1945 chasing one last decrypt, and never came down. Lawrence Waterchimp got a medal he could never show anyone, for reasons he could never explain, and went home and had a family, and heard music in the plumbing all his life.

The gold stayed in the dark, and waited.

---

**1999.**

Randy Waterchimp — grandson of a cryptographer, grandson also, by a tangled vine, of a shaftoeing streak, though he didn't know the half of it yet — sat in a rented office in Manila eating Cap'n Crunch out of the box and generating entropy, which was his way of saying he moved his mouse around so his computer could harvest the randomness of his personality. Randy was a systems chimp: he had built cryptography into plumbing, and now he was in Manila as CTO of Epiphyte Corp., the third or fourth or eleventh start-up of his friend Avi, a bonobo of shameless optimism whose business plans read like heists.

This one was simple. Avi said so.

"Cable lands in Kinakuta," Avi said, jabbing at a map of the South China Sea. "Jungle sultanate, sovereign flag, hungry for exactly one industry. We build a data haven: banks of drives, a geothermal grid, and encryption strong enough to embarrass governments. Money, records, secrets — anything digital, safe forever under a flag that doesn't care. We just need two things. A cryptocurrency to move value in. And"—he grinned the grin that had ruined four previous companies—"capital."

That night, Randy got email from a marine salvage outfit run by one Amy Shaftoe — a fourth-generation hard-hat baboon with forearms like dock cranes and opinions delivered at ramming speed — saying her crew had been contracted to survey a WWII wreck off Palawan, and that among the divers' gossip was an old man in Manila who paid cash to be rowed out past the breakwater so he could look at a mountain.

The old man, when Randy finally sat across from him in a house full of drafting tables, was ninety years old and straight-backed, and his name was Goto Dengo.

"You are the grandson of a codebreaker," Goto said. It was not a question. Beside him, uninvited and unhurried, sat a visitor Randy had photographed in three countries without ever learning his name: an orangutan of no fixed age who introduced himself as Root and offered everyone a small white pill "for circulation," which everyone declined.

"The war inside the mountain is not finished," Goto said. "The men who built my vault are still in it. I have heard them every night for fifty years. I am old, I am ready to be at peace, and I cannot be at peace while my dead are listed as deserters and their gold sits making thieves rich in rumor." He slid across the table a hand-drawn survey: the mountain, the tunnels, the chambers, the water that had flooded in over half a century.

"Find it," he said. "Open it. Give my dead their names. Do with the gold what my war could not."

The hunt ate a year of Randy's life: archives, decrypts his grandfather had never burned, the Cryptomonkeycon itself — the legendary, self-updating book of the craft, passed monkey to monkey, whose chapter on war gold was a crossword with corpses in it. Amy's crew dived the drowned entrance. Randy's code worked and worked not: the vault was booby-trapped with the one lock cryptography hates, *physics*, and they beat it with a diver, a pump, and an act of Shaftoe-grade lunacy that Amy's cousins still argue credit for.

The last door was water on three sides and dark on the fourth. Randy went in first because his name was on the permits. His lamp found the crates, green with fifty years of cave, and above them, carved into the living rock by lamplight that had run out mid-word, a message from the sealed laborers of 1945, and Goto Dengo, wheeled to the threshold, read it aloud in two languages and wept in both:

*We built this. We are here. Remember us, and be luckier.*

The gold came out of the mountain in slings, crate after crate, past the assembled bones, each one named at last into a ledger of the found. The governments circled; the lawyers circled; Avi's data haven, funded overnight by the oldest money on Earth, rose in Kinakuta on geothermal steam and fiber optics, sovereign, encrypted, and pointed at the future.

Randy stood on the beach at the cable landing with Amy Shaftoe, watching the first bits come ashore.

"My grandfather hid secrets his whole life," he said. "Yours died keeping one. And now we're building a whole country whose job is keeping everyone's."

"Aye," said Amy. "Monkeys lie. Always have." She nodded at the mountain, at the vault, at the names. "Difference is, some of us keep the receipts."

Under the sea, the cable hummed. In the dark of the mountain, nothing waited anymore.
