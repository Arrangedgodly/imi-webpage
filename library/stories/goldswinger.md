# Goldswinger

*A parody of "Goldfinger"*

At the Jungle Shores Hotel in Miami, a chimp in a borrowed tuxedo watched a middle-aged gibbon in a mustard-yellow suit lose four straight pinochle hands. The chimp's name was Banna. James Banna. He worked for the Jungle Service, drank banana smoothies shaken and never stirred — a chimp's paws are precise — and he was, by 00-banana license, officially allowed to be insufferable.

"Friend of yours?" he asked the loser.

"Him?" The loser shuddered. "That's Auric Goldswinger. He cheats like a flea in a diocese. Somebody up in that penthouse is reading my cards with a telescope."

Somebody was: Jill Masterton, a capuchin in a high branch, whispering hands into Goldswinger's earpiece. Banna climbed up, took over the earpiece, and proceeded to lose Goldswinger a sum best described as a plantation.

"Strictly as a matter of curiosity," Banna told him kindly, cashing out, "is losing ten thousand bananas a hobby, or a cry for help?"

Goldswinger smiled a smile with too much gold in it, capped and crowned, top and bottom. "You amuse me, Mr. Banna. Drop by my estate in Kentucky. Bring your nerve."

The next morning, Jill was found in her suite — dead, painted from head to tail in gold banana oil, "skin suffocation," the vet said gravely. "A monkey's fur must breathe."

Banna's tail went cold. He canceled his beach weekend, collected a lanky capuchin named Felix Liter from under a coconut palm ("You're shaded, Banna"), and went to Kentucky with a five-iron and a grudge.

They met first over jungle golf, where Goldswinger's caddy — a hulking orangutan called Oddjob, who never spoke and never blinked — demonstrated his signature trick by hurling his steel-rimmed bowler through the neck of a stone garden gargoyle.

"Man's been practicing," Banna observed.

"He does not practice," Goldswinger said. "He performs."

Banna won the golf by switching the ball, because cheating a cheater is not cheating; it is scholarship. That night, over a dinner Goldswinger treated as a lecture, the gibbon laid out his philosophy between courses. "Man has climbed Everest, chimp has swung every tree," he said, buttering a roll with a gold knife. "But nobody in this jungle will be happy until he owns every banana he sees. I merely admit it. Society calls it greed. I call it appetite with a tailor."

"And Oddjob?" Banna asked. "What does society call him?"

"Insured," said Goldswinger fondly.

Jill's sister Tilly Mistertoon ambushed them in the verges with a rifle and a plan she'd read about; the bowler found the rifle first, and Felix filed the report under "weather" and drove her home personally.

Q Branch, meanwhile, had issued Banna the Aston Banana-B5 — jungle racing green, with smoke screens, tire slashers, a passenger-side ejector seat ("I never joke about my ejector seat," Q said, to nobody's amusement but his own), and a glovebox first-aid kit containing brandy, bandages, and a card reading YOU'LL WANT THE BRANDY. On the way out of the estate, Banna tested every option at once: two pursuing baboons met the tire slashers, a third met the smoke screen, and a fourth, grabbing for the passenger door, met the ejector seat and became the first monkey in Kentucky to file a flight plan involuntarily. He landed in a hay field, asleep and radicalized by comfort.

Then came the invitation to the estate, the model room, and the moment the whole Jungle Service had flown across the ocean for: a scale model of Fort Knothole, the U.S. Bunch Depository, under whose granite sat the greatest concentration of gold bananas in the Western canopy. Goldswinger had gathered the jungle's crime boards to invest — Midnight Mangabey, Strap the snoozing orang, and Solo-mango, a sifaka with a briefcase and doubts. Solo-mango declined the plan, left in his limousine, and came back a tray. His share of the gold went to the crusher with him, which cut overhead and witnesses simultaneously. "Fifty-eight years of radioactive bananas," Midnight Mangabey had objected, doing sums on a napkin. "What do we spend meanwhile?" "Patience," said Goldswinger. "It's the only currency that ripens."

"Operation Grand Palm," Goldswinger announced, with visible joy. "My associates will handle the rest. Miss Galore's Flying Circus will spray the perimeter — a lovely pilot, Miss Peachy Galore, and her squadron of macaques — my little yellow truck will carry the device into the heart of the depository, and the gold bananas will be irradiated for fifty-eight years. Useless. Untouchable. And the value of my gold —" he kissed his own fang — "will multiply five times by breakfast."

"You're insane," said Banna.

"That is the fashionable word for vision, Mr. Banna."

They strapped Banna to a table beneath a gently humming peeler-ray, its golden beam crawling up his ankles toward the tender parts of his tail. Goldswinger stood over him, warm as a sunbeam.

"Do you expect me to talk?"

"No, Mr. Banna. I expect you to split."

Banna did not split. He talked — just enough nonsense about the Grand Palm being known in Nairobi, Hong Kong, and the Geneva zoo — until Goldswinger, greedy as gravity, left to renegotiate. Then Banna did what any chimp would do: he flexed, sweated, tipped the table with his heels, and used a gold bar to short the peeler-ray, because in a lair made of gold, everything is a conductor and everybody is a critic.

He escaped to Peachy Galore's airfield, where the Flying Circus was fueling. Peachy was a no-nonsense pilot macaque with a crop-duster and a firm policy against smooth-talking spies.

"Your gas bombs," Banna said. "He'll fire them on U.S. troops. Swap them for pollen and sleeping dust, and I'll owe you dinner in any canopy in the world."

Peachy looked at him for a long moment. "Why, Mr. Banna," she said, going visibly ape against her own policy, "I believe I'll take you up on that."

At Fort Knothole, Grand Palm split on the vine. The bombs bloomed into clouds of harmless pollen — Operation Rock-A-Bye, the crime of the century became the jungle's largest simultaneous nap, sixty thousand troops sleeping in the grass with pillow creases and rehearsed groans. Goldswinger's little yellow truck reached the vault door with the device ticking like a metronome at a funeral; it never played. The troops woke on schedule, a general with one eyebrow raised examined the truck, and the mob of jungle bosses who'd gathered to watch the crime of the century were arrested over cold punch. Oddjob, hurled into his own bowler's flight path with a live cable touching his steel rim, was shocking, briefly, and then shockingly still. And the atomic device in Goldswinger's little yellow truck never went off, having been towed to a meadow and watched, at a safe distance, by an entire army eating sandwiches.

Goldswinger made it as far as a private plane and one last attempt to make Banna permanently airborne. Banna made the window instead. Goldswinger, at thirty thousand feet, went out for gold and got the gold gone — sucked into the blue like a very wealthy seed. The Service recovered the plane, the gold, and a very small insurance claim for one window.

In a jungle hut by the sea, a radio crackled. "Say again, Banna. Report."

Banna, flat on the roof beside Peachy, watching the stars, keyed the transmitter.

"Strictly bananas," he said. "Strictly bananas."
