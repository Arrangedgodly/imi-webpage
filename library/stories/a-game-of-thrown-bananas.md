# A Game of Thrown Bananas

*A parody of "A Game of Thrones"*

The monkeys of the North had one saying, and they said it the way you'd grip a branch in a storm: *Banana season is coming.*

Brannoc loved to climb. He was the young of Lord Eddard Stark-eyes of Bananafell, Warden of the Northern Canopy, and he climbed everything — the towers, the fig trees, the walls — never falling, until the day he climbed too high and saw, in the upper branches, something he was not meant to see. The queen, Ceresy Bananaster, golden-furred and cold-eyed, and her twin brother Jaimie, in a compromising grooming arrangement that was nobody's business and everybody's scandal.

"The little monkey saw us," Ceresy said.

"The little monkey falls," said Jaimie, and gave the branch a gentle, courteous shove.

Brannoc fell forever, and never hit the ground the same monkey again.

All of this mattered because King Robert Barbary was coming north, huge and loud and boozy as a barrel of fermenting fruit, to ask his old friend Ned to be the Grooming Hand — the right-hand monkey of the realm, the one who grooms the king's back, and by ancient custom, the one who knows every flea on it.

"You're too honest for politics," Ned's wife, Lady Cattail, told him.

"The troop needs an honest monkey," said Ned.

"That's exactly what an honest monkey would say," she said, and she was right, which is the tragedy.

On the road south with the king, Ned's daughters fared worse than he did. Sansa Stark-eyes, dreamy and betrothed to the crown, believed every song she'd ever been sung about princes; her sister Aryia believed none of them, and got a fencing teacher instead — Syrio Forel, a slight, bald Braavosian with a wooden practice blade and a duelist's serenity. "What do we say to the God of Death?" Syrio asked her, tap-tap-tap. "Not today." She repeated it after him, the way other children repeat table manners.

Aryia would need the lesson ahead of schedule. When the queen moved against Ned's household, the troop of Lannifur guards came for Aryia and found Syrio holding a hallway with a wooden sword and a theory about first steps. She ran while he taught it to them. "What do we say to the God of Death?" — she never learned what Syrio said at the end. She knew it was not *please*.

Meanwhile, Lady Cattail, riding south, had arrested the wrong Lannifur on the road — Tyrion, the dwarf chimp, wit of the family — and hauled him to her sister's mountain fastness, the Eyrie, ruled by Lady Lysa Arryn, who had lost her marbles in a fruit cellar and never found all of them. Tyrion was given a cell with a floor that sloped and a wall that didn't, open to the sky, in the customary Eyrie fashion of executing prisoners by attrition and tourism; then a trial featuring the Moon Door, a hole in the great hall's floor with delusions of grandeur. Tyrion demanded trial by combat, got a sellsword named Bronn, who fought the knight honor was owed to — Lysa's champion, huge and slow, wearing honor as armor — and won in the traditional sellsword way: he did not fight fair, he fought first, and he pushed. The Moon Door opened, for once, to an invitation.

Far away, across the Poisoned Sea, Daenerys Treetopgaryen — last daughter of the fallen dragon-kings, sister to the insufferable Viserys — was married off to Khal Drogo-fur, warlord of forty thousand riding baboons. Her wedding gift: three petrified dragibbon eggs, stone-cold and dead as doorstops. Everyone said dragibbons were extinct.

Everyone was wrong, but we'll get there.

Viserys, meanwhile, wearied of baboon customs, of waiting, and of being warned. He drew steel in the sacred city, a deed for which the penalty was understood by everyone but him, and demanded his crown at the worst possible dinner. The khal gave him one: they melted gold, poured it over his head, and Daenerys watched her brother become what he had always suspected he was, entitled to the very end. "He was no dragibbon," she said, oddly calm. "Fire cannot kill a dragibbon."

At Bananafell, Ned's bastard son Jon Mocha took the black and rode north to the Thorn — a colossal wall of woven brambles, seven hundred feet high, manned by the Night's Watch, misfits and exiles who guarded the troop from what lived beyond it. His uncle had vanished out there; the recruits swapped stories about the Frostbiters — pale, blue-eyed things in the haunted timber, moving like wind through dead grass. On the way, Jon found a direwolf litter — the pups not three weeks old, one for each of the Stark-eyes children, and the runt, white and mute and staring, for the bastard; and any monkey who has raised a direwolf knows the North's other saying: *you never own one. You take its side.*

"You know nothing, Jon Mocha," said Ygritte, a wildling girl with flaming fur, when the Watch found her. She said it about everything. She was usually right.

In Kong's Landing, meanwhile, Sansa was learning the capital's gentler lessons. Her assigned shadow was the Hound — Joffreypoo's scarred shield-ape, half his face an old burn, all of his temper one — and when a bread riot surged, it was the Hound who cut her out of the mob: "Little bird. Behind me, and don't squeak." Chivalry, she was learning, didn't always knock first.

Down in Kong's Landing, Ned did what honest monkeys do in politics: he found the truth — the king's heirs were all Ceresy and Jaimie's twins' twins — and told it to the wrong monkey. Robert died in a "hunting accident," young Joffreypoo took the Banana Throne, and Ned was arrested for treason.

The Banana Throne itself deserves mention: a monstrous seat woven from every banana ever thrown in anger, layered and blackened by centuries of political disputes, always rotting, never once comfortable. That was rather the point of it.

Ceresy offered Ned a deal: confess, and take the black, and live out his days on the Thorn. Ned, for his daughters' sake, did the humiliating thing. He stood in the great square before the whole troop, swore his false oath — and Joffreypoo, delighted, vicious, drunk on his first taste of power, waved a paw.

"Off with him — to the petting zoo!"

The crowd went silent. Worse than death, the petting zoo: human children, all day, every day, sticky hands, pats, labels reading NICE MONKEY. Ned Stark-eyes, Warden of the North, was driven away in a labeled cart, and the North would not forget, and the North would not sit, and war came to the whole canopy like monkeys to an unguarded fruit stand.

"When you play the game of thrown bananas," Ceresy told her son, "you win or you go to the zoo."

And across the sea, Drogo-fur died of a festering scratch, Daenerys built him a pyre, walked into the fire with her three stone eggs, and walked back out unburnt — trailing three shrieking, hatchling dragibbons, who nursed at her shoulders and set the horizon briefly on fire. The baboons who had watched knelt, one by one, in the ash.

The North remembered. The South schemed. The Frostbiters gathered in the haunted timber, patient as winter.

And in Bananafell, a broken little monkey named Brannoc woke from his long sleep with a new sentence, which he would say for five more books:

"I dreamt it all. I dreamt the whole troop."
