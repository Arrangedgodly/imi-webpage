# The Amazing Loris and His Educated Rodents

*A parody of "The Amazing Maurice and His Educated Rodents"*

The scam worked like this — and stop me if any part of it sounds legal:

The rodents — capybaras, agoutis, one gambling chinchilla — poured into a town and made a nuisance of themselves. Next morning, a quiet young orangutan named Keith wandered in with a pipe. The town discovered a plague of rodents, consulted the noticeboard, found a poster offering a piper's services, and paid. The rodents, all educated and all professional, followed the pipe out in a tidy, smug line. Everybody won, and the team moved on with the money.

The brains of the outfit was the piper's manager: a slow loris in a waistcoat, who styled himself the Amazing Maurice. Slow lorises look permanently guilty, and Maurice had monetized every gram of it.

"The secret of looking wise," Maurice told the rodents, "is never to hurry."

"Why are we doing this?" asked Dangerous Beans — a small albino capybara, blind, calm, the nearest thing the rodents had to a conscience. "We can *think* now. We changed when we ate the wizard-trash behind Unseen University. Thinking ought to come with better manners."

"Because you can't eat morals," said Maurice. "I've checked the food groups."

The rodents were changing, and they knew it. Peaches kept the Clan's most sacred possession: a chewed children's book called *Mr Monkums Has an Adventure*, in which monkeys wore waistcoats, rodents had tea, and nobody ate anybody. It was a stupid book. It was the Clan's bible. Hamnpork, the Clan's grizzled old leader, hated everything about it — the book, the thinking, the not-biting, and most of all the smooth-talking cat — because he remembered being just a rat, and the remembering had no manners either. Sardines, the youngest tap-dancer in the world, defused him by dancing; nobody can stay furious at a rodent in a little hat, going *tiddle-tiddle-pom.*

Then the team came to Bad Brunch, a little mountain town with a nervous mayor and cellars. The cellars were wrong: traps everywhere — new ones, cruel ones, decades of them, in the dark, under a town that had somehow never needed the piper scam run on it.

The town was also, immediately, a problem, because the mayor's daughter was Malicia. Malicia was pretty, brisk, and entirely convinced that life was a story with herself as the narrator, a condition she had developed from reading too many books and being believed too rarely. She identified Keith as "the mysterious stranger," Maurice as "the talking animal sidekick," and herself as "the girl who works it all out," within one morning.

"How would you know a piper scam?" said Maurice. "It's hypothetical. It's theoretical. Cats can't even talk."

"In *stories* they can," said Malicia. "And this is obviously a story, because I'm in it."

The two official ratcatchers of Bad Brunch — large, idle, and officially the laziest monkeys in the mountains — were, it turned out, running the scam's mirror image: they had been stealing the town's food themselves for years, storing it in the deep cellars, and blaming the disappearance on rats, for which they were paid. The traps were set mostly for anyone who went looking. This arrangement suited everyone except the town, the truth, and the rats who got caught in it.

And in the deepest cellar, behind a wall of bricks, the rodents found the spider's web. It was not shaped like a web should be. It was shaped like a plan.

"I have been waiting," said a voice made of many voices, "for something that can *understand* me."

It was a Rodent King — eight rodents, tails knotted and fused over years of dark dragging, all bound into a single mind that called itself Spider. It had grown strong in the cellars, feeding on fear, and it had a helper's hideout full of secrets: it knew the ratcatchers' stores, the mayor's guilt, and the inside of every mind in Bad Brunch, and it reached into them. It reached into Maurice's.

And in Maurice's mind, it found a confession, which was the one thing Maurice had never made.

"You're a predator," sneered Spider, pressing close. "You eat your friends' kin and feel nothing. You and I are the same."

"Used to be," said Maurice, and his voice came out very small and very slow. "I'll tell you a thing I never told anyone. When I got clever, in the rubbish behind that university — I don't know what I ate, back when I was just a cat. Do you see? Maybe I ate one of the ones who'd eaten the rubbish. Maybe I got clever the way you got big. Off somebody else's chance." He looked up, and for once his guilt looked exactly like what it was. "Then I met this stupid book. So no. You and me had the same beginning. That's all."

It all ended the way these things end: in the dark, fast, and expensive. Dangerous Beans, the gentle idealist, walked toward the Rodent King with a lit match and *Mr Monkums* pressed to his chest, because ideas are flammable; and Spider, which had only ever fed on fear, had never once been *argued* at. It did not know what to do with a small blind capybara who was not afraid, and while it was failing to learn, Maurice went in — moving faster than any loris had ever moved, which is to say at a brisk walk — and there were teeth, and darkness, and a fall.

And Maurice died.

He knew it was death, because there was standing in it a seven-foot skeletal ape in a black cloak, looking down at a small, wet, waistcoated loris.

"WE MEET AGAIN," said APE. "THOUGH, ADMITTEDLY, FOR YOU, BRIEFLY."

"Am I—" Maurice looked down at himself, which took some time in a loris. "Oh, barnacles. I *am*, aren't I. Is the little one alive? The beans one?"

"YES."

"Right," said Maurice — a con-monkey to the bone, with one move left. "The nine lives, then. I've been counting. I've still got one coming. Give it to the Beans one. No — hang on—" he thought, and it hurt. "Give it to *me*, and I'll give it to *him*. Deal?"

APE considered him. Lorises are supposed to be slow; it is, the universe admits, unfair that history's fastest deal was struck by one.

"DEAL," said APE.

Maurice woke in the cellar, alive, badly bitten, and absolutely not going to mention any of it, ever. "I've had better nights," was all he said. "I've also had worse. Don't ask."

Afterwards, in the square of Bad Brunch, with the traps smashed, the ratcatchers arrested by their own town, and the Rodent King gone wherever dark knotted things go, the town council met the rodents across a table, Keith's pipe lying between them like a border.

"We can talk," said Peaches, chin high. "We can think. So can you, presumably, on a good day. We propose a treaty: your grain, our ratters' fee, no traps, no tricks."

The mayor looked at the pipe. He looked at his options, of which there were none.

"Paid how?"

"Monthly," said the Amazing Maurice, from the mayor's own hat, where he'd been for some minutes. "In cash. I take ten percent, because I'm adorable and somebody in this story has to understand business."

Malicia wrote it all down, of course — the first treaty between a town and a Clan, notarized by an ape with a pipe and witnessed by a loris in a hat — and if her version was three-quarters true and the other quarter had a sword fight in it, well. That's how the best stories work, and she was the best at stories.

"Mr Monkums," said Dangerous Beans, at the ceremony, "would have liked this."

"He'd have wanted tea," said Peaches.

"He'd have gotten tea," said Hamnpork, gruffly, and there was tea.
