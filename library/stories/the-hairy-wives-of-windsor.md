# The Hairy Wives of Windsor

*A parody of "The Merry Wives of Windsor"*

In the fat fig-forest of Windsor, Sir John Falstaff — a huge, greasy old silverback with a belly like a prize pumpkin — sat in the Garter Inn and counted his bananas. There were two.

"Two," said Falstaff. "I, who once drank the Thames dry — all right, a tributary — am down to two bananas. But I am a monkey of resource. Behold a scheme so cunning, so hairy, so magnificent—"

"Is it a trick?" asked Bardolph, his red-faced crony.

"It is a *plan*. Mistress Ford and Mistress Page, the two finest, hairest wives of Windsor — I shall woo them both. They govern their husbands' banana-hoards; their husbands govern nothing; therefore, in wooing the wives, I possess myself of the whole orchard. I have written each a letter. Identical, mind. Why gild the fig? 'Thou wonder of Windsor, heaven is my judge, thou art the lodestar of my life — love like a shadow flies when it is loved, but I am thy Sir Jack, the lumbering moon of thy affection.' Poetry, Bardolph. She shall be my monkey of Windsor."

"She'll be your ruin," said Bardolph, who had seen more schemes than bananas.

His followers Pistol and Nym refused to carry the letters on grounds of conscience — an unfamiliar word they had overheard in church — and promptly marched off to tell Master Ford that Sir John Falstaff loved his wife. Ford, a long, grey monkey with a jealous streak a mile wide, turned pale, then green, then a color marmosets use for warning.

"My wife? Loves — er, is loved — by Falstaff?" he cried. "I will find this out. I will be suspicious of everything, including the fruit."

The two wives, comparing letters over grooming in the orchard, found the same flourishes, the same "incomparable monkey of Windsor," the very same pawprint.

"He loves me," said Mistress Ford.

"He loves *me*," said Mistress Page.

"He loves our bananas," said Mistress Ford.

They laughed until the branches shook, then wiped their eyes. "Wives in Windsor shall be mocked no longer. Come — we shall lead that greasy gorilla a dance he will remember."

For a go-between they recruited Mistress Quickly, Doctor Caius's housekeeper, a monkey so fond of being in the middle of things that she carried messages for all parties at once, including two parties yet to be invented. "He loves you past the saying of it," she told Mistress Ford, "and he abuses Master Ford past the bearing of it — calls him a gorilla of no table manners, which, between us, he heard from me."

Mistress Ford invited him for two o'clock, when her husband took his wallow. Falstaff arrived preening. "Divine creature! The stars shone when you were born. I swoon at your grooming."

A clatter below: Master Ford, home early, roaring about a "fat rogue in my tree." Quick as a flea, Falstaff was bundled into the great laundry-basket of discarded grooming-fur, old fig-skins, and floor-sweepings — and, after a terrible interval in which Ford stabbed the basket twice with a fig-fork — dumped with a mighty squelch into the river-mud wallow.

Falstaff hauled himself out, dripping, unrecognizable, magnificent in his rage. "Thrown into the wash! Me! The finest fur in Windsor, sent out with the slops like a rotten fig-skin!"

"Back at three tomorrow," said Mistress Ford's messenger.

"Am I not to be boiled, baked, and basted?" cried Falstaff. "I'll come. Fortune, you hussy, play me no more tricks!"

That evening at the Garter Inn, a stranger was announced: "Master Brook," a nervous monkey with a ruffled tail — Master Ford in a moss wig, so jealous he had to investigate his own wife by proxy.

"Sir John," said Brook, fawning horribly, "I adore Mistress Ford from afar. Woo her for me! Fund my suit! I'll pay fifty bananas down."

"Fifty!" Falstaff's eyes went fig-shaped. "Sir, I am already deep in that campaign. Know that she loves me — she eats of my saying. And I have another appointment with her tomorrow, her husband being at the wallow."

Brook's fur stood on end so sharply his wig slid. "Her — her husband being out?"

"At the wallow, yes," said Falstaff, comfy as a caterpillar. "You may tell nobody, good Master Brook."

"Nobody," said Brook, in a voice like a snapping twig.

The second time, warned by Brook-that-was-Ford, the house came hunting early. Falstaff escaped dressed as "the old aunt of Banyanford" — a bent crone in a moss wig whom Ford loathed on principle — and was beaten about the shoulders with a fig-stick, pelted with peels, and chased half a mile. "If it hadn't been for my aunt's spirit in me, I'd have given that old woman's ghost a piece of my mind!"

"He beat you?" gasped the wives, secretly sore from laughing.

"Beat me? He made a fire in my aunt's honor — said I was a witch — threw me in the wallow again. I have been laundried, thrashed, and baptized in one week."

At last the wives told their husbands everything — including where Brook had really gone of an evening. Ford stopped mid-roar. Page began to laugh, and could not stop, and soon the whole troop was in on it.

"At Herne's Fig Tree at midnight," said Mistress Page, "he waits for me — that ancient fig where the horned owl-spirit walks. Let him come dressed as the horned hunter himself. And let all our friends be there as fairies — marmosets in flower-crowns, with strict instructions to pinch."

Midnight. Herne's Fig. Falstaff arrived with antlers tied on with vine, sweating. "The white-furred wives of Windsor! I am yours, my does!"

A shriek of tiny fairy-trumpets. Out of every bush burst marmosets, glowing beetles held aloft, pinching professionally: ankles, knees, haunches. "Fantasm and illusion! Fairy pinchers! Spare my poor monkey flesh! I burn, I freeze! Not the tail — not the tail!"

"I am Herne the Hunter, offended at thy lechery!" boomed Mistress Page's youngest, from inside a hollow log, in his most spirit-like squeak.

"Then I'll amend my life! I'll groom! I'll fast! Only call off the thumbs!"

Meanwhile the fairy squad had a second mission: that same night, young Anne Page was to be "stolen" away by three rival suitors, each guided by a different fairy signal — for her mother Doctor Caius was to steal her in white, her father Slender in green, and nobody had asked Anne, which was the family's whole mistake. Slender got the wrong monkey entirely. Doctor Caius got a small confused bushbaby in a bonnet. Fenton, whom Anne actually loved, got Anne.

Fenton and Anne stood forth, married by moonlight. Page blinked, then laughed. "Well then — she's yours, and I'll forgive you. You're the only one tonight who's made a monkey of me in a way I like."

As for Falstaff, he sat beneath Herne's Fig, smoking gently, pinched pink from ears to tail. The four pranksters surrounded him, grinning.

"Sir John," said Master Ford, "you have been well pinched, as you deserve. Laugh with us now — and know that I have been your Master Brook, your fifty bananas, and your fool all week."

"Then," said Falstaff slowly, "I did woo my own punishment at second hand. I begin to perceive I am not only an ass, but the whole braying caravan. A baboon. A regular Windsor knot of a joke."

"Nay," said Mistress Page merrily, "you're part of the troop. Breakfast at Ford's fig-tree, Sir John — and afterwards we'll all laugh this off together."

And they did — the hairy wives of Windsor famous ever after as the monkeys who knew that the way to keep a troop honest is a good joke, well timed, and mercilessly pinched.
