# The Lice-tigators

*A parody of "The Litigators"*

David Zinc was thirty-one years old, a chimp in a good suit, and by ten o'clock on the Tuesday in question he had eaten eight fermented figs at the Banyan Bar, gone completely bananas, and decided he could not go back to work.

Work was Rogan Rothfur, the biggest law fig in the city: five hundred apes in suits on forty floors, billing in six-minute increments and grooming in fewer. David had spent five years there — antitrust, tax, and the reviewing of documents until the documents reviewed him back — and had developed a nervous tic, an ulcer, and the ability to read a contract while weeping.

On the ninth fig he swung out of the bar, caught the streetcar cables home hand over hand, and never went back.

Dana met him at the door with her coat already on, schoolbag in hand, and read him in one glance, the way she read her third-graders.

"You quit."

"I'm taking a sabbatical."

"We have sixty days of figs in the bank," she said, "and a mortgage with opinions. So the sabbatical has sixty days." She kissed his muzzle. "Go find something you can live with. You've looked like a boiled parrot for a year."

Which is how he came to be sitting, at two in the afternoon, outside a storefront that said FINLEY & FIGG — ATTORNEYS. Inside were two lawyers, a cappuccino machine, and the smell of ambition going quietly off.

Oscar Finley was a rumpled orangutan of sixty-five with two divorces behind him and a grudge against nearly everything else. Wally Figg was a spider monkey with a rainmaker's grin and a scanner for an ambulance; when the ambulances proved too fast, he had taken to chasing fruit carts, which at least stopped for red lights. Their practice was will contests, grooming disputes, bite cases, and the odd class arbor-tion. The firm met every morning at the cappuccino machine, which was leased, and held its assets there.

"Kid," said Wally, looking David over, "you look like a monkey who just discovered the Law of the Jungle is mostly a torts thing."

"Actually," said David, "I know about your NitWhack case."

Everyone in the monkey city knew about NitWhack. Vervet Labs had sold a fur dip by the tankerful, swearing it ended lice forever. Instead — the class action alleged — it had bred lice of heroic size and turned the fur of its users a delicate orange. Finley & Figg had signed up forty itchy clients at the annual Lice Survivors' Picnic, and Wally had ambitions measured in billboards: HAVE YOU ITCHED? CALL FIGG. Oscar kept taking the billboard money for rent, which Oscar called firm oversight and Wally called sabotage.

David's first weeks, meanwhile, were the education he had missed in five years at the big fig. Oscar put him on the firm's crown jewel: the Pinchley twins, two ancient spinsters suing each other over their father's fig tree for the fourteenth year running. "The tree died in 2011," David said, reading the file. "The tree died before I got out of law school." "The tree is immaterial," said Oscar. "The billable hour is very much alive." David also rode shotgun on Wally's ambulance calls, learned to smell a soft-tissue claim at four blocks, and drafted his first cease-and-desist to a parrot who had been repeating a client's will aloud at the baths.

Then the verdict in the first NitWhack trial came down, and it was a catastrophe of the first water. Wally had tried it like a revival meeting; the defense tried it like an autopsy. Their star client, old Mr. Burble, rose to testify, got three sentences in, was seized by the itching, tore off his own shirt, and had to be carried from the courtroom on a stretcher of banana leaves while the defense offered him, with beautiful timing, a complimentary towel. The jury went out for forty minutes and came back for Vervet Labs. Finley & Figg were, as Oscar put it, "one verdict from selling the cappuccino machine" — and, since the machine was leased, from explaining that to the leasing company.

Worse followed, the way it does. Vervet's lawyers, smelling blood, began circling the forty itchy plaintiffs directly, offering each one a car note and a fruit basket to settle alone. Two signed by Thursday. A mass-tort outfit from the coast sent shiny brochures promising a "consolidated global resolution," which Oscar translated as "they take the bananas, you take the basket." The second trial was docketed for the spring, and the firm's sixty-day arithmetic now had Dana counting along at the kitchen table, aloud, in italics.

David took the file home in a grocery bag and read it nights, sober now and itching himself with injustice. At Rogan Rothfur he had been paid to find problems in ten thousand pages; here, at last, was a use for it. He pulled the safety study behind the dip. The clinical trials, the label said, had been run on baboons — population, in the raw data, one. A single baboon named Gerald. And Gerald, it emerged from the raw footage, had never been dipped at all. The researchers had filmed him scratching before lunch and again after it, filed the results, and called the study double-blind because Gerald closed both eyes when he sneezed.

"Data fleabrication," said David.

"Worse," said Oscar. "It's actionable."

David tracked down the tech who had run the camera — a retired capuchin running a juice stand at the flea market, where she had gone to be somewhere nothing was under oath. She confirmed everything in exchange for a large papaya and immunity from her own grammar.

"Gerald never got dipped," she said. "Gerald was the control and the subject both. We called him the Control Group. And between you and me, kid, Gerald itched because they never cleaned his enclosure. There's your clinical finding: soap."

They carried the footage of Gerald up the fig-tower of Vervet's lawyers, laid it on the long table, and waited while thirty immaculate suits watched a baboon scratch in surround-sound. The general counsel held out for eleven minutes. On the twelfth, David advanced one frame: a researcher's clipboard, legible, on which the words SUBJECT DECEASED — USE GERALD AGAIN had been checked, initialed, and stamped.

"That stamp," said David, "is going to look wonderful at trial. Juries love a stamp."

The settlement, when it came, contained one hundred million banana notes, a fund for the depilated, free towels for Mr. Burble in perpetuity, and language so conciliatory it practically groomed them. Vervet Labs admitted no fault, in the sense that a monkey falling from a tree admits no gravity; it merely agreed to stop describing a dirty baboon as science, and to pay handsomely for the privilege.

Oscar Finley retired that spring to grow figs ("a life form with fewer appeals," he said, and meant it). Wally Figg bought a boat, banana-yellow, named *S.S. Settlement*, because of course he did. And David Zinc stayed on at the little firm with the cappuccino machine, which the partners now owned outright, taking as his first matter of the new era a pro bonobo case for the itchy school monkeys of the east branch, and losing not one night's sleep over it. Dana came by on Fridays, marked the firm's homework, and ruled it "adequate, with flair."

"You know," Wally told him from the deck of the boat, "for a monkey who went bananas before lunch, you ape-pealed pretty well."

"That," said David, "is the last time you use that pun."

It was not, of course, the last time. But that, as they say in the troop, is billable.
