# Bananabel Lee

*A parody of "Annabel Lee"*

It was many and many a year ago,
   In a jungle by the sea,
That a monkey there lived whom you may know
   By the name of Bananabel Lee;
And this monkey she lived with no other thought
   Than to love and be loved by me.

I was a chimp and she was a chimp,
   In this jungle by the sea,
But we loved with a love that was more than love —
   I and my Bananabel Lee —
With a love that the winged macaques of heaven
   Coveted her and me.

We were juveniles then, and the jungle was new,
   And the seasons were all in key;
We counted the stars through the fig-tree leaves
   Where nobody climbs but we;
And she said that the moon was a yellow 'nana
   That had fallen off heaven's tree.

I saw her first on a rope of vines
   At a party of high degree;
She swung the length of the canopy
   And lighted on the branch by me;
And I knew no feast would ever again
   Taste half so sweet to me.

She laughed like rain on a banana leaf,
   So bright, and silver, and free,
That the grumpiest mandrill of all her kinsmen
   Would laugh himself out of a tree;
And the sternest elder in all the jungle
   Would chuckle — quietly.

Her kinsmen were mandrills of masterly stripes,
   All perfume and pedigree;
They murmured, "He has not a grove to his name,"
   But she answered, "He's a grove to me;
For a grove," said she, "is wherever he hangs,
   And wherever he hangs, I'll be."

We shared every bunch from the tallest of trees,
   Just my Bananabel Lee,
And she always would hand me the best of the bunch
   And keep the small ones for me;
No monkeys beneath the whole green ceiling
   Were half so happy as we.

We groomed every morning from crown down to tail,
   In our jungle by the sea,
Till our fur shone like noon on a ripened 'nana,
   My Bananabel Lee and me;
And the parrots who watched us would sigh to the wind
   That such love could never be.

And the winged macaques of heaven looked down
   From their glittering gallery,
And they sighed, "They have what we have not —
   Somebody warm to groom, you see;
For a halo is cold, and a cloud is damp,
   However it comes to be."

And this was the reason that, long ago,
   In this jungle by the sea,
A wind came out of a rain cloud, chilling
   My beautiful Bananabel Lee;
And her tail, that had curled round my heart every night,
   Hung down as still as could be.

The cloud came up with the face of an ape
   That had never once climbed a tree;
And the wind came down, and the parrots went still,
   And the tide drew back from the lea;
And I felt her tail slacken its hold on my heart
   In that jungle beside the sea.

She chose the finest 'nana of all
   And pressed it into my hand:
"However far the fig may bend,
   You eat the best in the land;
And you mind," said she, "what the angels are owed" —
   And her voice went out like a lamp on the sand.

And the healers could do nothing, the elders said so,
   By the edge of the sounding sea;
So her highborn kinsmen came with their grim black faces
   And bore her away from me,
To shut her up in a nest in the highest tree
   In this kingdom by the sea.

The angels, not half so happy in heaven,
   Went envying her and me —
Yes! — that was the reason (as all chimps know,
   In this jungle by the sea)
That the wind came out of the cloud by night,
   Chilling and stilling my Bananabel Lee.

And her kinsmen mourn in their masterly stripes,
   Their gravity and degree,
But they never once bring her a ripe 'nana,
   Which shows how little they see;
For a love without fruit is a body without fur,
   However pinstriped it be.

But our love it was stronger by far than the love
   Of those who were older than we —
Of many far wiser than we —
And neither the brachiators in heaven above
   Nor the baboons down under the sea
Can ever dissever my soul from the soul
   Of the beautiful Bananabel Lee.

For the winged macaques had everything —
   The halos, the clouds, the key —
But nobody ever handed them
   The best of the bunch, you see;
And that is the whole of the matter, chimps,
   As plain as plain can be.

For the moon never beams, without bringing me dreams
   Of the beautiful Bananabel Lee;
And the stars never rise, but I feel the bright eyes
   Of the beautiful Bananabel Lee;
And so, all the night-tide, I lie down by the side
   Of my darling — my darling — my prime mate and bride —
   In her nest by the banana tree.

The troopers all say that my wits have gone ape,
   Old and gray as I grow,
And they mutter, and point, and they will not climb up
   Where the high fig throws its shadow;
But I keep a ripe 'nana each night on the sill
   Of her nest, for she loved them so.

And the juveniles swing on the lowest boughs
   And dare each other to see
The old gray chimp who talks to the moon
   And the wind in the banana tree;
And I let them swing, and I let them stare —
   Their grandmothers stared at me.

The elders offer me roots and rest,
   And the healers a leaf or three;
But no root in the jungle grows deep enough
   To cure a chimp of Bananabel Lee;
For a sickness of heart is a fruit of the soul,
   And soul-fruit ripens eternally.

Every bunch that I pick from the tallest tree
   Has a taste of the wind to me;
For the sweetest 'nana that ever was grown
   Is the one she always kept for me;
And a banana unshared is a sad brown thing,
   However gold it be.

And the sea, when it cannot sleep at night,
   Comes up the sand to me;
For the sea knew her first, and the sea knew her best,
   And the sea is good company;
We say nothing at all, the sea and I, and it suits us,
   Beside the banana tree.

And the leopards may prowl, and the vultures may circle,
   And the storm may shake the tree,
But nothing up in heaven above,
   Nor under the leaves of the sea,
Can ever dissever my soul from the soul
   Of the beautiful Bananabel Lee.

So I swing in the wind where the wild fig bends
   Down over the salt-white sand,
And I dream with my face in the perfumed dark
   Of the warm and yellow land;
For my prime mate, my prime mate, is waiting for me
   In the boughs of the banana tree.

I watch her nest in the highest tree
   When the moon climbs up with me;
And the sorrow I carry is heavy as fruit,
   And as sweet as fruit is she;
For grief, rightly ripened, is love with the shine on,
   And it shines eternally.

My fur has gone silver, my howl has gone hoarse,
   And my grip has lost its degree;
But I climb as well as an old chimp can
   That climbs toward Bananabel Lee;
And every branch of every fig
   Is a stair in her stairway, you see.

I scratch these lines on a curl of bark
   Where the salt wind comes to see;
If the rhyme runs ripe and the meter bends,
   Why, so did she and me;
And if the bark should fall to the sea —
   Why, the sea knows her name, does the sea.

And some fine night, when the moon swings low
   And the boughs hang loose and free,
I'll climb the last fig where the branches end,
   As far as far can be;
And I'll find her there with a bunch of her own,
   And she'll hand the best of it — you'll see — to me.

It was many and many a year ago,
   In a jungle by the sea,
That a monkey there lived whom you may know
   By the name of Bananabel Lee —
And this monkey still lives with no other thought
   Than to love and be loved by me.
