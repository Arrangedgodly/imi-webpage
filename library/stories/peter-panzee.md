# Peter Panzee

*A parody of "Peter Pan"*

Wendee Darling knew precisely the moment her mother had grown up: it was the night her mother stopped swinging in her sleep. Wendee had no intention of ever growing up, which was convenient, because a certain small chimpanzee was already outside her window looking for his shadow.

His name was Peter Panzee, and he had lost the shadow to Old Snap-Jaw, the turtle, who nipped it clean off at the heels as Peter napped in the branches. Peter had folded the poor thing into a drawer and gone off to Neverwood, where he was too busy being a chimp to notice he was shadowless.

Now he sat on the nursery branch, trying to glue the shadow back on with fig pulp.

"It won't stick," Wendee said, watching him. "It wants sewing. Hold still." And with a thorn for a needle and a strand of spider silk, she sewed Peter's shadow to his heels, and Peter crowed and looped the loop around the room.

"I'm going back to Neverwood," he announced, "where I'm captain of the Lost Baboons. And you can come, if you like. You'll tell us stories. Baboons are fearfully bad at endings."

"Can Michael and John come?"

"They can be baboons too. Now — faith, trust, and a little bit of firefly dust." Tinklebell, a furious golden firefly in a lamp of her own light, buzzed jealousy at Wendee's shoulder. "Second branch to the right," said Peter, "and straight on till morning."

And they flew.

Neverwood was a jungle with no floor and no rules — lagoons of mermaids, mangroves of wild monkeys, and the Jolly Fig, where Captain James Crook, an ancient orangutan with an iron hook for a hand, nursed his grudge. Once, the tick-tock crocodile had eaten his hand and liked it so much it followed him everywhere, hoping for the rest. It had swallowed a clock, and that tick-tick-ticking drove the Captain bananas — which was the only thing that ever had.

The island had a way of knowing when Peter was back: the beasts stood still, the lost baboons came out of their hollow trees, the pirates ran to their captain, the redskins ran to their arms, and the mermaids vanished under the lagoon. It was a jungle holding its breath — six kinds of monkeys all pointing at each other.

Tink got to the baboons first, with her lamp burning green. "Peter says," she buzzed, "shoot the falling Wendy-bird." And Tootles, the most unfortunate baboon in the jungle, fitted his arrow and shot Wendee out of the sky. She landed in the crown of a fig, unshot through, because the arrow had struck the acorn-button Peter had given her. Peter was furious; Tink was banished for a week; and Wendee, being neither dead nor a bird, became the Lost Baboons' mother on the spot, in a little house built around her body under the ground.

There followed the happiest season Wendee would ever be allowed to remember. She sewed the baboons' pockets, told the bedtime stories, doled out pretend medicine (which was only rainwater, and worked every time), and tucked them into their hollows. The redskins camped above them and stood guard; and one night, at the mermaids' lagoon, Peter saved Tiger Lily, the redskin chieftainess's daughter, from the pirates by imitating Captain Crook's voice so perfectly that Smee set her free without knowing why. "Pan, me savio," said Tiger Lily, which means "Peter, me friend," and the redskins' drums thumped Peter's praises till morning — while Peter, who could imitate anything except modesty, crowed himself to sleep.

But it was at the lagoon, too, that Peter made his famous remark. The tide was rising, Peter and Wendee were marooned on the rock, and the water was coming to carry them off. "To swim," said Peter, "will be an awfully big adventure." And before Wendee could tremble, the Never-bird — whose nest Peter had once saved — floated her chicks' cradle to him like a little boat, and they paddled home, safe, soaked, and slightly famous.

Captain Crook's great trap came in the autumn. He took Tiger Lily's silence, Smee's blunder, and a poisoned banana-milk, and he laid them all at the underground house. Wendee and the Lost Baboons were taken prisoner to the Jolly Fig, bound and hoisted, and the Captain gloated in his ginger fur while the boys were made ready for the plank.

Wendee walked it without a tremble, because Peter's voice was at her ear: "No splash — trust me."

There was no splash — only a monstrous ticking, imitated so perfectly by Peter that the Captain turned green and every pirate leapt the rail, straight past the real crocodile, which pursued the Captain's boat out to sea with the patience of clocks.

The baboons came up out of the hold like champagne. The deck ran with feathers and hooks. And then it was Peter and the Captain, alone, circling the mainmast.

"Crook," said Peter, "you're not monkey enough to fight fair."

"Panzee," said the Captain, "you're not grown enough to die."

But Wendee had told her stories on that deck a hundred nights running, and stories have consequences: Crook fought well, but he had begun — horribly, helplessly — to *care how the story came out*. And when his hook tore Peter's sleeve, and Peter let him pick his hook up off the deck instead of striking, the Captain could not bear it. He looked at his own paw, and at the boy's shining face, and he heard the tick-tick-tick coming for him, and Captain James Crook leapt overboard of his own free will — the last order he ever gave that anyone obeyed.

"Pan," said the crocodile, respectfully, and took him.

And it was Tink who saved the last night. The Captain had poisoned Peter's bedtime banana-milk before the trap sprang, and Tinklebell drank it down to warn him, and lay dimming in her little lantern.

"Her light's going out!" cried Peter. "She'll die if nobody believes in monkeys!" He called to every sleeping jungle and every dreaming child: "Do you believe? Say it — clap if you believe!"

And far away, in nurseries and tree-houses and hollow logs, thousands of sleepy voices said it together: *I do believe in monkeys. I do. I do.* — and clapped — and Tink's lantern blazed like a second morning.

Wendee flew home with her brothers and the Lost Baboons, and found the window open, which is a mother's way of saying she knew all along. Mr. and Mrs. Darling adopted the whole crew on the spot (there being no law against adopting baboons, and Mrs. Darling having always felt the nursery was under-populated). Peter stood a moment on the sill.

"Come with me, Wendee. You needn't grow up. I'll come every spring for you, and we'll go cleaning-season the crocodile's teeth."

"I can't come," she said gently. "I want always to have stories for people who'll grow up too."

"Grow up," sniffed Peter, "is monkey business."

And away he swung, and up, and off — second branch to the right, and straight on till morning.

He came back the next spring — but Wendee was at school, and it is a rule of the jungle that spring inside a schoolhouse passes for three. He came back the spring after that, and found her grown, with a tall collar and a grave face, and a little daughter of her own asleep in the old nursery.

"What's the matter?" said Peter, bewildered. "It's spring-cleaning time."

"I can't come, Peter," said Wendee, and her eyes were wet. "I've grown up. This is Jane. She'll be wanting a mother's stories, and you'll be wanting stories for her."

And Jane woke, and clapped, and said she knew the second branch from the right perfectly well.

And so it went, as it goes: Wendee grew old telling stories, and Jane grew up and was mother to Margaret, and Peter swung in and out of their springs forever, young and heartless and merry — as long as there are little monkeys who refuse bedtime, he is swinging still. The last of the Neverwood stories, the mothers never quite remember; but the children know the ending by heart, because it never has one.
