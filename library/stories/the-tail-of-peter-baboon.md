# The Tail of Peter Baboon

*A parody of "The Tale of Peter Rabbit"*

Once upon a time there were four little baboons, and their names were Flopsy, Mopsy, Cotton-tail, and Peter.

They lived with their Mother in a sandy bank, underneath the root of a very big fig tree.

"Now, my dears," said old Mrs. Baboon one morning, "you may go into the grove or down the liana path, but don't go into Mr. Macaque's garden. Your Father had an accident there; he was put in a pie by Mrs. Macaque."

"Now run along and don't get into any mischief. I am going out."

She took her basket and umbrella and went through the wood to the baker's. She bought a loaf of brown bread and five little bananas.

Flopsy, Mopsy, and Cotton-tail, who were good little baboons, went down the liana path to gather ripe guavas. They carried a little basket apiece, and walked two and two, and set every fruit in carefully, and did not eat one until they were told.

But Peter, who was very naughty, ran straight away to Mr. Macaque's garden, and squeezed under the gate!

First he ate some sugarcane and some baby papayas, and then — feeling rather collywobbly in his wobble — he went to look for some pawpaw.

But round the end of a plantain row, whom should he meet but Mr. Macaque himself!

"STOP THIEF!" shouted Mr. Macaque, dropping his hoe.

Peter was frightfully upset; he rushed all over the garden, for he had forgotten the way back to the gate.

Mr. Macaque came pounding behind him, muttering as he ran: "A baboon! In MY garden! After my best papayas! If I catch him, he shall go in a pie, see if he doesn't!" — and at the word *pie*, Peter remembered his Father, and his wobble got considerably wobblier.

He lost one of his little slippers among the plantains, and the other little slipper amongst the yam vines.

Then he ran on four paws and went faster, so that I think he might have got away altogether if he had not unfortunately run into a thorn-fence of bromeliads, and caught his little blue jacket, which had big brass buttons, on a spike. It was quite new, and it held him fast.

Mr. Macaque came up with a sieve, which he intended to pop upon the top of Peter; but Peter wriggled out just in time, leaving his jacket behind him, and rushed into the tool-shed, and jumped into a compost bucket that held quite a lot of rainwater.

It would have been a beautiful thing to hide in, if it had not had so much rainwater in it. Peter held his breath so long inside it that his cheeks bulged like a baboon storing food for later.

Mr. Macaque was quite sure that Peter was somewhere in the tool-shed, hidden perhaps under a flower-pot. He began to turn them over carefully, looking under each.

"A baboon," he muttered, peering under a pot of ferns, "after four rows of my finest papayas. In a pie. A small pie. No sense wasting pastry."

Presently Peter sneezed — "Kertyschoo!" Mr. Macaque was after him in no time, and tried to put his foot upon Peter, who jumped out of a window with a bounce, upsetting three pots of young papaya seedlings. Mr. Macaque hung the little blue jacket and the two little slippers on a scarecrow stake, "to frighten the fruit-bats," he said, and went back to his planting.

Peter watched him go from the shadow of the water barrel, with his ears flat and his heart going like a fig falling downstairs.

Peter sat down to rest; he was out of breath and trembling with fright. Also he was very damp with sitting in that bucket of rainwater, and he began to cry a little, very quietly, because even naughty baboons cry quietly in other people's gardens.

After a time he began to wander about, going lippity — lippity — not very fast, and doing his best, bumble-baboon that he was, to keep out of Mr. Macaque's sight. He crept past a little pond in the corner of the garden, where a white ocelot sat upon the bank, staring down at the gold-fish. She was so very still, and her eyes had gone so narrow and patient, that a cold shiver ran all the way down Peter's tail; and he tip-toed past her on the tips of his little bare toes, as quietly as a baboon possibly can, which is quietly indeed when it matters.

He found a door in a wall; but it was locked, and there was no room for a fat little baboon to squeeze underneath.

An old sparrow-mouse sat on a branch and asked Peter what he was crying for. Peter begged her to show him the way to the gate, but she put her head on one side, chirped a bit, and hopped off — for she had mangoes of her own to think about.

Then Peter tried to squeeze under the gate one more time. He wriggled and jiggled, twisted his tail this way and that, went as thin as a monkey on a diet — and POP — he was through, and away down the wood, minus his jacket and both his slippers.

Peter never stopped running or looked behind him till he got home to the big fig tree.

He was so tired he flopped down upon the nice soft sand floor of the baboon-hole, and closed his eyes. His mother, coming home with her basket and five little bananas, counted her children — one, two, three, four — and their slippers — two short — and asked no questions at all, for it had been that kind of day before, in her time.

She put him to bed and made some fig-leaf tea, which she gave him a tablespoonful of at bedtime!

"I'm sorry about the jacket, Mummy," mumbled Peter.

"Jackets can be mended," said Mrs. Baboon, tucking the leaf-blanket under his chin, "but little baboons cannot. One tablespoonful, and straight to sleep. And Peter — the next time you are tempted by somebody's papayas, remember your Father."

"Was he very naughty?" whispered Peter, already half asleep.

"He was delicious," said his Mother grimly, "which is the whole trouble. Straight to sleep."

But Flopsy, Mopsy, and Cotton-tail had guava, and brown bread, and honey, and two little bananas each, for supper. They whispered about the garden over the good food, and agreed among themselves that Peter had been very brave, and that they would never tell him they had said so.

That evening, by the light of one firefly lamp, Mrs. Baboon cut out a new little jacket from soft fig-bark cloth, with brass buttons from the mending gourd — for it is one thing to lose a jacket, and quite another to go without. She made it one button plainer than the last.

And Peter, snug in bed, dreamed sweet dreams of the grove and the liana path — and never once of Mr. Macaque's garden, where his little blue jacket flapped on a stake in the evening breeze, a warning to every fruit-bat in the valley.
