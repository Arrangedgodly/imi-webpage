# Cloudy with a Chance of Chimpfalls

*A parody of "Cloudy with a Chance of Meatballs"*

When my little brother and I woke up that morning, we found Grandpa Gibbon in the kitchen, flipping banana pancakes. He flipped one so high it came down right on my brother's head.

Grandpa paused, looked out the window at the sky for a long moment, and said, "That reminds me of a story."

He sat down between us, and this is what he told us.

"Way upriver, where the maps go fuzzy, there is — or was — a little town called Chimpandswallow, built entirely of treehouses in the crook of two enormous figs. Nobody in Chimpandswallow kept food in a pantry or grew fruit in a garden, because nobody needed to. All the food fell from the sky.

"Three times a day, the jungle beneath the town breathed in, the clouds gathered over the figs, and down it came.

"At breakfast there was mango-nectar rain, clear and sweet, followed by clouds of scrambled egg-fruit, and banana pancakes drifting down flat as leaves. Toast buttered itself on the way past, and jam arrived in fat dollops on the wind. When the wind shifted to pepper, the whole town sneezed at once, which shook the figs, which brought down more toast, so nobody ever complained.

"At lunch, the wind brought roasted plantains, warm from the sky. On foggy days, a thick coconut soup rolled in and hung about the lower branches until everyone had eaten their fill. Mustard and mango sauce came in little sunbursts. The children went to school with plates and forks instead of schoolbags, and the teachers graded on catching.

"At dinner — oh, dinner was the best. Grilled guava. Fried breadfruit. Salads of every green. And on special days, the horizon went purple with mulberry juice, and banana-jelly set in the low forks of the figs, and the children ate jelly straight off the branches with both paws, which is the correct way.

"For years, life in Chimpandswallow was a feast. Nobody went hungry, nobody went shopping, nobody washed a dish. The crumbs were handled by the Sanitation Department — a standing committee of toucans, who were punctual, cheerful, and only slightly unionized.

"There was a holiday for everything: Fritter Day, when the fritters fell crosswise; the Two-Soup Fog, honored every spring; and the day a light honey drizzle glued the mayor to his own podium, which is still celebrated in song. The town records for the golden year logged eleven inches of guava juice, four sandwich storms, and one whole afternoon of nothing but croutons, which nobody could explain and nobody dared repeat.

"But the weather, like a monkey with a stew pot, does not always stay predictable.

"One day, the sky sent nothing but hard green bananas — so hard the children used them for skittles. The next, the breakfast wind brought only overboiled breadfruit, grey and saggy, and the radio announcer apologized on behalf of the atmosphere. Then came a day of giant plantains, rolling and tumbling down through the branches, bowling over sheds and sun-shelters and one very unlucky mayor.

"The townsmonkeys held a meeting under the biggest fig. 'The sky,' said the mayor, rubbing his head, 'has gone bananas.'

"It had. A mango monsoon flooded the lower floors. A storm of enormous cream-cheese-and-banana sandwiches flattened the schoolhouse, though nobody was hurt — in Chimpandswallow, even the disaster food was soft — and school stayed cancelled for a week while the children ate their way back in through the roof. One grey Tuesday, a pancake the size of a trampoline came down over the music room, and the band played on inside it, muffled but determined.

"Food fell for breakfast, lunch and dinner. The branches filled up knee-deep, then hip-deep, then tail-deep. The forecast stopped being a convenience and became a threat, delivered each morning in a smaller and smaller radio voice: 'Today: light guava drizzle, clearing to plantain by noon. Maybe.' It never cleared to plantain by noon. Once it cleared to plantain at three o'clock, which felt worse, somehow, like an apology that arrives after the funeral.

"And then, on the worst morning of all, the radio in the town square crackled, and a trembling voice said the words nobody in Chimpandswallow ever wanted to hear:

"'Today's forecast: cloudy, with a chance of chimp falls.'

"And down they came. Chimpanzees. Raining out of the clouds like cats and dogs, though mercifully softer to land on than the plantains — and every single one of them annoyed. They fell through roofs. They fell into soup. One landed in the mayor's teacup and stayed cross about it for a week. Another hung from the weathervane in a wet blanket, complaining loudly about the town's famous service. They had not asked to be weather. Even the Sanitation toucans drew the line and went home.

"'Raining chimps!' the people cried. 'This is too much!'

"So the townsmonkeys did the sensible thing. They took their biggest napkins — the giant banana-leaf kind, saved for sandwich days — tied them to their backs and to the ends of long bamboo poles, and one windy morning the whole town sailed out over the branches and away, a fleet of leaf boats riding the bread-scented breeze. They steered by smell: away from the plantain. The chimps, being chimps, thought it looked like fun and followed, which was how Chimpandswallow came to have the best climbing instructors on the river. Somebody proposed naming the new town Pancake; the chimps outvoted them; it is called Chimpsandswallow now, and both halves take credit.

"They landed on a hillside far downriver, where nothing ever fell from the sky but rain — the first night, everybody flinched at the sound of it. They built a new town of ordinary markets and ordinary meals, and every single one of them took a job connected in some way with food — because after everything, they still loved it. The mayor opened the first shop, which sold umbrellas, and did a steady trade forever. The children missed the jelly in the forks of the figs; the parents missed nothing at all except the way the morning toast buttered itself; and by the second year they had mostly stopped flinching at falling mangoes.

"They never ate anything that fell out of the sky without checking it first. And they carried umbrellas, always — not for the rain, but just in case."

Grandpa Gibbon stopped and looked at my brother, who still had pancake syrup on his head.

"So!" Grandpa said cheerfully, picking the spatula back up. "Pass the bananas, and — heads up."

He flipped one more pancake. It rose, and rose, and for just one second I swear the whole kitchen held its breath.

That night, my brother and I sat at the window and watched the sunset, which looked exactly like mashed banana with a mango swirl. And when the radio said "tomorrow, partly cloudy," we looked at each other and said it at the same time:

"Partly cloudy — with a slight chance of chimps."

We take an umbrella to school now. And a plate, just in case of anything else.
