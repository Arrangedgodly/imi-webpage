# Where the Wild Swings Are

*A parody of "Where the Wild Things Are"*

The night Maxine wore her monkey suit and made mischief of one kind and another, her mother called her "WILD THING!" and Maxine said "I'LL EAT YOU UP!" and she was sent to bed without eating anything — not even her bedtime banana.

She had earned it, to be fair. That day Maxine had tied the baby monkeys' tails together into one thick plait and watched them unroll like a party streamer. She had swung from the door-vine into the fig bowl. She had taught the toucan to say "MOTHER SAYS WHAT?" at the dinner branch. Mischief of one kind, mischief of another — Maxine did both kinds, before lunch.

"I'LL EAT YOU UP!" she had shouted, and that was the trouble, because you cannot shout that at the one person who peels your banana, and expect bedtime stories.

Maxine sat in her nest and thought about running away. But before she had finished thinking, the wildness in her got busy.

That night, in Maxine's nest, a forest grew.

It grew creepers, it grew lianas, it grew great grinning fig trees, and vines drooped down like party streamers — until the ceiling hung with leaves and the walls became the world all around. An ocean tumbled by with a private boat for Maxine, and she sailed off through night and day, in and out of weeks, and almost over a year, to the place where the Wild Swings are.

And when she came to the place where the Wild Swings are, they roared their terrible roars and gnashed their terrible teeth and rolled their terrible eyes and showed off their terrible prehensile tails.

"OOOH-AAAHH-OOOH!" they howled, swinging upside down from everything, chewing the branches bare like a barrel of monkeys that had been left unsupervised for a century.

"I'll go APE on you!" they bellowed. "We'll peel you like a 'nana, little monkey suit!"

There was Mooshka the mandrill, with a face like a warning label. There was Barnaby the baboon, who laughed before jokes were finished. There was Emil the orangutan, who was mostly fur and opinions. And there were dozens more, all of them roaring at once, which is how Wild Swings say hello.

But Maxine stepped off her boat and said, "BE STILL!"

And she tamed them with the magic trick of staring into all their yellow eyes without blinking once. (A trick she had learned from her grandmother, the alpha-est alpha of the whole western troop, who could end an argument with one sleepy look.) She stared at Mooshka. She stared at Barnaby. She stared at Emil and eleven others besides, and not one of them outlasted her, and every one of them went quiet in a wave, like wind dying down in the fig leaves.

The Wild Swings were terrified. They called her the most Wild Swing of all — hairiest, hangiest, scruffiest, swingiest — and made her their queen.

"And now," cried Maxine, "LET THE WILD RUMPUS START!"

And the wild rumpus started.

They swung from the moon and the moon swung back. They hung by their tails in great hairy chandeliers and spun until the stars got dizzy watching. Mooshka drummed on a hollow log; Barnaby drummed on Emil; Emil drummed on anybody within reach, and the whole island thundered.

Then the Wild Swings made Maxine queen of the parade, and they marched trunk to tail — gorilla, baboon, orangutan, mandrill with a face like a warning label — while Maxine rode the biggest one's shoulders, hollering, "FASTER! MORE APE! GO BANANAS!"

And they went bananas. For hours. For whole moons.

They had a groaning contest under the biggest fig tree, and a grooming circle that turned into a tickle fight that turned into a tickle war that did not stop until everybody fell out of the tree, which was considered part of the game. Emil won the groaning contest; Barnaby won the tickle war by cheating; Mooshka won the falling out of the tree, which from then on was called the Grand Finale.

Then they swung so far out over the water that the waves applauded, which nobody had ever heard them do before. They had a howling choir, with Maxine conducting: Emil took the low notes, Mooshka took the high ones, and Barnaby took everyone else's. They howled at the moon until the moon, out of politeness, howled back, and the tide came in twice, once for each encore.

"Now stop!" said Maxine, and sent the Wild Swings off to bed without their supper. This was an extremely queenly thing to do, and they went, grumbling and gnashing softly, because that is the law of the Wild Swings: whoever can out-stare, out-queen, and out-swing, rules.

And Maxine, the queen of all Wild Swings, sat in her royal fig tree, lonely — lonely in the middle of a hundred hairy subjects, in a crown of twisted lianas, on an island that had no bedtime and no rules and no end of roaring, where nobody ever once said stop, or supper, or Maxine, come here, let me look at that scratch — and wanted to be where someone loved her best of all.

Then, all around from far away across the world, she smelled good things to eat — ripe banana, warm mango, a whisper of fig — and she knew exactly who was in the kitchen, and what it was, and where.

And so Maxine gave up being queen of where the Wild Swings are.

"Oh please don't go," said the Wild Swings, suddenly small-seeming in the dusk. "We'll eat you up — we love you so!"

"That's the trouble," said Maxine. "You say it like a menu. Somebody where I come from says it like a joke, and peels the banana first." And she stepped into her private boat and waved good-bye.

The Wild Swings gnashed their teeth and roared their terrible roars and swung their terrible swings — Mooshka drumming one last farewell, Barnaby laughing through his tears, Emil pretending he had something in both eyes — but Maxine swung her boat out into the night, and the farther she sailed, the smaller their roaring got, until it was only the sound the sea makes, which is the sea missing everybody.

Backward over the year she went, in and out of weeks, through a day, and into the very nest she had left — where the vines tucked themselves politely back into the walls, the ocean folded itself up and put itself away, and the forest drew back like a curtain after the best kind of show, leaving only her bedroom leaves, her blanket, and the smell from the kitchen.

And she found her supper waiting for her, on a plate that was hers, at the branch that was hers, exactly where she had left it and exactly where she had, all that long year away, been meaning to get back to.

Still warm.

With a banana, perfectly ripe, peeled at the top by somebody who loved her best of all.

Maxine ate every bit of it, hung her monkey suit on the bedpost, and fell asleep before she could even start missing the Wild Swings.

Which, if you have ever been queen of anything, is exactly how it ends: wild thing, warm thing, supper thing, sleep.
