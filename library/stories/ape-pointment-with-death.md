# Ape-pointment with Death

*A parody of "Appointment with Death"*

Hercule Pongo, the celebrated orangutan detective, could not sleep. The trellis of the Solomon Hotel in Jerusalem-in-the-Jungle carried voices up from the courtyard below, and one voice said, clear and young and despairing:

"You do see, don't you, that she's got to be killed?"

Pongo sat up among his cushions, whiskers bristling, and memorized the tones as other monkeys memorize fig trees.

The tones belonged to Raymond Boynton, and by the morrow's trek to Banapetra — the ancient city carved into rose-brown cliffs, its temples chewed out of living rock by monkeys long dead — Pongo was able to put faces to the despair. There was the Boynton family: Raymond, his brother Lennox, his sister Carol, and little Ginevra, trailing like prisoners on a rope. There was Lennox's wife Nadine, a still, level-eyed colobine, the only one of them who moved like a free monkey; and there was Jefferson Cope, a big amiable woolly spider monkey from the Americas, who admired Nadine openly, hopelessly, and with perfect courtesy, like a sunflower admiring a window. And there, in a vast chair carried by two hired porters, their stepmother, Mrs. Boynton — an immense, pale uakari with a face the colour of raw liver and eyes that never once blinked at anything kindly.

She was, Pongo observed, the only monkey in the jungle who was groomed by no one and feared by everyone. Her family did not wander, did not speak to outsiders, did not dare. "She collects us," Ginevra had told a young doctor called Sarah King, a brisk, handsome macaque, "like fleas she won't let anyone pick off."

Sarah King had tried battle on the first evening. "Your family are grown monkeys, Mrs. Boynton," she said, planting herself before the great chair. "You have no right to keep them on a rope."

The pale uakari looked her over, slowly, the way a python inventories a goat, and smiled. "I never forget a face," she said sweetly. "Run along, my dear, and bark elsewhere."

The other tourists pitied and prattled: Dr. Gerard, a French chimpanzee psychologist who took notes on the old monkey's cruelty as on a rare orchid; Lady Westholme, a booming patas monkey and Member of Parliament, who had opinions on watering holes; and Miss Pierce, a timid tourist with a parasol, who had opinions on nothing.

On the last evening at the camp, Nadine climbed to the chair at the mouth of the cave. Pongo, half-hidden by a carved pillar, heard every word. "I am going away tomorrow," Nadine said quietly. "With Mr. Cope. Lennox is free if he will take his freedom. I am done serving your pleasure." Mrs. Boynton listened, and blinked once, like a lizard sealing a letter. "You may go, Nadine," she said. "But Lennox stays. Remember — I never forget a face. None of you will ever escape me."

At sunset the party came down through the Siq, the long red throat of the cliff city, and camped among the carved façades. At moonrise, Mrs. Boynton sat in her great chair, an enormous crimson spider on a cushion, and told each of her family, one by one, in that sweet poisoned whisper: *remember. I never forget a face. None of you will ever escape me.*

An hour later she was dead in her chair, and the little puncture on her wrist was explained away as a thorn-scratch, and her family — every one of them — looked at the corpse with eyes like the doors of empty storehouses.

"I do not need to be told a crime has occurred," said Pongo to the assembled camp, gathered in the marquee at midnight. "The little grey fur follicles, messieurs, have been at work. Consider! Her syringe of digi-tail-is — the heart-medicine — was full at departure; yet she died of it. Dr. Gerard's own medical case was robbed of a syringe while its owner lay sweating with jungle fever. Her wrist-watch, madame, was cracked and had stopped — at an hour when two tourists will swear she still lived, for they heard her say so; the watch, in a word, has been adjusted, like a clock in a dishonest fig-shop. And who passed near her chair? Everyone. But who *needed* her dead? No one on this trek. Her slaves she had already. That is the brilliant, the ape-ocryphal difficulty."

He questioned them each in turn, and the answers fitted together like a quilt sewn by rival tailors. Raymond had been walking with Sarah, who defended him too quickly. Carol had been in her tent, weeping, she said, and the state of her fur confirmed it. Lennox had been reading, or staring at a page until it surrendered. Ginevra had been lying on her bed "being poisoned," which was her ordinary occupation and proved nothing but drama. "Your alibis," Pongo told them, not unkindly, "protect one another so beautifully that I conclude none of you needs protecting. You are covering for a crime each of you dreamed and none of you performed."

Miss Pierce, consulted last, offered her crumb with apologies. "I did see someone, a little before five — a man, I fancy, in Arab dress, bending near the chair. Tall, and, um, bulky. But it was far, and I am short, and parasols, you know—"

"Short, and bulky, and dressed in robes no Bedouin would wear to a private murder," said Pongo. He paused, and polished one whisker.

"But recall her words: *I never forget a face.* Forty years ago Mrs. Boynton was a matron of a cage-house — a keeper of imprisoned females, where privileges were sold and cruelties were free. Among them was one who has since risen high on polish and tail-brush. Her past, discovered, would destroy her. She saw the face at Banapetra, and knew it knew. Mesdames — I speak to the Member of Parliament."

The lamp hissed. Lady Westholme rose to her full grand height, and her voice filled the marquee like a drum.

"You insolent little orange monkey," she said. "I built myself from nothing — from a cage-floor. I buried her in honours and speeches, and she sat there at the end, in that chair, and *smiled* at me. Yes. I took the syringe from the Frenchman's case; I wore a burnous over my Sunday shoulders, which is why Miss Pierce saw a man the size of a haystack; I made her watch stop at an hour to confuse you all; and the thorn was mine. I had an appointment with her." She swept out into the desert dark, and by morning was a smudge of dust going east, resigning from Parliament by every fig-post on the road.

They found the tiny thorn-syringe in the sand, exactly where the little grey fur follicles said it would be.

Raymond Boynton came to Pongo by the great carved façade at dawn. "That night in Jerusalem — you heard us. We talked of killing her. All of us. We none of us did."

"I know," said Pongo gently. "You were all too busy dreaming of escape. It took an enemy from her own past to keep the appointment. Go — your troop is free. And mademoiselle the doctor," he added, twinkling, "prescribes for you, I think, a long life of being groomed at last."

Nadine had already gone back down the cliff to Lennox, who had held a page of his book for a night and a day without once turning it; the Cope suit was concluded over breakfast with such friendliness that Jefferson Cope stayed on a fortnight to teach the young ones American wrestling holds, and wept into his coffee only twice. Sarah King took Raymond's arm. Above them the rose-red cliffs of Banapetra turned gold, and the Boyntons, released, climbed down into the valley like monkeys trying the ground for the first time — cautiously, and then at a run.
