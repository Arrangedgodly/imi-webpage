# Bananaby Rudge

*A parody of "Barnaby Rudge"*

In the year 1775 of the great canopy, at the Vinepole — an old tavern of thirteen bending boughs, kept by John Willet, a fat publican who regarded every new idea as a personal assault — there hung the anniversary of a murder.

Five-and-twenty years before, on that same March night, Reuben Haredale of the Warren, and his gardener, had been done to death in their beds, and the deed had never been paid for. Every year on the date, the household of the Warren shuddered; every year a shape was said to drift through its dark walks — the ghost of the murdered ape, hooded, gliding, gone by dawn. Geoffrey Haredale, the brother who had inherited the ruin, kept his shutters shut and his blaster loaded.

Nobody at the Vinepole connected any of this with the widow Rudge, who lived quietly in a distant lane with her son — or with the son himself, Bananaby Rudge, a great grown loris of five-and-twenty with the trusting eyes of a sapling, all laughter and colored rags, who knew every monkey in the parish by the cut of their fur, and knew nothing else at all.

"He is not right," his mother said softly, "and so the world owes him gentleness — which is lucky, for I have nothing else to leave him."

Bananaby's one great friend and counselor was Grip, a large black toucan of a boundless vanity, who rode his shoulder like an admiral. "I'm a philosopher," Grip would announce to strangers. "Polly, put the kettle on." He could say "no pineapples" before the tale was done — he learned it from the crowd, as we shall see — and "Grip's the word," and once, memorably, at the bedside of a dying magistrate, "hold your noise."

There was loving afoot as well as haunting. Joe Willet, the Vinepole's broad-shouldered son, worshiped Dolly Varden, the locksmith's pretty daughter, a marmoset in a bedizened bonnet who was perfectly aware of it and perfectly unsure she minded. And Edward Chester, son of the silken, sneering Sir John Chester, loved Emma Haredale of the Warren, in defiance of a family feud kept warm by Sir John out of habit. Sir John yawned through his wickedness like an ape yawning at the sun; he had, unknown to all, one other son in the world, a wild golden creature called Hugh, hostler at the Vinepole, half-feral, wholly game for any riot.

And through all of it, at midnight once a year, there came to the widow's lane a shrouded figure with a staff, and an old blind orangutan named Stagg at its elbow, and the widow turned white and filled their palms and said, "Go, go, in heaven's name," and told Bananaby nothing.

Because the hooded thing was Barnaby's father. Rudge had done the Warren's murder, and let suspicion fall elsewhere, and had been bought silence ever since; and Stagg, the blind fence, milked him yearly like an old fig tree. This is a secret the reader may hold from the first, and the lane held it too, and the tale held it, until the night it could hold no more.

Into this brewing weather came Lord George Gordon — a slight, sincere, crack-brained young howler with a fixed idea, an unmanageable eye, and a secretary named Gashford who managed the fixed idea for his own profit. The fixed idea was Pineapples. Pineapples, said the Association, were a foreign, spiky, crown-topped, papist fruit, that would hook itself by fraud into every honest monkey's bowl; and nothing less than the total exclusion of Pineapples from the canopy could save the nation. Lord George believed it with his whole small soul. Forty thousand monkeys wore blue cockades within the month, and the cry went up and down the branches:

"No Pineapples! No Pineapples!"

It is astonishing what a grievance will do when it is also a party. Peaceful marchers first; then windows; then orchards of the spiky traitors burned; then the houses of pineapple-fanciers; then anyone with a pineapple-ish look. Gashford fed lists. Hugh, who wanted only to swing hard and be shouted at, recruited the worst of the canopy. Dennis, the public hangman — a mild, chatty, professional ape who followed crowds with an appraiser's eye — brought rope.

And Bananaby was swept up. He came home one night in a blue bonnet, with a banner, a tin chain of office, and a face of pure delight, having been shouted at beautifully for six hours.

"Mother! I am famous! Look! They say I am the boldest fellow in the troop! Three thousand strong, mother! They know me!"

"Grip knows you," said the toucan darkly. "Polly, put the kettle on. No pineapples."

The widow's terror did nothing. The riots grew teeth. They burned the Warren; they broke open the Great Nut-Gate jail and poured its prisoners into the streets like spilled grubs; the canopy blazed from end to end; and Dolly Varden, who had run to warn her friends, and Emma Haredale, were both seized by the mob, carried off, and locked in a Boot-tavern upstairs by Hugh's men, with witless gallantry and very real menace.

They were freed by a strange pair of rescuers: Joe Willet, home from the wars with an empty sleeve — he had run away from the Vinepole to be a soldier, having been told once too often that he was a boy — and Edward Chester, who loved Emma and swung a truncheon like a gentlemonkey. Both ladies were carried off their feet, and not entirely unwillingly, in the sense that Dolly never afterward allowed Joe to mention it, and married him.

The canopy came to its senses the way a sick tree does: violently. Troops moved in; the blue cockades turned into ordinary monkeys fleeing very fast in all directions; and among the prisoners taken were Bananaby — found still carrying his banner, bewildered, radiant — and the hooded Rudge beside him, who would not leave the boy. His mother got to the jail at last, and heard the whole of it: the murder, the ghost-walk, the bought years. She only held her son's hand tighter.

Hugh and Dennis and Rudge and Bananaby were condemned together, to hang on one scaffold. Hugh met it grinning, and shocked them all by going to his death rebelling; Dennis, who had hanged others for a living, discovered with indignation that the trade had no loyalty card, and turned king's evidence to save his neck; and Bananaby's mother stood beneath the beam while the rope was being fitted about her boy's neck — he laughing at the crowd, saying it was a fine game, and would they do it again tomorrow — and looking, with a strange composure, for someone in the mob.

The reprieve came at the last hour, hoof-beats and paper, an amnesty signed in a shaken hand — the King's mercy, moved by the widow's petition, by the intercession of the gentlemen, and by the testimony of Edward and Haredale and the Vardens. The ropes came off. Rudge was transported instead to the far plantations for life; Dennis, not being spared twice, kept his own appointment; Grip, from a shutter, called "No pineapples!" at the hangman, which was reckoned the best epigram of the season.

And that was not quite all; because Sir John Chester, playing one silky game too many, had baited Geoffrey Haredale into a duel by the river at dawn, and been run through his fine white waistcoat. He died elegantly, without pain, character, or excuse, and Edward, arriving too late, forgave his father the only way left: by marrying Emma Haredale and uniting the two houses whose feuds had filled the first chapter of everyone's lives.

The rest can be told in a breath, because the rest is happiness, which takes less time. Joe and Dolly kept a tavern of their own, and Joe never once told Dolly what to do. Edward and Emma lived at the Warren with the windows open on fine nights, just to prove they could. The widow Rudge and Bananaby — who wore his reprieve in his bonnet for years, and was made much of by the whole parish, and never once understood why — kept a little garden, and were happy. "The world is good to me, mother," Bananaby said. "It always was."

And Grip lived to an enormous age, in fame among the taverns, worshipped by Bananaby's grandchildren, growing more philosophical every winter, until one evening, refusing to say "Polly, put the kettle on" for a shilling, he fixed the company with his eye, informed them once more that he was a philosopher — and died with his boots on.
