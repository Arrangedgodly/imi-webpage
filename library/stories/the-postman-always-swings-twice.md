# The Postman Always Swings Twice

*A parody of "The Postman Always Rings Twice"*

They threw me off the banana boat about midnight.

I woke with sand in my fur and walked up the coast road until I saw a fruit stand with a sign palm-painted on it: TWIN BANANA PALMS. N. PAPAYADAKIS, PROP. BEST BRANDED FRUIT ON THE COAST. The "branded" was spelled wrong, but everything about the joint was spelled wrong, including me stopping there.

The man who owned it was a big, homely, good-natured older macaque, proud of his oranges and of Gyros Grove, the old country he'd crossed the water from. He'd built the stand himself, and he'd built it crooked, and he was the only thing in it that didn't seem to know that. He was saving, Cora told me later, to take her home to Gyros Grove some day. She used to say the only grove she wanted had neon on it.

"There's a job here if you want it," Nick Papayadakis said. "Not much pay. Bed's under the counter."

Then his wife came out to the register, and the sun came through the door behind her, and I understood I was no longer drifting. I was sinking.

Cora was hay-colored and restless, with a mouth made for trouble and a dream as big as the coast: "the swankiest fruit stand on the whole highway," she'd say, "with a neon banana on it, and a juicer, and doilies." Doilies. A female monkey can look at a dump with a fly-covered fruit rack and see a palace with doilies. That's how they're built.

I stayed. It was wrong and I did it anyway. She handed me a paw of figs the second morning and our wrists touched over the cash basket, and neither of us pulled away, and that was that.

"Nick's crazy about me," Cora said one night while he counted the register. "Crazy like a male who thinks he owns a female. I want out, Frank. I want that neon banana."

"You want me," I said, "to help you get it."

First we tried the clever way. The cistern pipe behind the stand had a bad splice; Nick took his shower under it every night at nine. Cora got the spanner for me and stood in the dark with her arms around herself, and I stood behind the shower curtain with my arm up, and I couldn't. Me, who'd swung every line from here to the Grey Gibbet. The lamps died then — storm took the power — and we stood in the dark three feet apart, and Nick, dripping, called it a miracle and shook my paw and gave me the whole next day off.

We went and sat in the surf-wash that night, up to our chests, and the gibbons at the hill temple started up, hooting and hooting, till it felt like the whole coast was telling us something. "Once is accident," Cora said, "and twice is us. Frank. He's got to be twice."

Second time, we did it right. She told him she was expecting, and it was the healer at Santa Banana-bara or nothing, and on the cliff road I sat behind him with the whiskey gourd and my swing measured out in my head, and the road did the rest. The sled went over. Nick never knew what hit him, because it was me.

Then came the Cat. The District Attorney was a lynx the papers called the Cat, on account of how he played with his food. He put Cora in one room and me in another and purred at us through the grilles, patient as a lynx can be when the food is frightened. He had my record, my arms, and the whole Pacific for a motive. And he had the insurance — it turned out Nick had a policy on that sled, two thousand bananas payable when the sled went over the cliff with the owner in it, which Cora collected while I sat in the county tree sweating.

The barrister, a silk-furred sifaka named Kapok, charged more than the stand made in a year and was worth it. "The law is a fruit salad," he told me through the bars, "and everything depends on what the chef calls it." He got the Cat to let Cora sign a complaint against me for the cliff, then got the insurance monkeys to settle for six hundred bananas and silence rather than try their luck with a jury that liked her face. Then Cora wept at the inquest, the jury wept with her, and I got sixty days for the cliff — a technical manslaughter, the chef called it — and Cora walked out with a fine and her figure back.

We were married quiet, and for a while we really had it — the stand, the juicer, a down payment on a neon banana. She'd sit on my knee eating brandied cherries while I counted the register. I was as happy as a male monkey gets, which is happy and listening over his shoulder.

Maybe that's why I wrecked it once, the way males do. The circus wintered up the coast that second spring, and Madge, who put her head in a python's mouth twice nightly for tips, put it elsewhere once, for free, and I let her. It meant nothing and it cost everything. Cora found out, and didn't screech, which was worse, and for a month we were two monkeys polishing the same fruit and speaking to the customers. Then one night she took my paw and drove me down to the surf-wash, the same one, and we stood in it up to our chests. "Once is accident," she said, "and twice is us. But this time, Frank, there's no third thing. There's just us." We howled along with the gibbons at the hill temple till our throats went, and I believed her, and I was right to.

The branch post only swings by twice, you see. Once with the bills. Once with the truth.

The truth came on a Tuesday. A python spooked the mule on the cliff road and the banana sled went over with Cora in it, and I hadn't done it — that was the joke, the joke I howled at through the whole trial — the one thing they hanged me for was the one thing I didn't do. The Cat looked at me across the courtroom with those patient lantern eyes, and I could see him doing the arithmetic: a male monkey plus a cliff plus a first wife's husband equals twice. The tribunal didn't believe in accidents anymore. Not from us. Not twice.

So here I sit, counting hours instead of bananas, and at dawn I'll hear the mail-monkey come along the line, hand over hand, singing his little song.

The chaplain came by yesterday, a gentle old bonobo with fig juice on his cassock. I told him the whole thing at last — Nick, the spanner, the whiskey gourd, all of it, everything except the Tuesday. He asked was I sorry. I said I was sorry about Cora. He said that wasn't the same thing. He was right. It never is.

The postman always swings twice, chum. Ask anybody on the coast. First swing is the letter.

Second swing is you.
