# The Green Vine

*A parody of "The Green Mile"*

This happened in the year of the bad rains, when I was head keeper of Block E at the Old City Zoo — the death block. It was floored in green vine-mat, faded the color of old moss, and the condemned walked it to the far door, where Old Sparky waited: the electric climbing post. We called the corridor the Green Vine. The monkeys called it the Last Liana. You didn't make jokes about it — except for John Cocoa, who never made any.

I had a urinary complaint that year, a burning that had me walking like a startled penguin and snapping at my crew. My crew was good — Brutus, Harry, and Percy Wet-nose, a smirking young macaque with connections on the Zoo Board and a stated ambition to work "the feeding shows upstate." Percy was what my mother would have called a nasty piece of fruit.

The mouse came first, in the fall, trotting down the Green Vine in the middle of the night like a tiny inspector with somewhere to be. Brutus named him Steamboat Willy and started leaving crumbs. Percy went after him with a boot and then a mop and finally a tranq dart, missing with all three and hitting Harry's lunch twice, and it was around then that Eduard Delacroix reached out his paw and the mouse climbed it like a ladder and settled in. Del was a little Cajun capuchin who had done a bad thing once and was sorry every single day. He called the mouse Mr. Jingles, on account of the thread collar and tiny bell he'd made him from a pull-toy, and taught him to fetch peels and run through a thread spool, and for a while the whole block was gentle. Toot-Twit, our oldest con, who ran the block's sweet trade in old banana peels and gossip, gave Mr. Jingles three-to-one odds against Percy and paid out cheerfully.

Percy never forgave either of them. He had asked to run the last feeding show — "upstate experience," he said, the way a monkey says a word he's only read on a sign — and when Del's day came, Percy made sure he'd run it. I've wondered since whether Del would have gone easier if Percy hadn't been saving him up. The cloth over the crown is meant to be soaked, you see, and Percy "forgot," standing there with his little smile while the current looked for a way out and found every wrong one. The lights of the whole zoo browned out that afternoon, and the Zoo Board asked questions in very small voices, and we did not talk about it at supper, or ever.

Before that — Wild Bill Wharf-tan came in, an orange orangutan with a lip tattoo and a head full of hornets, carried down the Vine strapped to a trolley and singing "Barbecue! Me and you and you and me!" at the top of his considerable lungs. He grabbed Percy through the bars just to hear him squeal, and stamped on Mr. Jingles for fun the first week he was with us.

Del screamed. Percy laughed. And into the block, ducking through the doorway because he was too tall for it, came the biggest gorilla I ever saw: seven feet of sorrow, arms like fig trunks, eyes wet and red-rimmed. John Cocoa, they said, for the killing of the little Howler twins — found holding their bodies and crying "It was too late, it was too late."

"Like the drink," he said softly, when I asked his name. "Only not spelled the same, boss."

He asked to see the mouse. He took the broken little body from Del's hands, cupped him, breathed into his fur — and this buzzing came off John Cocoa like a hive of good bees, and Mr. Jingles' heart started up like a toy motor, and the little bell rang. Del cried then, and I have never been surer of heaven than watching a condemned capuchin hold his resurrected mouse.

Later he healed my complaint the same way: big paw below my belly, the buzz, and the burning just... left. Then John opened his mouth, and a cloud of black specks flew out of him like a puff of gnats and dissolved in the corner. Bad things, taken out of us. He spat them like pits.

Word got to the warden, whose wife Melinda had a growth in her head that was eating her words. We smuggled John out to the Wildwood Reserve one stormy night in the potato van — Brutus riding shotgun on the tailgate with a shotgun he never had to use, John crouched under a tarp humming "Barbecue" until we got the doors shut on that memory for good. John held her head and buzzed, gentle as anything, and she sat up and said his name like she had known it all her life, and said: "I dreamed I was in the jungle, and there were no cages."

Coming back, in the rain, John grabbed Percy Wet-nose through the cell bars — quick as a frog taking a fly — and something passed from gorilla to macaque, and Percy marched straight to Wild Bill's cell and shot him six times with the tranq pistol, emptying it into him in front of the Zoo Board inspectors, doing his career no good at all with each squeeze. They carried Percy away in a soft-room van that same week, and last I heard he was telling the attendants about balloons.

That was when I understood, talking with John through the night, that he hadn't killed the twins. He had found them still warm, and tried to push their lives back in with his breath, and it was too late, and the keepers had come. "He kill them with they love," John said of Wharf-tan. "That how it is, every day, all over the jungle, and the jungle don't care. I'm tired, boss. I tired of the noise."

We could have stopped his execution. We had what we needed to try. John wouldn't hear it. "We each owe a death, boss," he said. "I seen mine in dreams. A warm country, no cages, bananas growing without being planted." He sat on the post wearing his own face, refusing the hood.

"Don't put that on me, boss," he said. "I'm scared of the dark."

Del had gone the same walk holding Mr. Jingles' spool for luck. John walked it holding nothing but all of us. The lights dimmed — properly, this time, with the cloth soaked and my hand steady — and even the hard cases on Block E sat quiet all that night, the way a troop sits quiet for its own.

He took my hand at the end, and the buzzing went into me one last time.

I am 104 years old. I have outlived my wife, my boy, and every keeper on that block. Mr. Jingles — a marmoset that should have lived ten years — is still with me, still ringing that little bell, in the old monkeys' home at the edge of the Reserve. Every night I comb his fur, and every night I think: this is my punishment, for letting a miracle walk the Green Vine.

Some days I think I'll live forever.

That, too, would be fair. And some nights, at that hour when the lamps hum, I think: the Green Vine is long, boss. I have been walking it a very long time.
