# The Scarlet Lemur

*A parody of "The Scarlet Letter"*

The colony of the Boston Banyan was a gray-furred, gray-eyed, gray-hearted place, and its magistrates liked it that way. On a morning of early fruit-fall, Hester Prynne, a young ring-tailed lemur, was led out of the prison-tree and set upon the scaffold-stump in the middle of the marketplace boughs, her kit — a small, staring, beautiful thing — at her breast.

Upon her bosom, sewn in scarlet banana-fiber and gorgeous with forbidden embroidery, was a great letter A. The magistrates had ordered it plain. Hester had stitched it into a flower-fire, which was her first offense and her whole defense.

"Speak!" cried Governor Bellowingham from the balcony above. "Name the father of thy kit, and thy letter shall come off!"

"Never," said Hester steadily. "The kit will seek a heavenly Father. She shall never know an earthly one."

The young minister, the Reverend Arthur Dimmesbranch — pale as unripe fruit — was pressed to urge her. "Speak, sister," he whispered. "Take this shame off thee—" but his voice failed, and the colony thought it eloquence.

In the crowd stood a small hunched traveler just come from the far forests: an aye-aye learned in roots and simples, with one impossibly long finger. He looked at the scaffold, at Hester, at the kit — and did not cry out. Later, in the prison-hollow, he came to her alone.

"I am thy husband, promised long ago, come two years too late," he said. "I am ruined, thou art shamed; we are quits. Keep my secret. The male is mine to find — not the stump's."

He called himself Roger Chillingsworth, and went to work.

The colony would have starved her, but Hester fed it. Her needle — the one paw in the colony that could make a fig-leaf sit like silk — dressed the magistrates themselves; she nursed the sick bough by bough; and when the poor had worn her charity threadbare, they began to say the letter meant something else. "She is our sister *Able*," said the poor. "She is the only lemur among us who can." The magistrates frowned at this, but the magistrates had never embroidered anything in their lives.

The kit, Pearl, grew wild as a wind-fig, pelting her mother's letter with seed-shells and asking, always asking: "What does the letter mean, Mother? Why do you wear it?"

And Hester answered: "The wags of the bazaar say it stands for *Aye-Aye*, child. Ask no more."

At the Governor's own tree, where Hester came to sew a burial-robe, the magistrates pounced. The kit was unsubduable, uncatechized, wild. "The colony cannot answer for such a kit," said Governor Bellowingham. "She shall be taken from thee and schooled gray."

"I will not lose her!" cried Hester, with such a fire that the gray council drew back — and it was the pale minister who settled it. Pearl, set the test-question, "Who made thee, child?", answered promptly, "My mother picked me off the wild fig-tree," and the council groaned — but Dimmesbranch said, with a strange smile: "The kit hath her mother's letter for a tutor, and it hath taught her truth of a kind. She is the letter, living. God give the mother strength to keep both." And they let it be.

Dimmesbranch pined; Chillingsworth tended him, moved into his hollow, grooming the young minister's fur daily with that terrible finger, tapping and tapping, reading the pelt like a map, growing stronger as the minister grew gray. The colony blessed the kind old physician; the minister felt the finger on his scalp and could not name why he shuddered.

One midnight, Dimmesbranch could not sleep, and slipped down to the marketplace alone, and stood on the scaffold-stump where Hester had stood, and tried to cry out, and whispered. By chance — by providence — Hester and Pearl passed, coming small and late from the deathbed of Governor Bellowingham; and the three stood together on the stump, hand in hand, while the sleeping colony never knew; and Pearl asked, "Wilt thou stand here with us tomorrow, at noon, before them all?" And the minister answered, "Not now, child; at the great day, at the last." Then the sky opened, and a meteor burned a great crooked A across the dark, and the colony, waking, read it ANGEL, for the dead governor; and the minister, looking up, read it otherwise, and Chillingsworth, at the edge of the square, looked up and read it best of all.

At last Hester met the minister in the forest, far from any branch of the colony, and told him what his physician was. He sank against the trunk. "I have no strength left to run."

"Then I will lend thee mine," said Hester. "Take it off."

She unpinned the letter, let it fall among the mosses, shook loose her tail-fur, and the sun fell on her like fruit-fall.

"Thou must call me no more Hester Prynne," she said. "I am thy Hester. There is a barge — across the river — to the old groves of the free. We three shall sail."

For one afternoon, the plan swung. Pearl danced ahead of them along the path. Only at the brook did she stop, and stand on the far bank, small and stern, pointing at her mother's bosom, crying that she would not cross to a mother she did not know. Hester looked at the fallen letter, at Pearl, at the minister — and pinned the scarlet A back over her heart, and paid, with that motion, for every hour of joy she had promised herself. Pearl kissed the letter, and crossed, and took the minister's paw of her own accord.

But the barge-captain, cheerful with secrets, told her the news as she paid: the old physician had booked passage too — a friend of the minister's, he said, traveling in the same party. Chillingsworth knew everything; Chillingsworth was coming; there was no old grove far enough.

Then came Election Day, and Dimmesbranch preached to the whole colony so that even the magistrates wept into their gray fur. And when it was done, he came down from the high bough of the meeting-tree like a dying vine, and paused at the scaffold-stump where Hester and little Pearl stood.

"Would that I had climbed here with you three suns ago!" he cried. "God is merciful! He hath proved his mercy, in my anguish!"

And he tore aside the grey fur of his breast.

There, upon his own breast, was the scarlet mark — whether printed by his own paw, or grown there by the gnawing of seven years of secret, no lemur in the colony could say. Pearl, who never yet had kissed him, at last kissed his brow. "Wilt thou stand with us now, at noon?" she asked; but the minister only smiled at her, and at Hester, and was done.

Chillingsworth withered within the year, his long finger curling like a dead vine — for the male he fed on was gone, and revenge, kept past its season, rots in the jar. He left his heaps of bananas and his roots and simples to little Pearl, the most unlikely heir in the colony; and it was said in after years that a rich and gracious lemur-lady of the old groves of the free wore no letter at all, and laughed often, and was kind to shame, in memory of two she had stood with on a stump.

And long after, one flat stone beneath the great fig carried, for two sinners buried near together, only this device:

ON A FIELD OF BARK, SABLE-DARK, THE LETTER A, BANANA-RED.
