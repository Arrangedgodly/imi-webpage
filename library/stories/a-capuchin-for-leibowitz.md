# A Capuchin for Leibowitz

*A parody of "A Canticle for Leibowitz" by Walter M. Miller Jr.*

Brother Francis Gerard, capuchin novice of the Albertian Order of Leibowitz, was fasting in the desert when the old bonobo spoke from the rocks.

"There's food if you look for it," said the voice.

Francis looked. There was no bonobo, only a footprint — or the mark of a very old paw — and a slab of ancient stone, and under the slab, a rusted door.

*Fiat voluntas tua,* whispered Francis, and hauled.

It was a fallout shelter. That word, *fallout*, was holy and terrible in those days, six centuries after the Flame Deluge, when the Simple Simons had burned every book, hung every engineer, and plucked every learned monkey bald for the sin of having built the fire. Leibowitz himself — engineer, monk, martyr — had died for it, and his Order had one holy duty: copy, hide, save.

In the shelter, in a strongbox, Francis found the relics of the Beatus himself: a lock of fur, a toolbox, and a folded sheet of blue vellum covered in fine arcane lines and a diagrammatic grail of numbers. It was, though Francis did not know it, a circuit drawing of the Peel Sequencer, Mark II.

Also a shopping list: *pound of dried 'nanas, six coconuts, can of evaporated milk.*

Francis bore it to the abbey in triumph, and the abbot — abbots being abbots — gave him penance for presumption. Then, monks being monks, they gave him twenty-five years to illuminate it. Francis worked the rest of his life on that page: gold leaf and lapis, the sacred 'nanas radiating like a halo, the milk can enthroned in a border of monkeys. Copies went to New Rome. It was said the Holy Father himself had smiled.

"He isn't canonized, you know," the old copyist warned. "The Beatus is only blessed. Not a saint yet."

"He is to me," said Francis, and went back to his leaf.

The old bonobo came back, in his season, to the abbey gate — the same fur, the same gait, surely six hundred years the same — and asked each generation of gatekeepers the same question: "Is it time?" The brothers logged him as a pilgrim, a hermit, or a sunstroke. Francis, who met him twice, logged him in his heart as *possibly* the Beatus, and said so at his canonization testimony in New Rome, which was one reason the process took so long. On the road home, at long last a full brother, Francis was robbed of his life by the painted young of the wild packs — they wanted his fur, not his manuscript, and left the manuscript, which was the one judgment of value the age had left in it.

*Fiat lux,* say the brothers at that hour of the office, and centuries obliged.

Centuries go. That is their one trick.

Twelve hundred years after the Deluge, the world remembered how to read the blueprints, and the abbey of the Capuchin stood where it always had, a lantern of monkey memory in the desert — a lantern now in more than metaphor, for the brothers had coaxed a dynamo out of the Memorabilia, and the abbey blazed with electric light of a night, to the scandal of pilgrims and the delight of the novices. Thon Taddeo Pfardentrott, the greatest scholar of the age, came with an armed escort from the court of Hannegan of the Wolf Pack — the wolf who wanted the abbey's knowledge outfitted for war, and the abbey's independence fitted for a collar — to study the Memorabilia, a thousand years of copied peels.

He came suspicious, as his century had taught him to be. The hoax theory was fashionable in the colleges: that the Church had forged the Memorabilia to claim priority over the ancient wisdom. Taddeo's scholars found a skeleton of the blessed martyr's age hanging in the abbey's own charnel and asked, delicately, whether it might be *two* monkeys, or none, or a legend with a collar.

"You cannot split a saint to fit a theory," said the abbot. "The Church tried splitting heresies. It only ever got more saints."

The Poet Sirrah, who wore bells because the age had no use for poets and he wanted it to hear him coming, summed up the visit in a couplet that got him exiled from three courts: "Nothing was lost, and everything was saved, and now the wolves would like it back, engraved." He died in the border riots before the year was out, and his bells stopped, and nobody rang anything like them again for six hundred years.

Taddeo left shaken, having discovered the most alarming fact in scholarship: nothing had been lost, and his own cleverness had grandfathers.

"The knowledge was saved," he said at the gate. "Whether the monkey was saved with it — that part wasn't in the vault." He hesitated, looking back at the dynamo's glow. "Hannegan will have it copied regardless. I would rather it had been copied for me."

"But it was your grandfathers," said the abbot, gently, "who burned the originals."

*Fiat voluntas tua.* The wolf's historians wrote it the other way around, as victors' historians do, and the second Flame Deluge came as it had always been going to, in under an hour, on the goodwill of everybody's weapons.

The abbey, which had prepared six hundred years for exactly one afternoon, did what it had been born to do: it opened its gates to the burned and the sickened, and it wound the cranes for the star.

In the last days, the government set up its Mercy Camps, and a decree went out that the burned and the sickened were to be carried to the coast for their own good. Abbot Zerchi forbade it from the chapel steps, in the last sermon anyone in that country heard:

"It was not the monkey that burned the world! Not the hunger, not the fur, not the troop! Every monkey alive has carried the fire and the kindness in the same two hands since the first of us picked up the first stone and thought — my God — *I could do anything with this.* We are not fallen animals, my children. We are risen ones, and that has always been the problem! Choose the stars. If you must burn, burn climbing!"

Mrs. Grale heard the sermon on her tomato cart, and was in the plaza when the sky came down. She was the tomato-peddler born with two heads, one her own and one, veiled and sleeping all her sixty years, that the doctors had named Rachel. They dug Mrs. Grale out of the abbey rubble alive. And under the dust, for the first time in the history of the world, the second veil stirred, and Rachel opened her eyes — not simple, as everyone had assumed, but quiet, and awake, and looking straight at the dying old abbot as if she had been waiting all her life for exactly this appointment. She brought him water. She would not be evacuated. When the last rocket stood on its cranes, she was still there, kneeling in the tomato leaves, holding the paw of anybody who needed one held, which was everybody; and the brothers agreed she was neither more nor less than innocent, which is more than can be said of any of the rest of us.

For they had, as it happened, a star.

*Fiat Voluntas Tua,* said the brothers of the Quo Peregrinatur, and carried the Memorabilia up the scaffold in crates, and the green flame of the age's last rocket went up from the desert with the Capuchin aboard — copyists, relics, a gardener, and the illuminated shopping list of a saint — bearing, against all odds, the whole silly precious memory of the monkeys, outward.

The last to board was a young novice who was afraid.

"Listen," said the old father, buckling him in. "Listen: are you breathing just a little, and calling it a life? Then climb. That's the whole order. Climb."
