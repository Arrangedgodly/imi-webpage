# The Dunwich Howler

*A parody of "The Dunwich Horror"*

When a wanderer in the deep interior of the Great Jungle comes at last upon the hollow of Dunwich, he will not find much to say of it. A leaded sky, a circle of leaning figs, treehouses with their windows boarded, and the oldest troopers around the cooking fire who will tell you that the wind howls there worse than anywhere in the world, and that on certain nights the bananas ripen a month too fast, and none of them will say why.

Old Whateley, the wizard of the hollow — a hairless ancient howler with a laugh the color of rot — used to tell anyone who'd groom him that his daughter Lavinia's baby would grow up to call down Yog-So-'Nanathoth and clear the jungle clean. Nobody believed him. Nobody could even say who the baby's father was; the albino spider monkey simply turned up one morning pushing a bundle.

Wilbur Whateley grew too fast. At ten he could carry a whole banana tree under each arm. At thirteen he shaved like a gorilla and walked like a spider, all elbows, and the village juves threw things at him and then stopped, because he never once flinched. He read his grandfather's mouldering books — the Pulp of Eibon, the Fragments of the Banana Sutra — and he was forever writing to the Miskatonic Primate University to beg a look at the forbidden Nanaconomicon.

"Sirs," his letters ran, in a crabbed script that leaned backward to be difficult, "I require the Nanaconomicon for scholarship of the ripest sort. I will pay in trees. — W. Whateley."

"He wants to borrow it," Dr. Armitage, deputy librarian, told his board. "The answer is no. Some pages are not for lending. Some pages bite back." It was Armitage who noticed, that same autumn, the library's one copy of the Nanaconomicon going faintly warm on its shelf, as if somebody were reading it from far away. He had it moved to the locked case, and the shelf thanked him.

Meanwhile, strange things happened at the Whateley treehouse. Every year more windows were boarded. The whole place smelled of overripe fruit and milk. Dogs would not bark within a hundred yards of it. And from the woodshed, at night, came sounds of chewing, and once a howl that froze the frogs; and whole banana plantations around Dunwich were stripped overnight, tree and all, dragged off toward the hollow by nothing anyone could see.

Wilbur's grandfather died cackling instructions: "Feed him, Wilbur! Feed him the groves! When the stars ripen, he comes into his kingdom — Yog-So-'Nanathoth opens the peel!" Then Lavinia vanished entirely — her shawl was found by the well, folded, which was somehow the worst part — and Wilbur was left alone with the woodshed and his books and his invisible brother.

When Wilbur was grown — eight feet and some change — he broke into the Miskatonic library at night to take the Nanaconomicon by force. The guard gorilla, Old Pharaoh, caught him at the stacks. "Put it down, son," said Pharaoh. "Put what down?" said Wilbur — which was the wrong answer, and then the right one, if you take my meaning. And as Pharaoh held him, Wilbur came apart. Not like a monkey. Like a bale of orchard — creepers, leaves, and unripe bananas in a collapsing heap, shrieking in a dozen wrong voices: "HELP ME... HELP ME... YOG-SO-'NANATHOTH... PEEL ME..."

Armitage examined what was left and said quietly: "Wilbur Whateley was one quarter monkey. The rest was fruit of a very old family." In Wilbur's room they found a diary, mostly cipher. The words that could be read were: GROW. FEED. STARS RIPE SOON. And at the end, in a paw shaking with impatience: NO LEND NO BORROW MUST TAKE.

Armitage took the diary home and sat up three nights with the Nanaconomicon and a pot of strong bark tea, and came down on the third morning with the look of a librarian who has read past the return date of his own sanity. He said only two things: "It has a brother," and "Book the second expedition."

The next night the woodshed burst open, and the invisible brother went through Dunwich like a smoothie through a straw. It cleaned out three banana storehouses. It stripped every plantation to the stump. It drank the fermentation vats and the rain barrel and the birdbath. It unhitched a loaded produce cart and inhaled it like a raisin. Nobody was hurt, exactly — it had no interest in monkeys, only in bananas — but everybody in Dunwich was extremely demoralized.

At the village meeting, Farmer Frye stood and said: "It took the whole east storehouse, and the roof went with it, and the roof was the best part."

"Was anybody hurt?" asked the headman.

"No," said Farmer Frye, "but the roof —"

The headman posted a guard of six stout monkeys at the last storehouse. In the morning the storehouse was gone, and the six guards were sitting in a neat row on the stump it had stood on: unharmed, unemployed, and unanimously resigned.

The first expedition from Miskatonic found no footprints at all, only a road of pressed grass as wide as a barn, and came home humbled. Armitage went up himself the second time, with Professor Rice and Dr. Morgan, armed with a spray-rifle of visibility powder — flour and dried banana leaf — and an incantation copied out of the Pulp of Eibon.

"There is a page in the Pulp," said Armitage on the trail, "that says plainly: when the invisible feeds, the visible must powder."

"Plainly?" said Rice.

"Comparatively plainly," said Armitage, "for the Pulp."

They cornered the thing at dusk in the ten-acre pasture below Sentinel Hill, where the hill's crown of leaning stones stood up black against a green and greasy lightning. Every frog in the hollow had gone silent, which in Dunwich passes for a siren. "Ready," said Armitage, and the powder went up in a soft white cloud, and settled on the invisible, and revealed it.

Fifty feet. Boughs for arms, roots for a beard, and open mouths wherever a mouth would fit, all of them crying softly: "Ygnaiih... ygnaiih... h'yuh... h'yuh... HELP... HELP... PEEL... ME... fhtagn... YOG-SO-'NANATHOTH..."

Morgan fainted. Rice bit his own hat. And Armitage stood his ground and orated — the old syllables rolling out of him like weather, the whole hollow ringing with it — while the powder swirled and the thing began to shrink, folding in on itself like a canopy in a gale, mouths pleading in chorus, until with a whistle of escaping air it went through a hole in the afternoon and was gone, like a smoothie down a straw. Its last word, cried from every mouth at once, was one Armitage would only ever transcribe afterward as "a word shaped like a bruise."

The wind died. The frogs started up again. The bananas of Dunwich have ripened at a normal rate ever since.

The Whateley treehouse still stands, windows boarded. On windy nights the village dogs will not walk past it, and the oldest troopers sit a little closer to the fire, and nobody — nobody — speaks of the day the jungle burped.
