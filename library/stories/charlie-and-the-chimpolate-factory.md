# Charlie and the Chimpolate Factory

*A parody of "Charlie and the Chocolate Factory"*

In the jungle's edge, in a hut hardly bigger than a nest box, lived Charlie Bonobo with his mother and father and all four grandparents, who were so old and knotted they stayed in one bed all day, heads at one end, feet at the other, like old vines in a basket.

There was never quite enough food. Supper was banana-peel soup, and on birthdays, one banana split seven ways. But there was one luxury nobody could take away: from the great grey factory on the hill came the smell of chimpolate, morning, noon, and night, and Charlie stood in the doorway and drank the smell like soup.

Only Grandpa Jo got truly excited. "Charlie," he would whisper, "did I ever tell you about Willy Wonkatang? The most amazing, the most fantastic, the most extraordinary chimpolatier the world has ever known! He built a palace of chimpolate for a rajah once — and the foolish prince wouldn't eat it, and it all melted in the sun! Chimpolate waterfalls, Charlie. Chimpolate rivers!"

"But why is the factory shut, Grandpa?"

"Because of spies, Charlie. Other chimpolatiers sent men in to steal his secrets. So one day — Poof! — the gates closed. No worker has ever gone in or out since. But the chimpolate still comes!"

Then came the announcement, bawled by every parrot in the jungle: FIVE GOLDEN TICKETS, hidden in five ordinary bars of Wonkatang chimpolate! The five finders would spend a whole day inside the factory — and one would receive a prize beyond imagining!

The first was found by Augustus Swoop, a gorilla so fat he had grooming done in shifts. "I eat the bars," he explained, "because it is the only way to be sure." The second went to Veruca Salt, a spoiled macaque whose father ordered a million bars unskinned by an army of hired helpers. The third to Violet Beauregarde, a gum-chomping chimp who had chewed the same lump three months running and parked it behind her ear at mealtimes. The fourth to Mike Teavee, a bossy young baboon welded to the tele-vine, who wanted nothing inside the factory except a closer look at the tele-vine.

And the fifth — the fifth Charlie found, on a dropped gold coin, in the very last Whipple-Scrumptious Bananadream bar a broken heart could hope for. (The first bar he'd bought held only chimpolate, which he shared around the great bed, and Grandma Georgina declared it the finest thing to happen to her since her teeth.)

"Run home, little fellow," said the shopkeeper, as strangers offered him fifty coconuts for it. "Run, and don't look at anybody!"

Grandpa Jo got out of bed for the first time in twenty years — which very nearly became a second emergency — and appointed himself Grandfather-in-Chief.

The gates opened at nine. Smoke curled. Whistles shrieked. And there in the doorway, leaping and twirling in a plum-purple tailcoat, was an orangutan with fiery eyes and a goatee like forest moss.

"Welcome, my dear little chimps!" cried Willy Wonkatang. "This way! Do come in! It is all monkey business from here!"

Inside, they gasped. A valley of jungle under glass, and through the middle of it a great brown river of melted chimpolate, churned by a waterfall.

"No other factory in the world," said Wonkatang, "mixes its chimpolate by waterfall! Waterfall chimpolate is chimpolate whipped by nature herself. And here — my workers!"

Up from the riverbank popped a troop of knee-high monkeys in dungarees: the Oompa Chimpas, who waved politely and went back to licking the walls, which were, after all, chimpolate.

"Don't!" cried Wonkatang, as Augustus Swoop leaned far out over the river with his mouth open. "The river must never be touched by human hands — paws!" But Augustus was already in, already drinking the river dry in patches, and already shooting up a great glass pipe toward the fudge room, waving his fists in outrage at the suction.

*Oompa choompa chimpa-dee-doo, / We've got a lesson or two for you. / Who greeds the river, greeds it whole, / comes up the pipe and pays the toll.*

"Where does that pipe GO?" shrieked Mrs. Swoop.

"To the fudge room, madam, by way of the strawberry-chimpolate churn. He'll be squeezed, rolled, and very possibly fudge-dipped. Do stop crying — it's all quite educational."

They boarded a pink paddle-boat rowed by sixty Oompa Chimpas and sailed down the chimpolate river to the Inventing Room, a cavern of hissing cauldrons and machines that went BLOOP for no visible reason. There Wonkatang handed each child a small striped ball. "Everlasting Bananastoppers! One lick and your tongue believes it is at a banana feast forever. Keep them safe. Do not give them away."

Violet Beauregarde was not listening. Violet had found a stick of untested gum on the workbench — three-course gum, said the label: banana soup, roast figs with vine syrup, banana-split.

"See here," said Wonkatang, "that gum is not ready. The pudding course comes out wrong."

"I'm the world chimpion chewer," said Violet. "I once chewed a chimp's tail by accident and finished my sentence anyway."

She reached the pudding course — swelled blue, swelled banana-shaped, swelled tremendous — and was rolled off to the juicing room by anxious Oompa Chimpas while her mother assured the room that her daughter was, whatever the evidence, a very good chewer.

*Oompa choompa chimpa-dee-day, / gum-chewing chimps get carried away. / She wouldn't take hints, she wouldn't take rest, / so now she's a banana and needs to be pressed.*

In the Nut-Sorting Room sat a hundred specially trained squirrels on little stools, cracking nuts.

"Squirrels," explained Wonkatang, "and only squirrels, can tell a good nut from a bad one. A bad nut goes down the chute."

"I'll take a squirrel," announced Veruca Salt. "I want one NOW. Daddy, buy me that squirrel, and that chute, and this whole room, and the one next door, in case I want two."

"Don't you dare," said Wonkatang.

"Daddy! DADDY!"

"The squirrels will want to test her," Wonkatang sighed, as Veruca plunged both hands into the bin and was tapped, judged, and chute-declared — quite rightly — a bad banana. Down she went, and her father after her, still apologizing for her.

*Oompa choompa chimpa-dee-doo, / a chimp who demands gets her overdue. / Spoiled and rotten, through and through — / the chute has spoken. Quite on cue.*

Last came the Television-Chimpolate Room, which held one enormous camera pointed at one enormous bar.

"I send chimpolate through the air in a million tiny pieces," said Wonkatang proudly. "Room-sized bars in, television-sized bars out!"

"Why only bars?" said Mike Teavee. "Why not monkeys?" And he leapt onto the camera platform before anyone could grab his tail. FLASH. And on the family tele-vine across the jungle, six astonished relatives watched a baboon no bigger than a thumb put his tiny paws on his tiny hips.

"Don't touch the dials!" bellowed Wonkatang. "He'll come out stretched!" Mike came out thin as a liana and had to be sent to the chewing-gum stretching machine to be pulled back toward size, and his father had to carry him home in a matchbox, which he rather enjoyed, being at last the biggest thing on the screen.

"Every one of them," sighed Wonkatang, "a bad banana in the bunch."

Then only Charlie was left — and Wonkatang turned on him a face gone suddenly cold.

"Well, Charlie Bonobo. I saw you in the Fizzy Lifting Bananaade room. You drank, you floated, you burped your way down, and you broke every rule I set. So: you get nothing. You lose! Good day, sir!"

Charlie's ears drooped. He laid his Everlasting Bananastopper gently on the desk. "I'm sorry, sir. It was yours anyway."

He was nearly at the door when Wonkatang's voice cracked like a whip of joy. "Charlie — you WON! Don't you see? The whole day was a test, and the Bananastopper the final exam! Augustus, Violet, Veruca, Mike — bad bananas, one and all, and their parents, heaven help the jungle, worse. But you gave back the only treasure you had. Come with me!"

They rose through the roof in the Great Glass Liana-vator, up and out and over the jungle, and Wonkatang spread his arms over his smoking, churning, sweet-smelling kingdom.

"It's all yours, Charlie. The whole factory. I needed a good, sensible, loving little monkey to leave it to — I'm not as young as I look, and I look nearly a hundred."

"Me?"

"You. Well done, Charlie. And Charlie — don't forget what happened to the monkey who suddenly got everything he always wanted."

"What?" asked Charlie.

"He lived happily ever after."
