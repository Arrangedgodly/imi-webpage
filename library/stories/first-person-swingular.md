# First Person Swingular

*A parody of "First Person Singular"*

These days I swing alone. Swinging alone is the one thing I do exceptionally well, the way some monkeys are good at chess or at remembering debts, and so what follows is told in first person swingular — one monkey, one branch, no spotter, past imperfect.

Whether all of it happened to me, I honestly can't say. Memory is a troop of one, chattering in the dark, and every monkey in it claims to be me.

—

When I was eighteen, in my last year of school, a boy I barely knew stopped me by the gate and handed me a leaflet. He was a heavy-lidded colobus with the manner of a museum guard.

"Sunday," he said. "The Center of the Peel Society. You should come. It's the real thing."

The leaflet showed a banana cream pie with a slice removed where the center ought to be, and beneath it, an address across town.

I went, because I was eighteen and Sundays were otherwise unstructured. I found the street. I found the number. There was no building — only an empty lot with weeds and an old stove, and on the stove, inexplicably, one clean teacup.

I stood there a while. A neighbor leaned out and said the land had been empty since the war.

For years I told this as a joke. Now I think it may be the most practical thing anyone ever taught me: some circles have no center. You can spend your life circling the missing slice — the career, the one who left, the perfect branch — measuring the hole. Or you can admit the pie is a fake and eat the crust anyway. It's about cream puffs, and it isn't. If you don't go, you'll never know; if you do go, the only thing you learn is that you had to be there.

—

I met the Shinagawa mandrill at a mountain inn, off season, in the empty bar.

He was a small mandrill, scrupulously groomed, sitting on the far stool with a posture like a retired bank clerk. When I ordered a beer he nodded at it. "May I?" he said. "One glass. Monkeys can't drink beer, generally speaking. I am not, generally speaking, a monkey."

"How's that?"

"I was raised in a laboratory in Shinagawa. Humans taught me speech the way they teach parrots arithmetic — patiently, with snacks. Then the funding ended and I was — how shall I say — surplus to budget. I've worked inns ever since. Snow monkeys heat the baths. I pour the sake. It's a living, and I have opinions about the guests."

He drank a third of my beer in respectful silence.

"I have a confession," he said, "and you're a stranger, which is the safest congregation there is. I steal names. Not purses. Not hearts — heaven forbid, hearts are heavy and spoil quickly. Names. Three, in my life, of women I loved, in my fashion. Chikako. Mizue. Akemi." He said each one the way you'd set down a full glass. "A name, once truly held, doesn't wash out of a monkey. I stole them, and I keep them, and on bad nights I take them down and look at them. A monkey who steals names — it's the loneliest crime there is. Nobody presses charges."

"Give them back," I said. "That's my advice, and it's free."

He smiled the way a mandrill smiles, which is a lot of face at once. "And be nobody's keeper? Then what would I be?"

I left him my shoes, that trip. They fit him, he said, like an apology.

—

I wrote poems that year about the Yakult Swoopers, my baseball team, who lose with more purity than any team in either league. I have written more poems about the Swoopers than about any woman I've known, and loved them more reliably. Baseball is the art of losing beautifully at a walking pace, in clean uniforms, before a modest crowd. It prepared me for everything.

My father took me to my first game. Peanuts, warm beer, an old scoreboard. In the seventh inning a backup infielder named Ogawa hit an unexpected home run into the rain, and my father, an undemonstrative monkey, gripped my shoulder and said, "Now watch. Now watch what a Tuesday can do." When he died, decades on, I couldn't cry at the service. I cried that October, in the stands, when an outfielder caught a ball he had no right to catch, for no reason any non-fan would accept. Grief keeps its own schedule. So does the bullpen.

—

The woman in the gray dress found me last spring, at a hotel lounge where I'd gone to be sixty-eight and inconspicuous.

"You," she said, setting down her glass. She was an elegant she-orang, silvered, furious, precise. "Karuizawa. The big fig by the lake. August. You said if it rained, meet me under the fig and we'd — and I waited, and it rained all afternoon, and you never came."

"I'm sorry," I said. "I think you have the wrong monkey."

"Forty-one years," she said, "and you tell me wrong monkey." She finished her drink, and her anger, all at once, and looked suddenly very tired. "No. You're right. It doesn't matter now. I only wanted, once, to say it to somebody's face." And she went out through the revolving doors into the light, and I sat there with my heart going like a drum in a suitcase.

I have never been to Karuizawa. I have never promised anyone a fig tree. But that night I didn't sleep, and here is why: I could not prove it. Not one August of my life could I produce an alibi for. The self is not the solid trunk we pretend; it's a rope bridge, and every plank was laid by a different, earlier monkey who never filed a report.

I thought of the woman on the street, years ago, walking with a record under her arm — With the Beatles, it said, held exactly level, like a tray — while beside her an organ-grinder's monkey in a red vest held out its cap, and for one second the free monkey and the owned one looked at each other, and I, passing, could not tell you which of us was which.

So I took a train to the mountains and found the inn, and there was the Shinagawa mandrill, older, immaculate, pouring sake for nobody.

"Tell me," I said. "A woman says I broke a promise I never made. Is that possible?"

"It's not only possible," he said, delighted, getting out a second glass. "It's the norm. We are, all of us, several monkeys in one fur, taking shifts, and none of them reads the minutes of the others' meetings. Chikako might be looking for her name still, in somebody's face. Are you drinking that?"

I was. We drank it.

Somewhere there is an inn, and a bar stool, and two old monkeys drinking beer poured with ceremony, one of them keeping three names warm like coals, the other keeping, at last count, nothing — no wife, no dog, no Swoopers pennant, only this testimony:

I swing. Alone, mostly, between the branches of what I did and what I was told I did. The rope bridge holds. It has never once explained itself to me, and I have stopped asking it to, the way you stop asking a cat to apologize.

First person swingular. Present tense, though.

The tense that matters is the one you're in.
