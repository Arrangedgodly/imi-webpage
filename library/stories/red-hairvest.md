# Red Hairvest

*A parody of "Red Harvest"*

The telegram said: *Come at once. I am in trouble and can pay. — Donald Willswang, Primateville.*

I took the vine-line east. Donald Willswang, I knew by the papers, was the heir of old Elihu Willswang, the silverback who owned the timber-yards, the fig-mills, the banana exchanges, and — variously by lease, loan, or threat — most of the monkeys in them. Primateville was what Elihu called the town. The monkeys who lived in it called it Crimeateville, and they didn't smile when they said it, which tells you the kind of town it was.

I didn't get to meet Donald Willswang. I got off the vine-line at dusk and a messenger took me straight to the morgue. Somebody had let the air into him with a slingshot-ball between the shoulders. That made my client dead before payday, which in my business is not unusual, only annoying.

The coroner, a chinless chimp with a cough, wrote it up as bandits before he'd washed his paws. Donald's rooms told a cleaner story. Floorboards pried up and nailed back too neatly; and in the cold ash of the grate, corner-ends of burnt ledger pages, figures still legible. Donald had been keeping books on the town that kept books on everybody. That, and not bandits, had let the air into him.

Old Elihu called me up to the big treehouse that night. He was a heavy old ape with fur gone ash-grey and eyes like a bill collector's.

"I hired the gunmen once," he admitted, "to break the fruit-haulers' union. Now they've unionized themselves. Chief Noo-nan runs the police for whoever feeds him. Max 'Whisper' Tailor runs the fig-houses. Lew Shrike runs the protection. Pete the Proboscis runs the docks. I can't get rid of them. Clean up my town."

"Why me?"

"Donald trusted you. And you're the only mandrill in the business with a face like that." I let that go. A mandrill's face — blue cheeks, crimson muzzle — is the only red flag that walks into a room announcing itself, and it's saved my tail more than once.

I started where the bullet had started: the Pink Fig, Whisper's fig-house, where the dice ran cold and the whiskies ran warm. Whisper was a grey gibbon with a voice you had to lean into, like wind in a chimney. "Everybody in this town is looking for the young fellow's killer," he told me, dealing himself a fig. "That's the beauty of Crimeateville, shamus. Everybody's looking. Nobody finds. Take my advice and take the vine-line back west." I told him I liked the climate. He smiled like a crack in a teacup.

I went to work the way a barber works: clip by clip. I told Noo-nan that Whisper was skimming his cut, and Noo-nan's constables raided the Pink Fig with warrants that spelled everything wrong but hit everything right. I told Whisper that Shrike had tipped the raid, and Whisper's boys burned Shrike's protection books in three different alleys for emphasis. I told Pete the Proboscis that Noo-nan was shipping fig-brandy through his docks without a taste, and the docks grew very lively that week. I was playing both ends against the middle, and the middle was me, and I kept my red face out of sight and my ears open.

Noo-nan took me in for it on the Thursday — a planted slingshot-ball in my coat, a night in his guest cage, a desk lamp in my eyes and two flat-feet asking me where I'd been the night young Willswang stopped being alive. "You're sweating me," I said, "which means somebody told you to. When you find out who, follow the tail." They turned me loose at noon; the frame had been built by monkeys who thought a mandrill's eyes were blue all the time.

After the sweat-box I did my penance on the docks, counting crates for Pete the Proboscis. His nose opened doors all over the waterfront; the rest of him followed as an afterthought. I watched one of Noo-nan's launches take on forty crates stenciled SUN-DRIED FIGS that sloshed, and sat on a bollard chewing a stem until I had signatures enough to hang three monkeys from a single vine.

They caught up with me properly on the fourth night — four of Shrike's gorillas in a mill doorway, and a sack over my head. We had a conversation. I use the word generously. When I came around, Dinah Brand — Shrike's girl, a languid spider monkey with a gift for keeping her balance while everything fell — was daubing my muzzle with fig-leaf juice.

"They think you know who killed the young one," she said. "Do you?"

"Yeah," I said, spitting peel. "It was Slug, one of Whisper's peelers, gone nervous on the job — Donald had the ledger-books of the whole fig-haul racket, and the racket couldn't afford a sentimental heir. Half of Crimeateville's boss monkeys have a reason, Dinah. I aim to see them compare notes."

She looked at me the way you look at weather you don't trust. "You're playing both ends against the middle," she said, "and one of these days you'll notice the middle is you."

I asked her why she was patching me up instead of letting me leak.

"Donald liked me, you know," she said, looking at nothing in particular. "He was the only monkey in this town who ever asked me a question he didn't already know the price of. Put that in your little book, shamus, under D." I told her she'd make somebody a lovely wife, and she said not to say that where the wrong monkeys could hear it, and meant it.

So I arranged it. I got each boss told, privately, that a peace conference was set at the Old Mill — and that the other bosses were coming armed. I mailed each of them a copy of the evidence the others held, care of each other. Then I sat in a fig tree across the creek with old Elihu and a bag of bananas, and we watched the fireworks. I never heard such a troop meeting. Whisper came in the front and Shrike came in the back and each had told his monkeys the other was there to bushwhack, so they obliged. Pete the Proboscis tried the window and got stuck in it. When it was over, the gangs had pruned themselves, Slug had traded silence for a cell, Noo-nan had been suspended by the grand jury from his own flagpole, and the honest fruit-haulers were hauling fruit.

I don't brag about the mill. Two monkeys who just hauled fruit stopped hauling anything, ever, and that soup boiled on my fire. I set the pot on; I'll carry the scald.

Elihu wanted to shake my paw. "You've harvested them," he said, almost admiring. "A red harvest."

"Red hairvest," I said, and opened my fist. It held a tuft of fur from every crook I'd clipped — crimson from one, grey from another, black, brown, white. "I take a little of each. It's how I remember my crops."

"Primateville thanks you," he began, and I told him what I'd told the grand jury that morning: that the monkey who had hired the first gangster, years back, to break the union, sat in the biggest treehouse in town, and the ledger proved it, and I'd keep one tuft empty until he paid the fruit-haulers back every banana plus interest.

He paid. They mostly do, when the alternative is printed.

I took the vine-line west at dawn. Crimeateville was calling itself Primateville again by the following spring. I never went back. A mandrill only has so much red to give.
