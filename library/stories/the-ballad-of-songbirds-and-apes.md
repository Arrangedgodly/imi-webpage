# The Ballad of Songbirds and Apes

*A parody of "The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes"*

Coriolanus Gnaw was the handsomest young snow monkey in the Capitol academy, and the poorest, and only one of those facts was public.

So when the tenth annual Hunger Apes rolled around and each student was assigned a tribute to mentor, Coriolanus drew the worst card in the deck: a scrawny she-ape from District 12 named Lucy Gray Banana-Baird, a traveling singer with no chance at all.

"She's a songbird in a pit of adders," his cousin lamented.

"She's my only shot at the prize money," said Coriolanus, who had learned early that sentiment was something you fed to others while you ate the winnings.

Before the games came the zoo, where the tributes were displayed to the public in a paddock, fed irregularly, and expected to inspire confidence. Dean Highbottom, head of the academy and inventor of the Hunger Apes in a lazier, drunker decade, surveyed the enclosure with ancient regret. "I call it the price of progress," he said, and drank, "and I bill generously." Coriolanus brought Lucy Gray's troop food from his own rations, telling himself it was strategy. Two tributes died of dysentery and neglect before the games even began, and the Capitol called this "natural attrition," the way the Capitol called everything it did something else.

But Lucy Gray did not perform like a dead ape walking. At the opening ceremonies, while the other tributes snarled or shrank, she took the microphone and sang:

*"There's a plain beyond the fence, where the wild bananas grow — you can clip my wings, old Capitol, but you'll never clip my soul."*

The Capitol lost its collective mind. Dr. Growl, the Head Gamemaker, an ancient mandrill with a muzzle like a bruised sunset, watched from her glass-tanked lab and said, "Now that, young Gnaw, is what control is really about. Not cages. Attention." Overnight the bookmakers had Lucy Gray at a hundred to one, the academy students bet their allowances against her, and her little cousin Maude Ivory — who could remember any song after one hearing, a dangerous talent in a tyranny — was immortal on every screen in the city.

Then rebels bombed the arena, and Coriolanus ran into the smoke and carried Lucy Gray out, which everyone agreed was heroic. In the paddock chaos afterward, a small desperate tribute named Bobbin came at him with a shiv, and Coriolanus stopped him with a plank of timber and a worse idea. Self-defense, he told himself. He was getting excellent at the telling.

The arena that year was an old zoo enclosure — concrete, broken swings, rusted climbing bars. The gamemakers dropped no food, for Dr. Growl believed hunger revealed character. What it mostly revealed was screaming.

But Lucy Gray had grown up on the road with the Covey, her band of musical vagabonds, sleeping in trees and charming strangers, and she vanished into the enclosure's drainage tunnels like a rumor. Days passed. The careers picked each other off above while she sang mockingmacaque songs in the dark and stole supplies through the grates.

Then Dr. Growl released the snakes.

A hundred of them, poured into the tunnels — the same snakes she'd been breeding in a tank in her lab, feeding by hand. "What better lesson," she mused, "than letting my pets meet the district's?"

Coriolanus, watching with his stomach in knots, did something unforgivably sentimental: Lucy Gray's scent was already on his handkerchief — she'd worn it as a headband during a studio interview — and he visited Dr. Growl's lab and with the same clever paws introduced that handkerchief to the snake tank. Just a scent. Just enough.

When the snakes found Lucy Gray coiled in a pipe, they tasted the air, found her familiar, and slid past her like commuters. She crawled out through the feeding grate an hour later, the last tribute alive, singing the anthem's chorus in victory.

Coriolanus won the prize — and then lost it. The handkerchief came out in the inquiry, sniffed by suspicious officials, argued over by suspicious lawyers, and he was shipped to District 12 as a Peacekeeper baboon-in-training with a medal he couldn't sell and a grudge he could. He found the only thing that made exile bearable: Lucy Gray, singing in the meadow by the lake where the forbidden ones danced.

"Back in the Capitol, I thought you were my lucky charm," he told her.

"I thought you were mine," said Lucy Gray. "Turns out we're just two apes who got lucky."

They planned to run away together, past the fence, out to the wild plain where the bananas grew untamed, where the mockingmacaques would learn her songs and no gamemaker would ever own a cage. But the wild had rules of its own, and the world kept forcing choices on him. His friend Sejanus, homesick and foolish, had gone native with the district's outlaws, and Coriolanus — presented with an opportunity to report him, the jabberapes helpfully repeating every word Sejanus had said in the trees — chose his own future over his friend's life. He told himself it was pragmatism. He told himself Sejanus would have been caught anyway. He stood at attention at dawn and learned a new trick: watching your own choices be buried without changing your expression.

The lake country held one more secret. A cache of contraband rifles, a magistrate's daughter named Mayfair Lip, and Billy Tapir, Lucy Gray's old bandmate — and one loud night, a rifle did what rifles do. Lucy Gray had seen Coriolanus's face when it happened. And she had heard, in the way singers hear things, about a tribute named Bobbin.

On the day of the escape, everything collapsed. A hanging. A murdered Peacekeeper. And in the rain-forest dusk, a loaded rifle that could be explained two ways.

She walked him toward the lake anyway, past the fence, through the wetlands, toward where the rifles were hidden — and the reeds held a rattle she knew about and he didn't. Something struck his ankle. He killed the snake and limped on, and began, at last, to do the arithmetic: a girl who knew all his secrets, a swamp full of silence, and only one of them planning to come back.

When Coriolanus returned to the lake clearing with the rifle and his rival in the mud behind him, Lucy Gray was gone. Only her scarf remained, snagged on a bush, and the mockingmacaques in the branches sang her song back at him in her exact voice:

*"You'll never clip my soul..."*

He searched the tree line for hours, calling her name, then stopped calling, then started telling himself things again: that she'd betrayed him, that she'd have ruined him, that she was probably dead in the wetlands — that, in any case, he was free.

A snow monkey lands on top, his grandmother used to say. Always. Somehow.

He took to growing roses after that. Anything beautiful, he decided, should double as a warning. He walked out of District 12 with dry eyes and a new understanding of what he wanted: not to escape the Capitol's cage, but to hold the key. Sixty-some years later, a president would look at a girl with a bow and see, in her defiance, the exact shape of a scarf snagged on a bush — President Coriolanus Gnaw, reaching for the key with both paws.
