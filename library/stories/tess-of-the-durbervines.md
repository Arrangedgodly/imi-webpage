# Tess of the d'Urbervines

*A parody of "Tess of the d'Urbervines"*

On an evening in May, in the Vale of Blackmoor, the troop of Marlott walked abroad in white fur and ribbons, and danced on the green under the new leaves. Among them danced Tess Durbeyfield, a fine, handsome young bonobo with a bananas-and-cream muzzle. A young ape passing through with his walking-stick joined the dance for a turn — with the troop, not with her, for she was at the far end and he was in a hurry — and went on up the valley. His name was Angel Claw. Remember him. Everybody in this book who passes Tess by gets to come back and be sorry.

That same evening, a poor peddler-monkey named Jack Durbeyfield was resting by the trail when an old parrot fluttered down and greeted him as "Sir John."

"Me? Sir?" said Jack. "I'm a poor banana-higgler with a bad knee."

"You are a d'Urbervine," said the parrot solemnly. "One of the oldest ape families in all England — descended from Sir Pagan d'Urbervine, who came over with the Chimpqueror in 1066. It is also said," the parrot added, with relish, "that the d'Urbervine coach is a thing of ill omen: the monkey who sees it hears the sound of an injury done long ago, that the injured one has not done with, ha, ha."

Nothing else was needed to make Jack go bananas. He celebrated his noble blood all evening with fermented sap and could not drive the family banana cart at dawn. His eldest daughter Tess went in his place, and in her sleepiness the cart collided with the mail coach, killing Prince, the family's old pack-mule.

"You might claim kin with the rich d'Urbervines at The Slopes," said the parrot, and guilt-heavy Tess went, little knowing that his family had merely bought the ancient name.

The estate's young master, Alec d'Urbervine — a dashing, smirking macaque with a curl to his tail and no curl to his conscience — took one look at her and decided to make himself useful.

"You want work picking bananas? Done," he said. "Call me Cousin Alec, and it's all done."

He was everywhere — offering her the first ripe banana of every bunch, feeding her strawberries off his own branch with his own paw, till she blushed beneath her fur. The famous wrong was done to her on a foggy night in The Chase, the ancient wood where the d'Urbervines once held their courts. Alec found her lost and weary among the trees, and what he did was nothing a gentleman does. She went home in disgrace.

A babe was born — little Sorrow — and when he sickened, Tess, fearing he would die unnamed, baptized him herself by lamplight.

"I baptize thee, Sorrow," she said, sprinkling dew from the leaves.

The child died, and the vicar hemmed and hawed about churchyards, and Tess buried him in the corner of the night-garden where the unbaptized and the forgotten go, and put on her courage like a cloak, and went to Talbothays Dairy, in the lush valley of the Great Fruits, where nobody knew her story.

There she met Angel Claw again — a young ape studying banana husbandry, walking on his principles and occasionally tripping over them — and this time he was in no hurry at all. He called her Artemis, then Demeter, then finally "Tess, my own," having been corrected. And three dairymaids — Izz, Retty, and Marian, who loved him each in her own loft — heard the banns and wept together one night in the hay, and agreed that of the four of them he had chosen the one who deserved him best, which they said in the way of the generous, and meant, and it did them no good at all.

They married on a winter's day. On the wedding night, by the fire of their new nest — an old d'Urbervine house, where the housekeeper told the coach story, and Tess shivered without knowing why — Angel confessed that he had once sown wild bananas in London. Would she forgive him?

"O Angel — I was almost glad to hear it," she cried. "It means you can forgive me! For I, too, have a history."

She had written it all to him, in fact, days before, and slid the letter under his door; and the letter had slid under the carpet, and lain there, and Angel, finding the room swept and the carpet smooth, had blessed the housekeeper and thought no more about it. So the letter was never read, and the telling of it fell to Tess herself, by the fire, with her paw in his.

She told him of Alec. And Angel Claw — the chimp of principles — turned to fur and ash before her eyes.

"The monkey I have been loving is not you," he said, "but a monkey with your shape."

He went away to Braz Nut, land of nuts. At the gate he stopped, in the last weakness of his principles, and asked Izz Huett, the dairymaid, if she would go with him instead. Izz, who had loved him since the spring, said yes — until he asked, idly, whether she could love him more than Tess did.

"No," said Izz. "Nobody could love you more than she did. She would have died for you. And I would have too, but it wouldn't have been the same." Angel went to Braz Nut alone, which was the first sensible thing his principles had done all book.

Tess went to starve at Flintcomb-Thorn, scratching mangoes from stony ground with bleeding paws. The great steam banana-press came to the farm that winter — a red tyrant of a machine, fed by an engine-man who never stopped, and Tess fed it too, fur powdered white, while the rope of fruit went in one side of her and came out the other, so to speak. And Alec returned, robed and repentant of his old sins and eager for new ones, mocking her stubbornness.

"You suffer for a monkey who has forsaken you," he said. "Why?"

Her family sickened. Her father died. The Durbeyfield leases expired, and the family was evicted, and Alec — appearing always where she was weakest — found them shelter. When Angel came limping home at last, repentant, sick, and humble, he found Tess dressed in finery at the d'Urbervine nest.

"It is too late," she said, cold as stone.

She came to him presently in the parlor, wild-eyed. "I told him what I thought of him — and the petrified banana came off the mantel in my paw." The ceremonial wedding-day banana — kept, by old custom, stone-hard on the mantelpiece of every d'Urbervine nest as a warning to brides — had done its work, and Alec d'Urbervine was retired to the great zoo in the sky. And the landlady downstairs, who alone saw the lady go, swore ever after that she heard, in the small hours, the wheels of a coach.

She fled with Angel, and they walked together, spent and strangely happy, across the great grey plain to Stonehenge, the old druid ring of standing stones. There, in the mild night, she laid herself down upon a fallen stone.

"I like it here," she murmured. "Let it be here."

They slept. And as the first grey of dawn came up over the plain, the keepers of the stones saw, moving in upon the sleeper, a file of monkeys with pikes. Justice — and the President of the Immortals — had ended their sport with Tess.

At the city of Wintoncester that day, a black banana was hoisted high upon a pole. "She is at rest," said Angel, and walked on with her little brother, toward the valley of the Great Fruits.
