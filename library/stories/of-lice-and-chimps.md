# Of Lice and Chimps

*A parody of "Of Mice and Men"*

George Milton was a little chimp with restless eyes and a temper worn smooth from constant use. Lennie Small was a gorilla of tremendous size who loved soft things — velvet moss, baby ferns, the downy fur of a rabbit — and forgot everything else.

"You forgot again," George said wearily, as they flopped down beside the jungle pool to drink. "You got that dead mouse in your pocket again, ain't you? Give it here."

"They was so little," said Lennie, miserably. "I didn't pet it hard, George. Honest I didn't."

"You killed it a-petting it, that's what. Give it here." George flung the mouse into the brush and Lennie's lip quivered, and George sighed the sigh of a chimp who has had this exact conversation at eleven different jungle pools. "Now — the works of it. We got a new grooming boss tomorrow, down in Soledad Valley. You remember where to hide if there's trouble?"

Lennie's face scrunched up with the effort of remembering. "Hide in the... hide in the..."

"The hollow tree by the big fig," George said. "Say it back."

"Hide in the hollow tree," Lennie repeated happily. "I remembered, George! I remembered!"

"And if you get in trouble like you done in Weed—"

"You won't let me tend no babies!" Lennie wailed, before George could even say it. Which shows he remembered more than he let on, because their dream was the one thing Lennie never forgot.

"Someday," George said, and Lennie's eyes lit up, because he knew this part by heart, "we're gonna have our own troop. Just us two. A little territory of our own, with a fig tree and a banana grove and a fern patch as soft as anything. And nobody can kick us out, because it'll be *ours*."

"And I get to groom the babies," Lennie said. "The little soft ones. And they won't run away, because I'll be gentle."

"Monkeys like us," said George, "is the loneliest monkeys in the jungle. We got somebody to talk to that gives a damn. But not us no more. If some ape takes your banana, I says, 'I ain't gotta share mine' — and there's the end of it."

At the ranch in Soledad Valley, the old boss was suspicious of a gorilla who wouldn't answer for himself. Slim, the head picker — a tall, slow-spoken langur who was the prince of the ranch without ever raising his voice — studied Lennie a long moment and handed him a marmoset baby from the new litter.

"Take one," said Slim. "He wants somethin' soft to tend. He's innocent as a fern."

The head grooming boss was Candy, an old orang with one hand and an ancient, half-blind marmoset on his shoulder. The boss's son, Curly, was a spiteful little baboon who hated big apes and strutted like the jungle owed him a banana. His wife — never named, always flirting, desperately lonely — drifted around the sleeping hammocks looking for someone to talk to.

"Maybe you just better go along with Curly's wife's talk," said Slim dryly. "She's a nice enough looker, but Curly's got a fist like a knot. There's no place for a female like that in a troop of pickers."

Trouble came fast. Carlson, a heavy-shouldered chimp, had been after Candy's old marmoset for days: "He ain't no good to himself. He ain't no good to you. Why'n't you let me shoot him, old thing? He won't feel a thing." And Candy, cornered by numbers and youth, looked at the wall and finally said, in a voice smaller than himself, "All right — take him."

The shot cracked in the distance. Candy lay in his hammock, stiff as a board, staring at the ceiling. And George and Lennie, in spite of themselves, talked about the little territory — the fig tree, the banana grove, the fern patch. Candy lifted his head.

"How much they want for a little territory like that?"

"You're nuts," said George. But Candy laid it out: three hundred bananas saved, fifty a month coming. "I got no troop, and my old marmoset ain't long for this world. Take me with you, and I'll ask nothing but a corner of the fern patch. I'll cook and tend the little ones. I ought to of shot that dog myself, George. I shouldn't ought to of let no stranger shoot my dog."

For one shining week, it was almost real. They talked about the fig tree at supper. Lennie memorized the fern patch. George actually picked the spot.

Curly's wife found Lennie alone in the shed that same week — but not before Curly had swung a fist at Lennie, and Lennie, terrified, caught the paw and held it until the bones creaked like green twigs. "I only meant to stop him hitting me," Lennie said mournfully, hiding the crushed paw behind his back while George made threats and excuses.

And in the grooming shed, over the body of Candy's old marmoset — Lennie had petted the pup he'd been promised just a little too hard, and it lay still in the straw, and Slim, understanding everything, quietly took the body and gave Lennie one of his own pups instead — Curly's wife drifted in, hungry for talk.

"I don' like Curly," she said. "He ain't a nice fella. Coulda been in the pictures, coulda had nice things." She laughed, hard and short. "What you got covered up there?"

"Nothin'. A pup. My pup."

She moved closer, not unkindly. "See this? Fur from my collar. Soft as anything. Go on and feel it."

Lennie stroked the soft fur. Too hard. She squirmed. He panicked and held on — the way he always held on, the way he'd held the mouse and the marmoset and Curly's paw — and when George found him, she was still, and Lennie was gone, running for the hollow tree by the big fig, running to the only place he could remember.

Candy found her, and old Candy looked at her and then at the dead pup in the straw, and said the whole of it in one croak: "I could of hoed in the garden and washed dishes for them guys." Then: "I ought to of let them strangers shoot my marmoset. I ought never to of let nobody shoot nothing of mine."

The whole ranch came hunting, Curly shrieking for vengeance, torches swinging through the vines, Slim calling quietly to George that maybe they ought to put Lennie in a cage.

George found Lennie first.

He came through the ferns quietly, and there was Lennie, crouched in the shadow of the tree, huge and terrified. "I done another bad thing," Lennie said.

"It don't matter," George said, and his voice was strange and far away. "Ain't you gonna tell me about the territory?"

Lennie's face broke open with joy. "The fig tree! The banana grove! And I get to groom the babies, George, the little soft ones —"

"Look across the river, Lennie," George said, and Lennie looked, describing the little troop that would someday be theirs while George raised the fruit-knife behind his friend's head, and brought it down, and sat in the ferns afterward and could not look at what he had done.

The searchers found him there. Slim took George by the shoulder and sat him on a log, put a gourd of nectar in his hands. "Never you mind," said Slim. "A guy got to sometimes."

But Carlson and Curly, looking at George sitting silent in the ferns, could not work it out at all.

"Now what the hell ya suppose is eatin' them two chimps?" said Carlson.

And the flies came to the ferns, and the river ran on through Soledad Valley, and somewhere downstream a fig tree bore fruit that nobody picked.
