# The Naked Bum

*A parody of "The Naked Sun"*

Banaley had never seen so much sky. Solaria had nothing *but* sky, and a mere ten thousand baboons scattered across it, each alone on an estate the size of old New Bark, tended by armies of bonobots.

He came, with the bonobot Apeivaw at his side, because a Solarian had been murdered — Rikaine Delmare, the Root Engineer, keeper of the great banana groves, dead of a blow to the skull in a household staffed by two hundred bonobots bound by the Three Laws. A bonobot cannot strike a monkey. A Solarian cannot bear to be *near* a monkey. The case was therefore impossible twice over.

"Welcome to Solaria, Earthman," said the murdered monkey's young wife, Gladia, appearing in mid-air in Banaley's guest room — a projection, crisp as a ripe peel. "Do not imagine for a moment that I would view with you in person."

"I wouldn't dream of it, ma'am," said Banaley. "On Earth we groom shoulder to shoulder, ten thousand to a terrace. My wife shares a grooming line with forty monkeys."

Gladia looked genuinely ill. "You *touch* each other? You show each other your — " her voice dropped — "your *bums*?"

"Ma'am, on Earth a baboon's bare rump is about as private as an elbow."

"That," said Gladia, "is the most obscene thing I have ever been told."

Viewing, Banaley learned, was the whole of Solarian society: full-hologram grooming, projection courtships, birthdays attended by fifty guests who were all light. *Seeing* — physical presence — was the unspeakable word. Solarians fainted at a handshake. Which raised the detective's problem: every suspect had a perfect alibi, namely, existence itself.

He viewed the murder scene by projection, walking hologram halls while his body stayed locked in the guest room. The household bonobots had witnessed everything and reported nothing.

"You were present at the killing," Banaley said to the estate's head gardener, a silver-backed machine.

"I was, sir."

"Describe the killer."

"I am unable, sir. The killer was a monkey. Describing a monkey's acts requires evaluating a monkey's choices, which my programming classifies as judging my betters. I have been standing here feeling terrible about it for eleven days."

Even the bonobots, it seemed, had been raised Solarian.

The security chief, Gruber, was poisoned mid-viewing by a bonobot's detached arm left where a kit could trip on it, and survived, furious. That made no sense either — the arm carried no poison a First Law machine could allow near a monkey, unless somebody had arranged the whole scene to look like what it wasn't. Banaley began to understand: the crimes were not impossible. They were *designed* to look impossible, by a monkey who understood bonobots the way a smuggler understands customs.

The Root Engineer's work took Banaley next to the gestation groves, where Solaria's kits were grown in warm rows of jars and raised by machines in the infant creches — never held, never carried, groomed at regulation distance by padded arms. The supervisor, Nurse Klorissa, projected beside him down aisles of wailing, immaculate babies.

"Don't pick that one up," she said sharply, as Banaley reached on instinct for a shrieking kit. "You'll imprint him. He'll spend his life wanting things, Earthman. We grow them clean of all that."

"Ma'am," said Banaley, jiggling the baby anyway, because some laws outrank the Three, "you are describing a grove of monkeys specifically engineered to end up alone." The kit gripped his fur with both fists and stopped crying. Klorissa looked away as if from an obscenity, which, on Solaria, it was.

Delmare himself grew clearer in the viewing. A cold monkey, by all reports, rigidly dutiful — and lately troubled. His estate logs showed he had audited the manifest of every bonobot shipment entering his district, and had flagged four lines of *detached arms* destined for private estates, and had requested a formal audience with the planet's Council before he died. "He was going to report something," Gladia said, wringing her projected hands. "He wouldn't say what. He said it was a betrayal of the whole world, and he said it with *relief*."

The manifest led Banaley to the roboticist Jothan Leafling, who refused even holographic eye contact, staring at the corner of the room while explaining, with staggering arrogance, that *he* understood bonobots better than anyone alive.

"Ideas," Leafling said, "cannot be obscene. I have designed bonobot ships, Detective — whole vessels with positronic crews. Solaria's future is a fleet that needs no monkey aboard to feel lonesome."

"A fleet," said Banaley. "Crewed by machines that can't hurt anybody. Ever? In any port?"

"Ideas," Leafling repeated, "cannot be obscene." And he looked, for a moment, exactly as far away as the stars he meant to own.

And little Gladia, grieving in her empty mansion, told Banaley about her marriage: a husband she had viewed for years and touched perhaps five times, assigned to her by breeding roster, both of them cringing through the duty like monkeys dunked in cold water.

"How did your husband die?" Banaley asked her, gently.

"I was *there*," Gladia whispered. "I remember being there. I remember a word — *bend* — someone put a word in my head like a seed in a furrow, and then I remember my arm, and then I remember him on the floor, and then I remember nothing, and I have been trying to unsee it ever since."

Banaley solved it in the open air. That was the joke of Solaria: it had no rooms. He made himself walk out of the guest house, away from walls, away from terraces, into a lawn that ran greedy and green to the horizon, and stood under the naked sun with his fear until the fear got bored. On Earth, sky was a rumor; here it was furniture. He let it hold him, and in the enormous quiet the case assembled itself like a bunch of bananas, finger by finger. Leafling had designed Delmare's household machines. He had delivered to Gladia's rooms a detached bonobot arm — to the bonobots, not a weapon, merely a *part*, no more to be feared than a dropped peel; the First Law never twitched. And he had planted in lonely, miserable Gladia a conditioned word that turned her, for ten seconds, into that rarest thing on Solaria: a monkey standing close enough to strike. Delmare had found the missing arms and traced the design; the fleet needed its loose Root Engineer buried; and the murder weapon was the whole planet — its taboos doing the work of ten thousand locks.

Banaley gathered every suspect in one grand assembly — Solarians projecting into a single hall, the closest thing to a crowd their nerves allowed — and laid the bunch down, finger by finger, while Leafling's projection grew stiller and stiller in the corner.

"You'll view with me one last time," Banaley told him, "or so help me, Leafling, I will board a ship, and I will walk to your estate, and I will stand in your doorway in the flesh — and then I will cross your lawn, and I will reach out, and I will *shake your hand*."

"You wouldn't," said Leafling, gray to the ears.

"Gladly. And afterward I'd have you over to Earth, where you'd be groomed by forty strangers before lunch."

The roboticist lasted eleven seconds. Then he bit down on the bitter pith he kept in his cheek for emergencies, and the screen went dark, and Solaria was short one monster.

They exiled Gladia to Aurora with a small trunk and a lifetime of apologies to herself. She viewed Banaley goodbye from the foot of the ramp, in person, trembling, four whole feet of miracle. Aboard the homeward ship, Apeivaw watched Banaley stare out at the stars.

"You solved an impossible crime," the bonobot said.

"I solved a lonely one," said Banaley. "Ten thousand baboons on a whole world, and not one monkey to talk to. No wonder their monkeys went rotten. Mark me, partner — a troop that stops touching breaks in the middle. Worlds break the same way."

And he fell asleep dreaming of the Peel's crowded terraces, ten thousand bums to the breeze, as civilized as anything in the galaxy.
