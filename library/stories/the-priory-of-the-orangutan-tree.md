# The Priory of the Orangutan Tree

*A parody of "The Priory of the Orange Tree"*

In the West, it was forbidden to speak of apes.

Queen Sabran the Ninth of Inys — young, golden-furred, weighed down by a crown of orange blossoms — sat in her chamber while her ladies-in-waiting groomed her back and felt none of it. A queen of Inys did not get to feel things. A queen of Inys got married, produced an heir, and kept the Nameless One asleep beneath the waves, as her ancestors had done for a thousand years. Preferably in that order.

The council had helpfully assembled a parade of suitors. There was a prince of the Trigny vineyards who talked exclusively about himself in the third person; an Aurient admiral who sniffed the orchard blossoms and asked what they were worth; and a viscount who took one look at Sabran's council and proposed to it instead.

"Your Majesty frets," said Lady Ead, working the comb with careful fingers.

"Your Majesty frets," Sabran agreed, "because her council would rather she wed a stranger than waste a thought on her own troop."

Ead said nothing. She was not really a lady-in-waiting. She was a guardian of the Priory of the Orangutan Tree, sent across the world to protect this one langur queen — and sworn to secrecy about it, which was awkward, since she was fairly sure she was falling in love with her.

That night, the assassin came.

It dropped from the rafters without a sound — a shadow-thing with too many teeth, one of the Serpentkin, writhing and changing shape as it moved. It went straight for the queen's bed.

Ead was faster. Light bloomed off her palms, and she spoke a word in the old tongue of the Priory. The shadow shrieked, curled up like a burnt spider, and dissolved into the floorboards.

Sabran sat bolt upright. "You're a mage. In my court. Under my law, I should have you exiled."

"Under your law," Ead said, "you'd be dead. Pick one."

The queen picked silence, a diplomatic way of picking both.

But the attempt changed everything, because it meant the Nameless One was stirring — the great Nameless Python, the Balless One, who had once swallowed the sun and been thwarted only by a queen of Inys and a sacred ape of the East, fighting side by side. So said the Western church, anyway, and the church had a version it liked.

"The Priory kept the truth," Ead told her, days later, on the balcony where the queen went to be nobody. "The history you were taught gives the whole victory to Saint Gala of the golden troop — one brave monkey, one shining spear. The truth is plainer and better: the wyrm was turned back by a southern gibbon called Cleoline, who carried a cutting of the first orange tree and knew what it could do, standing beside a Berethnet queen who told the story sideways afterward. Your histories cut the apes out and pasted a monster in the gap. Then they pasted a saint over the gap the monster left."

"You expect me to rewrite a thousand years of doctrine," Sabran said, "on the word of one grooming girl."

"I expect you," said Ead, "to be the kind of queen who would rather rewrite a wrong story than die inside a tidy one."

Far across the ocean, in the East, a sea-cadet named Tané broke her order's strictest law and hid a stowaway aboard a ship: an old orangutan called Nanagal, wide as a doorway, gentle as afternoon. The penalty was supposed to be death. Instead Nanagal looked at Tané with eyes like still pools, and spoke the word that bound them: *Chosen.*

"Ape-rider," breathed the harbormaster, and fell to his knees.

Tané — who had failed her swimming test twice out of pure terror, and had hidden the stowaway mostly because hiding things was the one skill her fear had taught her — looked down from Nanagal's shoulders at half the ocean and felt her stomach trade places with her spine.

"What if I fall?" she whispered.

"Then I will catch you," said Nanagal, "as you caught me. That is the whole of the arrangement, little swimmer."

In a tower nearby, an exiled alchemist macaque named Niclays Root scratched at the sand of a dead king's garden, hunting the recipe for the elixir of life out of spite and homesickness. He found instead a jar of fire-that-survives-water, the old alchemy of the orange tree, and when the world began to end he carried it west in his hatbox, grumbling, because an alchemist hates wasted luggage more than he loves his own bitterness.

So the West's oldest lie began to come apart. Nanagal carried Tané over the sea on her broad back, swimming like a golden ship, and they came to Inys in its hour of need — because the Nameless One had woken at last, rising from the deep with tides of writhing Serpentkin behind it, called up by traitors who thought a serpent on the throne beat a queen. The traitors had even fed the Python the royal fleet's anchors as a courtesy, which everyone agreed later was very poor manners.

The last stand happened on the cliffs of the Orangeline, and it was not elegant: howlers and sea-monkeys, marmosets and one enormous orangutan, a queen with a spear of orange wood and a lady-in-waiting with light in her hands, all going absolutely bananas on a cliff at dawn.

"I am rather frightened," Sabran admitted, as the sea began to boil.

"That's the Branch," said Ead. "Hold your piece of it."

The Python rose, jaws opening like a canyon — and the allied line held the way a held breath holds. Niclays Root lobbed his hatbox fire down its throat, which tasted worse than anything it had swallowed in a thousand years. Nanagal reared out of the surf to meet it, Tané on her shoulders, and gripped its throat with hands that had held back centuries. Ead's light seared its eyes; Sabran drove the orange spear home — Cleoline's weapon, the old cutting made wood again, the southern gibbon's tree arriving exactly where the history books said it couldn't — and the Nameless One came apart into the sea like a rope unravelling, its Serpentkin scattering back into the dark.

Afterward, on the cliff, the queen of Inys did something no Western monarch had done in a thousand years. She bowed to an ape. Then she did something no Berethnet queen had ever done at all: she told her own people the truth, from the cliff's edge, in her coronation voice — that the jungle had been saved twice, by two kinds of hands, and that Inys had spent a millennium thanking only one of them.

"Tell the Priory of the Orangutan Tree," she said, "the West remembers. And tell them—" she glanced at Ead, and her ears went pink, "—tell them I'm keeping one of theirs."

"Your Majesty," said Ead, "is going to be a great deal of trouble."

"I'd rather be a great deal of trouble," said Sabran the Ninth, "than a perfectly peaceful banana."

She married no prince that year. She produced an heir the modern way — by asking her council to define "produce," then ignoring their answer — and named Nanagal to the honor guard, Tané to the fleet, and Niclays Root Royal Alchemist, at which he wept into his hatbox and swore he'd only come for the weather.

And in the palace courtyard, grown from a cutting of the cutting, they planted an orange tree.
