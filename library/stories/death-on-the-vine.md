# Death on the Vine

*A parody of "Death on the Nile"*

Hercule Poirot, the celebrated vervet detective, was on holiday at the First Canopy Hotel, his whiskers waxed to two symmetrical points, when he overheard a young monkey begging.

"Linnet, please. Give Simon a job on the estate. Anything. He is clever, he is strong, and I love him."

"Anything for you, Jackie," said Linnet Ridgehowler, the richest young she-monkey in England. "Anything at all."

Anyone but Hercule Poirot — with his little grey fur cells and his passion for odor and method — would have missed the significance of that promise. Within a month the announcement appeared: Linnet Ridgehowler was to marry Simon Doyle. The groom was Jackie's Simon. And Jackie began to appear everywhere they went, silent and smiling.

Poirot begged her, gently, to stop. "To follow them like a vine around a tree, mademoiselle — the vine always wins in the end, and always regrets it. Do not make of love a jungle."

But there was a cruise to be taken: the S.S. Karnap, up the Great Vine to the second cataract. Poirot boarded with a toothbrush, three books, and a premonition. Also aboard: Colonel Race, a lugubrious gorilla; Mrs. Howlerbourne, who wrote romances (*Passion in the Passionflowers* was in its ninth printing) and drank her research; her daughter Rosalie, who watched her mother the way a monkey watches a falling branch; Mr. Pennington, Linnet's banana trustee, whose accounts were perhaps not entirely orderly; Dr. Bessner, whose clinic in Austria Linnet had once described, publicly, as a well-meaning sawdust nest; Miss Baboon Schuyler, an elderly dowager with a chest of braid and sticky paws; her niece Cornelia, a plain, pleasant chimp with a bottle of berry polish she lent to everybody; and a languid young gentleman named Tim Allbranch, who collected dowagers and paste glass in equal quantities. And Louise, Linnet's little maid.

At the temple of A-bo Simian, a boulder rolled off the cliff and missed Linnet by a tail's length. The tourists oohed. Poirot did not.

The second night out, Jacqueline de Bellyfort had too much palm wine and shot Simon in the leg with her little silver dart-pistol. It was loud, it was messy, and everyone aboard had an opinion and an alibi within the hour. Simon was carried to Dr. Bessner's cabin, and Jackie, weeping, was sedated and watched by half the troop — which, Poirot would later observe, is a very great deal of watching for one small macaque.

In the morning, Linnet Ridgehowler was found dead in her nest, one small hole in her temple, and on the wall above her, drawn in berry juice, the letter **J**.

"Ah," said everyone. "Jacqueline. It is signed."

"Pah," said Poirot. "It is signed *too well*. When a monkey writes her name above her crime, mesdames, she is writing somebody else's."

The berry juice was barely dry before the nest of lies began to unravel — and the curious thing, Poirot told Colonel Race, was that every monkey aboard was lying about something, and none of them, at first, about murder. He interviewed them on the sun deck in order, like a monkey counting mangoes.

Mr. Pennington lied about his ledgers. "You were at A-bo Simian, walking on the cliff path, when the boulder fell," said Poirot. "And the papers Linnet signed at Shellal — signed carelessly, at tea? Bon. Her real signature has a flourish, monsieur. Yours has a flourish *and a tremble*, and a monkey who has practiced it twenty times. It is the trust that is dead, not the heir, that you required." Pennington stopped sweating and began to pray, which was a step in the right direction.

Rosalie lied about the bottle she had flung into the Great Vine at the first cataract. "It was palm wine," said the girl, defiant. "It was my mother's." She said it the way monkeys say the thing they have guarded longest; Poirot patted her paw and said that of all the secrets aboard, hers was the most forgivable, being made of love, badly pickled.

Miss Baboon Schuyler lied about nothing, having kleptomaniacally taken the subject off the table: Linnet's famous pearl necklace was found in the dowager's sponge-bag. But when Poirot held those pearls to the light, he sighed. "Paste, madame. Of the very best manners, but paste. The real strand was exchanged — and exchanged by a monkey who knew exactly which strand a dowager would steal." Tim Allbranch, approached on the matter, went pale beneath the tan, returned the true pearls by the next post, unsigned, from a village that officially has no post office, and disembarked at the second cataract with his luggage and his reputation in separate boats.

And Louise the maid lied smallest of all, which killed her. "Had I seen something, messieurs, naturally I would say so — unless, of course, somebody found it worth a monkey's while, me being poor and all." She was found before dawn with a vine lace tightened round her throat. Mrs. Howlerbourne, who had seen everything from her own lattice with a novelist's professional interest, announced at breakfast that she had seen the killer with her own eyes, "and it was the little—" — and was shot through the lattice mid-sentence. The sentence, Poirot noted, had cost more than her advances.

In the saloon, with the Karnap tied to the bank and everyone's nerves going thoroughly bananas, Poirot arranged the lot of them and told the truth.

"The bullet holes, messieurs. Through the velvet stole that hung over the rail — one bullet, two holes, one wall. The saloon shot went *through* the stole into the panelling; there was never any blood at all, only Miss Cornelia's berry polish, borrowed and smeared. Simon Doyle was never shot the first time. While the good doctor bandaged a wound that did not exist, and every eye in the troop watched Jacqueline, *madame* here went up to the nest, then to the maid, and, at the last, to poor Madame Howlerbourne. He returned, wrapped the stole about his leg and the pistol both — thus, no powder, no noise, no escape — and gave himself the wound the doctor had already treated. The pistol went through the porthole into the river, where it snagged on a nail at the waterline: the only witness aboard with impeccable manners. Two monkeys, one alibi, three kills, one *J* drawn in borrowed polish — too obvious, always too obvious, the signature of a plot that could not afford a witness. And why? It was never the bananas, mon ami. It was not even the fortune. It was that she loved him — and he was a hollow fig."

Simon began to protest; it was an unconvincing performance; even the parrots winced. But it was Jackie who moved. She crossed the saloon, put her arms around Simon, and said, quite gently, "It's all right, darling. We have made such a mess of the jungle, you and I."

There was one small crack, like a branch breaking high overhead. When it was over, the little silver pistol was still in her sleeve, and Colonel Race had looked away, which was the kindest thing anyone did all cruise.

"You knew she had it," said Race at last.

Hercule Poirot looked out at the Great Vine, running green and gold toward the sea.

"Does it matter?" he said. "Jealousy, mon colonel, is the most patient of vines. It grows in every canopy, quietly, quietly — and you never see it until it has the whole tree. Now. Lunch. One must not allow murder to spoil the appetite twice."

The rest was sorted on the voyage home: Pennington taken off at the first cataract to explain the boulder to the troop's officers; Cornelia engaged before the second to a loud young revolutionary chimp who had admired her all cruise ("He is not rich," said Cornelia, "and neither, it turns out, am I — we consider ourselves even"); Miss Baboon Schuyler permitted to keep the paste necklace, "real enough for my neck, which is paste and seventy." And Poirot retired to his cabin with his three books, and read, of all things, a romance.
