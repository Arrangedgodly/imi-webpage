# The Da Vine-ci Code

*A parody of "The Da Vinci Code"*

Renowned symbologist Robert Langdong, professor of monkey symbology at Harvard Canopy University, was asleep in his Paris hotel hammock when the Judicial Troop Police called. The curator of the Jungle-Louvre had been murdered, and the dying ape had left one name at the scene: his.

Langdong swung down through the midnight canopy to the museum, a great glass treehouse glowing among the chestnut branches. In the Grand Colonnade lay Jacques Saunière, the venerable orangutan curator, shot in the belly and — with his final strength — arranged on the floor in the pose of the Vitruvian Monkey, arms and legs spread inside a perfect circle.

"A message," said Captain Bezu Fache, a bull of a baboon with a brow like a cliff. "Written in invisible banana ink. Three lines, then your name. Explain that, Professor."

Langdong read the shimmering letters:

O, DRACONIAN DEVILS! OH, LAME SAINT! SO DARK THE CON OF PRIMATE!
13-3-2-21-1-1-8-5

"Anagrams," Langdong breathed, his tail curling. "'O, Draconian devils' rearranges to LEONARDO DA VINE-CI. 'Oh, lame saint' becomes THE MONA LISA. And those numbers are a Fibonacci bunch, scrambled like a fruit salad."

A young bonobo in a zoo courier's cap pushed through the police line. Sophie Neveu, Saunière's granddaughter, was a cryptologist from the Puzzle Division, and she had decrypted Fache's radio chatter on the walk over. She leaned close to Langdong's ear.

"Captain Fache thinks you did it," she murmured. "The moment you stop being useful, you're in a cage. Those numbers are a deposit-box code. Grandfather is leading us somewhere. Walk with me and look guilty. You're good at looking professorial — same thing."

In the museum washroom she peeled the captain's tracking dot off the window ledge and dropped it onto a passing manure cart, and for one beautiful hour Captain Fache and forty baboons surrounded the Parc des Branches with commendable stealth.

The numbers opened a box at the Jungle Deposit Bank of Zurich — a bank so discreet it denied having branches — and the box yielded Saunière's gold key and a second marvel: the Vine-cryptex, a gourd of five rotating dials, each stamped with the letters of the great banana cultivars. When Fache's roadblocks closed, Sophie requisitioned the armored banana truck idling at the loading dock, and they rolled out past the police wearing the flat, self-important expressions of produce.

Saunière, it emerged, had been Grand Master of the Priory of Simian — the secret troop that for a thousand years had guarded the Bananical Grail. Not a cup, whispered the legends, but the Golden Banana of Queen Mango-dalen, bride of the Chimp King, whose seed-line still swung through the royal trees of Europe.

"So dark the con of primate," Sophie said softly. "The Big Cage Church buried the sacred She-Ape and taught the whole jungle to fear its own females. Grandfather spent his life putting her back on the branch."

That same night, in a cell of cold stone, an albino capuchin knelt and breathed his little litany. "Peel is good," Silas whispered, winding his whip of braided vine. "Peel is pain. Pain is also good." He had been a broken thing in a Man's cage until Bishop Aringarosa of Opus Daiquiri pulled him from the mud and gave him a god with excellent posture; he repaid the Bishop with obedience, and the world with grief. By lamplight he had already called on the four elders of the Priory, asked each the same question, and received the same proud answer — the secret dies with me — which Silas, a literal soul, accepted. In the church of Saint-Sulipice he found Sister Sandrine, the Priory's watchwoman, reaching for her telephone and her crucifix at once. She very nearly managed both.

Silas answered to a voice called the Teacher. So, unknowingly, did the Bishop. "The Grail will shatter the Big Cage Church," the Teacher had written Aringarosa, "and lift Opus Daiquiri above every dome in the jungle. Bring twenty million in bearer coconuts." The Bishop packed his faith and his luggage, which is how holy men come to bankroll the one monkey they ought to arrest.

At Chateau Villette, the banyan estate of Sir Leigh Teabing — a wizened British orangutan with leg braces of liana wood — the secrets poured out over banana crumpets. "The Grail is a she!" Teabing cried, hobbling a delighted lap around his print of The Last Snack. "See how the Chimp King's paw hovers over a golden fruit that four centuries of scholars mistook for a decorative plantain! And this — the Vine-cryptex itself. Marble gourd, vinegar heart. Turn the dials wrong and the vinegar eats the papyrus. Turn them right, and the Grail tells you where she sleeps."

Teabing, naturally, was the Teacher. When the parlor door closed that night, his manservant Rémy held the pistol while Teabing rose out of his braces like a monkey stepping out of a costume, and pressed a coconut to Sophie's head.

"The password, mademoiselle. Five letters. Think fruit."

"S-O-F-I-A," said Sophie. "My grandfather's name for me." The dials aligned; the gourd opened onto a second, smaller gourd. ("Your grandfather," Teabing sighed, "had the humor of a lesser monkey.") Inside gourd two: papyrus. Inside papyrus: worse news.

IN LONDON LIES A KNIGHT A PEACH INTERRED. YOU SEEK THE ORB UPON HIS TOMB — IT SPEAKS OF ROSY FLESH AND SEEDED WOMB.

They flew to London at midnight, hostages with warm nuts. In the Temple Church, eight stone knights lay on the floor like a troop that had lost an argument with freeze tag, and Teabing made everyone lift each effigy hunting the orb — which was when Langdong, politely, at pepper-spray range, worked it out.

"A knight a Peach interred. Not His Holiness, sir — you gorge on popes because the Church feeds you. Alexander Peach, the poet, who eulogized Sir Isaac Fig-Newton into Westminster Abbey. Newton, the knight who discovered gravity when a fruit fell on his head. The orb missing from his tomb is the fruit itself — and the only fruit this riddle trusts is the one that grows on a vine. G-R-A-P-E."

Fache's troopers stormed the abbey at that moment — the Captain having finally arrested the correct primate — and Teabing went out in irons, pleading to be allowed one last look at a plantain.

Silas, arriving late and seeing the whole rotten tree at once, set down his rifle and asked only for a smaller whip. Bishop Aringarosa was found at the airport with twenty million in bearer coconuts and nobody left to give them to; he retired to a monastery famous for topiary and is said to prune beautifully.

On the bumpy flight home, Sophie cracked the second gourd. G-R-A-P-E. The dials aligned, the vinegar kept its dignity, and the slip of fig-leaf paper inside bore a single verse:

THE BANANA BENEATH THE ANCIENT ROSE VINE WAITS.

"The Rose Vine," Langdong whispered. "The Prime Vine — the old line every orchard in Paris was measured from. It runs through the Jungle-Louvre. Sophie, he was never hiding her from you. He was hiding her beneath the one thing nobody looks at twice: a gift shop."

At dawn, in the museum courtyard, beneath the great inverted banyan pyramid — its roots in the sky, its crown in the earth — Sophie knelt at her grandfather's resting place and understood at last. The lost queens of the Priory slept here. The Grail slept with them, gleaming in the dark like a little sun.

"Remarkable," Langdong whispered, kneeling beside her as the light came gold through the leaves. "All the vaults, all the ciphers, all the albino monks — and the greatest treasure in simian history was hidden beneath a rose vine at a museum gift shop."

"Goodbye, Professor." Sophie smiled and offered him the thing every grieving bonobo offers a friend. "Pick a louse. It's on the house."

Langdong picked the louse solemnly, as one does. Then he limped off into the Paris morning, going quietly, gloriously ape, one branch at a time — a chimp who had looked into the face of history and found it grinning back with a banana.
