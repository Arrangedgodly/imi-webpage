# Things Fall Ape-art

*A parody of "Things Fall Apart"*

Okonkwo was well known throughout the nine troops. A chimp as lean and hard as a liana, he had brought honor to Umufruita as a young monkey by throwing Amaleap the Cat, the great gray gibbon of the southern boughs, clean off the wrestling branch. He ruled his household with a heavy hand and a thundering chest, because everyone knew his father.

Unoka had been lazy, a flutist who loved palm wine and the afternoon shade, owing figs to every neighbor and giggling about it. So when Unoka swelled up and was carried to the Evil Forest, Okonkwo swore a great oath: he would not be his father. Banana, the king of crops, would crown him — he who could feed his troop from his own plantation was a very great monkey indeed, and Okonkwo's bunches were the envy of the clan.

His temper was the envy of no one. In the Week of Peace, when no monkey might strike another, his youngest wife Ojiugo went to braid at a neighbor's tree and came back late, and Okonkwo beat her in sight of the whole compound, and the priest came to his gate and fined him a she-goat, a hen, and a length of clothless humility, saying the gods would not be beaten into ripening the bananas early. Okonkwo paid, and was angrier at the fine than ashamed of the beating. Another year, at the Feast of the New Yam, his second wife Ekwefi cut a few leaves from a banana tree, and Okonkwo shot at her — his ancient seed-popper exploded a charge over her head, and the whole compound agreed that it was the gun's opinion, not his. Okonkwo was not a cruel monkey, the clan said carefully. He was a monkey who heard his own chest louder than the world.

Among these monkeys, proverbs were the ripe 'nana pulp with which words were sweetened. "As the elders say," Okonkwo told his son Nwoye, "a young monkey that keeps company with the leopard soon learns to bite." Nwoye would lower his eyes and feel something inside him go soft, like a bruised fruit. His friend Obierika, when the clan left twin infants in the forest, because twins were an offense to the earth, sat a long time and said nothing, and later asked, quietly, as if asking the fig wine: if the earth kills us for killing, what does she do about the ones she plants herself? No one answered him. It was not a proverb yet.

Then the elders gave Okonkwo a boy to look after. Ikemefuna came from the Mbaino troop as a peace offering, a bright-eyed young bonobo who called Okonkwo *father* and made the compound somewhere you could hear laughing. He made Nwoye feel grown. He made the compound feel like a troop.

The locusts came that season, and the monkeys were glad. But it was the day the locusts came that the elders came too, and walked away with heavy shoulders.

That night old Ezeudu gripped Okonkwo's arm. "That boy calls you father. Do not bear a hand in his death."

They set out with the boy across the far ridges, and at the stream one of the clan drew his blade, and Ikemefuna looked back and cried, "Father, they have —"

Dazed with fear, Okonkwo drew his own blade and cut him down. He was afraid of being thought weak.

He could not eat for two days. "When did you become a shivering old baboon?" he asked himself. The troop said nothing. Nwoye's soft inside turned to something quiet and waiting.

One night the priestess Chielo came to Okonkwo's compound with her own voice packed away and Agbala's in its place, and pointed at Ekwefi's daughter Ezinma, the child everyone loved too much to say so. "Agbala wants to see her in the hills," she said, and carried the girl off on her back, and walked through all nine troops of the dark, singing to the night, while Ekwefi followed thirty paces behind with her heart in her mouth and no weapon but a banana stalk. The priestess walked and walked; Ezinma slept on the spirit's back; and toward dawn Chielo set the girl down at her mother's own door and went home an ordinary ape. Whatever the hills wanted, the hills kept to themselves. But Ekwefi counted her daughter's fingers twice that morning, the way of mothers who have lent something to a god.

More trouble came with the thunder-stick. At old Ezeudu's funeral, Okonkwo's ancient seed-popper exploded in his hands and its charge struck the dead elder's son. A crime against the earth: the troop burned his compound and exiled him seven rains long to his mother's troop in Mbanta. His kinsman Uchendu received him, and called the family together, and asked Okonkwo why the exile was to the mother's troop, and answered his own question: *Nneka.* Mother is Supreme. "You have eaten your father's food all your life," said Uchendu. "Now go and eat your mother's, and learn why she was keeping it."

It was there that Nwoye first heard the pale-furred strangers singing. They had come with a strange new troop that had no branches and no elders, called a church, and its hymns fell on Nwoye's ears like rain after the dry season. The missionaries asked for a plot of land, and the elders, smiling behind their hands, gave them the Evil Forest, which ate the unwanted and the abominated. The clan counted days. When twenty-eight had passed and the forest had not swallowed them — not one pale monkey, not one convert — the clan was amazed, and the church stood, and the hymns kept falling like rain. The poetry of the new songs did something to the marrow in Nwoye's bones; it answered a question about Ikemefuna that he had been carrying like a stone. So Nwoye went to them, and Okonkwo wanted to strangle him with his own hands. "You have all seen the abomination of your brother. He is no longer my son." A living fire, the clan might have said, begets cold, heedless ash.

When the seven rains were done, Okonkwo returned expecting war, and found a Reserve. The pale strangers had built a compound of iron and glass, and a court where monkeys who broke the pale laws were locked in cages. The new troop had taken in the outcasts no troop would groom, the osu, who now shook the pale apes' paws with both hands. The clan was not one troop anymore but two, and things were falling ape-art, for the old center could not hold.

Trouble tipped over at the annual ceremony, when an over-ripe convert named Enoch rose and jeered at the egwugwu — the masked spirits of the ancestors — and the spirit's raffia muzzle slid aside, and all Umufruita saw it: the masked one unmasked, the ancestor out of the suit, an abomination plain as daylight. The egwugwu assembled and burned the church to its sills.

Okonkwo and five of the clan's leaders were summoned to the iron-and-glass compound to talk, and were trapped — the doors swung shut on them like a fruit-fly's snare. The pale ones plucked the elders' whiskers, their badges of honor, plucked them one by one, and locked them in cages without water until the clan paid two hundred bags of bananas, and the elders came out into the sunlight bald-faced, weeping like infants, and Umufruita wept to see them.

The day the court messengers came to break up a meeting of the clan, the first messenger strode up and said, "The pale ones have ordered this meeting to stop."

In a flash, Okonkwo's blade rose and fell, and the messenger lay in the dust. The clan looked at him and said nothing, and let the other messengers escape. Okonkwo knew then that Umufruita would never go to war. The troop of his fathers, which had thrown leopards and judged with nine voices, had become a troop of monkeys clapping for their keepers.

They found him at last at the top of the great silk-cotton tree, the tree no monkey was permitted to climb, and he was not holding on anymore. It was an abomination to take one's own leave that way, so the clan buried him like a stranger, and his friend Obierika told the pale strangers, bitterly: "That man was one of the greatest monkeys in Umufruita. You drove him to the top of the tree. He was our flame, and you made ash of him, and now you will make a paragraph."

The District Commissioner, a plump vervet with pith helmet and notebook, covered the body with his handkerchief and considered it. One could almost write a chapter on this strange chimp. Perhaps not a chapter — a reasonable paragraph, anyhow, in his book: *The Pacification of the Primates of the Lower Canopy*.
