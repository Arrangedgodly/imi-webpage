# The Howler of the Baskervilles

*A parody of "The Hound of the Baskervilles"*

Dr. Mortimer, a monkey of science, came to 221B Banyan Street carrying an old manuscript and a younger fear. He read the legend aloud: how Sir Hugo Baskerville, a gorilla of wicked temper, carried off a bonny bonobo farmer's daughter, and how that night, as Hugo hunted her across the moor, a great black howler of the jungle-hells tore him apart, "a screaming thing chased by a screaming thing."

"Superstition," said Sherlock Howlmes, filing a mango seed.

"Three nights ago," said Dr. Mortimer, "Sir Charles Baskerville died on the moor. Heart failure. He was found at the end of the ylang-ylang avenue, face contorted, and — Mr. Howlmes — two hundred yards away were the pugmarks of a gigantic howler."

Howlmes stopped filing.

Into the affair strode Sir Henri Baskerville, a young gorilla from the Canadian colony, cheerful and doomed on paper. Almost at once, oddness collected: an anonymous warning pasted from The Jungle Times ("As you value your life or your reason keep away from the moor"); a black-bearded stranger in a mango cart who shadowed his every step along Regent Bough; and the theft, from his hotel room, of one new climbing boot.

"I'll come to the moor myself," said Sir Henri.

"You'll go tonight," said Howlmes, "with Dr. Wattail. I remain in the city — detained by the case of the Second Banana, which demands my whole attention." Wattail, wounded in his pride, went along as protector and correspondent.

Before they left, Howlmes ran two quiet experiments. First, a telegram to Baskerville, addressed to the butler — and it was delivered into Barrymore's own hands at the Hall, so the bearded stranger in the mango cart was somebody else. Second, an advertisement in the lost-and-found: *FOUND, ONE CLIMBING BOOT.* The next morning the new boot was smuggled back into Sir Henri's room — and the old brown boot, the comfortable boot, the boot a howler's nose would truly know, vanished in its place.

"He corrects his mistake," said Howlmes. "Note it, Wattail. A boot is not a clue. A boot is a scent bottle with laces."

The moor was a grey mangrove waste: mists that walked, gnarled trees like tortured monkeys frozen mid-gesture, and at its heart the Great Grimpen Muck, which shimmered innocently and swallowed anything careless enough to test it. Baskerville Hall stood heavy-browed on the bluff, staffed by the Barrymores — an orangutan butler and his weeping wife — and by the second night Wattail had begun to envy the ghosts.

At midnight he saw candlelight moving in a distant window. He and Sir Henri crawled the moor to catch the signaler and caught instead the Barrymores' secret: a basket of fruit and an old climbing coat, sent out nightly to Selden, the escaped zoo-chimp known as the Banana Hill Menace — Mrs. Barrymore's own brother.

"You'd have done the same," said the butler heavily. "He's kin. He's cracked. But he's kin."

"I'll forget I saw it," said Sir Henri — and as he said it, far out on the moor, the howler began.

It was a rolling, impossible cry that Wattail felt in his own chest, and every monkey on the moor heard it, and no monkey on the moor slept. The next morning they found Selden dead at the foot of a cliff, dressed in Sir Henri's stolen clothes, the beast having hunted the wrong gorilla by scent.

"Which," wrote Wattail excitedly to Banyan Street, "explains the stolen boot! Someone gave the howler Sir Henri's scent!"

Among the moor's oddities were the Stapletons: Jack, a lean grey lemur with a collecting net, and his beautiful dark "sister" Beryl, who seized Wattail's arm on the path and begged, "Go back! Go straight back to London!" — before realizing he was the wrong gorilla and pretending she'd meant the muck. She tried the same performance, in softer words, on Sir Henri himself; Sir Henri, who was falling in love, heard it as concern.

Stapleton talked of nothing but moths — the leopard moth, the heart-and-dart — with such insistence that Wattail filed him as "a scholar and a bore." Only once did the mask slip: when Sir Henri spoke of courting Beryl, Stapleton's net snapped a sapling in two, and he laughed it off as enthusiasm for entomology.

There was also Frankland of Lafter Hall — a litigation-mad old langur who had sued his neighbor for casting a shadow, and won. He delighted Wattail with tea and grievances, and through his telescope, "for the fun of spotting strays," had watched a small figure carrying food across the moor. Wattail assumed he meant the convict. Wattail, as usual, assumed wrong.

It was through Frankland's garden, on his daughter's forbidden path, that Wattail found the stone hut on the hillside — stocked with figs, a sleeping fur, and a familiar shape hunched over a butterfly journal.

"Howlmes!"

"Hush," said Sherlock Howlmes. "I have spent a week in this hut being the world's worst houseguest, eating preserved figs, and pretending to be a bearded stranger in a mango cart whenever I needed supplies. There never was a Second Banana. There was only ever the First Baskerville."

"But — why hide from me?"

"Because, my dear Wattail, your face broadcasts your thoughts to every monkey within scent range. But come. Groom the portrait gallery."

Wattail did, and the fur rose on his neck. Above the fireplace hung the wicked Sir Hugo — and beneath the wig of the past, the jaw, the eyes, the face of the lemur himself.

"That is Stapleton's face," Wattail whispered. "Then he's a Baskerville — next in line for the estate, and his 'sister' is his wife, planted to charm Sir Henri into danger while Stapleton daubs a howler in glow-paste and sets it hunting."

One thread remained, and they pulled it the next morning: Miss Laura Lyons, a hard-used typist monkey in the village, abandoned by Frankeland's pride and promised marriage by Stapleton. It was she who had written to Sir Charles at the gate that last night — dictation, she thought, from a friend raising money for her divorce.

"He told me Sir Charles would fund my freedom," she said, and her voice dried up. "He stood over me while I wrote. Ten o'clock, the ylang-ylang gate. Mr. Howlmes — he told me it was charity."

"It was stagecraft," said Howlmes gently. "You were the letter; the legend was the envelope."

That night they used Sir Henri as bait: he dined at Merripit House, walked home across the moor in the fog — and out of the fogbank came the Howler of the Baskervilles.

It was a howler the size of a young elephant, black as the inside of a cave, muzzle and chest blazing with phosphorescent glow, fire in its eyes and dripping from its jaws.

"Shoot it!" cried Howlmes, and they did. The beast went down among the bracken: not a demon, but the largest howler ever bred in captivity, its muzzle caked in glowing mangrove paste — phosphorescence, Howlmes noted, available to any naturalist who knew where the deep muck glowed. They found Sir Henri at the muck's edge, white as a coconut, alive by exactly one shout's worth of distance.

They found Beryl Stapleton bound in Merripit House; she'd refused to the last, and it was her telegram that had brought the trap. And they followed Stapleton's lantern along his secret pathway into the Great Grimpen Muck — a path marked with little wands, a path only a naturalist who had mapped the muck for years could walk — until the lantern stood still, and no lantern ever stood still unless no monkey held it.

"The jungle itself gave the verdict," said Howlmes at breakfast, laying it out like figs on a plate. "He was heir to the estate through a disowned grandmother — check the portrait, the jaw never lies. He knew the legend, the glow-paste, the muck. He married off his own wife as bait, forged a courtship, lured Sir Charles to the gate with a lonely lady's letter, and starved a captive howler until it would follow any scent given it — the scent of an old brown boot. And mark this — every monkey on that moor believed, because every monkey wanted to. Fear outruns evidence in any troop."

"The bearded stranger?" asked Wattail.

"Was never bearded and never a stranger. He was our lemur, in a costume, keeping watch on his own trap. I wore the same beard to the grocer's; we are lucky the moor never saw us both at once."

"Elementary, then," said Wattail.

"The game's a-branch," Howlmes agreed, "but the moor, my dear fellow — the moor plays for keeps."

Sir Henri, restored, planted figs along the ylang-ylang avenue and married — sensibly, and with Howlmes's blessing — a monkey with no connections to moors, mists, or moths.
