# The Apes of Wrath

*A parody of "The Grapes of Wrath"*

The dust hung over the hollow like a bad smell. Old Tom Joadpaw picked his way along the cracked riverbed, his feet sore from walking, his fur gray with the powdery residue of the drought. The bananas were gone. Every last 'nan had shriveled on the branch, and the great canopy that once shaded three generations of Joadpaws stood bare as a broom.

Tom was four years out of the McAlester Rockpile, where he'd done time for cracking a skull at a dance-hall brawl, paroled and humming. Which is why, when he found Jim Casy under a dead fig tree — the old orangutan preacher turned philosopher, his long arms draped over his knees — Tom made him swear on a rotting fig he'd never tell.

"Tol'able," said Casy, when he heard the news. "I been thinkin', Tom. Maybe it ain't just a troop. Maybe all us apes — ever' hair, ever' whisker — we's one big troop. When a young monkey swings, I swing with him. That's holy, ain't it?"

"Holier than a barrel of monkeys," Tom allowed.

At the old home place they found dust in the nests, dust in the water gourds, and a scraggly baboon named Muleypaw Graves sitting in the doorway like a rug with eyes.

"They come with cats," said Muleypaw. "Cats crawlin' ever'where, and tractors snortin' through the groves. The company owns the trees now. I ain't a-goin'. I'll hide in the brush like a ol' ghost ape. A troop's got to have someplace to haunt."

"Then haunt it in good health," said Tom.

They reached the family nest at sundown. Ma Joadpaw, a broad, gray-muzzled matron, was stirring a pot of fig-leaf tea over a fire. Grampa was asleep in the crook of a stump, dreaming of the old days when a fellow could pick peaches with both feet. Pa was nailing the family's possessions — two gourds and a spare fur brush — to a handcart.

"We're goin' to the Promised Grove," Pa announced. "California. They's bananas there the size of your leg, droopin' off the trees so heavy the branches bow to the ground. Work for ever'body. Bananas for ever'body."

"Sounds like a bunch of monkey business," muttered Uncle Johnpaw. But he helped push the cart.

They pooled every banana and bought a used canopy-truck from a sales ape in a yellow checked vest, a monkey who smiled with his whole face and lied with his whole body.

"She's a satanic beauty," he said, patting the rust. "Ran fine when we parked it."

"He'd sell his own grandmammy's teeth," said Al, the second son, who loved that truck the way other monkeys love their mothers, and drove it the way other monkeys love disaster.

The great migration began. Down Canopy 66 they went — a river of apes, monkeys, and one very smelly baboon family, all heading west. Grampa died the first week. He'd eat half a rotten plantain, cackled "I'm goin' out with the peel still on," and passed in his sleep. They buried him beneath a rockslide and Casy said the words: "This ape belonged to the troop, and the troop don't forget."

At the last river crossing, Tom's older brother Noah stopped dead. "I can't leave this water," he said mildly, and no argument on earth could move him. They left him fishing in the shallows, and Ma held her face stiff the rest of that day.

They crossed the desert at night. Ruthie and Winfield, the littlest monkeys, rode on the cart, playing "count the cacti." Ma sat like a stone with her eyes on the horizon, and when the axle cracked in the pass, it was Ma who found a mesquite branch, and Ma who held the cart up with her shoulders while Pa fixed it.

"We got to hang on," she said, wiping red dust from her brow. "We're the people. The ape-people. We go on."

At the state line a bored guard ape looked them over. "Any bananas?"

"One, and it's green," said Pa.

"Confiscated. Welcome to California."

California was not the Promised Grove.

There were bananas, true — miles of them, green and gold in endless company rows. But there were also fences, guards, and signs: NO APES. WAGES: ONE 'NAN A DAY. And behind every sign, a hundred hungry monkeys waiting for the same job.

In the first squat camp — a Hooverville of lean-tos and smoke — a lean spider monkey named Floyd laid out the arithmetic.

"They print up five thousand handbills, an' twenty thousand apes come. Wages drop from thirty cents to two bits to one 'nan a day. It's a scent they put on the paper. A hungry monkey can smell it three counties off."

"Then we oughta organize," said Casy, eyes kindling.

That night a deputy burned the camp on general principles, and the Joadpaws rolled on to Weedpatch — a government camp with clean water, toilets that flushed (the little ones lined up just to watch), hot showers, and a dance every Sunday. The troop ran it themselves. No cops allowed inside the fence.

The Growers' Association did not love Weedpatch, so it hired two rough baboons to start a riot at the dance, that the law might be invited in. The camp committee was waiting for them. As the fiddles played, the two baboons swung at a picker's partner — and eight polite committee apes folded them into a truck, drove them to the county line, and suggested they think things over. Outside the gate, the waiting deputies were informed the evening had been dull. It was the proudest night of Pa's life.

Then came the peaches: the Hooper Ranch, the whole family picking for half peels — two and a half cents a box, with starvation four thousand boxes down the row. Outside the fence, in a stick tent, Tom found Casy again, hoarse and shining, leading a strike.

"Look here," Casy was saying, standing on a stump. "If we all of us gripe together — one big holler — they got to pay the whole 'nana. Apes, unite! You got nothin' to lose but your chains... and your bananas!"

The lanterns came. A heavy pick-handle swung, and Casy fell, calling out — "You don' know what you're a-doin'—" — and Tom, in a fury of teeth and fists, went ape on the nearest guard and had to run for his life, a hunted animal once more.

He hid two days in a cave of wild grapevines while the manhunt beat the brush. Ma brought him meat and warning.

"They's work camps ever'where, Tom. You can't fight for a troop you never see no more."

"Then I'll be ever'where," said Tom. "Wherever they's a ape beatin' a ape, I'll be there. Wherever they's a monkey so hungry he can't swing, I'll be there. I'll be in the way apes holler when they're mad, an' in the way the little ones laugh when they're fed. I'll be ever'place at once — a ghost ape with a warrant."

The family moved on. The cotton was good for a while, whole families picking, white sacks dragging like a procession of ghosts. Connie, Rose's husband, studied fruit-picking by correspondence course and then skittered off down the road alone. Ruthie got into a fight and boasted her brother was hiding, a murderer now, so Tom's cave was empty by morning.

Then the rains came — gray, endless, flooding the low camps. Rose of Sharon, Tom's sister, heavy with a baby that came too soon and too small, huddled with Ma in a leaky boxcar while Pa led the men bailing, defeated, and Ma quietly took over the troop for good. Al found love with Aggie Wainwright and stayed to build a platform against the flood.

At the far end of the flooded boxcar, an old male ape lay dying of hunger. No teeth left. Couldn't chew a peel. No milk anywhere.

Ma looked at Rose. Rose understood.

The old ape drank, and lived.

"I don' know if Tom's ever comin' back," Rose whispered afterward, cradling the stranger's gray head. "But we keep a-goin', Ma."

Ma Joadpaw pulled her shawl tighter and looked up through the rain at the ragged sky.

"We're the people that go on," she said. "Ape don't wipe out ape. We keep comin'. We're the people that live — they can't lick us. We'll go on forever, Ma-like... 'cause we're the people."

And out beyond the flooded camp, in the souls of the wet and weary people, the bananas of wrath were ripening — filling, growing heavy, getting ready to drop.
