# The Final Proboscis

*A parody of "The Final Problem"*

It is with a heavy heart that I take up my pen, for these are the last words I shall ever write about the greatest mind our troop ever produced. The jungle deserves the truth about Sherlock Chimpness, Professor Moriarty, and the affair of the Final Proboscis.

I returned one evening in the spring to our rooms at 221B Banyan Street and found the shutters drawn, the lamps unlit, and my friend pressed against the wall like a startled loris.

"Have you brought your service pistol, Watson?"

"I have. You told me to."

"I did. I have never needed your company more." He held up a curious object that had come through the window an hour before my arrival: a blowpipe crossed with a blunderbuss, in oiled jungle-wood, the ugliest thing ever aimed at a monkey's head. "The practice shot, Watson. He prefers his instruments the way he prefers his crimes — silent, and at range. Have you ever heard of Professor Moriarty?"

"Never."

"Then you lag behind the best monkeys in the troop. He is the Proboscis-oleon of crime — a proboscis monkey with a nose like a war drum and the patience of a ripening fig. Every crooked macaque in London swings from his branches, and no stolen banana has ever been pinned to him. Hereditary, Watson: his brother is a stationmaster, which is how the troop's contraband always knows which trains not to take. For months I have been climbing his tree branch by branch. Three of his gang are turned crown witnesses. The rest are netted. Tomorrow morning the troop's officers will take every monkey left — if the Professor has not first taken me. If I can free the troop of him, I shall be content to climb one last time — and let go."

"That is going too far, Holmes."

"It is exactly far enough. Come — the game's a-branch!"

We went out the back window there and then, over the wall into the mews, and away in a hired growler while our luggage — which had left an hour before in the charge of a soft-spoken fruit merchant with whom Holmes had made a private arrangement — rattled toward the station by another road entirely. As our train pulled out I saw, too late, a tall figure on the platform, nose lifted like a instrument of precision, and Holmes drew his head in from the window with a grin.

"He chartered a special," said he. "It will follow us to Canopyberry Junction. We shall get off at the stop before and watch him pursue us to the coast." And so we did, from behind a fruit stall, eating roasted figs with the composure of two monkeys on a picnic while the Professor's train went by at forty branches an hour, carrying nobody but his fury.

We swung south for three days through the Great Forest, and Holmes's mood rose with every mile — past the fig orchards of Strasbourg-bough, along the river arm at Geneva-vine, until at Meervinegen we came at last to the Falls of Reichenfur, where the river throws itself off the canopy in one long silver peel.

We had just taken our nests at the Englischer Baum when the innkeeper handed Holmes a note. I read it over his shoulder:

*"You did not expect, sir, that I would lose your trail. A certain monkey's nose always knows. — Professor Moriarty."*

I went grey about the muzzle. Holmes ordered two plates of figs and grew positively chatty. It was the only sentimental evening I ever shared with him.

"When the troop's officers finish their roundup," said he, "there will be no branch left in London for that gang to sit on. My career will close upon its finest bough, Watson, and your memoirs will round into the complete account of it. Then I shall retire to Sussex and keep figs. A quiet figgery, Watson, and a textbook — *The Whole Tree of Detection*, by John H. Watson, medical practitioner, and occasional narrator."

"Could you not have written the textbook yourself?"

"Any monkey can list the branches," said Sherlock Chimpness. "You are the only one who ever made the troop hear them creak."

The next morning, as we set out for the falls, a young langur came galloping up the trail with a telegram. A dying Englishwoman at the hotel — could Dr. Watson come at once? I hesitated, but Holmes urged me kindly to go. "I shall stay and admire the falls," he said, "and keep the biggest problem for last."

An hour later the innkeeper, wringing his paws, admitted there was no Englishwoman, no doctor, no telegram. I raced back up the trail, heart hammering like a howler at dawn.

At the foot of the falls I found his walking stick pressed against a rock, and one half-peeled banana, carefully laid down, as though its owner meant to come back for it — which, I now know, he did. Two sets of footprints and two long tail-drag marks ran to the very lip of the falls, and there they ended. Below, the Reichenfur thundered into a boiling white gorge. No climbing monkey alive could have survived it.

Tucked under the stick was a letter, three pages torn from his notebook.

*"My dear Watson,"* it began. *"The Professor, whom I have just seen for the second and last time, came up the trail an hour behind you. (A certain monkey's nose, Watson, works in both directions.) The troop will never again need fear that nose. We fought branch by branch above the gorge, and ended locked together, two wrestlers going over — going ape, I regret to report, the whole way down. It was worth a whole bunch of wounds to know the depth of the loyalty in that heart of thine. Tell Inspector Lemurstrade that the papers concerning the monkey-eagle smuggling ring are in box M of the Garrick Troop-House, under the bananas; and that his roundup on Tuesday last took every branch of the Professor's organization but one. That one is dealt with. And do not grieve too long. The game, dear friend, was never afoot. It was always a-branch.*

*Your friend, Sherlock Chimpness."*

I searched the gorge for three days with the Langur police. The white water gave back nothing — not a collar, not a tuft, not one hair of either monkey — and I turned for home a poorer monkey than I have ever been since.

In London I found the troop already grieving, and Inspector Lemurstrade already famous, the roundup having taken thirty-two smugglers, three magistrates on the take, and a crooked customs colobus who wept into his ledgers. Box M supplied the evidence that hanged the remainder. But grief, in the jungle, is never left in peace for long. There came letters to the papers — over the signature of the Professor's brother the stationmaster — declaring that Sherlock Chimpness had lured his brother to the falls and murdered him; that the letter was a forgery; that the tail-drags told no honest tale. I answered every one of them, laying out the telegram trick, the footprints, the Englishwoman who never existed, and the profound sorrow of a friend; and the troop, which had cheered the smugglers' fall, quieted, and let us both rest.

I have written these pages because the troop should know what it lost at the Falls of Reichenfur: the best and the wisest monkey I have ever known.

I will add one small thing, and let the jungle make of it what it will. A porter at the coast swore that on the night boat to Borneo there boarded a quiet chimpanzee of scholarly bearing, travelling under a false name, with a rubber proboscis a full size too large folded in his hat — as though a certain Professor, of all monkeys, were not the only one who could wear a nose when it suited him.
