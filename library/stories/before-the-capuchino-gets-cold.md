# Before the Capuchino Gets Cold

*A parody of "Before the Coffee Gets Cold"*

Down a narrow stairwell beneath the Kanda bridge there is a basement café that has served the same brew for a hundred and forty years. The sign says TOKITEI in faded gold. The regulars say it more plainly: the place where, if you sit in the right chair, you can go back.

Not forward. Back. And only under the house rules, which the owner, Nagare Tokita — a methodical old capuchin who measures coffee the way monks measure sin — recites to every first-timer in the same flat voice:

One: you may only travel to a time when this café already existed.

Two: you may only meet those who have, at some point, sat in this café.

Three: whatever happens, you may not leave the chair.

Four — and here Nagare always looked up — you must return before your coffee gets cold.

The chair sat by the pillar, worn silver at the armrests. A coffee was poured. A moment was chosen. And when the cup went cold, the traveler was back, having changed nothing at all, because of the final, cruelest rule: the present does not change.

The regulars all asked: then why would anyone go?

The regulars all had their answers.

**The Lover**

Fumiko Kijima came in every afternoon for a week before she could say it. She was a tailor's daughter, and two years ago the man she loved — Goro, a drummer with beautiful timing and terrible prospects — had boarded a steamer for America without proposing, and she had pretended to be glad for him, and the pretend had hardened into a wall she now lived behind.

"I want the morning he left," she said. "He came here. Eighteenth of April. He sat right there."

"Understand the rules?" said Nagare.

"I understand he can't stay and I can't change it. I understand he's married now in San Francisco. I just want to know what we were. One cup's worth."

The coffee was poured. The steam rose. Fumiko closed her eyes—

—and opened them on cigar smoke and morning light, and there he was across the table, young, filling the whole room the way he always had, drumming his fingers on his hat.

"You came to see me off," said Goro, delighted.

"I came to work out what I'm supposed to do with you," said Fumiko, in a voice from a braver decade.

"Fumiko — I'm not leaving because I don't love you. I'm leaving because I'm a coward about it. If I asked you to wait for a drummer with no money, I'd be spending your life. So I'm spending mine instead, and I want it on the record: there was nobody before you, and there'll be nobody after."

"You can't put that on the record and get on a boat."

"I'm a drummer," said Goro. "I put impossible things on the record for a living."

He slid a small folded square across the tablecloth — paper, soft with handling — and before she could open it, her coffee, off to the side of time, was cold.

Nothing had changed. Goro was still married in San Francisco. The wall was still a wall.

But her fist was closed, and inside it — this is the part Nagare never explained — was the square of paper: a train ticket stub from the eighteenth of April, on which a drummer had written, in pencil, *Ask me. I was going to ask. Wait for the last train and ask me.*

Some things cross over. Fumiko held it a long time, then laughed and cried in the same breath, and Nagare marked her tab *paid*.

**The Wife**

Kohtake was a nurse with a capuchin's calm and a husband named Fusagi who was forgetting her — not all at once, but shelf by shelf, like a library closing for the season. He'd begun leaving notes to himself labeled THIS IS YOUR WIFE, and she had begun pretending not to find them.

"The morning of his diagnosis," she said, in the chair. "Two Junes ago. He came straight here from the doctor."

In the past, Fusagi sat alone with an untouched coffee and the face of a monkey holding a bomb gently, so it doesn't go off.

"You know already," Kohtake said, sitting across from her own husband in another June. "You've just come from the doctor. You've told me it's nothing."

"It's nothing," said Fusagi, automatically, and then looked at her, and something in him gave up the lie like a hostage. "It's everything. It's everything, and I have maybe three good years, and I'm not going to spend them making you watch."

"You don't get to choose that part," said Kohtake. "I do. I'm choosing. So here's what I want: write it down. Write me letters while you still know me. Write them now, tonight, while I'm still in there. So that on the worst days, I can put a letter in your paw and you'll remember who you were to me, even if you can't remember who I am."

Fusagi stared. "How did you know I—"

"Because I'm your wife," said Kohtake, "and you write everything down."

The coffee went cold. The present came back, unchanged: Fusagi upstairs forgetting, shelf by shelf.

Except that that night, hunting for his old reading glasses, Kohtake found, in the locked bottom drawer he called his "important box," forty-one letters, dated forward, the last one labeled only *for the day after the last day* — every single one beginning the same way: *Even if you have forgotten me, I have not forgotten you. Kohtake is the one who comes with tea. Trust her entirely.*

He had started them the very night of the diagnosis. She went back in time to ask for what he'd already done. That, Nagare said, refilling her cup, is the best reason to go.

**The Sister**

Hirai ran a hot-spring inn up north and pretended she didn't; down in the city she wore city fur and called herself a flight attendant, and her sister Kumi ran the inn alone and wrote every month, and Hirai never answered, and then one spring Kumi's letters stopped, because Kumi had been driving to the station in snow, and the road had other plans.

"I don't want the accident," Hirai said, gripping the armrests. "I want the week before. She came here. She sat here. She told me—" Hirai's voice went to gravel. "She told me she was sick of me. I want to unsay what I said."

"Nothing unsays," said Nagare. "The present doesn't change."

"Then I'll settle for hearing her say it wasn't true."

In the past, Kumi sat with her coat still on, snow in her fur, younger than Hirai ever let herself remember.

"You look terrible," said Kumi, fondly.

"You look provincial," said Hirai, and it came out with love on it, plain as day, and they both heard it and pretended not to. Then Hirai said, "Kumi. Everything I said that night — that I'd rather swing buses in the city than carry one more tray in that stupid inn — I said it to hurt you because you got to stay and I got sent away, and it was a lie, and I've been living in the lie because it has better lighting."

"I know," said Kumi. "It's our inn, silly. The whole house is just waiting for you to get tired of being impressive."

Coffee, cold. Present, back. Kumi still gone.

Hirai gave notice at the airline that evening. The inn would open in spring under the second generation. Some cups, she told Nagare on her last visit, are worth drinking even knowing exactly when they'll go cold.

**The Daughter**

The last traveler that year was Kei, Nagare's own wife — pregnant, and warned by every doctor in the city that a birth, for her heart, might be a door that only opened one way. She had stared at the chair all through her pregnancy, and one snowy night she finally sat in it.

"I want the future," she told Nagare, knowing it was against the rules.

"Only the past," said Nagare. "House rules."

"Then pour," said Kei, "and watch who walks in."

She closed her eyes on the steam, and when she opened them, the café was exactly as it had been, except that a young woman in a coat too warm for any season was sitting opposite, twenty-some years old, with Nagare's exact ears.

"You're her," said the daughter, wonderstruck. "You're before."

"You're her," said Kei, hands over her belly, over her dangerous heart. "You're after."

"I found the chair in the ledger," said the daughter. "I pour here now, did you know? Second generation. You sat me on your lap once and told me the four rules, and then a fifth one, the unwritten one. Say it to me now, so it's true twice."

Kei took her daughter's paws — paws she had not yet built, in a future she could not be sure of reaching — and said it.

"The coffee always gets cold," she said. "That is why it must be drunk warm."

Her cup went cold at 9:41. The snow fell on Kanda. And years later — this is the ledger's testimony, not mine — the café's second-generation proprietress would show visitors a photograph of a mother with her exact ears, and pour, and recite the four rules, and never once, not ever, let a customer's cup go cold.
