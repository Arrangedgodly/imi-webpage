# The Screecher and the Spindle

*A parody of "The Sleeper and the Spindle"*

Three days before her wedding, the young queen of the Canopy could not sleep.

This was considered charming, in a bride. It was not charming at all. The queen — a howler, crowned young, with a screech that could be heard three valleys off and a habit of telling herself the truth when the truth was unwelcome — lay awake in her bower of white flowers and thought: *I am not sure I want this.* The prince was decent, and dull, and the marriage was an arrangement between two troops, and the queen was seventeen.

So she was awake when the three small tamarins came tap-tapping up the royal branch at midnight, with dust in their magnificent mustaches and a report no monkey wants to give a bride three days before her wedding.

"There is a sleep coming," said the eldest tamarin, "out of the lowlands. It came up the valley like flood water. We miners were beneath it, or it would have had us too."

"It took the village of Fallen Fig last Tuesday," said the second. "Every monkey asleep. Mid-chatter, mid-'nana. The fruit ripened and fell and nobody picked it."

"It took the garrison at the Thorn Gate on Thursday," said the third. "Soldiers asleep at their posts, and the vines already growing over them. Majesty — it is coming this way. It will be at the borders of the Canopy in a week."

The queen sat up among her wedding flowers.

"What do we know of it?"

"Only this," said the eldest. "At the center of it, in the lowlands, there is a tower. Everything sleeps in a circle around the tower. And in the tower, they say — though those who said it are asleep now — there is a girl who has been asleep for sixty years."

The queen got out of bed. She took off her betrothal beads and put them away in a carved box, which is a thing that means something when a queen does it in the middle of the night. She dressed in traveling clothes, and under her cloak she put on a vest of woven mail-bark.

"Tell the palace," she said, "that I have gone to look at the flood before it looks at me. Tell the prince — " she considered — "tell him I have a headache. It will be true by morning."

They went down through the sleeping borders of her own country, and the tamarins dug, for tamarins are miners and diggers by birth and guild, tunneling beneath the sleep like punctuation under a long dull sentence. What they found when they surfaced, the queen never forgot.

Villages of sleepers. A mother asleep with her baby asleep upon her, both breathing slow as sap. A hunter asleep in the fork of a tree with an unpeeled banana in his open hand, moss growing gently up his arm. A whole troop, elders and infants, curled together like figs on a platter, dreaming in unison, tails entwined. And over everything, growing softly, the vines.

They came to the tower at dusk, in a courtyard walled in thorned bramble like a cliff. The tamarins dug a passage beneath it, and they climbed the tower by the ivy, and the queen thought, as she climbed: *I am three days from my wedding, and a week from the end of my kingdom, and there is no monkey on this earth who would not turn back. Which is why it must not be a monkey who turns back.*

At the top of the tower was a room. And in the room were two things.

The first was an old woman, a colobus crone, bent as a bow, who sat at a spinning wheel and spun. She spun banana fiber, impossibly fine, into thread that glittered like gold, like the promise at the center of a banana that the peel makes you work for.

The second was a bed. And on the bed lay a young monkey, no older than the queen, sleeping. She was beautiful in the way that frost is beautiful. Her fur was black as the inside of a cave, and her breath was so slow it was barely a rumor.

"I don't get visitors," said the old woman, not looking up. Her voice was papery, and bitter. "I have never once had visitors who came on purpose."

"Who is she?" asked the queen.

"She is the reason for everything you have seen on your way up the valley," said the crone. "She has slept for sixty years. My whole life, I have kept her wheel turning and her sleep unbroken. Fools have come to kiss her awake. Princes. Heroes. You can see how many." She waved one crooked hand at the shadows, and the queen's eyes had grown sharp enough by now to see the sleepers heaped in the corners of the tower room, dreams upon dreams upon dreams, like drifts of leaves.

"You should go home," said the old woman. "You are young. You will want to be kissed awake someday yourself."

But the queen had already climbed onto the bed, because the sleep was coming for her kingdom like water rising, and because there are things a ruler does that nobody else can, and because — though she never said this aloud, even to herself — she was three days from a wedding she had never agreed to, and the price of one kiss seemed a small private thing to pay for the whole world.

She kissed the sleeper.

The kiss took more than breath. The queen felt harvests of days leave her — years of mornings, seasons of running through wet leaves — draining out through her lips like sunlight down a hole, and when she pulled away, gasping, there were gray hairs at her muzzle that had not been there that morning.

The sleeper's eyes opened. They were bright and cold and amused.

"Oh, well done," said the girl on the bed, and stretched like a cat waking from a nap sixty years long. "It's so much nicer being woken by a queen. Queens have so much more to give."

The old woman at the wheel began to laugh, a sound like husks. "Sixty years I have spun for you," she said to the girl. "Sixty years your shell and your servant. And now you go off young again in her stolen mornings, and I am done."

And the crone crumbled, all at once, into dust and husks and one long golden thread, and the spinning wheel stopped. The queen understood then what she had kissed: not a cursed girl, but a witch, who slept to bank her youth like a fire and woke by taking it out of the mouths of rescuers — and the plague of sleep around the tower was only her hunger, breathing outward while she dreamed.

"I shall wash," said the witch pleasantly, "and then I shall travel. There is a wedding in three days, I believe, in the Canopy." She smiled with all her new white teeth. "It will be lovely. Everyone will say how well the bride looks."

And the queen, feeling a hundred years old in a seventeen-year-old's fading mornings, and angrier than she had ever been in her life, did the only thing a howler knows how to do.

She screeched.

It was the queen's own dawn-roar, the roar that shakes figs from fig trees and tells three valleys where the borders run — the oldest alarm in the world. It rattled the tower like a storm, and rolled out across the lowlands, wave after wave, and the sleepers heard it the way stones hear thunder: in their bones. Mothers stirred. Hunters twitched. The rising sleep stopped rising, hissed, and began — slowly, like a tide reversing — to draw back.

The witch put her hands over her ears and laughed. "You cannot fight me with noise, little queen. I have been lullabying kingdoms since before your troop had a name—"

"No," said the queen. "That was the doorbell."

And she held out the golden spindle, spinning wheel's own tooth, the thing that had put the sleep on everyone it had ever pricked.

"You dropped this," said the queen.

And the witch — who had slept sixty years and woken ravenous and new — reached for it, out of pure sixty-year habit, as you or I would reach for a tail we no longer had.

"Ouch," said the witch.

And fell asleep.

They laid her on the bed, in the tower, in the circle of the going-down sleep, and the tamarins packed their hats with the golden thread (for tamarins never miss an opportunity), and the queen climbed down and walked home through a lowlands that was waking up, grumbling, stretching, picking up its half-peeled bananas and wondering where the week had gone.

"Does it count as rescuing," asked the youngest tamarin, "if you also rescued yourself?"

"I don't know," said the queen. "Ask me in a year."

She went home. The prince was told the wedding would be a little late, and being decent, and not entirely dull, said he was relieved, as he had been meaning to ask for more time himself.

And the queen looked older, thereafter, than her years, in the mornings around her muzzle, and did not mind.

She had lost a handful of springs to a witch's mouth, and found, sleeping in the space where they had been, a whole life of her own choosing.

There was, after all, no hurry.
