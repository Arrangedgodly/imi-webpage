# Something Wicked This Way Climbs

*A parody of "Something Wicked This Way Comes"*

By the itch of my thumbs, something wicked this way climbs.

The first strange monkey of October was a drummer of thunder: Tom Furry, a wiry little galago with a knapsack of lightning rods, who arrived in Green Canopy panting. "Storm's coming, fellas. Worst in two hundred years. Gonna peel this town like a ripe fruit. Two bits a rod, and I'll guarantee the lightning goes into the ground instead of into you." He tapped one against the barber pole. "Finest rods in the rainforest, pure copper, honest as the day is damp."

Two boys took him up the tallest fig. Jim Nightshade was a black-furred colobus, born one minute before Halloween, who wanted to be older more than anything in the world. Will Hollowbough, steady as his name, born one minute past midnight on the last day of October, wanted Jim to stay exactly thirteen forever. They had argued about this so often it had become a game, and the game had become a friendship, and the friendship had become the only thing either of them never argued about.

And that night, at three o'clock in the morning, with only the smell of kettle corn to announce it, a carnival swung into the old orchard meadow and unfolded like a nightmare getting comfortable.

By dawn there were pennants, a calliope moaning low and sad — playing a tune nobody had heard before, because it was being played backwards — and a poster nailed to the great oak:

COOGER & DUSK'S PANDEMONIUM SHADOW TROOP — BANANA-SHAPED MEN! THE DWARF! THE DUST WITCH! THE GREAT VINE — RIDES FORWARD AND BACK!

"Forward and back," Jim whispered, who had read the small print. "Forward makes you older. Back makes you young. Will — I could ride it. Two turns. Nobody'd ever call me runt again."

"Nobody calls you runt," said Will.

"Nobody loud," said Jim.

They spied from the orchard wall at dawn, and this is what they saw: Mr. Cooger, thin as a whip, feeding the Great Vine a banana to test it. The banana rode forward two turns and came off the spool green and hard as a doorknob. Mr. Cooger pocketed it and wrote something in a ledger.

"A clock for people," Jim breathed. "Will, it's a clock for people."

The Great Vine was a huge liana wound on a great wooden spool, with carved macaques instead of horses, turning to calliope music that had been dropped on its head. Over all of it stood Mr. Dusk, the Illustrated Mandrill: his fur was dyed all over with pictures — a snake, a falling boy, a small sad gibbon, a witch — each a member of his troop, worn like trophies.

That evening they watched old Mr. Cooger ride the Vine backward and dwindle to a bundle of dry sticks in a suit, and their schoolteacher, Miss Foley, ride forward too far and step off a lost little girl, crying for a mother who had been a girl herself when the carnival last came. Will walked into the mirror maze on a dare from nobody and came out ten minutes older, with a bruise on his cheek he had not had going in: nothing in the maze was behind him, but one of them had been.

And Jim watched the Vine itself the way a hungry monkey watches a window full of pies.

"We have to tell somebody," said Will.

"Tell them what? Nobody would believe it unless they rode it — and that's the trap."

Mr. Dusk found Jim alone by the hoop-toss that afternoon and stooped to him, the pictures rearranging themselves into a smile. "Free ride for the runt," he said, honey-low. "Two turns, big fella. Thirty years old by supper. Nobody calls a thirty-year-old runt."

Jim's paw was on the brass rail before Will's shout reached him — and came off it after, because Will's shout reached him.

They went to Will's father: Charles Hollowbough, fifty-four, night janitor at the Green Canopy Free Library, a man who had been young once and had never quite gotten over the surprise of stopping. He listened, and he didn't laugh.

"By the itch of my thumbs," he said. "All right, boys. Something wicked this way climbs."

"Dad, are you afraid of them?" asked Will.

"Son, I'm fifty-four. I'm afraid of the ladder in the stacks. But fear's only wind, and you can lean on wind if you know which way it blows."

Mr. Dusk came hunting them at dusk, walking down the middle of Main Branch, calling their names in a voice like a closing door. "Will-i-am. Jiiiim. I have your names now, boys. I'm wearing them." And on his shoulder, sure enough, a new picture was prickling in: two small faces, side by side.

The boys hid under the library's floor-grate, breathing through reed straws, while the Dust Witch floated past on a wet cardboard balloon, sniffing for their heartbeats with a nose like a cold raisin. Twice her shadow crossed the grate. Twice Will held Jim's wrist and thought cold library thoughts — card catalogues, overdue slips, the smell of old paper — until the shadow moved on.

Inside, Charles Hollowbough stood in the doorway, and the witch turned on him. She made a fist in the air. His heart stuttered — squeezed — and the old janitor felt the dark come up like water.

And then he laughed.

It hurt her. She squeezed again; he laughed harder — a big, rusty, library-flavored laugh, with footnotes in it. She could stop a frightened heart, but not a glad one; and Charles Hollowbough had read enough to know that the wickedest thing you can do to wickedness is enjoy yourself. "Ha!" he roared, at her, at the dark, at fifty-four. "HA!" The witch dropped like wet cardboard.

Then Mr. Dusk came up the library steps, and Charles took him in his arms — a tremendous, fatherly, unkillable hug — and roared with laughter while the mandrill's dyed troop peeled off his fur like old posters, and out on the meadow the Shadow Troop stumbled apart into small bewildered monkeys, blinking, some of them asking politely for their tails back.

But Jim was lying under the Great Vine. He had ridden it, two turns, the moment the witch's shadow lifted — and it had taken more than it gave; he lay pale and cold as a green banana. Will and his father knelt over him and did the one thing the carnival could never replace: they sang, badly; they danced, worse; they loved him loudly and off-key. And Jim's heart, humiliated into beating by that serenade, thumped once, twice, sat up, and asked for a sandwich.

They never rode the Vine again — except once, gently, backward two minutes, for luck, so that Jim could say he had chosen it, and it had not chosen him. And October came again, as it does. But no calliope ever played in Green Canopy after dark, and no poster worse than LOST: ONE PARROT, ANSWERS TO "PRETTY BOY," SCARCELY LIVES UP TO IT.

On the first of the next October, Will checked his thumbs at dawn. Not so much as an itch.

"Dad," he said, on the way home, "next October—"

"I know, son," said Charles Hollowbough. "We'll keep the thumbs scratched."
