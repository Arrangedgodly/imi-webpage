# By the Picking of My Thumbs

*A parody of "By the Pricking of My Thumbs"*

*By the picking of my thumbs, something wicked this way grooms.* — old jungle rhyme

Tommy and Tuppence Beresford — retired agents of the Great Canopy Intelligence, silver of muzzle and unrepentant of tongue — went to visit Great-Aunt Ada at Sunny Perch, the retirement canopy where old monkeys of good family sat in the sun and complained about the service.

Ada, a fierce elderly drill with opinions like darts, received them under her heated lamp like a queen receiving a slightly tardy deputation. The food, she said, was boiled fibre and dishwater. The staff were thieves — she had counted the teaspoons twice and both counts disagreed with the matron's. The central heating was a plot to roast the elderly for their fig-hoards, and the whole establishment was going to the dogs, whoever the dogs thought they were. She then demanded to know why Tommy had never brought her a title, ate three macaroons to steady herself, and asked whether Tuppence was quite well, which in Ada's mouth was an accusation.

On the way out, a gentle whiskery loris in the next nest clutched Tuppence's arm. Her name, the door-plate said, was Mrs. Lancaster.

"You were asking about the trees," the loris whispered. "Was it your poor child? The one behind the bark? Behind the bark in the chimney tree — that's where they put it. Dead hands pass me my milk."

"Tuppence, old thing," said Tommy in the car home, "that loris has bats in her fig tree."

"Mrs. Lancaster," said Tuppence slowly, "has sadness in her tree, and something else besides."

Aunt Ada died the following month — suddenly, of her heart, said Dr. Murray, and everyone said what a shame, and only the canary in the day-room looked unconvinced. Among Ada's effects lay a small bark-painting of a crooked fig tree beside a dry streambed, given to Ada by Mrs. Lancaster — who had wanted, Tuppence learned, to give her *water-colours* instead, only there wasn't any water. But Mrs. Lancaster, the matron said, had been taken away by her people — a smooth grey titi called Mr. Eccles — to another home, and another, and another, and no forwarding branch.

"The odd thing," Tuppence told the matron, "is that Ada said she never gave any painting *to* the loris at all. She said it was left in her nest, the way things are left."

"Old monkeys mislay their memories, dear," said the matron, in the voice of a monkey who had mislaid one on purpose.

Tommy was summoned to a hush-hush conference on the standardization of banana weights, and left Tuppence on her honour to touch nothing. His letters arrived nightly. *Day one: the sub-committee has divided on the metric banana.* *Day three: the metric banana has been shelved; the committee now fears the curved banana.* *Day four: two delegates not speaking; the conference may last the month.* For four days Tuppence was as good as gold, which for Tuppence was a personal best. On the fifth she propped the bark-painting on the passenger seat and went tree-hunting.

Estate agents were no use ("Sultana Cottage? Sold before the war, madam; the file has since been eaten by weevils, like all our best files"). Matron Partridge at Sunny Perch was less than no use. So Tuppence did it the Beresford way: village by village, buying figs, complimenting infants, and asking at every post office whether anyone had a dry stream with pretensions. On the ninth day, in a valley that smelled of wet slate and elderflower, the crooked fig walked out of the painting and stood still: bark, bend, and shadow. Marsh Bonkey. The streambed was dry as a sermon; and tucked under the fig, exactly as the loris's paws had painted them, stood a little treehouse with a hollow chimney, called Sultana Cottage.

The vicar's wife gave Tuppence tea and the whole story, which was forty years stale and none the better kept for it. A young couple had kept the cottage; there had been a lodger, courteous and rich, who paid a year's rent in advance; there had been a baby that nobody ever saw carried out. One night there was crying, which everyone afterwards agreed never to have heard, and by morning the couple was gone, the lodger was gone, and the cottage was sold with the furniture, including — the vicar's wife lowered her voice — a chimney with a hollow in it that no sweep would poke.

"You went inside, of course," said Tuppence to herself an hour later, because of course she had. The current owner, a kindly woolly monkey, let "the lady from the heritage people" look all she liked. The cottage kept its secrets well; but the chimney did not. Behind the loose bark, exactly where dead hands had pointed in dreams, Tuppence found small bones, and a tin whistle gone green.

She was still kneeling there when Mr. Eccles himself came through the doorway, smooth as a peeled stem.

"That was never yours to open, Mrs. Beresford," he said. "Accidents happen in the canopy, you know. Old ladies fall. Aunt Ada fell. Mrs. Lancaster will fall, when I find which home I mislabeled her in. The inheritances fall to me."

"You've been harvesting old monkeys," said Tuppence, "like overripe figs."

"I've been pruning," said Mr. Eccles, and stepped forward. "And you, Mrs. Beresford, are a very ripe fig indeed. Your husband, I am told, is at a conference. How dull for him."

"You've no idea," said Tuppence. "He writes to me about bananas."

He had not reckoned on two things: that Tuppence had kept her war-time arms, and that Tommy's conference had been dull beyond endurance — so dull that on day five he had resigned from the metric banana entirely, wired three old colleagues from the Service, and followed the route map she had helpfully left under the marmalade. They took Mr. Eccles together in the chimney corner, a pincer movement with macaroon crumbs still on it, while constables summoned by the neighbours' screams — screams of delight, for once — came up the trunk two at a time.

The fig-paste in Mr. Eccles's knapsack did not agree with the Government analyst, and neither did his ledger. Aunt Ada, it emerged, had fallen; so had two elderly aunts before her, all of whom had left their fig-hoards to the smooth grey titi who arranged their removals. And the courtyard-lodger of forty years back, the constables found, had been Eccles's own father — a courteous monkey with a wife in another county and a second baby he could not afford; the child had stood between two fig-orchards, and the family had been tidying ever since. Tidiness, Tommy observed, was hereditary in some trees. The bones behind the bark were laid to rest at last under a headstone paid for by the Beresfords.

Mrs. Lancaster was found a month later at a far sanctuary by the sea, happily painting trees, every one of them with the bark on the chimney loose. "They gave me my paint again," she told Tuppence, patting her hand. "The milk here comes with no hands at all, it's the most wonderful milk. And I gave the little one back its whistle." For buried with the bones, when the constables went down, they had found it: re-buried, freshly, with a wreath of fig leaves.

"You know what fascinates me," said Tommy that night, grooming the twigs out of her fur. "The rhyme had it wrong all along."

"By the picking of my thumbs," said Tuppence drowsily, "something wicked this way grooms — and gets groomed right back, once it's caught."

"And your thumbs?"

"Merely itched," said Tuppence. "Merely itched."
