# Frankensimian

*A parody of "Frankenstein"*

*(To Mrs. Saville, Mango-land — letters from my voyage.)*

You will rejoice to hear that no disaster has accompanied the commencement of my expedition up the North Branch in search of the Origin Banana, the fruit from which, as the old songs say, all bananas descend.

Already we have had a wonder. As we grounded among floes of drifting papyrus, we beheld a figure of gigantic stature — a gorilla-shaped thing stitched of many colors of fur — driving a raft of tangled vines drawn by a team of startled tapirs. Later, in a fog, we found another: a wasted doctor of philosophy, adrift and raving. His name — given at last, hoarsely — is Victor Frankensimian. To you alone, Margaret, I confide his tale as he told it to me, night after night.

"I was born in the orangutan city," he said. "Gentle, loved, coddled. At Mango-lyt Academy I out-studied every ape. Professor Cramp told me my natural philosophy was so much monkeyshine; Professor Waldmarmoset told me, with tears in his wise old eyes, to 'break through the peel of nature and pour light on the fruit within.' Between the two opinions, Margaret, I chose the flattery, as young apes do. I resolved to do what no naturalist had done: assemble the perfect simian. Not beget — build. The parts I gathered as a housewife gathers bananas: firm backs, ripe limbs, never bruised. I visited the old zoo's morgue by night, the compost pits by day. I made my creature two full branches tall, so the details would be easier — a mistake in every way but one, as my eight-foot grocery list now proves.

"On a night of November rain, from my chamber at the top of an old kapok, I flew my kite into the storm. The vine-line crackled. The yellow eyes opened.

"IT'S A-BRANCH!" I cried. "IT'S A-BRANCH!"

"I have never since called anything alive without first checking my own heart. I fled that night and did not stop until I collapsed in the city, where my dear friend Henry Clever-ape took me in and nursed me through a fever of my own brewing. He asked what I had been working on. 'A giant,' I told him, 'of whom we shall speak no more.' Henry, being a good friend, asked no more. That is the kind of monkey Henry was.

"The creature, meanwhile, went to the forest — and here, Margaret, is the part that shames me most: he was kind. He hid in a hollow beside a capuchin family's fig tree and stacked their firewood at night, groomed their low branches, and let the little ones call him the Good Spirit of the Branch. He learned their words from the parrot that gave the youngest his lessons. He read what the family threw away: *Paradise Lost in Translation*, and the *Lives of Illustrious Primates*. He wore his own fur like a borrowed coat and hoped daily it would be returned.

"When at last he entered their cottage to speak with the blind grandfather — the only one who could not see his patchwork fur — the old monkey touched his face and called him a friend. For one whole minute, Margaret, my creature was happy. Then Felix came home, and shrieked, and the mother fainted, and the cottage burned down — that part was an accident, though a large one — and he fled with a fig-shaped bruise over what would have been, in a luckier creature, a heart.

"He found me on the glacier above the city. 'I am your creature,' he said. 'You made me. I ought to be your Adam — instead I am your add-on. I am alone and miserable, and only an outcast's friendship could make me otherwise. Make me a prime mate, one as wretched as I, and I shall take her to the deep jungle and trouble you no more. If I cannot have a mate, I shall be with you on your wedding branch.'

"He said it sorrowfully, not fiercely, which frightened me more. I agreed. What else does one say to two branches of sorrow on a glacier?

"But first he made me watch what my carelessness had already cost. My little brother Walnut had gone out walking and was stilled — and it was the creature's doing, he confessed it, a punishment laid on me for making him and leaving him — and a young nanny called Justine, gentle Justine, who had helped raise us all, was framed with a seed from my own laboratory. I held the proof of her innocence in my tent. I said nothing — I was 'unwell,' Margaret, I was a coward with a diploma — and Justine confessed a lie to save her neck and was condemned anyway, and the troop said what a monster she was, and I stood in the crowd and let them. 'I did not murder him,' she told the bench, calm as morning, 'but I am ill and confused, and perhaps I did.' They liked that better than the truth. Courts usually do.

"I set to work on an islet of the river, alone with driftwood and guilt. She would have a gibbon's arms and a mandrill's smile. And then I looked at my own reflection in the mixing trough — hollow, wild, the whiskers of a monkey who has not slept in a season — and I thought: if I succeed, I have made her misery; if I fail, I have made him a war. I destroyed her, torn, on the floor. Better a dry world than a race of devils. From the window, over the sea, came a howl of grief and promise both. Better a dry world, I told myself again, louder.

"What followed must be covered by a tasteful banana-leaf, and I will not lift it far. Henry Clever-ape came for me and was found utterly, permanently un-groomed. My own dear Liza — it was our wedding night, and he was as good as his word. My father died of grief before the leaves had finished falling. Each time, one step behind me, one stride ahead of the law: my creature, collecting his inheritance.

"I have followed him since, across the branches of the world, north, always north, to the ice. He leaves me bananas. Ripe ones, at intervals, on the floes. 'Eat,' they say. 'Keep your strength. Our business is not finished.' He is the only monkey alive who has never once considered eating me — and somehow that is the worst of it."

He died in my cabin before the equinox — quiet, at the last, reaching for a banana that was not there. And as I finished his funeral rites, the cabin window darkened: the creature, weeping, hung from the sash.

"He was my father," said the creature, "and I am a wretched orphan. That is also my funeral pile. I shall go far away, where no monkey grooms or is groomed, and let the ice take me, and be at peace. Do not follow. You have a voyage, and I have mine."

He leapt from the window onto the drifting vines and was lost in darkness and distance.

I shall come home, Margaret, sadder and wiser — and inclined, do not laugh, to buy my bananas at the market like everybody else.

— Walnet, Captain
