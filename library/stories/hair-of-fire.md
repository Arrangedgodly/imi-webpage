# Hair of Fire

*A parody of "Heir of Fire"*

The assassin arrived in Wendelychee with hollow eyes, a heart full of grief, and a trunk full of weapons. Caelana the Great — champion killer of Adarlan, breaker of every rule in the Guild of Deadly Things — now spent her days drinking coconut wine and staring at the sea like it owed her money. For a creature once the most feared assassin on two continents, she was now losing arguments with a hammock.

Rowan Whitewhiskers found her there. A langur prince of the Mist Canopy, silver-maned, immortal, with an expression carved from disapproval. He'd been sent by Queen Maeve to collect her.

"Train with me or run," he said. "Choose fast."

Caelana threw a banana at his head. He caught it without blinking and ate it, which was somehow the most infuriating response possible. Then he bit her forearm — a claiming bite, old magic — and said the two most annoying words in her life:

"Get up, *fireheart.*"

He was, in every sense, her prime mate. She hated this.

The training was misery. He knocked her into streams. He knocked her out of trees. He made her carry firewood up a mountain whose name meant "Regret" in the old tongue. She had once burned legions of men — the King of Adarlan's shadow-fire had nothing on hers — and now she couldn't summon so much as a warm thought.

"You will not yield," Rowan said, every single dawn.

"Watch me," said Caelana, and didn't.

His cadre came to inspect her: Rowan's cousins and companions, elite warriors to the last whisker. Lorcan, gravely allergic to joy. Fenrys, who brooded competitively and turned into a wolf to sulk. Gavriel, the Golden, the only one who apologized when he won. They circled her like housecats deciding about a new kitten. Lorcan circled longest, and gave her the nod he otherwise reserved for sharp objects, which from Lorcan was a bouquet of flowers. Then they stayed for dinner, where they argued for three hours about a two-hundred-year-old sparring call. Gavriel won on a technicality; Fenrys bit him; the kitchen staff placed bets.

"They eat like a family," Caelana told Rowan, "which is to say, while threatening each other."

"Wait until the holidays," he said.

Far away, the world turned badly without her. In Adarlan, her cousin Aedion — the Wolf of the North, a grinning general who had survived by pretending loyalty, the family talent — armed a rebellion one lie at a time. He kept her old banner folded under his armor, which was either treason or hope, and dared anyone to ask which. In the glass castle, young King Dorian's ice magic woke in secret, and Chaol, his guard, loved his king more than his own certainty and watched every door anyway. That winter Dorian began icing his bathwater by accident, which is how magic announces itself in polite castles, and Chaol began writing letters he never sent.

And on the black cliffs of the Wastes, Manon Blackbeak — a black-crested macaque witch with iron teeth and thirteen loyal sisters — rode her great lizard into the storm. Her mount was Abraxos: battle-scarred, calm, and famous among the covens for one thing. Mid-attack, he paused to smell a yellowflower.

"Focus," Manon hissed, riding the wind.

"Worth it," said Abraxos's tail, eloquently.

Manon pretended not to approve. She did. Her grandmother, the Matron, ruled the Blackbeaks with teeth and tradition; her second, Asterin, smiled too much for a killer, which was why she was so good at it; and Manon herself was beginning, to her own disgust, to have *opinions* about which orders deserved following. Once, on a long patrol, Abraxos spiraled down without being asked and landed in an entire hillside of yellowflowers, and Manon let him, because the Thirteen were out of earshot and the wind was honest. Asterin took off her helmet. Nobody spoke. The Matron would have called it weakness. The Matron wasn't there.

At night Caelana climbed to a ruined watchtower and carved names into the stone with a trembling claw. Nehemia, who had died for her. Her parents. Her people, scattered and slaughtered. Ten thousand names she would never finish. Rowan found her there once, and without a word began carving on the other side. By the end they had filled the whole wall — hers in fury, his in grief, because an immortal langur carries his own dead, and his had a name too: Lyria. She had been his mate, and she had died young while he could not — the grief an immortal carries, and the reason his disapproval had a floor plan.

After that they stopped pretending to be strangers.

The demons came before she was ready. The Valg — shadow-things that wore stone towers like fur and whispered out of the cracks — had rooted themselves in the dark places of Wendelychee, feeding. When one took a village and began whispering *kill them all, little fireheart, you know you want to*, Caelana stopped numbing. She stopped running.

"Fire is not rage," Rowan had told her that morning. "It's breath. Stop throwing it. Breathe it."

She breathed. The furnace she had kept banked under her ribs since the day Nehemia died opened, and she let it.

The tower burned like dawn. When the smoke cleared, Caelana stood in a crater of warm ash with fire in her hair and the Valg prince's whisper gone from the world.

"That," said Rowan, "will do."

But the Valg had brothers. They came against Mistward, the frontier fortress where the demi-Fae sheltered, walking out of the dark in numbers nobody had counted on. Caelana stood the wall with Rowan's cadre and burned a moat of fire in front of it, and held it hour after hour, breathing as he had taught her, until the last shadow-thing hissed apart and dawn found her still upright, her hair gone to embers, her fear gone with it.

Then Maeve — beautiful, ancient, terrible queen of the Mist Canopy — revealed what she had truly wanted all along: not a champion. A *cage*. She demanded Caelana's blood oath, swore to keep her like a jeweled weapon, and clapped iron on her wrists along with the truth: everything the girl believed about her own blood had been arranged like fruit on a tray — arranged by Maeve, who wanted a fire-bringer she could own. Her cadre stood silent, ordered to stand down.

"Kneel," said Maeve, "and be heir to everything."

"I've met your everything," said Caelana. "The upkeep alone is monstrous."

But iron chains do not hold fire. That's rather the point of it.

Caelana melted her shackles at the wrist and held the cooling rings up like earrings. Rowan came through the canopy like the wrath of an old forest; Fenrys and Gavriel chose him over their queen's orders in the space of one heartbeat; and Maeve's court discovered a langur prince has friends, and teeth.

The night before they flew to war, Caelana stood on the highest bough, and Rowan called her by the name she'd refused for a year.

"Don't call me fireheart," she said. "I know who I am now. My name is Aelin Ashryver Whitethorn Galathynius, and I am the hair of fire."

Below them, an empire's banners caught the sunrise — and, one by one, they began, very quietly, to burn.
