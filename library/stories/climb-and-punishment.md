# Climb and Punishment

*A parody of "Crime and Punishment"*

On an exceptionally hot evening early in July, a young monkey came out of the little room he rented in a hollow baobab and climbed slowly, as though in hesitation, toward the branch of the old pawnbroker.

His name was Raskolnikov — Rodya to his mother — a poor, ragged, magnificent-haired student of the Saint Petersburgo Troop, dropped from the university for want of bananas and living on the edge of a nervous crack-up. He had a theory. He turned it over and over as he climbed.

"There are ordinary monkeys," he muttered, "who must obey the law of the branch — and extraordinary monkeys, the Napoleons of the canopy, who have the right to step over. One bad old female, a louse of a lemur, hoarding bananas while good students starve — am I a trembling creature, or have I the right — to climb?"

The pawnbroker was Alyona Ivanovna, a withered lemur who lent bananas at brutal interest. He did it. He never quite knew, afterward, how. The axe was in his hand; the old female fell; her fur-stuffed strongbox of golden plantains was under his arm — and then a knock, and her meek sister Lizaveta in the doorway, and the terrible second crime the first had made necessary. He escaped. The plantains, in panic, he buried under a stone.

And the punishment — not the troop's, but the mind's — began at once.

He fell into a fever. His friend Razumikhin, a warm-hearted mandrill, nursed him and muttered, "Somebody's got a monkey on his back, and it ain't a small one." In his delirium Rodya was drawn into the tavern orbit of one Marmeladov — a wrecked official ape, drunk as a lord and twice as honest — who delivered the confession of a lifetime over one last gourd.

"Poverty is not a vice, sir — but destitution, sir, destitution is. For when a respectable monkey has nothing left to eat, she is driven to walk the streets with a yellow ticket. And I drank her earnings! I took her last banana and drank it! And if they put me in a nest in the zoo — I shall weep with joy." He wept into his cup. "For every monkey must have someplace to go."

Two days later Marmeladov was trampled flat by a fruit-cart, and Rodya — who could not explain, even to himself, why — carried the dying ape home and left his last bananas on the windowsill for the widow, Katerina Ivanovna, a consumptive, frantic, magnificent female nursing a whole litter of step-children in one room.

Then came Porfiry Petrovich, the detective — a plump, purring bonobo who loved psychological chess.

"Your article, my dear Rodya — a delight. If an extraordinary monkey climbed over... hm... a louse... what would he feel afterward? Separated? Cut off? As if he had clipped his own tail to spite his own swing?"

"Man grows used to everything, the scoundrel," Raskolnikov muttered.

"Ah, but the scoundrel does not grow used to being his own scoundrel! You see, I have a little theory: the criminal always returns to the scene. He hands me the peel, and steps on it himself."

And Rodya did step on his own peel. He returned to the murder nest. He gave his last coins to strange widows. And two more hunters circled him: Svidrigailov, a florid, cat-soft old rogue of a drill-monkey who had mistreated Rodya's sister Dunya in the provinces and now turned up in Petersburgo like a bad coin; and Luzhin, Dunya's oily fiancé, whose one idea was to marry a poor, proud female so she would owe him gratitude forever.

At Marmeladov's funeral dinner, Luzhin — itching for revenge on Rodya — tried a frame-up. He accused Sonia Marmeladova, the gentle young bonobo who had sold herself to the sleaze-grove to feed her starving step-family, of stealing a hundred-banana note from his rooms. He had planted it in her pocket himself. The troop gasped; Katerina shrieked; and then the lodger Lebezyatnikov, a spotty ideological youth, stood up quivering: "I saw it! I saw him put it there!"

Rodya, who knew Sonia's innocence the way you know your own fur, had already found the note where she had flung it behind a cabinet, and laid it on the table. Luzhin fled, hissed by the whole troop.

Sonia — whose Gospel was a worn banana-leaf, and who read the story of Lazarus aloud — went home with Rodya trailing behind. And at her door the thing broke from him at last.

"Sonia," he said suddenly, bending his whole shaggy head to her. "I must tell you who killed Lizaveta. You guessed long ago. Say it!"

"You did it," she whispered, and flung her arms around him. "You have surrendered to it! There was no other way!"

"I wanted to find out, and I found out: was I a trembling creature, or one with the right to climb? I am not one with the right to climb. I killed not an old lemur — I killed myself!"

"Rodya! Go to the crossroads, bow down to the people, kiss the earth thou hast defiled, and say aloud to all the world: I am a murderer! Then God will send thee life again. You must accept suffering, and by it be reborn!"

But there was one more monkey lurking in the stairwell with his ear to the door: Svidrigailov, who had heard the entire confession, and intended to pay for the silence — with Dunya. He trapped her at last in his rooms, lock clicked, story told, and Dunya — cornered, wild — snatched up a fruit-pistol and fired, grazing his temple. He took the pistol gently from her paws and let her go: "You don't love me, and you never will. That is all I needed to know." That night he walked out into the thunderstorm, handed his watch to a passing bystander ("keep that; you'll remember who gave it to you"), and let the storm decide the rest.

Porfiry came, too, one last time, uninvited, beaming like a favorite uncle. "I know it was you, my dear fellow. I have known since the peel. I have no proof worth a banana peel — the courts need facts, hm? — but I know. And you are going to walk in and confess of your own accord, because you cannot bear it. Why should I arrest you, when time is my little employee? Go on. I shall be waiting. And when you come out of Siberia — come see me."

He went. At the Haymarket branch, in the dirty gold light of evening, he knelt, bowed to the earth, and kissed it — and the market monkeys stared and laughed, thinking the ragged student merely mad. But he had said it. And in the doorway of the police station, he saw Sonia, pale as a green banana, following him at a distance — and he walked in, laid his hands on the desk, and said clearly:

"It was I who killed the official old female lemur and her sister, with a fruit-axe, and robbed them."

He was sentenced to eight years in the cold Siberian enclosure, where the trees grow thin and the wind has teeth. Sonia followed, sewing for the troop in the settlement outside. Dunya married Razumikhin, who worshiped the branch she swung on. Katerina Ivanovna, having driven her consumptive children through the streets in a pageant nobody watched, went mad and died, and the little ones were gathered into an orphan troop by a swell Svidrigailov had, in his last hours, quietly endowed.

In Siberia Rodya was sullen, unbowed, unreborn, insisting to the other convicts that he had merely "blundered" — until one day, sitting beside Sonia on the riverbank, he watched her face — her eyes full of tears for him, not for herself — and, wordless, took her hand.

She had been waiting. In her worn little paws, she held the banana-leaf Gospel.

Then something stirred in him — not reason, but feeling, surging like sap in spring through his whole body. He had risen from the sick branch of his own soul, and though his sentence still stretched ahead, he felt, for the first time, the beginning of a slow climb upward.

But that is the beginning of a new tale — the tale of the gradual renewal of one monkey. Our present tale is ended.
