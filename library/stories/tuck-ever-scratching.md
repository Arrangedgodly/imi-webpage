# Tuck Ever-scratching

*A parody of "Tuck Everlasting"*

The first week of August hangs at the very top of summer, like the highest branch of the tallest kapok when the wind forgets to blow. Even the mosquitoes are too hot to be personal.

That was the week Winnie Foster, aged ten, decided to run away.

Winnie was a capuchin of the Foster troop, owners of the oldest fig tree in the valley and a great deal of pride about it. "A Foster," her grandmother said daily, "sits up straight and grooms in the good light." Winnie was not allowed past the fence, was not allowed down the tree unsupervised, and was not allowed, on the whole, to be ten in any way that showed.

She had taken to telling all this to a jungle toad under the gate stone, because the toad never once said "sit up straight." He just blinked, with the deep patience of somebody who has heard every family's nonsense and outlived most of them.

Twice that August, her grandmother had heard music from beyond the wood — a rattle-pod tune, small and tinny and impossible — and declared it ELF MUSIC, in the voice she reserved for things that were beneath explaining. Winnie had heard it too, and thought it sounded less like elves and more like somebody's keepsake box falling downstairs.

Then, on the first very hot day, she slipped under the fence into the wood she had never entered, and found a boy monkey scratching his back against a tree — seventeen forever, gold-limbed, drinking from a thread of water that slid out of the roots of a vast strangler fig.

"I want some of that," said Winnie.

"Ah — no," said the boy. "Sorry. Truly. It looks like water, but it's a bad idea with a very long shelf life."

But she reached for it anyway, and that was how she met the Scratch family. Mae Scratch, in her blue shawl, with her rattle-pod that played music when you wound it — the elf music, as it turned out — came out of the green dark looking dismayed. "Jesse," she sighed, "now we've done it." And behind her came Miles, the elder son, broad and grey and quiet, with the shoulders of somebody who had carried things for a hundred years.

They did not hurt her. They simply set her on their plodding old tapir and carried her off through the trees, which was somehow worse.

"Why can't I just promise not to tell?" Winnie demanded, from the tapir.

"Because we've heard promises before," said Miles. He said it without meanness, the way stones are without meanness. "I had a wife once. And two little ones. After twenty years of me not changing at all, she took them and went to the coast. I sent money. It came back." He looked at the path ahead. "Forever is a long time to be the same age as your own children."

The Scratches lived by a pond: Tuck, broad and sad and comfortable as an old barrel; golden Jesse; quiet Miles; and Mae. Eighty-seven years ago they had all drunk from the little spring, and after that they had simply stopped. No growing older. No falling ill. No falling out of the world. They moved every few years, before the neighbors could notice that not one of them had so much as a new grey hair.

"There is a side effect," Jesse admitted, scratching his shoulder. "An itch. A little perpetual itch. We call it the Ever-scratching. It reminds us we're alive — which, after fifty years, is the only change of any kind we ever feel."

"And I'm sick of being ten," Winnie said. "I want to DO something."

"Then do it slowly," said Jesse. "Here's a thought — drink the water when you're seventeen, and then be seventeen with me forever. We could see everything. The coast, the mountains, all of it."

"Marry you?" said Winnie. "You're forty-three."

"One hundred and four," said Jesse cheerfully, "but I'm holding up well."

That evening Tuck took Winnie drifting on a fallen log out on the pond, and tried to make her understand. He talked the way the water moved.

"See how it flows," he said. "Everything's a wheel, turning and turning, and we're on it — everything that is, except us. We're stones the wheel threw off, sitting still while the whole jungle swings on past. Being part of the wheel, Winnie Foster, dirty and growing and changing and done — that's the blessing. Don't be scared of dying. Be scared of the unlived life."

And Winnie, who had come into the wood wanting nothing but to get away from her own troop, lay awake that night in the Scratches' little tree-house, homesick for the first time in her life, and could not have said which side of the fence home was on.

But a stranger had followed her. A golden lion tamarin in a magnificent yellow pelt, with a smile like a banana peel and a nose for opportunity, he had heard everything from the bushes: the spring, the itch, the not-dying. And he wanted everything.

He went first to the Fosters, grand and oily under the gate stone. "I know where your Winnie is," he announced. "Perfectly safe. Sign the grove over to me — the wood, the fence, the whole property — and I shall return her by supper." The Fosters signed, because there is nothing a frightened family will not initial.

Then he went to the Tucks and swaggered.

"That water is mine now," he said. "I shall bottle it, my dears! A sip of forever, to the tired rich of every jungle! Immortality at a fair price, with a family discount!"

"You'd sell the wheel to the stones," said Tuck, "and the stones to the wheel. You haven't understood one word of it."

When the tamarin laid hands on Winnie to drag her home as proof of contract, Mae — mild, blue-shawled, unendingly patient Mae — swung her rattle-pod and bonked him on the head, and the yellow stranger dropped like a sack of mangoes.

The troop constable, an old baboon, took Mae to the lock-hole tree, and the elders sentenced her to be bound at the Dry Tree for forty days. "If your famous spring is real," the constable said, "she'll not wilt — and then we shall all know something." That could not be allowed to happen.

So Winnie did the bravest, smallest thing of her life. In the dark before dawn she slipped into the lock-hole in Mae's blue shawl, and Mae slipped out on the old tapir. The constable, who had settled down with a gourd of coffee to watch the trail, saw only a small capuchin in a blue shawl, hunched and miserable, exactly as advertised, and drank his coffee with the satisfaction of a job being done. By the time he had finished it and noticed the shawl smelled like ten-year-old, the trail was cold, and the sentence, the elders had to admit, had technically been served.

The yellow stranger woke with a sore head, remembering only mangoes, somehow. He moved to the coast and sold umbrellas.

And that same night, lightning split the great strangler fig. It fell across the little spring and buried it under its roots forever. Winnie, standing in the rain, felt she ought to be sadder about it than she was.

Many, many years later, a plodding old tapir came down the old road with two passengers of a certain age, both scratching idly, forever. They found the fence fallen and the great fig gone to moss, and in the old burying ground they read a marker: WINNIE FOSTER, TROOP-MOTHER. SHE LIVED, SHE SWUNG, SHE WAS LOVED.

Tuck took off his cap and wept the only tear he had.

For Winnie, given a little gourd of the water by Jesse — "drink it when you're seventeen, and then, you know, marry me" — had kept it on her shelf all her life, deciding each year, not yet, and at last deciding, no. Seventeen had come, and gone, and been followed by all the others, and she had swung her own children and her children's children up that same old fig tree, and sat up straight in whatever light she pleased. A life, not an outlasting.

She poured every drop over her friend the jungle toad.

"It's you who need it," she told him. "You've been very patient."

And so, by the gate stone, in the sun, there sits a toad that never ages, blinking — the only creature in the valley who remembers everything, and tells no one. Except once a year, in the first week of August, he croaks all night; and those who have known the family a long time say it sounds, if you listen closely, exactly like a rattle-pod, playing.
