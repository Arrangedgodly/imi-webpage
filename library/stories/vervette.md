# Vervette

*A parody of "Villette"*

There was no possibility of taking a walk that day, and Lucy Vervet — a small gray monkey with a gray coat, gray eyes, and a gray way of saying nothing whatsoever about herself — took a ship instead.

Lucy had been left alone in the world the English-canopy way: quietly. Her employers dissolved, her kin died, her one friend sailed away, and Lucy, whose alarm calls were all turned inward where nobody could hear them, packed a little box, sailed across the Channel of Vines, and washed up in the city of Villette, in the jungle-country of Labassecour — which is to say, the Farmyard — with a fortnight's 'nanas and no plan whatsoever.

Fate, which handles quiet monkeys roughly, handled Lucy roughly. On her first night her purse vanished. She wandered the foreign streets until she dropped into a church, where a kindly old priest-ape gave her a token for bread, and where, at midnight, a tall, golden, self-possessed young monkey steadied her fainting body with the casual strength of somebody who had done it before.

"Drink this," he said, in English-canopy accents. "You are a countrywoman of mine. And no, don't thank me; I can see you'd rather not."

It was Dr. John Graham Bretton — though Lucy didn't learn that for some time, and never told him she knew. Fate had dangled before her eyes the one face from home that could still make her gray heart go vivid.

The token led her, through the kind of coincidence only a provident novelist can arrange, to the pensionnat of Madame Beck — a large, calm, utterly unshakeable baboon who ran her school like a chessboard she could also read. Madame Beck's system was simple: know everything. She went through Lucy's trunk, Lucy's drawers, Lucy's letters, with the serene thoroughness of a monkey delousing a favorite child; she counted the 'nanas in the larder nightly; she had eyes at every keyhole and a report from every corridor. It is a mark of what Lucy was that she observed all this, said nothing, and stayed.

"Madame will suit you," said the little sharp-nosed teacher Zénobie, "if you hold your tongue and groom only where you are told."

Lucy was given the younger monkeys' English and sewing. She was competent, cold, correct. And then the school year's great event arrived: the vaudeville. M. Paul Emanuel, professor of literature — a small, vehement, black-maned macaque with a voice that ranged from a whisper to a thunderclap and a heart worn on both sleeves and every branch — decreed a play, and decreed, absurdly, that the gray English vervet must act a coquette.

"A coquette!" cried Lucy. "Impossible. I am gray all through. I couldn't flirt if you wrote the part on my paws."

"You can, and shall," said M. Paul, with a sovereign nod that knocked over the ink. "You say you cannot be what you are not. Bah. Every monkey contains forty monkeys. I have counted."

He drilled her, hectored her, contradicted her, criticized her grammar, corrected her accent, reproached her coldness, complained of her friendship with Dr. John — and lent her his own books with strange little notes in the margins, and left, on her desk, a bunch of 'nanas with no card whatsoever. Lucy, whose policy was never to feel anything where anyone could see, felt a great deal, and filed it away as carefully as Madame Beck filed her reports.

Dr. John came often, as the school's doctor — and his eyes were not for Lucy, and Lucy saw it before he did. He was for Paulina — little Polly de Bassompierre, a bushbaby no bigger than a blossom, all silk and shyness, who had adored him since she was four years old and he had carried her on his shoulders like a lantern. Watching them together, Lucy performed the quiet amputation that quiet monkeys do so well: she took her own hope, folded it, pressed it flat, and put it away under everything else, where it never once made a sound. Almost never.

It was around this time that the nun began to walk.

She came at twilight, always in the garden — a black-and-white figure, veil and gown, gliding where no monkey should be. She appeared the evenings Lucy sat with M. Paul; she peered into the classroom window while he read; she was seen on the stair, at the gate, under the great pear tree. The servants crossed themselves. Zénobie fainted by request. Madame Beck raised her brows exactly one millimeter and said nothing, which was the most alarming response available.

"A specter," said M. Paul, rattled for once. "This is a Protestant school of yours, Mile. Vervet — can you not lay it?"

"I can lay anything," said Lucy, "if it is flesh. If it is spirit, it must lay me."

The nun came closest on the night Lucy, restless and heart-sore, climbed to the dormitory attic and found, folded in an old box, a nun's habit of real cloth, with a real monkey smell in it. She sat down on the floor and shook. And then, because there is a limit to what even a gray vervet will endure, she carried the thing downstairs, out into the wet garden under the pear tree, dug a hole between the roots, and buried the ghost with her own hands, patting the earth down flat and saying aloud, to nobody, "Lie there."

The school did not believe her until years later, when it came out that the specter had been a lovesick young jackanapes named de Hamal, smuggled over the wall to court Zénobie, doing his courting in costume. The supernatural, in Villette, was always somebody's sweetheart in bedclothes.

Meantime the plot thickened around M. Paul like custard. His confessor, Père Silas, and Madame Beck — who had her own calm designs on him for Zénobie, and her own calm objections to a gray heretic Englishwoman — laid their heads together. M. Paul was shipped off, with ecclesiastical efficiency, to manage a mission plantation on Banana Island, three thousand miles of water away. Before he went, he took Lucy into the garden, under the buried ghost of the pear tree, and raged and stammered and finally produced the truth: that he loved her; that he had loved her since the vaudeville; that he was leaving her the school itself — the pensionnat, the keys, the desks, the whole humming hive — as a nest against his return.

"You will be nobody's servant," he said, glaring at her as though she had personally invented the ocean. "You will have your own door, your own fire, your own fowls. I have quarreled with you four hundred times. I expect to quarrel with you four thousand more. When I come back, Mile. Lucy — that is, Lucy — we will see about the rest. You will write to me, but not too much, and not too often, and not sadly."

Then he kissed his fingers to her, improperly, and was gone.

Lucy opened the school under her own name. She was strict and fair and secretly kind, and the little Labassecour monkeys grew fat and unafraid under her gray government, and for three years she kept a fire in the study and a chair turned toward the sea.

It was on a night of equinoctial storm — the kind of storm that tears the vines up by the roots and makes the very bell rock — that the letters came, and then the shipping lists. A wreck was reported off the coast of Banana Island. Names were withheld; then names were given. Lucy read the paper by the fire, laid it down, and went on being strict and fair and secretly kind; that was all anybody ever saw.

And here the tail must stop, because it never knew for certain. Say, if you like, that the chair was never filled, and the fire kept burning for nobody, and that this is the saddest kind of tail there is. Or say — since the sea is wide, and monkeys are improbably good swimmers, and the shipping lists of Labassecour were forever wrong — that one spring morning a small, vehement, black-maned macaque came up the street with his hat knocked crooked, quarreled with the door, quarreled with the fowls, quarreled with Lucy for crying, and stayed.

The old alarm call rings either way. It is only a bell. Peace, peace, little vervet — the gray coat kept you warm.
