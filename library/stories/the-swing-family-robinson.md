# The Swing Family Robinson

*A parody of "The Swiss Family Robinson" by Johann David Wyss*

For many days we had been tempest-tossed, emigrating in the hold of a troop-carrier to New Guinea, when the crew of mercenary macaques abandoned ship in the night, taking every longboat, and left only the passengers: my husband William, a burly gorilla of great resource; myself, a chimpanzee of, I hope, greater good sense; and our sons — Fritz, bold and restless; Ernest, bookish; Jack, daring and too clever by half; and little Franz, aged four, and famous chiefly for his appetite.

"Children," said my husband at dawn, when the storm had set our ship fast upon a sandbank a stone's throw from a green and mountainous shore, "help is not coming. We shall help ourselves. A family that swings together, stays together."

We made tub-boats of the great calabash gourds, lashed in pairs, and, provisioned with biscuit and bananas, we paddled for shore with Turk the wolfdog swimming behind and the she-goat Grizzle balanced amidships, dignified as a dowager.

The island was a paradise of coconuts, oysters and sugar-cane, and for a day we lived like castaways — which is to say, uncomfortably — until my husband said the fateful words:

"We are going to branch out."

For ten days we ferried to the wreck and back, unloading a cargo so complete that Providence was plainly packing for apes specifically: tools and sailcloth, a chest of seeds, knives and shears, a portable forge, powder-flasks for fireworks, hens, another goat, ham and cheese and vinegar, and two dogs' worth of loyalty in Turk alone. My husband accounted for every nail; Ernest accounted for every species; Franz accounted for every ham.

"Where," I asked, carrying the butter crocks ashore, "does a troop-carrier to New Guinea carry butter?"

"Foresight, my love," said my husband. "European foresight."

We oystered on the rocks; we sugared our biscuit with cane; we lectured. My husband never missed the chance of a lesson, and no monkey on that island ever missed the chance of being lectured. When Ernest asked if the fig tree was fruitful, he received forty minutes on the Moraceae family; when Franz asked if he might eat the pretty berry, he received a funeral oration on alkaloids instead of the berry.

But it was the great sycamore fig that decided us. It stood in a grove of its own, vast as a cathedral, its crown a green hall of limbs.

"Here," said my husband, patting the trunk, "we shall build Falconhurst," and he set every monkey of us to work. We raised a platform of planks among the great boughs, roofed it with bark and sailcloth, partitioned off sleeping-nooks for each of us, and wound a stair of plaited vine about the trunk — with a drawing-up ladder at the top, "for," said Fritz, "we are Robinsons, but we are not foolish Robinsons."

We named every place as we went, as castaways must: Safety Bay, Tentholm, Wood Grange, Falconhurst, and, for a certain rocky promontory where Jack fell in twice, Jack's Folly.

The menagerie grew faster than the house. Jack adopted a young tapir that followed him like a puppy; Ernest adopted a toucan that stole his buttons and hid them, with scholarly method, by color; Fritz tamed an onager foal to carry panniers, and later a great buffalo, which he rode into camp at a gallop, scattering hens and dignity; and Franz adopted, in his heart, everything, including a grasshopper.

"We are becoming," said Ernest, reading beneath the fig, "less a family and more a traveling menagerie."

"We are becoming," said my husband, "a colony."

We discovered, in time, a cave of grottoes behind a waterfall on the far shore, and fitted it with shelves, a forge, and doors of bark, and called it Rockburg, our winter house; and when the rainy season came, we sat out the storms in our dry stone rooms like monkeys of consequence. Our only true enemies were the wild raiding monkeys, who stripped our plantings of green bananas whenever the dogs dozed — until Jack strung the garden with vine-tripwires and tin pots, and the raiders retired, offended by the percussion. Once a great python took a notion to our hen-coop, and my husband met it with a powder-flask and a thunderclap of his own devising; the python departed for the far shore, and the hens laid double for a month out of pure patriotic feeling.

By the third year we had candle-berries strung through Falconhurst, a wheel going at the stream, pottery burned in our own kiln, and a calendar carved on the trunk — which Franz consulted chiefly before meals. My husband declared us the smallest kingdom in the jungle, with himself as prime minister, myself as treasury, and the boys as the entire civil service, the army, and the opposition, frequently at the same hour.

Years passed in this manner, busy as a barrel of monkeys and twice as happy. And then one season Fritz, exploring in the far south, came racing home out of breath.

"Penguins," he gasped. "A whole regiment of penguins — and, Father — among the penguins — a monkey!"

Her name was Jenny, a young capuchin in trousers patched from seabird feathers, marooned on that rocky isle by a mutinous crew, her story speedily told: a father in New Guinea, a ship taken over by pirate macaques, a long and cold sojourn among the penguins, who were kind, she said, but terrible gossips. Fritz rowed her home in the gourd-tub through the reef, half in love before they touched the shore, and I took her to my heart on the spot, and into the nook next to mine.

"Jenny," said I, "you shall be our daughter, and Falconhurst shall be your nest."

She taught us all her penguin-gossip, corrected our tree-stair railings, and proved a better engineer than any of my sons, which Fritz took with suspicious grace.

When at last, in our tenth year, a sail stood into Safety Bay — a real ship, with honest seamen — we ran up a flag of our own devising from the fig tree: a green banana upon a white field, with the single word RESCUED.

The ship brought letters, and news, and Jenny's father — who embraced his daughter, then my Fritz, then Fritz again with a long look that settled several matters — and an offer of passage home to Switzerland. The captain walked up our vine-stair like a pilgrim, touched the plank table, and confessed that in thirty years of landfalls he had never once climbed a staircase that came down to meet him.

We held a family council in the crown of the fig, with the toucan asleep on Ernest's shoulder.

"This island is our home now," said Fritz. "It is New Chimpania."

"Agreed," said Jack. "Besides, no one in Switzerland has a treehouse with a drawbridge ladder."

"I should like to see a university," said Ernest wistfully, "with books in it that no ape on this island has read six times."

"Some of us must fetch goods and greet the world," said my husband mildly. "Some of us must stay and mind the bananas."

And so it was arranged, in the Robinson way, with everyone content: Fritz to sail with Jenny and her father to New Guinea (the two of them were, by now, barely separable by crowbar); Ernest to the universities of Europe, with a crate of his own notes on the toucan; Jack and Franz to stay and help us plant, and defend the banana groves with tin pots.

"And I," said I, looking round at my hairy, happy family in the green dusk, "am the mother."
