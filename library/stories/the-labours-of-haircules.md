# The Labours of Haircules

*A parody of "The Labours of Hercules"*

In the whitest, best-ordered treehouse in Landon, Hercule Pongo examined his magnificent whiskers in the glass and announced his retirement.

"I shall grow bananas, Miss Lemur. Marrows are for commoners; bananas demand theartist's soul."

Miss Lemur, his secretary, who filed everything under B for Banana and had never once been surprised in twelve years, held up the morning's letters. "Fourteen new clients, monsieur. Two duchesses, an ambassador, and something from a countess that smells of paste jewels."

Pongo sighed the sigh of a monkey whose egg-shaped head carried the world's weight. "Fate, Miss Lemur, named me Hercule. Very well. Haircules, the strong-ape of the old myths, performed twelve labours before his rest. I shall do the same. Only cases that mirror the Twelve Labours — *those* I accept. The rest may go to lesser primates."

"The lesser primates will drink your health, monsieur," said Miss Lemur, and opened the first file.

**The Nemean Lion Tamarin.** The first was easy. The Empress's golden lion tamarin — finest coat at the All-Britannia Primate Show — had vanished from its cushion, and a ransom note had arrived oiled with flattery. Pongo inspected the substitute left in its nest and curled his lip. "The Nemean Lion's hide, they say, no weapon could pierce. This tamarin's coat, no comb could counterfeit. Observe — the fur lies brushed *against* the grain, and the show-ring never brushes against. A monkey who has never won a ribbon dressed this stand-in." The fur-forgers' ring confessed by teatime.

**The Lernaean Hydra.** The second labour grew two heads for every one he cut off. A whisper at the Great Perch — that a certain dowager had sold her troop's fig-trees — bred by morning nine whispers, each worse. "You do not fight the Hydra with scissors," Pongo told the dowager, and gathered the entire troop at grooming hour. "Gossip, mes amis, dies only when named. *I* begin the whisper. *Watch* who blushes to feed it." The originator, exposed, went off to grow bananas somewhere distant.

**The Erymanthian Boar.** The fourth was a hunt. A stone-killing gorilla called Ape-Hand Grimm — wanted in three cantons for the fig-express murders — lay holed up above the snow-line at the Roche-de-Singe hotel, beyond the reach of warrants, dining well on silence and other guests' tribute. Pongo did not bring gendarmies; he brought a reputation, and let it be whispered at dinner that the stolen fig-express securities rode in his own kit-bag. "The boar comes to the hunter, Miss Lemur, when the hunter is the only meal on the mountain." Grimm came down the corridor at midnight, and found the corridor pre-sold to the cantonal police at the price of one orangutan's arrogance. The myth says the Boar was carried back alive, squealing. Grimm merely sulked.

**The Augean Sleeping-Ledges.** The fifth stank, literally. The Old Baobab Registry's records — thirty years of dung dropped nightly upon honest ledgers — had buried the truth of half the county's inheritances. "Haircules diverted two rivers through the stables in a day," said Pongo, rolling up his sleeves. "We shall divert two streams of testimony — the junior clerks and the night clerks — through the ledgers simultaneously." By dawn the muck had run off, and four vanished estates floated to the surface.

**The Stymphalian Birds.** The sixth was a flock. The Stympheasant sisters — two birds of paradise the colour of stolen jewellery — fed handsomely through the Landon season on the secrets of others, picking careers clean and dropping the husks in the gossip vines. "The myth prescribes bronze rattles," said Pongo, "for birds that breed in a marsh of talk. Very well. We shall out-screech them." He bought, through an intermediary, one week of a dying society column, and filled it himself — with kindness, inventions, and flattery so rich it stuck to the beak. Starved of secrets by a louder and sweeter screech, the Stympheasants migrated to the continent, complaining of the climate.

**The Cretan Bull.** The seventh bellowed only at night. A young bull-macaque of a proud old house woke each dawn to wreckage — bark stripped, trellises down — and trembled before the legend of the Cretan Bull in his blood: his grandsire's madness, come again. "Your lineage, monsieur, is mad only in its account-books," Pongo told him. "Observe the wrecked trellis: broken from the *outside*, by a light climber wearing garden gloves. Observe your morning headache: not lunacy but durian-wine, administered nightly in your cocoa. The bull of the family is not you." The light climber proved to be a dear cousin whose own claim to the orchard had, until that week, been browsing quietly in second place.

**The Horses of Diomedes.** The eighth were fed on travellers. The Dragon-Fruit Stables ran the sweetest ruin in Landon — durian-wine and faro for the young of good families, their fig-hoards harvested at the door, their letters home written for them in a warm, loving paw. An old senator brought Pongo his daughter, who had begun to lie beautifully, and Pongo went in as a rich uncle with a weakness for faro. The wild mares of the myth, he reminded Miss Lemur, were tamed by being fed their own keeper; the jungle being civilized, Pongo instead fed them their customers — one anonymous list, posted to a bishop, three magistrates, and the Morning Fig. The stables closed in an afternoon, and the senator's daughter wrote her own letters again, badly, and with relief.

(Labours the third, ninth, and tenth Pongo declined to file. The Arcadian Stag involved a duchess, a Titian, and a discretion he had sold cheaply; the Girdle of Hippolyta involved the same duchess wearing less Titian; and the Flock of Geryon involved, said Miss Lemur, locking the drawer.)

**The Golden Bananas of the Hesperides.** The eleventh was delicate: a golden banana, family treasure of the Hesperide bonobos, vanished nightly from the crystal grove where a dozing monitor lizard guarded it — yet each morning returned, faintly peel-warmed. "The dragon sleeps; the fruit walks; nothing is ever missing by daylight," Pongo mused. "Then the thief does not steal the banana — the thief *practices*. It is the youngest daughter, rehearsing her inheritance." She confessed, sobbing, into his whiskers. He prescribed a gold-painted plantain for practice, and everybody's honour survived.

**The Capture of Cerberus.** The twelfth came at last: an invitation — perfume, paste rubies, a familiar scrawl — from the Countess Vera Rossakoff, the one adventure of Pongo's heart, now mistress of the Hot Springs, the deepest, most exclusive grove-club in the jungle, guarded by Furberus: a triple-voiced gatekeeper howler who challenged each guest thrice, once per head. Within, the Countess was smuggling something; stolen garnets, the whisper ran, moved through the club like fleas through a troop.

Pongo descended into the steam, past Furberus's triple challenge, and bowed over the Countess's paw.

"You have come to arrest me, my little orange sausage," she said sadly.

"I have come," said Pongo, "to demonstrate that the garnets travel in the three collars of Furberus, whose heads quarrel and never compare notes. The thieving groomer who used him has fled. Your club is innocent, madame — as I always knew. As I always *chose* to know."

The Countess laughed until her paste rubies rattled, and kissed him on both cheeks, and ruined one whisker.

"Twelve labours concluded," said Miss Lemur that spring, entering the last file. "The banana grove, monsieur? I have ordered the seedlings."

"The banana grove," said Hercule Pongo, gazing into the middle distance, where a countess's laugh was presumably ringing, "may ripen without me. The labours of Haircules are finished, Miss Lemur. The labour of a detective begins afresh each morning — and the world, unhappily, never runs out of monkeys."

He sent out for two bananas, and opened the first letter of his un-retirement.
