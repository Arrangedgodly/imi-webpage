# And Then There Were 'Nanas

*A parody of "And Then There Were None"*

Ten strangers were ferried to 'Nana Island, a rocky fang off the Figmeshire coast, each holding an invitation from one Mr. U. N. Bananan. "Unknown," muttered the old judge. "Curious."

They were a mixed troop: Justice Wargruff, a hanging baboon too old to hang anyone but still fond of the expression; Captain Longarm, a soldier monkey with a revolver; Vera Vine-thorne, a young governess; Dr. Armstrong, a fashionable physician; Anthony Monkeystone, a rich young chimp with a fruit-cart racer and no conscience; Miss Emily Brent, an elderly gentlemonkey of iron virtue; General Macaque, grey and lost in time; William Blore, an ex-policeman posing as a guide; and Mr. and Mrs. Rogers, the hired orangu-servants.

On the dining-room table stood a centerpiece of ten little porcelain bananas.

That evening, over the soup, a voice filled the house — the gramophone in the next room, recorded by their absent host. It accused them, each by name.

"Vera Vine-thorne — you let a small chimp drown in your care. Captain Longarm — you left twenty coconut-pickers to die of thirst. Justice Wargruff — you hanged an innocent monkey. General Macaque — you sent your troop, your literal troop, to its death. Dr. Armstrong — you operated drunk. Anthony Monkeystone — your racer struck two young chimps and you drove on, laughing. Emily Brent — you cast out a young grooming-maid in ruin. William Blore — your false testimony hanged a prisoner. Mr. and Mrs. Rogers — you withheld the old bonobo's medicine for a legacy."

"Lies!" "Slander!" "I hardly knew the woman."

"Monkeys," said Justice Wargruff, "we are accused of murder. All of us. The law could not touch us. It appears something else intends to."

The first 'nana went that night. Anthony Monkeystone raised his banana-wine, toasted "to life!" — and choked. When they carried him out, one porcelain banana had snapped off the centerpiece and rolled under the table.

*Nine little 'nanas sat up very late; one overslept himself, and then there were eight.*

The storm cut the island off — no boat, no signal could leave. The next morning Mrs. Rogers did not wake; the sleeping draught had been generous. Rogers stood over her with a tray, shaking. "I only put out the glass," he whispered. "I only ever put out the glass."

*Eight little 'nanas traveled in the fig shire; one said he'd stay there, and then there were seven.*

General Macaque walked in the mango grove, waiting, he said, for the end — "I sent them out and none came back; a monkey can only wait so long" — and was struck down by a driftwood limb. Seven little 'nanas — and now everyone read the rhyme framed above the mantle, compulsively, the way a tongue finds a sore tooth:

*Seven little 'nanas chopping up sticks; one chopped himself in halves, and then there were six.*

They searched the island: no eleventh monkey, no hidden boat — only themselves. Which meant one of them was doing it. They stopped sleeping, ate nothing unwatched, and sat in a prickling circle. Rogers was struck down splitting firewood, the axe somehow not in his own hands. Miss Brent was found as if stung by a wasp — one tiny mark, a syringe gone from the doctor's bag — and on her sheet, in kelp, someone had written the name of the maid she had ruined.

*Six little 'nanas going out for law; one got stuck in Chancery, and then there were five.*

Justice Wargruff took charge. "We are the only ten — no, five — suspects on this rock. I propose a scheme: nothing unwatched, nobody alone." His dark eyes glittered with something the others mistook for vigor. At breakfast he was found in his chair, robed in a red curtain, a judge's wig askew, berry-red trickling from his temple, a mock wig-line drawn in juice. "Shot," said Dr. Armstrong, "or drugged and painted. I can't tell which — the light is vile."

*Five little 'nanas going out to sea; a red herring swallowed one, and then there were four.*

The four left alive — Longarm, Blore, Vera, Armstrong — made a desperate arithmetic of it. Blore was blunt: "It's the doctor. Only he could fake his own readings." Armstrong protested his innocence to the moon. They agreed to signal the mainland at dawn from the sea-rocks. And in the night, Armstrong slipped out to meet someone — and vanished. They found him tangled in the seaweed shallows at low tide, and the tide, at least, had an alibi.

"Which means," said Longarm on the rocks, reloading, "it's one of us three. Since the doctor is dead and I am not the killer, Blore — it's you."

They went back for breakfast. Blore, first indoors, paused under the balcony — a sound like the sky clearing its throat — and the great carved stone gorilla came down off the rail and sat on him. Vera and Longarm, returning with figs, found only the gorilla, looking smug, which is the permanent expression of stone gorillas.

*Three little 'nanas went to the zoo; a big bear hugged one, and then there were two.*

*Two little 'nanas sitting in the sun; one got frizzled up, and then there was one.*

On the sea-rocks, tired past fear, Vera wrestled the revolver from Longarm's tired grip. "You'd have lasted the night, Captain," she said, almost kindly. "I've been lasting it for weeks." One shot cracked; Longarm fell. And Vera walked back through the empty house, past nine little gaps in the centerpiece, to her room — where a noose hung ready from the beam and a small chimp's voice asked, "Miss Vine-thorne, why did you let me drown?"

*One little 'nana, left all alone; she went and hanged herself, and then there were none.*

Weeks later a fishing boat brought the police, and ten bodies brought ten questions: every death matched the rhyme, and the only explanation — a killer who was also one of the dead — was impossible. The police reconstructors produced eleven theories, ranked them, and disliked all eleven. The rope that hanged Vera was handy; the stone gorilla was heavy; the revolver lay in the corridor, though Vera's hands had been empty; and Justice Wargruff had been shot through the temple with a pistol no one could find.

The answer floated ashore at last, in a corked bottle.

I, Lawrence Wargruff, wrote it — the confession of a hanging judge, in love all his life with justice and, secretly, with the theater of it; a monkey with a terminal illness and an itch to know what it was to kill with the law's own hands. All his life, he wrote, the guilty who escaped him had buzzed in his skull like wasps. He invited nine of them to an island.

Item by item, the judge itemized, with the relish of a monkey arranging fruit: Monkeystone's poison, in the one glass only a conscienceless chimp would claim. Mrs. Rogers' overdose, while her husband was out of the room. The General's driftwood limb, delivered from behind and from above. Rogers' axe, borrowed from the woodpile of his own guilt. Miss Brent's syringe — she had refused the juice and got the wasp. And then his own death, staged with Dr. Armstrong's help, for the doctor believed they were setting a trap for the killer and never understood he was the bait. Armstrong came to the rocks at midnight believing Wargruff would be waiting, alive and judicial; what waited was the sea.

The girl, he conceded, he did not kill. He merely cleared the stage and let her own memory do the rest — which, he wrote, is the only way a monkey is ever truly hanged.

His last paragraph was the trick, and he was proudest of it: after Vera swung, the judge rose from among the "dead," tied his revolver to a stretched liana band, put the muzzle to his head, and let go. The band snapped the pistol out the open window, over the balcony, into the sea — and the hanging judge lay back among the dead he had judged, the eleventh secret on an island of ten.

The bottle ended: "Let the jungle decide: was I a monster, or a monkey of the law? There were ten little 'nanas. Now there are none."

The jungle decided, mostly, to stay out of it. But ever after, no tree on 'Nana Island would grow a banana — and no monkey would take a ferry to Figmeshire again.
