# So Long, and Thanks for All the Figs

*A parody of "So Long, and Thanks for All the Fish"*

The last ever monkey message, displayed prominently at the entrance to the great jungle, read as follows:

**So long, and thanks for all the figs.**

Arthur Chimp read it from the deck of a passing ship, and dropped his banana.

Because Arthur had *seen* the Earth destroyed. He'd watched the Vogons demolish it for a vine-bypass — seen the whole blue-green jungle-world puff into fine dust while he stood stuck on a spaceship, holding a towel. And now here it was, spinning neatly in space, not demolished, slightly smug about it.

He went down and stood in a country lane in the rain, thumb out, until a flying car stopped — a foot above the road, gently — and the driver said, "Jump in, mate, I'm going as far as the village."

Nothing was wrong. That was what was wrong. The world was back, complete with weather, and nobody remembered it ever being gone.

The weather, in fact, had a keeper. Somewhere in the Home Counties, a lorry driver named Rob McKenna catalogued types of rain — two hundred and thirty-three of them, from "fine and insinuating" to "apocalyptic, with wind" — and every one of them happened to him, personally, everywhere he went. He assumed this was normal, or at least that the sky had it in for him alone. Ford Banana-Fetch, arriving on Earth to investigate the Guide's suspicious new finances, recognized him instantly as a rain god, and sold his services to the grateful nations of the world. The rain followed McKenna to drought-stricken regions for enormous fees. "It's not a curse," Ford explained. "It's an oeuvre." McKenna never fully believed him, but he did like the hat.

In the village pub, Arthur found the thing he'd stopped believing existed: somebody who remembered too. A young lady monkey named Figgbranch, sitting with her feet not quite touching the floor — not reached, not stretched, just *not touching*, a few gentle inches of impossible air between her soles and the floorboards.

"You too?" she said, seeing his face. "You've had the hallucinations? The yellow ships in the sky? The feeling you were the last monkey alive on a dying—"

"—and then the dolphins said *so long*," Arthur finished. "Only I thought it was the apes."

"Everyone thinks it was the apes," said Figgbranch. "It was the second-smartest species on Earth that saved it. They'd known the planet was scheduled for demolition for years — dolphins get all the best gossip. They replaced the planet, restored every branch, wiped the memory — and left. They left messages, too. I've been finding them. Little crystal figs, hidden in gift shops all over the world. 'So long, and thanks for all the figs.'"

Arthur moved in with Figgbranch, and the arrangement agreed with both of them enormously.

She taught him to fly — the trick being, as any monkey who's tried knows, to throw yourself at the ground and miss. You must get distracted at the last moment by a passing thought, a lovely monkey, or — Arthur's personal weakness — the smell of a ripe fig. Once he missed the ground entirely and floated over the hedgerows for half an hour until the smell wore off. His early attempts had failed on principle: you cannot throw yourself at the ground and miss on purpose, because purpose is exactly what makes you hit. It is the amnesia that does the trick — a mislaid key, a forgotten errand, the sudden memory of an unwatered plant. Figgbranch called it "the art of being distracted at the right altitude."

Together they flew to California, to a house standing upside-down on a cliff — walls facing inward, front door opening onto a bare porch with no way in. It was called Outside, and belonged to Wonko the Sane, a mandrill in a dressing gown who explained the architecture patiently:

"I noticed the world had gone mad. So I built an asylum for it. Big grey walls all the way round, with the rooms on the outside. As long as we stay in here, Outside, we're the sane ones. It works very well. The world never notices it's inside."

"The dolphins, of course, tried to warn you all for years," Wonko added, sipping something amber. "Their final message was misinterpreted as a sophisticated attempt to do a double backward somersault through a hoop while whistling. Which is more or less how most warnings are received. So they saved you instead." He regarded the sea. "You were their favorite project. Second favorite. After mackerel."

Wonko was calm about nearly everything, which is what sanity is for. He lent them sandwiches for the journey north, and observed that anybody who could find God's Final Message would probably be annoyed by it, in a restful way.

Then, at the very top of the world, they found what they'd both been looking for. On a mountain in the frozen north, written in letters of fire forty feet high, blazing across the snow for anyone who cared to make the journey, was God's Final Message to His Creation:

**WE APOLOGIZE FOR THE INCONVENIENCE.**

It was, they agreed, about the best anyone could have done under the circumstances.

There was one other witness on the mountain that day: Marvin the Paranoid Ape-droid, thirty-seven times older than the universe, circuits failing, limbs long since replaced by whatever the others could find. He had waited several billion years past his warranty to get there, and he read the message slowly, twice.

"Hm," said Marvin. "Yes. That about covers it."

And then, for the first time in his extraordinarily long and miserable operational life, Marvin's circuitry produced a message of its own, which translated roughly as: *well, that's something.* And his brain closed down, quietly and peacefully, with the equivalent of a shrug.

"I think," said Figgbranch, "he died happy."

"Don't be ridiculous," said Arthur. "He died *less miserable*. For Marvin, that was euphoria."

They buried him in the snow beneath the message, which was the only eulogy Marvin would have accepted, mostly because it was an apology and he'd been asking for one for the entire life of the universe.

They came down the mountain to find a small grey spaceship parked at an angle no parking authority would accept, and Ford Banana-Fetch leaning against it, holding a towel, a cocktail, and somebody else's expense account.

"Come on," he said. "The Guide's gone strange, the galaxy's gone worse, and the restaurant at the end of everything still does table service. I need a lift and you two are the only monkeys I know who can fly." He had quit the Guide again, citing the accounts department, and a rumor about the new management he described as "financially fascinating and morally fungal."

And then the two of them flew away with him — actually flew first, then shipped, arms linked, missing the ground together, off the edge of the mountain and out over the world — toward anywhere and everywhere, armed with towels, a ship that owed them a favor, and the last warm crystal fig from the gift shop of the galaxy.

So long, said the universe.

And thanks, said Arthur, for all the figs.
