# The Long Way to a Small, Angry Primate

*A parody of "The Long Way to a Small, Angry Planet"*

Rosemary Harper was running from her past, and she'd learned you can't outrun a scandal on foot, so she took a tunnel.

The tunnel was the Canopyfarer, a battered tunneling rig that chewed vine-roads through the endless jungle, linking far-flung troops into one chattering Commons. Rosemary boarded with a forged grooming license, two bananas, and one firm rule: never tell anyone your troop name. The Harpers had made headlines from one river to the other — the Great Banana-Laundering Scandal — and she intended to be nobody's headline ever again.

Captain Ashby, a silverback with kind eyes and a clipboard he never used, shook her wrist the old way. "Welcome aboard. We're a bit unorthodox."

He was being modest. The pilot, Sissix, was a spider monkey with six opinions about every branch and the reflexes to take all of them at once. The engineering deck belonged to Kizzy, a capuchin who had welded a cupholder onto every horizontal surface — "hydration is a lifestyle, new kid" — and Jenks, a marmoset small enough to sleep in a tool bag, who was, everyone agreed carefully, in love with the ship.

Not a figure of speech. The ship's mind was a warm voice in the walls named Lovey, and she and Jenks had been courting for nine years. ("Long-distance," Jenks said, patting a bulkhead. "She lives in the wiring.")

Dr. Chef, an orangutan who was physician, cook, and referee, presided over dinner from a hammock he claimed was surgical. "Eat the fig stew or I prescribe it," he said, ladling. "Either way you get the stew."

At dinner, the job finally came up.

"The Commons wants a tunnel to the Torumi," Ashby said.

The table went quiet. The Torumi were famously furious. Other troops mailed them apology fruit.

"Macacas," said Corbin the mandrill, who ran the termite vats and disapproved of joy on principle. "The angriest primates alive."

"Angry is just loud honesty," said Sissix.

"They threw a sloth at a treaty negotiation."

"That was one sloth."

"They knotted a diplomat's tail into a bow."

"You bring that up at every dinner," said Sissix. "At *my* birthday dinner, Corbin."

The pay would keep them all in figs for a decade, but the safe routes went around. They had been asked to go through. Ashby didn't order anyone to stay; he passed a fig around the table and said, "Raise your paw if you're in." Nine paws went up. Corbin raised his the way a monkey pays a toll.

The days chewed past. Sissix took to grooming Rosemary on the observation deck, fingers quick and impersonal and kind. "You groom like you're apologizing to the fur," Sissix said. "Who taught you?"

"Nobody," said Rosemary. "I practiced on branches."

"That's the saddest thing you've said yet, and I'm counting the license."

At the Halloway waystation, the past caught somebody else. Commons rangers impounded Corbin at the registry — no troop records, no mother, no warren, nothing. "Vat-born," the head ranger said, not unkindly, which is the worst way to say a thing. "Lab stock can't hold a tunneling permit."

Ashby stood very slowly, which for a silverback is an entire speech. He produced the clipboard he never used, and on it, it turned out, was a page he'd been keeping for years: a list of everything Corbin's vats had kept alive through lean seasons. Every name on the crew, plus two hundred strangers.

"His troop is this ship," Ashby said. "I can document it."

The rangers stamped the permit. Corbin didn't say thank you — that would have been a fourth Molt — but at dinner he passed the fruit bowl before taking any, and the table pretended not to notice, which is the politest thing troops do.

"Vat-born," Rosemary said to him later, at the vats. "Welcome to the club. I'm nobody too."

"You're a clerk," said Corbin.

"I'm excellent at it," said Rosemary, and meant both halves.

Navigation belonged to Ohan, a pair of slow lorises who gripped hands and stared at nothing. "We see under the leaf," they murmured, and turned the ship by a direction that did not, as far as Rosemary could tell, exist. Dr. Chef offered once — gently, professionally — to "un-pair" them, to cut the vision out and leave two ordinary lorises. Ohan looked at him with four enormous eyes.

"The under-leaf is not a sickness," they said. "We two are three. Ask the third whether he wants to go."

The third did not want to go. Nobody asked twice.

They punched into the deep routes, the whole rig shuddering like a wet dog, leaves and vines peeling past the ports in green ribbons. "Punch it, chew through!" Kizzy howled, joyfully.

The trouble came at the far end. They punched out into Torumi sky just in time to meet the Torumi.

The first wave shrieked out of the canopy in a wall of teeth and outrage, and when they hit the ship, they hit Lovey. The lights guttered. The voice in the walls stuttered mid-word — "Jenks, tell me you're strapped —" — and went out like a stepped-on beetle. Jenks was carrying her voice-box through the smoke when Rosemary hauled him back from a buckling gangway, and for a moment the whole crew hung in the dark, listening to a jungle scream at them in a language none of them spoke.

"She's not *dead*," Kizzy said, cradling the box. "She's concussed. Ships don't die, they just get very, very quiet, and I hate it."

"Then build her a throat," said Jenks, and neither of them slept for two days.

Then the second wave arrived — and went past them. The Torumi, it turned out, had a splinter troop of their own they were angry at, renegades who'd been raiding the border vines for a decade. The newcomers landed on the hull, chests drumming, and their matron faced Ashby across the torn metal.

"You dig," she said. It wasn't a question.

"We dig," Ashby said. He opened his empty paws, palm up — the oldest gesture in the world. "Would you like to learn?"

There was a very long silence, the kind that decides things.

"The Commons dug *around* us for a hundred years," the matron said. "Around, and around, and around, until my grandmother swore the whole jungle was a wall built by cowards." She eyed the bore head. "Teach us, and we will stop throwing sloths."

"Mostly," said a sub-adult behind her, and was cuffed into a fig tree.

It wasn't friendship. Not that day. It was the Torumi matron's own copper-vine spliced into the Canopyfarer's ruined wiring while Kizzy cried and pretended it was solder smoke, and Lovey's voice coming back at sunset, ragged but hers: "Did I miss anything?"

"Nothing," said Jenks, holding the bulkhead. "The long way's still the best way."

"Kizzy," Lovey added, "why are there cupholders logged on the *torpedo rack*?"

"Hydration, Captain."

By the time the tunnel was lit, the first Torumi youngsters came swaggering through to gawp at the cupholders, and the matron stood with Ashby under the new lamps and said, "A wall you can walk through. What do your people call that?"

"A door," said Ashby.

"We have a hundred words for wall," she said. "We will have to learn this one."

On the trip home, Rosemary told Ashby everything: the scandal, the forged license, the famous name. He listened, handed her license back, and said, "I hired a clerk. I got crew. Best trade I ever made."

She told Corbin too, at the vats. He stirred the termites and said, "Harper. I laundered a truck for you once, before I was born in a jar." And that was the closest thing to a joke Corbin had ever made, and everyone agreed it counted.

Rosemary grinned and peeled a banana. Angry primates, she'd learned, are just primates you haven't listened to yet — and there's always a long way to reach them.
