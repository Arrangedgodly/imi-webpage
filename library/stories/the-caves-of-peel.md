# The Caves of Peel

*A parody of "The Caves of Steel"*

Elijah Banaley had lived all his life in the Peel — the sealed vault-jungle of New Bark, five hundred terraces of stacked canopy under a steel sky, where banana-yeast vats fed eight billion monkeys who considered the outdoors a rumor invented to frighten kits.

So when Commissioner Enderbanana summoned him at 0600 and said, "You're assigned to Spacetown. A Spacer scientist has been murdered," Banaley's fur stood straight up.

"Ripe bananas," he swore. "Why me?"

"Because the Spacers asked for you personally. And because you'll be working with a partner." The Commissioner's eyes slid away, the way a monkey's do when he knows where the bad banana is buried. "R. Daneel Apeivaw."

Apeivaw was waiting on the express vine-strip: tall, immaculate, chestnut-furred, with the unhurried calm of a monkey who had never once worried about the yeast quota. Banaley studied him all the way to the airlock and finally could not stand it.

"You're one of *them*," he said. "A Spacer. Sent to sneer at how we live, stacked up in our Peel like fruit in a crate."

"You are a monkey of little faith," said Apeivaw pleasantly, and then he told Banaley the truth while Banaley's stomach dropped six terraces. "I am not a Spacer, partner Elijah. I am a bonobot."

"Great green rind." Banaley gripped the handrail. The Three Laws of Bonobotics ran through his head — *a bonobot may not injure a monkey or, through inaction, let a monkey come to harm; a bonobot must pick the banana given except where this conflicts with the First Law* — none of which stopped a machine from making a fool of an Earth detective.

The dead Spacer was Dr. Sartree, and here Banaley learned the case's first ugly fact at the airlock, when the Spacers showed him Sartree's likeness: the murdered monkey wore Apeivaw's exact face. Apeivaw had been built in his maker's image. Identical — unless you knew where a bonobot kept his patience.

Sartree had been bludgeoned inside Spacetown's own bubble, where no Earth monkey could enter unscanned. It was impossible. Which meant, Banaley growled, it only *looked* impossible, and looks were his whole business.

The case dragged him through his own city like a thorn. He took Apeivaw to eat at the Communal Kitchens of Terrace 402, eight hundred monkeys at one trough-terrace of yeastloaf, shoulder to shoulder, tail to tail. "Privacy," Banaley explained, waving a loaf, "is a Spacer perversion. A monkey who eats alone is either sick or plotting."

"Then observe the vats, partner Elijah," said Apeivaw, nodding at the view-shaft down which, forty terraces below, the great yeast rivers bubbled — banana mash and timber sugar, tended night and day. "Your whole species drinks from that pipe."

"Don't remind me. When the vats coughed in '3273, four territories went hungry and ate the Dictionary." It was the Peel's whole secret, and the Wildists' whole grievance, in one view-shaft: eight billion monkeys, one recipe.

Banaley got dusted at the Spacetown checkpoint coming back and was ordered into the public dunk-baths for decontamination, where a squad of ancient aunties scrubbed him to the skin while discussing his case, his diet, and his marriage in front of forty strangers. He emerged pink and enlightened. It was the most privacy he'd had all week.

At home it got worse. Banaley's wife, Jessie, was out three nights running, and when he checked the bedding he found what he was dreading: a Wildist pamphlet, printed on real paper, which in the Peel was practically a crime. THE SKY IS FREE, it said. THE VATS OWN YOU. TEAR THE ROOF OFF.

"Jessie. You're a Wildist."

"I'm a *curious* Wildist," she said, chin up, ears flat. "I go to the meetings for the singing. Elijah — the Peel is full and the sky is not. Our grandchildren will be stacked nine to a branch. Is it so mad to look at the door?"

"It's mad to push on it," said Banaley, "before you know what's behind it." He did not report her. He did, however, follow her to the next meeting — into a cellar full of hooded kit-radicals raving about tearing open the Peel and returning to the open jungle like the ancient wild ones. "The sky, brothers! The SKY!" — and one of them threw a fruit-cart at Apeivaw, and Banaley saw, in the wreckage, his own Commissioner's face looking guilty in a place the Commissioner had no reason to be.

That was the thread. He pulled it, and the pulling led to the Madison Bowl.

The Bowl was the Peel's pressure valve: a cavernous arena where, by ancient custom, mobs were allowed to be mobs — screaming, drumming, burning effigies of whatever the terrace hated that season. That night the effigy was a bonobot built of straw and salvaged tin, and twenty thousand monkeys were chanting for its sparks. Apeivaw insisted they walk the floor itself.

"You're mad," said Banaley. "They'll tear you apart."

"Statistically, they will tear *you* apart, partner Elijah, and the First Law forbids me to allow that. Also, I must scan faces. A rioter lifts his hood to shout. It is the only honest moment this city offers."

They were halfway across when the crowd understood what was walking among them, and the Bowl came apart like a dropped coconut. Banaley went down under a wave of furious fur — and felt himself scooped, iron-gentle, behind a chest like a bulkhead. Apeivaw carried him out through the mob, taking thrown fruit and a chair without complaint, and stood him on his feet at the exit.

"Statement," the bonobot said. "You are bruised in four places and alive in all of them. You are welcome."

And in the doorway, catching his breath, Banaley saw the last thing he needed: the Commissioner's own aide, hood back, wild-eyed, cheering the burning tin.

At dawn, Banaley led Enderbanana out through a service airlock, onto the naked dirt beyond the Peel, under the open sky — the one place a vault-monkey's nerves come apart like wet paper. Apeivaw and the Spacer observers watched from the airlock, and Banaley laid it out as he had rehearsed it, as a good detective serves a banana: peeled, in two hands, no rind to hide behind.

"Two solutions, Commissioner. Solution one: a Spacer did it. Spacers live sixty generations past violence and couldn't bludgeon a wasp. I discard it. Solution two: an Earth monkey entered Spacetown legally — say, on police business, a Commissioner's pass goes unscanned twice — walked up behind Dr. Sartree in the half-dark, and struck at what he believed was a machine. A Wildist sabotage, you'd have called it. Destroy the bonobot that proves monkeys can be replaced. One problem." Banaley watched the small gray monkey begin to shake, the endless blue pressing down like the lid off a barrel of monkeys with the barrel removed. "You couldn't tell them apart. Nobody can. That was the whole point of him. You killed the monkey and missed the machine."

"I loved the Peel," Enderbanana wept. "I only wanted us to stay inside. Safe. Stacked and safe."

"You'll have a cell with a ceiling," said Banaley. "You'll love it."

The Spacers got what they wanted anyway — proof that bonobots could pass among Earth monkeys, that the Peel could be cracked, that the future belonged to whoever branched out first. Banaley stood a long while at the airlock that night, looking at the stars, which were just more ceiling when you got used to them.

"It troubles you," Apeivaw said, "that the box is opening."

"It troubles me that we built the box ourselves and called it the world," Banaley said. Then, grudgingly: "You did well, partner. For a machine."

"For a monkey," said Apeivaw, "you did adequately."

It was, by the standards of their two species, a declaration of eternal friendship. Somewhere far overhead, past the last terrace, past the Peel, past everything Banaley had ever trusted, the sky went on being enormous — and for the first time in eight billion years of monkey history, one small detective in it did not look entirely away.
