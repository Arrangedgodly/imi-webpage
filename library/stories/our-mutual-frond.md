# Our Mutual Frond

*A parody of "Our Mutual Friend"*

In these times of ours, though concerning the exact year no particular monkey need be particular, a boat, dark and quiet, and very like an evil leaf, went breasting up the great brown river under the great brown canopy, with two monkeys in it.

One was old Gaffer Hexam, a hooked and hooded water-rat of a macaque, and the other was his daughter Lizzie, a young monkey with quick dark eyes, holding the sculls. Their trade was to follow the river down and fish up drowned monkeys, holding the bodies for reward. It was Gaffer's proud creed that he never touched a living monkey's pocket — only a dead one's — and he called this honesty, and the river called it nothing, because the river never says anything to anybody, whatever it does to them.

"Hold hard!" cried Gaffer, hauling at something heavy in the dark. "We've got a gold one tonight, my girl. Papers!" He searched the sodden coat with terrible tenderness. "A monkey of property, Lizzie. Our mutual friend, the drowned."

A harder-looking monkey in another boat, one Rogue Riderhood, Gaffer's discarded partner, slid alongside with evil ceremony and gave him good evening — which in their trade was very nearly an accusation. And it was Riderhood who would afterward swear, for spite and reward, that Gaffer made his living helping monkeys into the river as well as out of it.

The drowned monkey of property that night wore the clothes of John Harmon, heir to the great Harmon fortune — the fortune in dust. Not ashes: dust. Old Harmon had made his millions in "dust," the mountains of jungle-sweepings, bark, litter, husk and frond heaped on the city's edge, sorted and sold; and he had died the kindlest of tyrants, leaving his will like a trap: his son John to marry one Bella Wilfer, a stranger, or lose everything. John Harmon was returning from abroad to marry her when he went into the river — or into the river's reputation.

Because the drowned monkey was not John Harmon. It was George Radfoot, killed in a scuffle by Riderhood, wearing Harmon's clothes. And Harmon, hauled out of the water alive and penniless and unknown, stood in the crowd at his own inquest and heard himself sworn to, and got his own death confirmed, and did the thing a rich man's son with a bankrupt heart might well do: he declined to be resurrected.

He took a room under an assumed name, then employment under a second one, as John Rokesmith, secretary to the inheritance's residual legatees: old Nicodemus "Noddy" Boffin, the Golden Duster — Harmon's illiterate, warm-hearted old servant, a rumpled silver-back baboon in a coat of dust — and his comfortable wife. The Boffins, risen in one bound from the dust-heap to the mansion, were the only rich monkeys in the canopy who had grown kinder with every 'nana, and the canopy never forgave them for it.

"Gold don't sit easy on us, my dears," Mr. Boffin confessed, "we bein' made of dust and not of diamonds. But we'll polish up. We'll have style. Mrs. Boffin, we'll be as fashionable as the drawing-room of a Teapot."

They also took in, at Rokesmith's quiet suggestion, the very Bella Wilfer whom dead John Harmon was meant to have married — a beautiful, pouting guenon with a noisy little laugh, who had been poor all her life and had made up her mind to marry money, because money, she had observed, was the only thing that never let a girl down.

But the dust-heap had its own vermin. In a wooden house against the mounds lived Silas Wegg — a seedy, splay-footed monkey of literary pretensions, with a wooden tail in place of a leg lost to a river-snare — hired by Boffin to read to him evenings out of a volume called *The Rise and Roost of the Roamin' Empire*, pronouncing everything with confidence and nothing with correctness. And in a murky shop of preserved things, Mr. Venus, a pale capuchin who articulated monkey skeletons for a living, sat sighing among his bones, in love with Pleasant Riderhood, and unhappily in league with Wegg — because Wegg, grubbing in the mounds at night with his wooden tail sticking out behind like the moral of the story, had found buried in the dust an old Harmon will, and meant to squeeze the Golden Duster with it.

Meantime, up and down the river, the other plots swam. Lizzie Hexam, rescued from her father's trade by nothing but her own refusal, worked and hid; her brother Charley, whom she had kept in school with her own sculling, got on and was rather snobbish about it; and over Charley's shoulder hovered his schoolmaster, Bradley Headstone — a uakari, correct, tireless, and red-faced by nature's own accident, unfortunate, as his face blushed scarlet for every black thing he suppressed. Headstone, respectable to the last button, fell in love with Lizzie like a hot coal falling down a shirt; the more she recoiled, the more he burned.

And upriver of them all lolled Eugene Wraynam, a lanky young sifaka of languid insolence, idling after Charley's instructions only to get near the water-girl. "I am," said Wraynam, yawning to his friend the lawyer Mortimer Lightwood, "the idle apprentice of the canopy. But I have made one discovery: a monkey can be idle and earnest in the same skin. I met her on the river."

Lizzie fled them both — the scarlet respectable one and the lazy insolent one — upriver to a paper-mill among the locks, which accomplished nothing but distance. Headstone followed in his Sunday best with murder in his hat. Wraynam followed in his easy clothes with nothing in his hat at all, which is sometimes worse. And on a black night at the lock-side, Headstone came up behind his rival with a stone-crusher's swing, flung him into the river, and watched the current close over him — while Lizzie, who had been dreaming with her window open, heard the cry of the river, ran down in her nightclothes, and pulled Eugene Wraynam out of it, damaged but convertible. An idle monkey half-drowned is an earnest monkey re-shipped.

Justice on that river was never done by law. Rogue Riderhood, who had seen too much of Headstone's clothes and gait, squeezed him; and Headstone, respectable to the last button, went down to the lock one wild night dressed as Riderhood — high-shouldered leathers, fur cap and all — to end the squeezing forever. They met on the slick timbers above the sluice; and there, in the dark, one monkey dressed as the other, and neither knowing which was which, they locked grips — the grip of their own choosing, the old jungle grip — and went over together into the lock-chamber, and held on, and held on, and were found next morning still holding, the canopy's whole doctrine of greed in one drowned knot.

The mounds, too, came to their end. Wegg, with the found will in his wooden house, blackmailed Boffin grandly, demanding the fortune in slices, and would have had Venus's help in the division — but Mr. Venus, anatomist and honest, had already articulated the matter for himself: he went over to Boffin's side of the skeleton. And when Wegg came at last to dictate terms in the Bower of Study, Mr. Boffin rose up very large and very quiet, with his wife's arm in his, and crossing the mounds behind him like a landslide:

"Mr. Wegg," said the Golden Duster, "the only thing you ever found in my dust was my patience, and you've dug through it. Get out, sir. Take your tail off my property."

And Wegg went, wooden member and all.

Then Boffin sat down by Bella — who had already, to her own astonishment, done the one thing her whole mercenary little plan forbade: she had fallen in love with her poor secretary Rokesmith, and married him poor, and liked it; discovering, as she wrote to her father, that money was only 'nanas, but that being loved was dinner — and the old baboon, beaming through his dust, performed his master-stroke.

"I've been a-wearing a mask, my dear," he confessed. "A mean, gripey, miser's mask — a-pretendin' to turn grasping so as you should see what gold does to a monkey's soul, and take warning. And now the play's acted out, and here's the property." For John Rokesmith was John Harmon; the drowned heir stood up alive in the drawing-room; the fortune forgave him for existing; and the Boffins, giving it back with both paws, kept only the mounds they loved, and a cart, and a song about it.

There was a wedding, and a squalling little Harmon to be swung; Mr. Venus got his Pleasant; Jenny Wren, the little crook-backed dolls' dressmaker, made the bride doll-beautiful; the Lammles, two handsome fortune-hunters who married each other by mutual mistake and found neither had one, were exposed, ruined, and removed to the Bosphorus.

And still the river ran, taking its monkeys down to the sea as it had always done, utterly indifferent to their banking-accounts — which was the whole moral of the book, and of most books, and of the 'nana trade into the bargain.
