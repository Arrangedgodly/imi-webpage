# Telegrape Avenue

*A parody of "Telegraph Avenue"*

Brokebranch Records sat on the Oakland end of Telegrape Avenue, wedged between a tire shrine and a palm reader, two doors from the Berkeley line — a dusty cathedral of vinyl run by two monkeys who had been best friends, business partners, and worst enemies in equal measure.

Nat Jaffe was a small, bespectacled bonobo who could identify any record by the smell of its sleeve. Archy Stallworth was a broad, handsome chimp with a slow smile and a genius for being exactly where he wasn't needed. They opened at eleven. Archy usually arrived at eleven-forty.

"Morning, prime mate," said Nat, on the morning everything began to go bananas, without looking up from a crate of 78s. "You're only forty minutes late. The crate-diggers have already looted the jazz section."

"This store," said Nat, "is nothing but dust and heartbreak. We owe four months' rent, our wives deliver everybody's babies and get sued for it, and — small item — did you read the Chronicle?"

Archy took the newspaper. He read it standing very still, the way a monkey reads news that will require him to become a different monkey.

GIBBONS "G-BAD" GOODE, TENTH-RICHEST MONKEY IN AMERICA, TO OPEN GRAPEVINE MEGASTORE ON TELEGRAPE AVENUE.

"Ninety thousand square feet," Nat said. "Vinyl, vines, smoothies, a climbing wall. He wants the avenue, Archy. He wants the last two blocks of independent monkey business between here and the hills."

Archy set down the paper. "One-Stop sold, then."

"One-Stop sold. Flute City sold. We are the last crate on the last branch."

Then the bell rang, and in came the day's first customer, holding a record like a newborn.

---

Gwen Shanks — Archy's wife, six months gone with their second child, a gorilla of a woman with a midwife's hands and a pirate's temper — did not read the newspaper that morning because she was busy keeping another monkey's baby alive.

At a home birth up in the hills, the cord had slipped where cords must never slip, and Gwen and her partner Aviva — Nat's wife, small and unshockable — had done everything right: knees to chest, cord looped loose, screaming ambulance ride to Alta Boughs Hospital. The baby lived. The obstetrician on call still gave them the look one gives something on one's shoe, and two days later a letter came from a law firm: complaint to follow. Gwen stood in the kitchen holding it, and when Archy came home — late, smelling of vinyl and apology — she was calm, which was worse than the alternative.

"Four months' rent, a lawsuit, a megastore, and a baby coming," she said. "And your father is here."

That stopped even Archy's smile.

Luther Stallworth had been gone twenty-three years. Once he had been the kung-fu king of the drive-ins, star of *Squirrel-Monkey Fu* and *Enter the Banana*; then the money ran out, then the jobs, then the marriage, then the country. Now he stood on Telegrape Avenue in a white suit gone grey, like a ghost who had dressed for a better decade.

"Son," he said.

"Pop," said Archy, in a voice like a dropped needle.

Behind Luther stood a boy. Fourteen, thin, solemn, with a record store's worth of sorrow in his eyes and — Archy felt it before he understood it — a jawline exactly like his own.

"Titus," said Luther. "Say hello to Archy."

Titus did not say hello to Archy. Titus looked at the racks, walked to the jazz section, and pulled out a Cochise Jones record as if he'd been born knowing where it lived.

---

Julius Jaffe — Nat's son, fourteen, luminous, the kind of boy who wore his brain on the outside — was supposed to show Titus the neighborhood. Instead the two of them went up to the roof with cream sodas and a turntable, put on Cicero Lookfar's *Nana Groove*, and did not speak for three sides.

"I want to tell you something," Julius said finally, to the sky, because the sky had never once looked disappointed. "I like boys. I like monkeys, you know, but — I mean —"

"I know what you mean," said Titus. "Me too."

They sat with that, swinging their feet over the avenue, over Goode's looming sign, over everything. Below them the city bristled. Above them nothing did.

"Also," said Titus, staring straight ahead, "I think Archy's my father."

Julius put his hand flat on the roof. Titus put his on top of it. It was not a graceful gesture. It was the first one they had.

---

The revelation detonated at Sunday dinner, in front of both families and both pot roasts. Tamara Joyner, fifteen years ago; a baby; nobody had told Archy.

"Nobody told me either," said Gwen, in a voice with ice on it, "that when I married you I was getting a ready-made son and a father-in-law on the run. What are you running from, Luther? Besides me?"

Luther's story came out the way old records play, with pops and skips. In 1971 — the year of the Telegrape Hill job — Luther and two confederates had come away with a satchel of money that was never recovered. The others were gone now: Cicero Lookfar dead, and old Cochise Jones, the pianist, ancient and silent above the pawnshop with his hornbill.

"I never took a dollar of it," Luther said. "Cochise hid it, and Cochise is dying, and Chan Flowers says I owe him or I'm going in the bay. Chan Flowers is a gorilla," he added, "in the other sense."

"Both senses," said Nat, "and coming here Thursday."

Gwen stood up, seven months pregnant and seven feet tall in spirit. "Get out of my kitchen. All of you get out of my kitchen. I have a birth at midnight and a lawsuit at nine and I am *not* going bananas. I am the last monkey on this avenue who is not going bananas."

---

Cochise Jones died that week, at his piano, mid-phrase, which is the way old jazz monkeys order it. The funeral filled a church on Telegrape: crate-diggers and midwives, hornbills and hustlers, and in the back, Gibbons "G-Bad" Goode in a suit that cost a record store.

Afterward, Nat and Archy were asked to appraise Cochise's collection. They worked through the night, and at four in the morning Archy slid a battered 78 from its sleeve: Cicero Lookfar's *Nana Groove*, the only pressing in existence, and heavier on one side than any record has a right to be.

Inside the sleeve, wrapped in a page from the *Oakland Tribune* of 1971, lay the Telegrape Hill money. Moldy, silverfished, but money.

Chan Flowers arrived at dawn, as promised, with two cousins and a tire iron. He found Luther Stallworth waiting for him in the middle of Brokeland Records, holding the satchel — and Luther, who had run for twenty-three years, set it on the counter between *Squirrel-Monkey Fu* and *Enter the Banana*, and did not run.

"Cochise held it," said Luther. "Cochise, who never spent a nickel, held it for all of us and left it in the one place he trusted. I'm not going to tell you it's clean money. I'm going to tell you it's paid back, every dollar, with the bad luck included. Take it and the interest in something other than my son."

Chan counted it twice and found it exactly right, which ruined his whole plan and somehow improved his mood.

"We're square," he said. "Nana's honor."

And G-Bad Goode stepped out from behind the country section, where he had been crate-digging, unrecognized and unmolested, since midnight.

"Man's a thief," said Goode.

"Man's a customer," said Nat. "Buy something or leave."

Goode bought *Nana Groove*'s empty sleeve for a dollar — "the realest thing on this avenue" — and made his offer on the way out: Brokeland Records to survive as the vintage room inside Grapevine, with Nat as curator. A salary. A sign. A leash.

The two partners looked at each other across the counter they'd leaned on for a hundred thousand arguments.

"Here's the counteroffer," Archy said. "We stay broke, on our side of the line. But you put a wall of local records in your megastore — Brokeland's Choice, curated by us, consignment — and you send every monkey who asks about soul 45s to the avenue."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because the tenth-richest monkey in America," said Nat, "still can't find a Cochl & Cicero dupe, and I have two."

Goode smiled like a monkey closing a deal. "Vinyl is final," he said, and went.

Gwen's labor started at the funeral repast and ended at sunrise at the birth center, with Aviva's hands and Gwen's iron calm and Archy holding on for dear life. They had a daughter, and named her Cicely, for the pianist.

"I'm still angry at you," Gwen told Archy, over the small rooting head.

"I know."

"We're going to be a whole troop. You, me, Cicely, and a fourteen-year-old boy who plays chess like his grandmother. You're going to show up, Archy. Twelve o'clock sharp, every day, for the rest of your life."

"Show up," Archy repeated, as if tasting it. "That I can do."

On the roof of Brokeland Records that evening, Julius and Titus launched paper gliders into the violet air over the avenue, one after another, loop after loop — while below them, through the open transom, a needle dropped into a groove, and the store, that dusty cathedral of vinyl and heartbreak, opened its doors on time.
