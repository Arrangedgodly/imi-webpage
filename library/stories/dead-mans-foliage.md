# Dead Man's Foliage

*A parody of "Dead Man's Folly"*

The telegram that summoned Hercule Poirot to Devon read: *For the love of all that furred and reasonable COME AT ONCE. — Ariadne Olivebranch.*

Mrs. Ariadne Olivebranch, the celebrated crime writer, was a large energetic howler who produced a novel every spring and a grievance every hour. She was staging the Murder Hunt for the summer fete in the grounds of Nestcombe House, and she met Poirot at the lodge gate in a state of bristling despair.

"It's all being *changed* on me, Monsieur Poirot. Little things. The clues moved. A rope re-rigged. My victim's instructions rewritten. Somebody in this house is tampering with my Murder Hunt — and when a monkey tampers with a game of murder, I begin to wonder if it isn't a rehearsal. Do you know the feeling? Of course you don't, you're the one who's always *right*. That's why I sent the telegram."

Nestcombe belonged to Sir George Stubs, a broad, breezy, self-made gorilla who had bought the estate, coat, boat-house and all, and delighted in playing the squire; and to his young wife, Lady Hattie, a small dreamy cotton-top tamarin heiress from the West Indri islands, who drifted the grounds in enormous hats and said very little. Also present: Amanda Brewis, Sir George's brisk guenon secretary; Michael Weyman, a flirtatious young architect macaque, who had designed for the fete an ornamental tower — a "foliage," a sham ruin overrun with creepers, beside the river; Alec and Sally Legge, a married couple coming apart like cheap knitting; old Mrs. Folliat, last of the family that had owned Nestcombe for four hundred years, who lived in the lodge by arrangement with Sir George; and, expected that very day by announced visit, Hattie's elegant cousin from the islands, Etienne de Sousa.

And on the day of the fete, in the boathouse, where the Murder Hunt's official "victim" was to lie prettily in wait for the searchers, sat fourteen-year-old Marlene — a Tree Guide, daughter of the tavern, granddaughter of old Merdell the ferry mandrill — strangled with her own Guide scarf, a packet of sweets beside her. Lady Hattie had vanished off the grounds by dusk, leaving one enormous hat floating in the weir, and was never found.

"It is my Murder Hunt come true," said Mrs. Olivebranch, gray to the muzzle, "and I wish to heaven it had stayed a fiction."

The obvious suspect was Etienne de Sousa, whose arrival and the murder had shared an afternoon; but de Sousa proved the alibi of any well-bred sifaka, which is to say he had been visibly, boringly, expensively at tea in three separate counties. Suspicion then dabbed at Miss Brewis, who had been seen taking refreshments down to the boathouse victim that afternoon — on instruction, she said, in a voice like steel wool: "Her ladyship asked me to see the child had her lemonade. I do not murder Tree Guides, Monsieur Poirot. I *file* things."

Weeks went by. Poirot walked the grounds with old Merdell, who had drowned in the estuary that winter — "fell in," said the county, "monkey of ninety-two, these things happen" — and Merdell's surviving sayings troubled him like burrs. *There's always been Folliats at Nestcombe*, the old mandrill had told him, eyes wet and merry. *Always a Folliat at Nestcombe, sir, one way or another.* And Marlene, it emerged, had been in pocket money all spring — coins pressed on her by a monkey she would not name. Old Merdell talked to his granddaughter. Old Merdell had seen something in the woods, once, years back, the winter before Sir George bought the estate: a woman's body, he said, going into the ground by the new foliage. He had told it as a fuddle story. Merdell was ninety-two. These things happen.

At the end Poirot sent for Mrs. Folliat, and they walked together to the folly — the "foliage," the sham ruin, creepered to the eyebrows — and there, standing under it in the evening light, he told her the truth, quietly, because she was old, and because it was hers.

"Madame, your sons did not die in the Troop War, whatever the county believes. One died. The other came home a deserter with a talent for spending other monkeys' money, and you did what mothers do: you married him, quietly, to the richest little heiress in the West Indies — dear simple Hattie, with her cotton-top crest and her fortune — expecting that the estate might be saved, that he might grow. He did not grow. He *swindled* her. He took her money, bought back Nestcombe under the name of Sir George Stubs, and when the true Hattie sailed over to claim her husband —"

Mrs. Folliat said, "Don't."

"— there was already a wife, madame. An Italian wife, married in the war years, never mentioned. Bigamy, fraud, desertion: a whole coat of crimes. And so, on her way from the boat, in the woods by the new foliage — the fake Hattie was killed on the first day of her marriage, and buried in the foundations of a sham ruin, and her cousin Etienne has spent every year since wondering why Hattie never answered his letters. The creature who has drifted these grounds in enormous hats is the Italian wife, playing Hattie: islands accent, islands manners, and no island cousin ever near enough to embrace her. And this summer the *foliage* was to be rebuilt — the architect's works — the foundations opened. And old Merdell had talked, and Marlene had listened and repeated. That is why a Tree Guide is dead in the boathouse, and a ferry mandrill dead in the estuary. That is the murder hunt your son was rehearsing in Mrs. Olivebranch's game: he did not change her clues, madame. His wife did, for an alibi. One of them. Which one can hang, I no longer trouble to distinguish."

He watched the old lady. She had not moved. There's always been Folliats at Nestcombe.

"He was my son," said Mrs. Folliat at last, in a small, finished voice. "And I sold the house, and I told myself the buying of it back was love of the place. One way or another, Monsieur Poirot. Yes. That is what Merdell meant, and I have known it, I think, since the first summer, and would not — could not—" She stopped, and then, with the breeding of four centuries: "You will want to dig. There is no need to break the tower with hammers. The plans are in the architect's office; the marks are on the north pier."

They dug. The false Hattie was taken on the road to Plymouth, in tourist boots, with a rucksack — the identity she had worn to the ferry hostel the day before the fete, switching, all that day, between two selves, the drifting lady and the drab traveler, the hat floating in the weir timed to the minute. Sir George Stubs, questioned, made a speech about his position in the county; nobody listened to a word after the first sentence.

Poirot went back to the lodge that evening to take his leave. Mrs. Folliat met him at the gate, upright, ironed, quite finished with weeping.

"You will be kind, Monsieur Poirot, and go now," she said. "There are things I must put in order. Whatever is done with me — with him — do not let it be in the county papers until after the fete receipts are out. The Guides made four pounds for their tent."

She turned in at her gate. There's always been Folliats at Nestcombe — and there would be now, one way or another, forever, under the foliage, in the pier-marked north foundation, in the four hundred years that a house keeps.

On the train, Mrs. Olivebranch, who had been silent for one entire hour, a record, said suddenly: "You know what I cannot bear, Poirot? I *invent* these. Six a year. Locked boathouses, body in the library, and I have never once — never ONCE — had a monkey meddle with my clues before. It was a rehearsal, and I saw it, and I sent for you, and it happened *anyway*." She filled her pen viciously. "I am giving my Finnish detective a cold. A LONG one."

And Nestcombe's foliage was left standing, creepers and all, by order of the parish council — the cheapest monument in Devon, though nobody now will be married under it.
