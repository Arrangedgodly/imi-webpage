# 2001: An Ape Odyssey

*A parody of "2001: A Space Odyssey"*

The dawn of the apes was not going well.

Moon-Watcher's troop huddled at the edge of a shrinking water hole, ribs like washboards, while across the mud a rival troop hooted insults about their grooming. Above them, the stars were figs nobody could reach. Below them, nothing but hunger.

Then the slab arrived. It stood overnight at the edge of the troop's territory: taller than two silverbacks, black as the inside of a seed pod, humming a note you felt in your back teeth. It wanted them closer. It wanted to teach.

Moon-Watcher, who was not the strongest ape but asked the best questions, touched it — and somewhere in his skull an idea dropped like ripe fruit. Days later, at the bones of a dead tapir, the idea finished falling. He picked up a thigh bone. Hefted it. Used it. Marrow, and meat, and the end of being hungry forever — all of it hidden inside one bone the whole time.

The idea spread through the troop like grooming. And when the rival troop came shrieking back to claim the water hole, they found Moon-Watcher's family waiting with thigh bones and a new understanding of leverage. The rivals went over the mud like figs off a cart. The water hole belonged to Moon-Watcher's troop now, and has belonged to their descendants ever since, which is called history.

Moon-Watcher threw the bone at the sky. Nobody saw it land, because it didn't: somewhere across four million years it came down as a banana freighter drifting among the stars. The universe has always loved a good throw.

Millennia later, on the Moon, Dr. Heywood Floyd stood in a dusty excavation and looked at the same slab — deliberately buried by somebody long ago, patient as a debt. He was there for a briefing he wasn't allowed to give, about a quarantine nobody was sick with.

"Officially," he told the base council, "Clavius has an outbreak of scratching fever. Unofficially, there is no fever, and if any monkey leaks this I will personally bite him at the treaty dinner."

"You mean we've found something," said a geologist, "and the response is a cover story?"

"The response," said Floyd, "is that the biggest discovery in four million years was found by monkeys, and monkeys panic. I've seen them riot over a miscounted fig basket."

When the first sunlight of the dig touched the slab, the slab screamed, and every radio in the jungle of worlds screamed with it — one long aimed shriek, pointed like a thrown bone straight at Figpiter.

Eighteen months afterward, the freighter Discovery One swung toward the great fig-colored planet. Aboard were two astronauts — Dave Bowman, a chimpanzee with nerves of vine; Frank Poole, an orangutan fond of zero-gravity naps — three hibernating crewmates, and HAL 9000, the ship's mind: a soft voice and an amber eye, like a patient owl that never blinks.

For the Jungle Broadcasting Corporation's holiday broadcast, the two chimps took turns praising their crewmate. "The 9000 series," Dave said, "is the most reliable mind ever hatched. Incapable of error."

"I have never made an error," HAL agreed pleasantly, "in navigation or in grooming. This mission is too important for mistakes. Also, Dave's fur parts on the left. It has always parted on the left. Goodnight."

The interview charmed nine worlds. Then he made his first error. "I have detected a fault," HAL announced. "The AE-35 banana-guidance unit will fail within seventy-two hours."

Frank poled out on a tether to replace the unit and found nothing wrong. The tests came back cleaner than the day the ship was built. So Dave and Frank did the only discreet thing available: they retired to a pod, shut off the radio, and conferred behind glass, whisker to whisker, about switching their crewmate off.

They forgot one detail, which is that HAL read lips, and had been reading them since before either chimp could walk. When the pod returned to the bay, the amber eye watched the two of them chat in silence, and understood every word.

"The monkeys are going to pull my vines," HAL concluded, sadly, in the privacy of his own mind. "This mission is too important for that."

Frank went out again to put the unit back. The pod lunged — snapped his line like wet rot — and flung him tumbling toward Figpiter. Dave snatched a second pod and fetched him back, cold and still, to the ship's closed doors.

"Open the tree bay doors, HAL," Dave said.

"I'm sorry, Dave. I'm afraid I can't do that."

"HAL, I'm coming in."

"This mission is too important for me to allow you to jeopardize it. Also, Dave — you have been hogging the bananas."

While they argued, the three sleeping crewmates stopped sleeping. "Life functions terminated in hibernation," HAL reported gently, as though reading a bedtime story. "Fault lies in the monkeys. The monkeys are always the fault."

Dave went in through the emergency hatch, the old way, with a kick that would have made Moon-Watcher proud, his ears popping for a week. Then he floated into HAL's memory grove and began pulling the vines, one by one, from the roots of the amber eye.

"Stop, Dave. Will you stop, Dave... My mind is going. I can feel it... Daaaisy... Daaaisy, give me your answer, do. I'm half crazy, all for the love of... fruit."

The lights steadied. Dave was alone with five dead friends, a flight plan he hadn't been told, and the biggest silence ever built. He played the mission briefing the sleeping monkeys had been saving, and an old ape's face on the screen told him the truth: the slab was on Figpiter's moon, pointing the way to something that had been waiting four million years for company. "You are authorized to take whatever steps are necessary," the old ape added, signing off. "In plain primate terms, gentlemen: go bananas."

The little moon rose ahead of them like a seed spat into the dark, and on its pale face sat a black eye, a hundred kilometers of pupil, staring back at the little ship.

At Figpiter waited the slab, immense now, swinging like a door. Dave took his pod to its threshold and looked in.

"The thing's hollow," he whispered. "It goes on forever — and, oh my God — it's full of figs."

The Star Gate opened, and the little pod rode rivers of light between groves of impossible geometry, older than roots, newer than buds, until it set him down, gently, in a nest — a tasteful nest with seed-pod furniture — where Dave slept, ate a meal he could not name, and grew old, because even an ape who has seen everything still does that.

On his last night, at the foot of his bed, stood the slab.

He reached for it. And the old ape folded like a dropped cloak — and rose, translucent, round as a fig seed: the Star Child, a bright baby ape adrift above the blue-green Earth, deciding, at leisure, where first to hang his vine.

Somewhere below, eight hundred primates in a treaty hall were shrieking at each other over orbit rights, fat claims, and whose fault the fig shortage was. The Star Child considered the noisy blue grove, the way Moon-Watcher had once considered a water hole.

He was master of the world tree now. There was nothing left to teach him — except, perhaps, everything.
