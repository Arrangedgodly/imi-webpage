# Days at the Lorisaki Bookshop

*A parody of "Days at the Morisaki Bookshop"*

The man Takako had dated for seven years told her over lunch that he was marrying someone else, and that the someone else was not, technically speaking, a surprise to him.

"We were on a break," Kensuke said, in the tone of a monkey reading insurance terms.

"We have been on a break," said Takako, "since the day we met, apparently."

She walked out of the restaurant and stood in the Tokyo noon with nowhere to put herself. Her apartment was a shoebox with a lease problem. Her job was a staffing agency where she moved numbers between cages. Her chest had a Kensuke-shaped hole in it, and the city, which had never once slowed down for her, went on swinging overhead at full speed.

Her uncle Satoru — her mother's younger brother, whom she'd met a handful of times in her life — was a slow loris who owned a secondhand bookshop in Jimbocho, the used-book district, and had, through the family grapevine, heard about the lunch.

"Come stay in the room above the shop," he said, on the phone, at the pace of a loris, which is to say the message took some time. "It's yours. Rent is you don't let the coffee pot go dry."

"Uncle, I don't read."

"Nobody reads," said Satoru, serenely. "Then they do."

---

The Lorisaki Bookshop stood at the mouth of an alley off Yasukuni-dori, narrow-fronted and deep, stacked floor to rafters with second-paw books, the smell of which — dust and vanilla and old paper, the smell of ten thousand closed doors — hit Takako like weather when Satoru unlocked the grille on her first morning.

The shop opened slowly, because Satoru did everything slowly, being a loris; the two of them moved like a film running down. Takako, a young macaque still set to city speed, found the whole district unbearable at first. Jimbocho at eight a.m. was shutters and sweeping; Jimbocho at ten was quiet men carrying paper bundles; nothing anywhere went bananas, nothing swung, and her heartbreak, with no noise to drown in, sat up all night with her on the futon in the room above the shop.

On the fourth sleepless night she went down — the lamp was still on; Satoru read until two every morning, slowly — and pulled a book at random from the nearest wall, out of spite, to prove the shop had nothing for her.

It was a small novel, its jacket gone soft as fruit skin, about a loris who leaves Borneo on a cargo ship and crosses the world one slow night at a time. Takako sat down on the ladder to skim the first page.

She surfaced two hours later on the floor, stiff-necked and hollowed out and damp around the eyes, having finished it, having — there was no other word for what had happened in the dark — *slept* for the first time in a week, right there between the poetry and the travel writing, with the book open on her chest like a small hand.

Satoru, folding blankets at dawn, said nothing, which in a loris is a paragraph of praise.

---

After that, the days changed shape. Takako worked the till in the afternoons (slowly; the customers preferred it; there is no browsing like slow browsing), and Satoru taught her the grammar of the trade: which spines talk, which editions lie, why you never, ever rush a customer holding a tankobon the way you never rush a loris holding a branch.

"The books sit here for years," he said. "Decades. Waiting. And then somebody comes in with exactly the hole in them that this one book fits, and you watch it happen, and you understand the whole shop was arranged for that one monkey. Second-paw books, Takako. Every one of them has already been loved once. We're just the middle of the story."

She started to notice the district: the tofu shop man who collected Edo maps; the university girl who came every Thursday for novels about the sea; and Watanabe, the young gorilla from Obunsha two streets over, all shoulders and shyness, who delivered misdelivered stock and stayed, increasingly, for coffee. He and Takako argued companionably about whether a bookshop could have a soul ("It has dust," she said; "Same thing," he said) and walked the festival stalls at Jinbocho's autumn market, where he won her a paper lantern and dropped it twice.

Somewhere between October and the new year, without either of them going bananas about it, Takako realized she had stopped setting an alarm. She woke with the light, read until the coffee smelled right, and could not remember why she had ever lived at the other speed.

"You're changing," said her mother on the phone, half worried, half jealous.

"I'm slowing," said Takako. "It turns out slow is where I live."

---

The second spring, Satoru fell ill — a bad quarter of the heart, the hospital said, fixable, but only by surgery and a long stillness afterward, and stillness, for a loris, is the full-time job. He delegated the way he did everything: slowly, thoroughly, in one sentence.

"The shop is yours till autumn," he said from the hospital bed. "Water the plants. Don't let Watanabe reshelve the Kawabata."

Takako ran the Lorisaki through that summer like her fur depended on it. She opened the grille at ten, read at two, bought collections off grieving families at four, and learned what her uncle had meant: the shop was a net, and every day somebody fell into it needing exactly what was stacked there. A teacher hunting a poem for a retiring colleague. A boy buying his mother's favorite novel, gone twenty years, because now he was ready to read it. The shop caught them all, slowly, one at a time.

She also — up a ladder in August, hunting a botany folio for a customer — found the note.

It was tucked in a 1972 poetry collection, in a woman's hand: *To S. — for the evenings we argued about this poem. I am leaving for Kyoto, and I am taking the argument with me. If you ever figure out who was right, you know where to find me. — Midori.*

At the hospital, she held it out. Satoru looked at it for a long, long time.

"I never figured out who was right," he said at last. "So I never went."

"That's the worst reason not to go to Kyoto I've ever heard."

"It's a loris reason," said Satoru. "We go slowly, and then we don't."

"Then write to her," said Takako. "Slowly. You have a whole summer."

He wrote seventeen drafts. Watanabe mailed the last one in September. And in October, when Satoru came back to the shop — thinner, slower, upright — a woman with grey fur and a Kyoto umbrella was waiting at the grille when he unlocked it, holding the poetry collection, purchased second-paw, decades later, in a district she'd had to ask directions to.

"Forty years," said Midori. "Do you know how long that is to wait for a loris to answer a note?"

"We answered the important part," said Satoru, holding the door. "I kept the shop. You kept the book."

---

The Lorisaki Bookshop opens at ten now, and never a minute sooner, and the regulars will tell you the coffee is the best in Jimbocho because nobody is ever in a hurry to drink it. Takako runs the floor; Satoru and Midori read in the back like two bookmarks in the same volume; and Watanabe, who finally kissed Takako under the paper lantern at his fourth autumn festival, still misdelivers stock to the Lorisaki on purpose, which everyone pretends not to notice, because a bookshop, like a loris, believes in taking its time.

On the shelf behind the till, spine out, is a small novel about a loris who crosses the world one slow night at a time. There is a card in it now, in Takako's hand, that says: *Ask the monkey at the counter. Some books wait for you. This one waited for me.*
