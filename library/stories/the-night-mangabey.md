# The Night Mangabey

*A parody of "The Night Manager"*

Jonathan Pine was the night manager of the Grand Baobab Palace, the finest hotel in the whole Cairo canopy, and he ran the dark hours the way a jeweller runs a shop of small, priceless things: quietly, precisely, with a smile kept folded in his waistcoat until needed.

She came at two in the morning for a pot of fermented fig-tea. Sophie was her name; the mistress of Freddie Hamid, a thug olive baboon with delusions of tail-brush. She was lonely, lovely, and frightened, and she drank her tea in the empty ballroom and made Pine laugh about the guests, and looked over her shoulder while she did it. "You're the only monkey here who doesn't want anything, Mr. Pine," she said. "It makes you restful." One night the folder was already under the teapot: bills of lading, charter fees, and one name in a fine gold hand at the top of every page. Pine sat with it until the dawn bell, weighing Sophie against the warlords downstream, and posted it to a friend in the Banyan House, the jungle's quiet intelligence tree, before his conscience could vote twice.

The papers named a supplier. They were buried by important monkeys who did not wish them read, and Sophie went into the river one morning, and the river did not give her back.

Pine flew home to Cairo, closed the ballroom at the usual hour, and filed his grief where he filed the guest complaints he could not act upon. It did not stay filed.

So when Burr came — a burly old colobus with a face like a bark-knuckle and no time for the Banyan House's games — Pine did not need convincing.

"The supplier is Dicky Onslow Roper," Burr said, laying out photographs like a paw of cards. "Philanthropist, conservationist, friend of ministers. Smuggler of the instruments of jungle war: thorn-guns, dart-cannons, crates of exploding seed pods. Sold to whichever warlord outbids the last one. Some call him the worst monkey in the world. I call him a harvest I mean to reap. You managed a hotel, Mr. Pine. Manage yourself into his troop."

"How?"

"The honest way. Find the rotten fig in his basket, and hold it up — then get beaten half to fluff for your trouble, by his enemies, where his own bodyguards can rescue you. Monkeys trust the ones they've rescued. It's the oldest rope in the canopy."

It went as Burr drew it. Pine, working a season at an island hotel in the Cara-bean, found the skim in the accounts of Roper's lieutenant, pointed it out politely, and that evening was set upon by strangers with cudgels — strangers arranged by Burr — and rescued, battered and magnificent, by Roper's own enforcer, a scarred drill called Tabb.

Tabb carried him out of the cudgels like a parcel he approved of, and stood over him at the infirmary for two nights, saying nothing, deciding. What he had decided by the third night came out as a reference: "You didn't cry out. Not even when they did the knee. Mr. Roper collects monkeys like you."

Roper himself was a tall, silver-charming gorilla in a linen suit, with a laugh like warm honey poured over gravel. "You cost me nothing and earned me much, Mr. Pine," he said. "A monkey of resource. Join us. We're only merchants of fruit and cheer, you'll find."

"Of course, sir," said Pine, and poured, and said nothing, ever, with his face.

He was vetted first by Major Corkoran, Roper's recruitment colobus, a wispy elderly ape in a hat with a dead macaw on it, who spoke in a warm treacle of spoonerisms. "A thrill of dillight, Mr. Pine. You're either a bona fide gentleman or a very rum fellow — a fum rellow — and frankly we hire both. Apo will do the money. Apo," he explained, "is a broker baboon of planetary width. Do not shake his tree."

Then there was Apo, who oiled every payment; and the yacht, the Iron Pawshaw, white as a dental cabinet; and Lord Langbark of the Banyan House, whose hobby was conscience and whose income, twice removed, was Roper.

He climbed the troop from within — yacht, ledgers, the little kingdom of the Cara-bean — and passed what he learned to Burr by the only unwatched channel left to a social primate: the grooming line. A nit left in a certain fur-row meant *shipment*; two nits on the shoulder-blade, *Cara-bean*; a long steady stroke down the forearm, *send the boats*. Roper's own soldiers carried the messages of their own downfall cheerfully between camps, one flea-hop at a time.

His cover was comfort: the right fig at the right temperature, the right silence at the right hour. He learned which of Roper's soldiers cheated at cards, which beat their mates, and which — one, only one — sent bananas home to a mother in the hills. He filed them all. A night manager forgets nothing; it is the whole of the job.

The complication had soft eyes and a quick tongue. Jed, Roper's capuchin companion, saw Pine exactly as clearly as Pine saw her, which in that troop was dangerous as a lightning-strike in a fig tree. She caught his nit-code the third week — "You groom like a monkey leaving messages," she said, "which you are" — and told nobody, because in that troop a secret was the only gift that kept giving. She talked to him instead about the sea, and gardens, and what she'd be if she were free, which was everything.

"If you ever run," she said one evening, not looking at him, "run at the start of the rains, when the dogs can't hold a scent and the drones stay home."

"I don't run," said Pine.

"No," said Jed, watching him. "You manage."

The last shipment assembled at dusk: thorn-guns for the warlord of the Broken Canopy, payment in gold bananas and blood-plums, and the Banyan House's own tame lawyer, a silky gibbon called Palmfret, sitting in the club car purring about immunity. Palmfret, it seemed, had earned a bonus: the drop-site had been changed, and the new site came with an ambush attached, and the Howler-ican authorities would arrive to be the arrest instead of the arresting.

Or so it would have gone, had the change of drop-site not reached Burr in fourteen fur-rows and two nit-yolks, filed by a night manager who had finally allowed himself one small smile. At the new drop-site, in the green dawn over the estuary, the Howler-ican authorities were waiting in force — in rather more force than a silk lawyer's tip had promised — and the ambushers were ambushed by the oldest rule of the jungle: the quiet monkey had combed everyone.

Roper was handed over on the quay, linen suit immaculate. "You were the quietest monkey I ever fed," he said to Pine. "That should have warned me."

"The quiet ones are always managers, sir," said Pine. "We only look like furniture."

There remained an accounting with the Banyan House, which had buried Sophie's papers and rented out the spade. A selection of ledgers reached the jungle's newspapers in fourteen separate parcels; Palmfret was disbarred by breakfast and Lord Langbark retired to grow orchids under supervision; and certain important monkeys discovered the price of gravel-voiced friendships.

Jed, of her own free paws, testified for an hour with her chin up, and was never anyone's companion again.

And in the last chapter, on a small boat going anywhere warm, a capuchin with soft eyes asked a mangabey with a healed rib what he'd do next.

"Keep the night," said Jonathan Pine. "Somebody has to manage it."
