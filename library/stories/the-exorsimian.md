# The Exorsimian

*A parody of "The Exorcist"*

Chryss MacNeil was the star of *Bandar Nights*, shooting on location in a jungle so deep even the tarantulas had commute problems. She had brought her daughter Reggie along because the sitter canceled and, honestly, what could go wrong in a jungle?

Reggie was a small macaque, nine, and a good kid. She played with the local children and held séances at the termite mound with a spirit board made from an old crate stenciled ORCHARD FRESH.

Her imaginary friend was called Captain Howler.

For a while the Captain was good company. The board spelled out compliments. IS REGGIE NICE, Reggie would ask, and the planchette would slide to YES, YES, YES. Her mother did not worry. Film-set kids invent friends the way rivers invent bends.

Then one evening Reggie asked the board if she could have dessert, and it spelled NO. She asked it who was answering, and it spelled CAPTAIN HOWLER, and then, in a different rhythm, slower, like something much larger using a very small typewriter, it spelled RUN FLIPPER RUN — and nobody on that set was named Flipper. Reggie put the board away and did not play with it again. That should have been the end of it. Captain Howler disagreed.

Then Reggie's cot began to shake at night. Just a rattle at first, like a monkey with a grudge under the bed. Then a great thrashing thump that moved the cot two feet across the floor while the legs did a snare-drum solo.

Dr. Bananaseed found nothing. "Children shake cots," he said. "It's their whole thing."

The specialists also found nothing, at length, and at cost. There was a spinal tap that drew nothing but smoothie. There was an electroencephalogram, which the technicians stopped discussing. There was a machine that went bing. Reggie's brain waves, one vet was heard to mutter, were perfectly normal — for a much older monkey.

Then Reggie started speaking at dinner in a gravelly grown voice — like a barrel of baboons rolling downhill — and, worse, in languages. A linguist traced it to "an old Pliocene gutter dialect," alarming in a child whose vocabulary was otherwise limited to *please*, *thanks*, and *can I have the remote*.

Then came the strength. She hugged a grip and cracked three ribs with genuine warmth. She did a full split over breakfast, which was impressive, then turned her head a hundred and eighty degrees to look at her mother, which ended breakfast.

"REGGIE!"

"Yes, Mama?" said the head, from the wrong side.

The last straw was banana. Chryss was spooning her daughter a nice banana puree when Reggie's face went ancient, the cot rose three feet off the floor and hovered with the smugness of a stage magician, and the barrel voice said:

"Filthy grooming habits in this house. Your mother picks fruit in hell."

"That does it," said Chryss, who could take a lot, but not that, because her mother ran a very respectable fruit stand. "Get me a priest. Get me a monkey priest. Get me two."

The diocese sent Father Karaboos, a young gibbon with a doctorate in monkey psychology and a faith best described as pending. Karaboos had taken a parish in the city to make a career, and while he was making it, his mother had died alone in her tree-top flat, and he had been hearing about it from himself ever since. He checked his notes while the cot performed.

"Medically," he said, "possession presents like any number of syndromes. It could be a lesion. It could be her larynx. Macaques do screech."

"IT COULD BE YOU," said Reggie, warmly.

On Reggie's forearm — this was the detail that ended the medical file — the fine fur had been groomed, somehow, from the inside, into letters. They read, backwards, as if written by something facing the other way: HLEP US.

So they sent for Father Mango Merrin, an elderly orangutan archaeologist who had spent forty years digging up old temples and older rumors. He had met this one before, at a dig upriver — a small temple, a small god, a big grudge. He stood in the doorway with his case of holy oils and his great calm face, and the bed stopped instantly, the way a naughty troop goes silent when the troop-master arrives.

"He is a liar," Merrin said, unpacking. "He will mix lies with truth to confuse us. And he will do voices. Do not engage when he does the voices. It only encourages him."

"He does voices?" asked Karaboos.

"I do you," said Reggie, in Karaboos's voice, perfectly.

"What an excellent day," said the barrel voice from the top of the stairs, "for an exorsimian."

The exorcism began at midnight. "Depart, unclean spirit," intoned Merrin, "by order of the Big Troop above!"

"NO," said Reggie, and threw an entire bowl of banana puree at Karaboos with the accuracy of artillery.

"The power of figs compels you!" they chanted, pressing the blessed fruit forward. "The power of figs compels you!"

Reggie's head spun. The room went cold as a mountain stream. Captain Howler roared through her, the curtains stood straight out, and somewhere the sound guy's ribs ached in sympathy.

Hour after hour the thing talked. It knew things it could not know. It did the production accountant. It did the pope. And then, when the night was oldest, it did a small old lady's voice, cracked and kind, from the far side of the cot: "Why you do this to me, Boosy? Why you never come, Boosy?"

Karaboos wavered like a sapling. Merrin, very old and very tired, stood up to take the demon's attention onto himself — and then sat down slowly on the edge of the cot, and said, "This one is very tired," and died the way old leaves let go, in the middle of the work, without any fuss at all.

And that left Karaboos alone with it, at the bottom of the night, with no faith to speak of and a dead friend and his mother's voice coming out of a nine-year-old.

So the gibbon stepped close to the bed and did the thing you are never, ever supposed to do.

"Take me instead," said Karaboos. "Come into me. I invite you. Me."

The demon, being a liar, took the deal — and Karaboos, feeling it land on him like a monkey on his back, did the math in half a second and leapt out the window.

He caught a vine on the way down — of course he did; he was a gibbon — and swung, screaming holy words, straight into the river pool below, which washed the thing out of him entirely. Gibbons, as everyone knows, are naturally baptismal.

In the room, Reggie sat up, small and rumpled and herself.

"Mama?" she said. "Is Father Splashy okay?"

They buried Merrin on a hill above the dig he loved. Karaboos, soaked and hoarse, said the service, and half the jungle came, and nobody did any voices at all.

Merrin's notes went back to his university. Karaboos requested a transfer to a desert, and got it. The film wrapped. Chryss won her award and thanked, through tears, "a monkey priest who shall remain nameless, and a Father Splashy who will not."

Reggie grew up remembering none of it — though to this day she cannot abide banana puree, will not play with crates, and has never once in her life asked a board for permission to have dessert.
