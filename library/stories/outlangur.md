# Outlangur

*A parody of "Outlander"*

The standing stones at Climbh na Dun hummed like a hive the morning Clara Randall touched them, which she later considered the second-worst decision of her life — because it had competition.

She and her husband Frank, both healing hands for the troops after the war, had come to the Scottish Highlands for a second honeymoon and the local festival, where the troop women sang to the dawn around the ancient circle. The legends said the stones were a door, that a troop had once been stolen away through them. The legends, Clara felt, deserved more footnotes.

"Look," said Frank, "they even groom in the old way — see, the matrons pick through each other's fur by rank."

Clara laughed — and then the hum started, and the stones leaned in like a listening ear, and the world turned inside out.

She came to on the ground with the same stones above her and an entirely different forest around them: older, darker, the troop-signs unfamiliar. She walked down the hill to what should have been a road and found a game trail. In the distance, a red-coated patrol spotted the lone she-ape and gave chase with the courtesy you'd expect from a Blackback on the make.

The paws that yanked her out of sight behind a boulder belonged to a young langur with his hood up and a price on his head: Jamie Frasier, outlaw of Troop MacKenzie, twenty-three and already twice flogged for a thing he didn't do.

"Can ye climb, lass?"

"Can I — I'm a Randall, of course I can climb."

"Climb fast, then. And dinna flash your teeth at the redcoats. They take it personally."

He delivered her to the MacKenzies at Banyan Leoch, where the laird, Colum, a brooding silverback with a bad hip and a good spy network, decided a healer was too useful to release. Clara became the troop's medicine ape by day; by night she plotted to get back up that hill — to the stones, to Frank, to 1945. Meanwhile Colum's brother Dougal, the war chief, dragged her along on rent-collecting rides so she could see exactly what English justice did to Highland troops — a flogging here, a burned bothy there — which was, Clara had to admit, extremely effective recruitment propaganda, in that it recruited her, against her will, to a side.

There was a handsome young warrior named Jamie, who kept getting injured in ways that required her hands on him, and who looked at her like she was the first spring fig after a hard winter. And there was Captain Blackback Randall, Frank's ancestor, a sadist with a red coat and an inspector's warrant, who wanted Jamie's hide for a whipping he'd already given him once — and wanted Clara for worse.

When the English grabbed Clara on suspicion of witchcraft, she was thrown into a Cranesmuir cell beside a flame-furred gelada named Gelida Duncan, who ran herbs, prophecies, and the local aristocracy with equal insolence.

"Ye have the look of a woman born in the wrong troop," Gelida observed. "They'll burn us both for it, and I'll be guilty and ye'll be innocent, and that's the whole of the law."

At the trial, Gelida saved her — declaiming, disrobing, and declaring herself the witch of the two until the court was too scandalized to keep Clara — and Jamie hauled Clara out through the crowd on a horse, at which point the troop's solution to everything became clear: marry the lass to Jamie, make her a MacKenzie, and put her beyond Randall's reach.

It was a business arrangement, they agreed. Strictly business.

"You're shivering," Jamie said on their wedding night, in a bothy with rain on the roof.

"I'm from a heated enclosure," said Clara. "We have trunks."

"Then come here, and I'll tell ye about mine."

They fell in love the way cliffs fall into the sea: slowly, then completely. She learned the troop, and the Highland gestapo of table manners that was Mrs. FitzGibbon's dinner service. She rode to the Gathering with the clan, where Jamie put himself between Dougal and a boar's tusk and then claimed, bleeding, that he'd merely tripped in a helpful direction. "Ye did that on purpose," Clara said, stitching him. "Aye," said Jamie. "And ye're sewing me on purpose too, so we're even." And when she finally stood again at Climbh na Dun, palm against the humming stone, with 1945 one hum away and Jamie waiting at the foot of the hill — she chose the hill's foot.

"I had a husband," she told Jamie. "I have one now. I'd like you to be the one I keep."

"Je suis prest," Jamie said — *I am ready* — which, as the Frasier crest ran, meant: *I am a primate, and I intend to stay one.*

The Blackback did not take the news gracefully. He took Jamie to Fort William and whipped him a third time — and Clara walked into the wolf's den with a lie, a bribe, and a hatpin, and walked out with her husband. Barely.

But Randall was not finished, and Jamie's luck ran out at a place called Wentworth, where the Blackback held him in a cell and planned things that have no place in a family troop. So the Frasier clan did the sensible thing: Jamie's grim godfather Murtagh rounded up every outlaw, cousin, and disreputable macaque in three glens, and they drove a stolen herd of cattle through the prison gates on market day. Thirty furious cows make a door wherever they want one. They peeled Jamie off the wall, and Clara put him back together with her two hands and a will that refused to discuss failure.

They came home to Lallybough, Jamie's own small troop in the wild hills, and for one autumn there was honey in the comb: harvest, dancing, the future looking almost level.

But history is a river. Culloden was coming — the rising that would gut the Highland troops in one spring — and no one had yet found a way to make a river run backward.

"Ye knew," Jamie said one night, watching her face. "When ye came through the stones. Ye knew what comes."

"I know what came," said Clara. "I know what the books say. And I know we're going to try to change the ending. The ship for France sails with the dawn. There are princes to argue with, and ledgers to lose, and if we cannot stop the foolishness, we can at least be unhelpful to it."

"Then we'd best get climbing, Sassenach," said Jamie Frasier, husband and — for the moment — the happiest langur in the Highlands. "The dawn willna wait, and neither will the Redcoats."

At the dock she took his paw and laid it flat against her belly, and said, "The ending we're changing had better have room for three." Jamie Frasier, twice-flogged outlaw of Troop MacKenzie, wept exactly once, in the rain, where no one could prove it.

Above them the stones of Climbh na Dun kept their own counsel, and hummed, and waited for the next ape fool enough to touch a legend without footnotes. Years later, the village would tell of a langur in Highland dress who sometimes watched the stones from the ridge at dusk — a story, nothing more, the way all the truest ones start.
