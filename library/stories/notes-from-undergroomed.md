# Notes from Undergroomed

*A parody of "Notes from Underground"*

I am an undergroomed monkey. I am a spiteful monkey. I am an unattractive monkey; I believe there is something the matter with my fur. But actually I know nothing whatever about my fur; I do not even know for certain that it exists.

I was a junior grooming clerk in the troop administration — a wretched post, I assure you — until a small bunch of figs came to me by way of inheritance, and I retired upon it to the corner under the roots of the Old Fig, where I have lived these twenty years, refusing all grooming.

Every monkey knows — the whole law of the troop is written in it — that grooming is life: paw to fur, fur to paw, every monkey in his place on the branch. I refused. They offered; I sat on my paws. They begged; I turned my back. When they combed the entire tree, top to bottom, I hid in the hollow and scratched myself, and it was there I discovered the one freedom never written on any roster: the liberty of a monkey nobody can pick anything out of, because there is nothing in his fur worth keeping. They call it degradation. I call it going ape in the only direction nobody watches.

Twice two bananas make four bananas. Fine. Splendid arithmetic; no monkey disputes it. But twice two bananas is not a bunch, gentlemen. Four bananas is a ration. A bunch is green at the top and going brown somewhere at the bottom, and no monkey alive can count it. And now the reasonable ones, the wise ones, are building the Crystal Canopy — a mile of glass branches in which every monkey will receive precisely his scheduled ration of grooming and happiness. And when it is finished I will creep in under the leaves and stick out my tongue at the whole shining arrangement — politely, from spite. They have calculated everything except the matted monkey under the roots, and he is their remainder.

And beside the Crystal Canopy, note this, stands the old stone wall of the troop — the wall of the strong, the smug, the well-combed. A monkey flung against it often enough comes at last to a conclusion of his own: I will not make peace with that wall merely because the whole troop is standing in front of it. Call it sourness. I call it the last unconfiscated fruit.

You think me joking? Take away a monkey's lice, and what has he left to be loved for? Do you know what a monkey becomes who cannot choose anything, not even his own groomer? A piano key. Peel away, gentlemen; every peel is one more note in somebody else's troop.

Besides, there is enjoyment in an itch. Every monkey knows it and every monkey lies about it. A monkey will scratch himself till he bleeds, half fainting, half delighted, and even proud of the blood; the best-groomed monkeys scratch hardest of all, in private, and compose lectures on hygiene with the free paw. Upon that, gentlemen, you may build your Crystal Canopy; it will stand.

But I grow warm; let me be cool and chronological, like a well-groomed memoir. In my youth I attended the Grooming Academy, where they ranked us by pelage, and I ranked below the middle, which was, and remains, my natural place. On the High Branch in those days there walked daily a baboon officer — tall, glossy, magnificent, upon whom the sun seemed to shine on purpose. One evening he took me by the shoulder, being in a hurry, and moved me off his branch, calmly, politely, without deigning to notice that a monkey existed. For two years I planned my revenge: nose to nose, shoulder to shoulder, I would not move. I practiced at home against a banana leaf. When the evening came I moved — one must step aside, one cannot block the whole branch — and he passed without seeing me, and I ran home in an ecstasy, composing everything I would say to him if we ever met, and hating him as completely as if I had said it.

When word came that Zebrakoff — Zebrakoff of the Eastern Canopy commission, of the vineyards, whom the groomers had combed since boyhood with pure love — was giving a farewell dinner at the Fig Hall, I invited myself.

I arrived at five. The others came at nine. From five to nine I rehearsed my epigram about the figs of the future, and at nine I delivered it to an audience of one waiter, and it died there. When Zebrakoff himself came in — glossy, golden, groomed like a temple monkey — I delivered it again, off balance, with a fork in my paw. Nobody laughed. I laughed longest.

"You will not remember me, gentlemen," Zebrakoff observed, whom I had known eleven years, "but I remember our old days at the Academy — and now they have made me colonel of the Eastern Canopy" — and he described his promotion, his vineyards, and his fine new whiskers, while I hated him with such precision I could have groomed him blindfold. At the third bottle I rose and challenged him: tomorrow morning, a slap across the muzzle, sir, at the fig tree; my seconds would wait upon his. At three in the morning, sober and freezing before my sleeping classmates, I could not do it. "I have come to — to apologize," I said, to a paw thrust out from the leaves, and ran home forgiving them all in a transport that lasted until noon.

We ended that night — compose yourselves; it was a respectable establishment with blue curtains, where tired monkeys got their backs done — at the parlor of the three sisters, where the one called Liza, still almost a child, groomed me with gray honest eyes. And out of me, Gentlemen, out of me — came an aria. I told her she was a flower in a dungeon; that they had given her no love, and she was dying of it; that if she fled into the light she would find her own troop, honest monkeys, trees of her own. I grew quite tearful over her future, being at that moment the only monkey present with no future whatever.

"Where do you live?" she asked, meaning to be kind.

"In a corner, under the roots."

"But you will rot there!"

"Only the monkey at the very bottom," I replied, "can see the whole tree." Ha — that is not even true of monkeys, is it? But I gave her my address, which no monkey had ever asked for, and lay awake until Thursday feeling noble.

Thursday came. She came. She saw the den — the damp, the single leaf-cup, the shame hanging on everything like wet fur — and I, seeing her see it, passed from chimpion of her innocence to small spiteful monster in nine seconds. I lectured her. I wept. I forgave her in advance for everything the troop would ever do to her. And when she fled, I thrust a banana into her paw, because nothing says virtue like fruit.

At the gate she flung it away. My banana! In the mud. In the mud, gentlemen.

These notes of this preening, matted, paradoxical monkey do not end here — under the roots nothing ends; it only itches — but the editor has his limits, and so, I suspect, do you. It is enough.
