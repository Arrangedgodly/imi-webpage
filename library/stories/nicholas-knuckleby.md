# Nicholas Knuckleby

*A parody of "Nicholas Nickleby"*

There once lived in a cellar in the great canopy, a poor gentlewoman of the name of Mrs. Nickleby, with two fine young monkeys to keep: Nicholas, hot-hearted, generous, and built like a youth destined to hit something eventually; and Kate, his sister, pretty, patient, and long-suffering to a degree that should have been listed among the heating fuels.

Upon the death of the father — a monkey of gentle incapacity who had ruined his family by speculating in the one thing nobody could eat, expectations — they applied to the one relation fortune had left them: Ralph Nickleby, an uncle of the money-lending species, a grey, flinty ape with a smile like a crack in stone.

"A pretty family," said Ralph, counting them like coins that proved counterfeit. "I can do nothing for the mother. The girl I can place with a mantua-maker. The boy — I can give the boy a chance. There's a school in Yorkshire. Dotheboys Hall. An admirable institution. Wackford Squeers, the proprietor, is below now, dining on cold loin of mutton."

Mr. Wackford Squeers was a large, scowling baboon with one eye, which he turned upon Nicholas like a turnspit. "Boys, sir? I'm all for boys. I can teach 'em everything — cheap. Dotheboys Hall, Mr. Nickleby: them as can't pay, comes free. My advertisement says so. 'All the -ilities, gentlemen: readin'-ility, writin'-ility, cipherin'-ility — and them as wants more, there's scrubbin'-ility.'"

And so Nicholas was carried north through the snow to Dotheboys Hall — a house of cold porridge, broken windows, and little monkeys of every size in every stage of green wretchedness, each wearing the brown-and-yellow that comes of brimstone, treacle, and no hope. There was Mrs. Squeers, who administered the brimstone-and-treacle "ceremony" with a wooden spoon and washed the boys' faces once a quarter, using the same water. There was young Wackford Squeers, the Hope of the establishment, a pampered little baboon who ate his fill before forty starving eyes. And there was Smike.

Smike was the drudge: a shambling, hollow-eyed young monkey, age indeterminate, brain ill-used, back bent by fifteen years of kicks. He crept to Nicholas the first night, seeing a new assistant master, and asked him the question of the whole book:

"Is the world — is it all like this? I have been here so long, I have forgotten. Is there another kind of monkey anywhere?"

"I am going to find out," said Nicholas. "And if there is not, I shall invent one."

The term taught Nicholas what the Circum-Looshun Office could not. And when Squeers, one grey morning, took his cane to Smike — on the principle that the drudge had dropped a tray — Nicholas heard something in his own blood go off like the boiler of a banana-steamer.

"Wretch!" he cried, hurling off his gown. "Touch him at your peril! I will not stand by and see it done. My blood is up, and I have the strength of ten schoolmasters, for my heart is pure!" And Nicholas Knuckleby, seventeen and immortal, snatched the cane out of the master's fist and flogged Wackford Squeers soundly about his own schoolroom, before his own Mrs. Squeers, his own offspring, and all his own unhappy boys, who watched with their porridge spoons suspended, learning more in those three minutes than in five years of -ilities.

"You shall pay for this!" howled Squeers, from under the forms.

"Send the bill to my uncle," said Nicholas, and took Smike by the hand, and walked out of Dotheboys Hall forever. "You shall come with me. I have beaten your master; nobody shall beat you again."

London gave them nothing. Ralph shut his door — "you have struck at my character, sir, in striking at my agent" — and Nicholas and Smike walked to Portsmouth with nine shillings, where the theatre took them in. The company of Vincent Crummles! — Mrs. Crummles the Tragic Guenon ("I am never to be what I have been, never, never! — say the line as I do, Knuckleby, from the diaphragm"), the phenomenon, and the Infant Phenomenon, a marmoset of ten who had been ten for a decade, preserved, like tropical fruit, by constant admiration. Nicholas played a bandit with his own hair; Smike played a specter, which required no costume; and for one golden season the two of them were almost warm.

Kate, meanwhile, was living the other half of the story. Ralph, to sweeten a rich client, lent his niece's society to Sir Mulberry Hawk — a leering, lorgnetted saki of quality — who hunted her from box to supper-party to carriage-step, until Kate went home and cried into her plain bed-curtains, and told nobody, because her mother was telling everybody about a gentleman who had bowed.

Nicholas heard it from Newman Noggs — Ralph's clerk, a broken gentlemonkey who drank and laughed at the fire and had a heart under his rags like a pearl under a heap of dust. Nicholas's blood, still up, remained up. He ran Hawk's carriage to ground in the lane, wrenched open the door, and dragged the gentleman out by his collar into the mud, with perfect courtesy and total violence.

"I am the brother of the monkey you have persecuted," he said. "You will avoid her society from this hour. If you need a reason, remember my hand on your windpipe and improve your memory."

Hawk's own partner in vice, the fat young Lord Verisopht, sick of him at last, called him out over Kate's honor, took the bullet meant as theater, and died apologizing to the man who killed him. Hawk went abroad, where such monkeys congregate; and Newman Noggs laughed alone at his fire for an hour straight, which was his applause.

The tide turned with the Cheeryble twins — two chubby, chuckling, bottle-green brothers, merchants and millionaires, who existed for the twin purposes of commerce and overwhelming kindness. They made Nicholas their man of business; they found work that made his soul fit his pelt; and they put him in the path of Madeline Bray — a quiet, lovely monkey keeping her proud, dying, debt-ridden father on nothing but gentility — whom Nicholas loved at first sight and rescued, at the second, from being married at her father's creditors' convenience to an old miser named Gride, on a morning Nicholas arrived just late enough to be dramatic and just early enough to matter.

But the past was not done. Smike — failing now, coughing now, slow and sweet — was kidnapped back by Squeers and dragged again to Yorkshire, and once more Nicholas fetched him out, with Newman's help, through a window, over the snow. Smike had only one want left: to stay near Nicholas. "When I am dead," he whispered, "bury me under the tree where we rested, by the road from that place. For I loved you, and you were the only monkey who ever saw me."

He died in the spring, under the blossoms, in that belief.

And then the story's one great ledger balanced itself. The truth about Smike came out through Newman Noggs and a blackmailer named Squeers's old accomplice — the boy was Ralph Nickleby's own son; born of a gentlewoman Ralph had ruined and cast off, told the child was dead, farmed secretly into the Bramblesea of Dotheboys Hall, and worked to the bone within a mile of starvation for fifteen years — while his father, three streets away, counted interest on him like any other account.

Ralph heard it once and refused it twice. The third time, he went to the window of his own counting-house and looked down into the dark street where his clerks had long ago stopped expecting him to have a soul, and understood, with the arithmetic that was his whole nature, exactly what he had spent and what it had bought. They found him the next morning, swinging gently from his own beam, alone with the one sound account he ever kept.

The Cheerybles settled everything else at a speed that suggested they had arranged it years since: Nicholas got the business and Madeline; Kate got the younger Cheeryble's nephew Frank, after apologies all round; Mrs. Nickleby got a cottage and a neighbor who was not a lunatic gentleman, whatever she said to the contrary; Newman Noggs got sober and a good place, in that order. And under the tree by the Yorkshire road, every spring, the blossom came up over Smike — Ralph's son, Nicholas's friend, and the only character in the whole canopy who got out of every single book he was ever in, and into only one that mattered.
