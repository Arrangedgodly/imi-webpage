# Swingworld

*A parody of "Ringworld"*

Louis Wu threw his two-hundredth birthday party straight around the world. He was a bonobo of restless tastes, and when midnight caught him in the Eastern Groves he stepped into a transfer booth and stepped out in the West, where the party was young again — and so on around the whole planet, chasing his own birthday like a monkey chasing his tail, for exactly twenty-four hours, at which point a sensible bonobo, he always said, stops chasing.

At the last stop, at the party's final flickering hour, his plate of celebratory figs vanished from his hand.

Nessus had one of his two heads hold the plate. Nessus was a Brachiateer — a double-headed gibbon of the wealthiest, most timid species known to science — and, by the standards of his own kind, a raving lunatic, being the only Brachiateer in living memory willing to leave home. His species' whole civilization had packed up and fled the galaxy for the Greater Mango Clouds ages ago, for safety, which tells you what the galaxy is like.

"Louis Wu," said Nessus's left head, while the right head ate his fig, a Brachiateer habit that cannot be unlearned, "the Hindmost of my troop requires a crew."

"The Hindmost," said Louis, "is your leader, whom nobody is allowed to meet, because he might be killed by a stranger."

"Correct. You have met him. You will pretend you have not." The right head swallowed. "There is a thing in deep space. A ring of woven branch and vine, circling a sun. A band of living canopy six hundred million fig-trees wide — a million miles wide, if you must have it in your cold numbers — spinning, verdant, and *artificial*. Nobody built it that we know. The Hindmost wishes to know. He has selected a crew."

The crew was Louis Wu; a gorilla diplomat called Speaker-to-Animals, who was eight feet of muscle wrapped in formal diplomacy and had accepted the invitation mainly because it annoyed his own government; and a young golden lion tamarin named Teela Brown, whom Louis had picked up at his own party — bright, bafflingly cheerful, and, as Louis learned only aboard the ship, the seventh generation of her line to be born to winners of the Birthright Lottery, a lottery quietly sponsored for a century by the Brachiateers, who had been *breeding* for something the galaxy's greatest cowards wanted on their expedition.

"Luck," said Nessus, when Louis demanded the truth in open crew council. "My troop has been selecting your species for luck for a hundred years. Teela Brown is the flower of the experiment. We are taking her with us because statistically, Louis, nothing terminal will happen to whatever branch she is sitting on."

"Have you considered," said Speaker-to-Animals, in the deep unhurried voice of a gorilla composing a diplomatic incident, "that you have merely bred for monkeys who win lotteries?"

"No," said both heads of Nessus at once, and the matter was closed.

They went out on the *Long Shot*, an experimental ship in a Brachiateer hull — a woven hull absolutely impervious to anything the universe had so far thought of, which is why Brachiateer hulls cost more than small planets. Three monkeys and a Brachiateer rode it through wit-warping distances, and arrived, and stopped talking entirely, because there is a size at which awe shuts monkeys up.

The Swingworld filled a third of the sky: a horizonless ribbon of canopy looping around its private sun, green beyond green, with a rim wall a thousand miles high to hold the air in, and night provided by an inner ring of vast spinning shadow-leaves that blocked the sun by appointment, like a clock made of shadows.

"The engineering," Speaker-to-Animals said at last, professionally. "The *weave*. Someone braided a world."

"Someone," said Nessus, "has fallen behind on maintenance," and he pointed with both chins at the hole in the sky of it: a whole shadow-leaf stalled at noon, a raw sunburnt blister of dead canopy a million miles long beneath it.

They took the lander — the *Cheeky Monkey*, an invulnerable Brachiateer-hulled pod with thrusters and a smug name — down toward the great braid. And the Swingworld, which had not been touched by anything from outside for a very long time, reached up with an induction fist of pure field and swatted every motor, thruster, and light aboard it dead.

The *Cheeky Monkey* fell out of the sky like a very confident stone. The hull, of course, was unscratched; the passengers arrived as sculpture.

They survived — Louis rigged a sail-wing of canopy-vine, Speaker calculated drift, Teela found the one thermal that happened to be going their way, which everyone noted and nobody said aloud — and came down on a prairie of shoulder-high grass gone strange with ages. The crew-mind's greatest discovery, walking out under an alien sun onto a woven world, was that the Swingworld was *abandoned*: not dead — there was jungle for ten million miles in every direction — but empty of its builders the way an old nest is empty, and older than most civilizations, and the machinery of heaven was coming apart above them like an untended loom.

They learned its fall from the Night Troop — gibbous-eyed, soft-footed, far-hungry monkeys who came down out of the high shadow at dusk and traded news of a million miles of grass for news of the crew, and were cheerful about eating the dead, being the Swingworld's undertakers, its mapmakers, and its library. The builders had risen, said the Night Troop, and built, and chained heaven to a clock — and then, ages past, a plague of their own making, or a war of their own weaving, or simply the long sleep of a world with no wolves left to sharpen it; the old groves failed, the loom-tenders forgot, and the great canopy began to fray. "The branches rot from the center," the eldest of the Night Troop said, chewing reflectively on a hero's-farewell jerky Louis did not ask about. "And the fruit falls. Always, in every world, the fruit falls."

In the floating ruin of a police station — a whole palace of the old order, still drifting on its ancient lift-vine motors above a drowned city — they found Prilla: a macaque woman of the lost builder race, raised in the ruins on oldromance and older machinery, sharp as a fruit-knife and twice as bright. She showed them the drifting towers, the arch where Seeker kept the last of the working power — Seeker being an orangutan the size of a doorway, a classic Seeker: a pilgrim guarding a fading wonder of the world because nobody else had — and it was Seeker's arch that Teela Brown fell in love with, or with Seeker, or with the idea of a danger worth keeping.

For that was the trap at the bottom of the Birthright experiment, and Louis finally saw it whole. Teela's luck did not protect Teela from pain. It protected the *bloodline* — by arranging her life to be an endless series of survived lessons. Luck, bred true, is not a charm. It is a curriculum. And Teela Brown, to Louis Wu's lasting ache, was its happiest student: she chose to stay, swinging off into the deep grass at Seeker's side, radiant, down the long bright corridor of everything that would nearly kill her and never quite manage.

"That," said Speaker-to-Animals quietly, "was the experiment's result. The Brachiateers will be pleased."

"The Brachiateers," said Louis, "can breed their own heartbreak."

The escape came, as escapes do, from Louis Wu finally asking the *engineering* question. The Swingworld's problem was not damage; it was attitude — the ring needed active guidance to hold its sun, and its makers had omitted, or lost, the correcting hands. "It's unstable," Louis said, over the charts, drumming the table. "Don't any of you see it? *The Swingworld is unstable.* It has been wobbling for a million years and nobody is steering — that stalled shadow-leaf, the dead blister, the fraying braid, all of it. Whoever stabilized it, we're their only heirs in a thousand light-years."

They raised the police station's lift-motors, taught it Prilla's old commands, and flew it — a floating palace, four primates and a ghost of a civilization — a million miles to the Fist of Kong: the great mountain where, in some forgotten age, something from outside had punched a meteor clean *through* the Swingworld's floor, leaving a shaft through the whole woven world and out the underside of heaven. And through that impossible hole, riding the palace's last strength, they punched back: out into the clean black, where the *Long Shot* was waiting, and where the Hindmost — trembling in two languages behind a screen the whole time — was very, very eager to bargain.

Louis named the price. The Brachiateers, heaven's most professional cowards, would return with the knowledge, the engines, and the nerve to put steering back in a broken ring — and take, in exchange, the only thing they had ever wanted: the certainty that nothing big and artificial was aimed at anybody.

"One day I'll come back," Louis Wu said, looking down through the hole in the world at the green running under everything, a million miles of canopy and one tamarin somewhere in it, swinging.

"Statistically," said both heads of Nessus, "she'll be fine."

And Louis Wu, two hundred years old and wiser, said nothing at all, because there are things even a bonobo cannot say with a straight face.
