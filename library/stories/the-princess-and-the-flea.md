# The Princess and the Flea

*A parody of "The Princess and the Pea"*

There was once a young prince chimp — Prince Pomelo of the Coconut Crown — who wanted, more than anything else in the jungle, to marry a real princess. Not a sort-of princess. Not a princess by marriage, or by rumor, or by particularly good posture. A real one, all the way through.

So he traveled the world. He interviewed princesses on mountain thrones and river thrones, princesses with golden fruit services and princesses with very persuasive heralds. One turned out to be a duchess with a good publicist. One was a princess of real estate. One was mostly hat, and behind the hat was a very confident parrot running the whole court. One was genuinely royal, but nocturnal, and slept through the betrothal banquet in the soup.

In the mountain kingdom of Prunella he met a princess who wept at everything, which her court called sensitivity; when the prince rode away, she wept, and the court called that a sign as well. In the river kingdom he met one who claimed to feel a pea through forty mattresses, but when the court tested her quietly with a pebble, she felt nothing at all and blamed the pebble's attitude. The prince's respect for produce-based testing grew enormously that season, and his faith in princesses shrank to fit.

And the prince came home sadder than a monkey in a mango-free month, dragging his tail behind him.

"Princesses there are plenty," sighed the prince. "But whether they are real princesses — that, Mother, is another banana entirely."

"But how," asked the old queen, "would you ever know one when you met one?"

"Ah," said the prince, "if only they came with certificates."

The old queen said nothing, because old queens know exactly how tests are made, and none of them involve certificates.

The king, for his part, had opinions of his own. "In my day," he said, "we married the nearest princess and complained about it for forty years. It built character."

"Then you'll be delighted," said the queen, "to see how character gets built in THIS generation," and she had the good silver figs polished, on the chance that the jungle was listening.

One evening there came a storm the like of which the jungle had never seen. Rain drummed the great leaves like a hundred tiny drummers; lightning stitched the sky; thunder rolled about the canopy like a barrel of monkeys with the lid nailed shut. And through all of it, somebody knocked at the gate of the city.

The gate-keeper baboon, who had been a soldier for thirty years and a pessimist for forty, peered out with the lantern. There stood a little she-monkey, soaked to the whiskers, water streaming off her elbows and her tail, her fur plastered flat as a leaf in a hurricane. The rain had put out the lantern twice, and she had waited, both times, without complaint, until it was relit.

Yet she stood as straight as a sapling, and she said:

"I am a real princess."

The gate-keeper reported this at the palace with raised eyebrows. "A likely story," murmured the court monkeys, when she was brought inside, dripping on the good mats. "Real princesses do not drip."

"Real princesses," said a chamberlain, "arrive with an entourage."

"Real princesses," said the treasurer, "arrive with a loan."

But the old queen said nothing at all. She only thought, *we shall see about that.* And she went quietly to the finest guest room in the palace and made up the bed herself, dismissing the servants, since a test is only a test if nobody talks.

First she took one single jungle flea — a queen always keeps one on standby for exactly this sort of emergency; it lives in a hazelnut shell and is fed on schedule — and set it, very gently, upon the bottom-most banana leaf. Upon that leaf she stacked twenty mattresses of woven green banana leaves. Upon the mattresses, twenty kapok comforters. And on the very top, the royal red cushion.

"There, my dear," said the queen. "Sleep well."

The princess looked at the bed, which stood against the sky in the manner of a small weather system, and at the ladder, and said, "Thank you," in the voice of royalty being extremely game about something.

"Thank you," said the princess, politely, though she did look at the ladder to the bed — for by now a ladder was required — with an expression the court chronicler recorded as "royal resignation."

All night the storm howled, and nobody in the palace slept much anyway. The king got up twice to check that the treasury hadn't leaked. The court, meanwhile, ran a quiet betting pool on the guest's authenticity, odds of three to one on "dripping commoner," with a side wager on "runaway duchess."

In the morning the guest came down with shadows under her eyes and a certain careful way of sitting.

The queen asked brightly how she had rested.

"Oh, dreadfully!" cried the little she-monkey. "Miserably! Heaven knows what was in that bed! Something hopped about all night from leaf to leaf — flop, hop, bite — and I have a bump the size of a beetle on my royal rump to show for it! I scarcely closed my eyes once! And the ladder, if I may say so, is two rungs short of anything!"

At this the queen, the king, and the prince exchanged a look of wonder. Through twenty mattresses and twenty comforters of woven banana leaf, the little she-monkey had felt one single jungle flea. No ordinary monkey could have done such a thing. Only a real princess could possibly be so magnificently sensitive.

"Heavens," whispered the lady-in-waiting who had made up the fire, and who had slept her whole life with an entire orchestra of fleas and never missed a wink. "I have truly been entertaining royalty."

"And the ladder," said the prince, taking her hand, "I will have lengthened myself. Two rungs. You counted."

"I count everything," said the princess. "It is a real affliction. Ask my fleas."

They were married that very season, with mango cake, a choir of howler monkeys, and a procession of twelve decorated tapirs (eleven of whom behaved). The king complained about the wedding for forty years, as planned, and built character the whole time.

And the flea — because you simply do not throw away evidence like that — was given a royal pension and a little glass display case in the palace museum, with a small brass plaque that reads: THE VERY FLEA.

To this day, every royal guest in the palace sleeps one night in that same room, on those same mattresses (the ladder was lengthened; two rungs), and is asked at breakfast how they rested. Kings say "soundly." Ambassadors say "deliciously." Only once has anyone said "dreadfully" — and that was the night the chamberlain's daughter came to stay, and she was made a princess on the spot, and married the admiral.

Now, whether it was truly a flea, or only a very ambitious mosquito, nobody could ever say for certain. But the bite was real, the wedding was real, and this — as the old queen tells it — is a true story.
