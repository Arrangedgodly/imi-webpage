# Gone Gorilla

*A parody of "Gone Girl"*

When I think of my wife, I always think of the back of her head — the sleek silver fur she groomed every night until it gleamed like a polished plantain. I used to fantasize about peeling back her skull, leaf by leaf, to see what she was thinking. What kind of husband does that? The kind whose wife disappears on the morning of their fifth annivers-ape-y.

It started with breakfast. I came down the trunk of our treehouse in North Chimp-age expecting Amy to pretend she didn't know I'd forgotten the date. Instead: the fruit bowl on the floor, the juice bar overturned, and a single banana — perfectly ripe, perfectly placed — lying in the middle of the room like a dare.

"Bongo!" I called to my twin sister, who ran our bar, The Branch, down by the river. "Something's wrong with Amy."

"Did you two fight?" Bongo said, clambering over.

"We always fight. We're married. It's basically the same thing."

Then came the police, the volunteers, the cameras. Detective Rhonda Boney, a macaque with kind eyes and a pitiless little notebook, found the first clue taped under the juice bar — we did a treasure hunt every anniversary, and Amy never missed a tradition. *Clue One: come to where we first split a 'nana, my prime mate.* That was the trouble with my wife. Her clues sounded like promises, and her promises were traps. Clue Two led to the beach where we'd talked about babies; Clue Three, to my woodshed, which was locked, which the police noted. Clue Four was a poem about a man who "likes them young," which was insane, and which I couldn't explain either.

I could explain some of it. Just not out loud. Not even to Bongo. Especially not to Bongo.

Item: I had been "teaching grooming basics" to a twenty-three-year-old colobus named Andie, whose fur smelled like green bananas, twice a week since spring. Item: my last eighty thousand — Amy's trust, technically — I had quietly handed to her parents, whose Amazing Amee books were being pulped by their publisher faster than they could print them. The famous perfect gorilla was bankrupting the family that invented her. Item: Rhonda Boney kept asking what I did that morning, and every honest answer made it worse.

The troop paper ran the photo from the first press conference — the one where I smiled, a half-second too long, at the wrong moment. GONE GORILLA, the headline said. By June I was the most hated male in Missouri-marmoset country. The only ape still grooming my back was Bongo; the only thing defending me was Amy's diary, which the papers loved, because it read like this:

*Diary of a Long-Tailed Lady. He smiles at that baboon bartender. He has never once peeled a banana and offered me the bottom half, which is the sweeter half, and which everyone knows is the measure of a man. I am a gorilla married to a shrug.*

Here is what I did that morning — Amy's version, later, with her feet up: I tipped over my own juice bar. I cracked a plum on the floorboards so things would look worse than they were. I left my husband a banana and a treasure hunt, because if you're going to frame a male, frame him with his own favorite snack. I was, I admit it, going full banana.

She'd had it planned for a year. The woodshed full of receipts — club memberships, tech gear, a crate marked ANNIVERSARY in my handwriting, bought with my card, my signature, my alibi dying line by line. The diary entries, forged backward through time, each one half-true, which is the only kind of lie that holds. The Cool Monkey speech, written on napkins, waiting: *He married a Cool Monkey. The Cool Monkey likes whatever he likes. She trots after him with a banana and no opinions of her own. The Cool Monkey is a lie, and I was exhausted from peeling her.*

She drifted the river to fake the drag-marks, then hiked to a rented campervan near a town called Hannibal, which she'd always had doubts about, name-wise. She lived on freeze-dried banana chips and spite, watching herself on the news, growing thinner and more pleased.

The plan had one flaw, and the flaw was other monkeys. At the Ozark cabins a chatty pair — Greta and Jeff, of course they were called Greta and Jeff — recognized the famous missing wife under the bad dye job, and robbed her of every last crumpled banana chip while she slept. And that is how Amy, broke and haircut ruined, came to be standing in the dark outside the lake house of Desi Sifaka.

The plan's other flaw was me. I hired Tannerine Bolt, a defense attorney so expensive he charged by the syllable, and did the one thing Amy could not script: I went on television and told the truth. That I loved her. That I'd been a bad husband to a good gorilla. That I wanted her back — and I looked, for the first time that year, like a male who meant it.

Amy watched it in the campervan, twice.

"That's not the husband I framed," she said, sulking. "He's supposed to keep lying. He's no fun anymore." And no monkey alive leaves a stage where she is the twist.

So: Desi. Silk-furred, gym-broad Desi, who had loved Amy since they were juveniles, and who, when she showed up claiming I'd driven her into hiding, fluffed the pillows and confiscated her nail file. Nobody keeps Amy. Amy keeps Amy. She mussed her own grooming, staged the bedroom, did things with a plum and a hand-mirror that we will not describe on a family program, and when I walked into the hospital six weeks later, my wife rolled in on a gurney, pitiful, with poor Desi's fur fibers under her nails and Desi himself, as they say, unavailable for comment.

"You banana-split witch," I whispered, when the lemons cameras couldn't hear. "You magnificent, overripe piece of work."

"Watch your tone," Amy said pleasantly, "or I'll tell them about the woodshed."

"Andie came forward," I said. "She's been on every show that would have her. Rhonda Boney knows, too — she says the timeline's a fruit salad."

"Rhonda Boney," said Amy, "wants to be proven wrong. Everyone does, eventually. They need me alive more than they need me guilty. I'm the gorilla who came back."

And the horrifying thing was that she was right. The charges evaporated like morning dew off a hot tin feeding trough. Desi's estate sued; the estate settled. Greta and Jeff sold their story and were believed for a full news cycle. The Amazing Amee books went back into print with a special tenth-anniversary foreword by the author's "miracle daughter." Even my mother liked her again, and my mother was dead.

That night I climbed into bed beside my wife, because a husband doesn't abandon his prime mate. Not with the troop watching. Not with a little one coming — she'd made sure of that, too, and told me, hand on her belly, eyes on mine, that motherhood was the one role nobody could take from her and nobody could write better.

"What are you thinking?" I asked her at last. "How are you feeling? What bananas have we split to each other?"

Amy smiled the smile I'd married, the one I'd never once learned to read, and turned out the light.

"Go to sleep, my darling," she said. "Tomorrow we do it all again."

And down at The Branch, Bongo poured herself a nightcap and updated a file she kept behind the bar, just in case anybody ever asked. It says one word: RUN.
