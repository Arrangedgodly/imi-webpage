# Around the World in Apey Days

*A parody of "Around the World in Eighty Days"*

Phileas Fogg fired his valet at 11:29 for bringing brushing water at eighty-four degrees instead of eighty-six. At 11:33 he hired a new one.

The new one was Passepartout, a French macaque who had been — by his own account — a singer, a circus tumbler, an assistant keeper at the Paris Zoo, and five minutes from jail. He liked his new master instantly, because the orangutan had clearly never done anything at all, ever, and intended to continue.

At 7:25 that evening, as always, Fogg went to the Reform Club, where elderly gibbons played whist and doubted things. That night they doubted aloud that any monkey could travel around the world in eighty days — the Bishop of Banbury had declared the world flat as a fig, and the gibbons respected a bishop — and Fogg, an orangutan of iron routine and immaculate fur, laid twenty thousand bananas on the table against their twenty thousand.

"I shall be back in this room," he said, "on the eightieth day, by 8:45 in the evening."

He was home packing by 7:50. Passepartout packed for both of them, and at 8:45 precisely they swung away east.

They were followed. At Suez, Detective Fix of the Zoo Constabulary, a plainclothes baboon with a warrant in his pouch, studied Fogg at the consulate and did not like what his whiskers told him: the Bank of England had been robbed of fifty-five thousand bananas, and here was a gentleman fleeing east around the world with unlimited fruit and no explanation. Fix wired London for the warrant and got Fogg's passport signed "for the file."

"You suspect my master of the robbery," said Passepartout, wounded.

"I suspect every monkey who is punctual," said Fix. "Honesty keeps no schedule."

In Bombay, Passepartout made the great mistake of his tour: he entered a shrine to look at the architecture and neglected to remove his shoes, his hat, and — the temple elders alleged — his tail-ring, which he had absolutely been told to leave in the cloak room, or would have been, had he spoken a word of the language. He escaped through a laundry basket and rejoined his master minus shoes, hat, and dignity. Fogg paid the fines without expression, and Passepartout concluded that his master's calm was the most expensive calm in the Empire.

In India the vine-line ended at Kholby: fifty miles of unswung jungle. Fogg bought an elephant named Kiouni — twenty bananas an hour, plus trunk insurance — and they pushed into the forest, where they came upon a procession bearing a sealed crate down to the docks. Inside, drugged on overripe fruit, lay the young monkey Aouda, being sent as tribute to the Maharajah's menagerie.

"We will rescue her," said Fogg, "or not leave."

The guides refused to help, so Passepartout put his circus years to work: in the smoke of the ritual fires, he carried Aouda out of the crate and onto Kiouni's back, and the elephant, who had apparently been waiting all day for someone to propose exactly this, charged off through the boughs with the three of them clinging to her ears. At the next station Fogg shook trunks with her gravely. It was, Passepartout swore later, the longest goodbye of the nineteenth century.

Aouda traveled on with them, discovering that her rescuer was the coldest gentlefur she had ever met — until the day he risked the whole wager, and every banana of it, to come back for her.

In Hong Kong, Fix changed tactics. "Your master is the bank robber," he told Passepartout over sap-wine. "I am an officer of the law. Help me delay him, and when I arrest him we split the reward — two thousand bananas."

"Monsieur," said Passepartout, "my master is the most honest orangutan in England, which admittedly is not a high bar, but he clears it." Then Fix bought the next round, and the one after that, and the macaque missed his boat, woke aboard the wrong vessel, and was stranded in Japan — where he joined a troupe of acrobats as "the Tumbling Tail," and was rebuilding a monkey pyramid on the very pier his master's steamer left from when a calm, familiar voice said, "Tomorrow, perhaps, we could rehearse the departure as well."

On they went: across the Pacific, across America by rail. En route the train was attacked by the Bandar-log, who were, in fairness, also monkeys, which made the paperwork awkward. Passepartout slipped beneath the cars and uncoupled the passenger tail, saving everyone aboard it and getting himself captured, so that Fogg had to march to Fort Kearney with thirty soldiers and buy him back. They crossed the snow on a sled with sailcloth, which Passepartout steered by leaning, and which Fogg described afterward as "brisk."

At New York they missed the steamer, so Fogg boarded a coastal packet called the Henrietta and simply bought her from Captain Speedy, a seasick gorilla in oilskins, for a stack of banknotes that made the old sailor sit down on his own spyglass. When the coal ran out mid-Atlantic, Fogg fed the furnace with the crates, the cabin doors, the deck planks, and finally the staterooms; Captain Speedy wept into the smoke and was heard to admit she'd always burned better than she sailed.

They reached Liverpool at noon on the eightieth day, with five hours in paw — and Fix, warrant finally arrived, laid his paw on Fogg's shoulder.

"Phileas Fogg, I arrest you in the name of the Zoo Constabulary."

Fogg was locked in a cell from noon to 8:40 that evening, sitting silent while the clock ate his fortune. At 8:40, pale and trembling, Fix reappeared: the real robber, one James Strand, had been caught that very morning in Edinburgh, and the warrant was now, in the legal sense, a very large banana peel. "Sir," Fix stammered, "a thousand pardons —"

Fogg rose, crossed the cell, and knocked the detective's beret over his ears with one dignified blow. He then apologized to the beret, ordered a special train, and reached London at 8:50 on what he believed was the eightieth day.

Five minutes late. Lost. Fogg thanked the crew, paid the wages he owed, and went home without bananas, without honor, and — Passepartout noticed — without seeming to care much about anything except that Aouda had someone to look after her now. That night she found him by the cold hearth. "You have lost your wager for me," she said. "I have nothing to give you but my troop, and my friendship, and —"

He had reckoned without the sunrise.

The next day, Passepartout was sent out on an errand at 11:40 and stood before the club clock at noon, staring, counting, going pale beneath his fur. They had traveled east, with the sun, and had gained — quietly, a minute at a time — an entire day.

"It is today!" he shrieked, and raced home and seized his master by the collar, dragging him through the streets in an irresistible fashion while Fogg checked his watch, appalled at the impropriety and, faintly, at the arithmetic.

Phileas Fogg entered the Reform Club at 8:45:57 on the eightieth day, won twenty thousand bananas, and became the most famous orangutan alive. The gibbons, who had doubted him in three oceans, now claimed to have believed in him throughout, and the Bishop of Banbury announced that the world was, on reflection, round the way a fig is.

Fogg split the winnings with his despairing crew of one, gave Passepartout the warmest handshake of the century, and let Fix keep the beret.

He had gone around the world in eighty days. But what had he gained? Nothing, say the gibbons. Nothing but a charming mate, the loyalty of a good macaque, and the discovery that the world is round the way a fig is: keep going, and you come home.

Aouda proposed to him herself. He accepted in under a minute. It was, for him, an eternity of wild abandon.
