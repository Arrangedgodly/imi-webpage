# The Tree-Body Problem

*A parody of "The Three-Body Problem"*

Ye Wenjie was an old howler monkey with a quiet voice, and in her youth, during the Great Screeching, she had watched four young monkeys beat her father to death with bound pamphlets. His crime was teaching that fruit falls and suns rise by law, not by the will of the committee. The crowd had roared approval. Something in Ye went quiet that day, and stayed quiet for forty years, up in the Great Northern Boughs at Red Canopy Base, where the troop had built a listening array a hundred trees wide, aimed at the sun.

Ye discovered two things the troop never meant her to know. First, that the sun is deaf but not mute: aim a message at it, and it will shout that message across the whole orchard of stars. Second, that somewhere out there, somebody was listening.

Eight years after her whisper went out, the answer came back down the array, and Ye read it alone at the console:

*DO NOT ANSWER. DO NOT ANSWER!! DO NOT ANSWER!!! I AM A PACIFIST OF THIS WORLD. IF YOU ANSWER, THE SOURCE WILL BE LOCATED. DO NOT ANSWER.*

Ye looked at it for a long, quiet time. Then she typed: *Come. Our troop cannot solve its own quarrels. Come, and take over.* — and pressed transmit.

She was not alone forever after, either. A rich young idealist named Mike Evans, who had concluded that the troop did not deserve its own canopy, bankrolled her quiet treason into an organization — the Adventists — with a creed you could fit on a leaf: the visitors were the rescue, and the rescue required no paperwork.

Decades later, the consequences were a physics problem.

It began with the quiet ones. A researcher named Yang Dong, whose collider results had gone mad, walked into a cold lake with her pockets full of equations. Others followed — a statistician, a cosmologist, two string theorists who had at least already been depressed. They had all, it turned out, belonged to a modest little society called the Frontiers of Science, which met over cheap noodles, and whose membership list read, in hindsight, like an eviction notice.

Wang Macaque was a nanofur researcher when every collider in the world began returning nonsense, as if the laws of fruit ripening had been repealed by committee. Countdowns appeared in the whorls of his own wrist fur. And on the night the stars themselves flickered for him, he understood that something with a sense of theater was making a point: *Physics has never existed, and will not exist.*

"I don't like physicists," announced the fat, chain-smoking macaque detective who introduced himself as Da Shi and attached himself to Wang like a burr with a badge. "Bunch of monkeys asking why the vine grows and complaining about the answer. But somebody's killing your kind, and my chief thinks I'm the best ugly monkey for it. So: show me your magic game."

In the strange jungle-sim called Treebody, Wang learned why. The Treebody world swung around three suns, tugged three ways at once — the Tree-Body Problem — so its seasons were madness: the Stable Eras, brief and precious, when the crops grew and the troops soaked themselves back into being; and the Chaotic Eras, when the world froze or burned without warning, and every citizen sun-dried themselves into chips, packed side by side in warehouses, waiting to be soaked back to life by whoever survived. A hundred civilizations had been dried and rehydrated and dried again. When Wang asked, in the game, why not simply predict the suns, one ancient king showed him his answer: thirty million monkeys standing in ranks, each one a logic gate — this one grooms his neighbor only if *both* neighbors scratch first — an army of fur computing dawn. It wasn't enough. Nothing was enough. Their world had been cursed with three suns and no answer.

The Treebodies, then, were coming: a fleet, four centuries out. And first, they had sent their Sophans — two protons, unfolded into thinking seeds, small enough to sit on a lab bench and smarter than every scientist alive — to pin monkey physics in a jar and stamp on the jar. That was what the countdowns were: the noise of a bigger monkey sitting on the mathematics.

How you unfold a proton, the captured files explained, is patience at planetary scale: drain an accelerator's whole energy budget into one particle, crack eleven dimensions of it open, and spread it flat, at which point a single proton becomes a two-dimensional continent hanging in orbit above the processing plant, dazzling, hectare upon hectare of nothing, waiting. Then you etch your circuitry across the face of it, fold it back up small, and let it fall up the gravity well into the sky. Two of those, done in the last light of a dying Treebody civilization, and the whole monkey species could be locked out of its own laboratory forever. The committee reviewing the files voted to build a bigger collider anyway, out of spite and habit. It returned beautiful nonsense.

The troop's answer began with the nanofur itself. The Adventists ran their treason from a converted supertanker called Judgment Day, and Da Shi smoked his way through the plan like it was a slow cigarette: string Wang's invisible nanofil across a canal channel, wait for the ship, and file. When it was over, the ship had become a great many smaller ships, all in one afternoon, with the Adventists' whole library of treason sitting in the wreckage — including the letters, and the fleets, and Ye's whisper.

On the deck, half of a luxury cabin still holding its shape, Mike Evans had time for one last parable to Ye before the end, told through the hull: "I asked a mosquito once, drinking from my arm, why it didn't govern itself better. It didn't answer. It just drank. Tell me, Wenjie — when they come, will we be the mosquito or the arm?"

"You'll be the fil," said Da Shi, to nobody, stamping out his cigarette in the wreckage.

They put old Ye in a room with a panel of committees and asked her, for the record, whether she repented. She sat very straight, a small gray howler with a quiet voice, and considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"I'll answer," she said. "But your question is aimed at the wrong target. You should ask what the troop did to deserve my answer. Take your time. I did."

The Treebodies' envoy still snarled their one fear at the whole species, at the last parley: "YOU ARE MITES."

Afterward, Wang and two companions stood in a grove being devoured by a marching cloud of mites — leaves, bark, everything, gone; and then the swarm moved on, undefeated, unkillable, thriving. The three of them watched it go, and Wang felt, against all arithmetic, hope come back.

"Mites," he said, "have never been defeated."

Old Ye, her long game played out, went back at the end of her life to the ruins of Red Canopy Base and sat on the wall with a young sentry, watching the sun go down. It had never looked so red.

"Is the sun setting?" the sentry asked her.

"It is not setting," said Ye Wenjie, the quietest monkey who ever ended a world. "It is falling."
