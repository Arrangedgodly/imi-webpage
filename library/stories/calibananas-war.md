# Calibanana's War

*A parody of "Caliban's War"*

Ganymede was the garden moon. Great mirrored wings hung over it, catching the sun, keeping the plantations warm; the whole Branch ate off what Ganymede grew. Praxidike Mango — Prax, to everyone who loved him, which was everyone who had met him — was an orangutan botanist there, a gentle, enormous academic who grew soy and spirulina and, on the bad nights of his life, recited plant names to stay calm.

One of those nights began with the mirrors falling.

A battle started in orbit — Mars shooting at Earth, everyone shooting at everyone — and then something came down *between* the lines, and it did not wear a suit, and it did not bleed. Sergeant Bobbie Draper, a gorilla marine of Mars, watched it walk through plasma fire like a monkey walking through rain. Her bolts bounced off like fig seeds off a bull's brow. It went through her squad — through Gouran, through Opteran, through everyone but her — and away, and left Bobbie alive in her cracked power armor with the mirrors raining down as burning snow and the crops starting to freeze behind them.

"We killed it," her lieutenant reported from the wreckage, not believing himself.

"You didn't kill it," Bobbie said, watching it swim up through the black smoke toward orbit. "You annoyed it. Those aren't the same, whatever the paperwork says."

They debriefed her on Earth, of all places — a Martian marine in full dress uniform, telling a room of UN admirals that the entire Ganymede incident had been stagecraft, somebody's lie with her squad as the makeup. Earth's gravity sat on her like a grievance. Halfway through, one admiral suggested, kindly, that she might be overstressed. Bobbie stood up, lifted the conference table with one paw to get her tail out from under it, and asked if that answered the question. The room voted yes.

In the blackout, Prax's daughter Mei — small, sickly, missing one enzyme her body needed — was carried out of the clinic by a monkey in a doctor's coat, and did not come back.

Everyone Prax begged for help wanted payment. He tried the honest way first: a public appeal, straight to the lens, no production values, just a thin orange botanist in a borrowed jacket saying, "My name is Praxidike Mango. My daughter Mei was taken during the blackout. She is five. She is missing one enzyme. I am not a rich man. I am asking for help." The Branch, which fast-forwards through everything, watched it to the end, twice. Money arrived in amounts that made an accountant cry; tips arrived in amounts that made a detective useful; and one reply arrived from a stolen gunship saying only: *Where do you want us?*

It was the shabby crew of that gunship that came: a chimp who broadcast too much honesty, a spider monkey engineer, a macaque pilot, and Amos, a big chimp mechanic who said things like "I am that guy" and never once said anything else about himself.

Prax, working the data the way other monkeys work a thorn, found the pattern in a night: every missing kit on Ganymede had Mei's condition. The same missing enzyme.

"Why take a bunch of sick kids?" Holden asked.

"Because someone is testing the cure," Prax said slowly, hating himself for understanding. "Whatever ate Eros eats us too. Our bodies reject it. *Except*, perhaps, for monkeys like Mei. Someone is testing which children the seed won't refuse. Someone is growing soldiers."

They were. On Io, in the labs of Jules-Pierre Macaque — Julie's father, who had sold his grief to the proto-banana project and called it an investment — Project Calibanana had tanks of them: hybrids, chimp-shaped, seed-lit, quiet as furniture. The testing had a sponsor in high places, too, and that was the other war, fought far away by an ancient macaque grandmother named Avasarala, who ran the UN's dirtiest office, ate fermented figs through every meeting, and frightened admirals for recreation.

"Am I the only monkey in this warren," she inquired mildly, nibbling, "with functioning grey matter? No, don't answer. Yours was removed at commissioning."

Her real fight was closer to home. Half the UN's security council had signed off on the protomolecule program in memos that had since eaten themselves, and her opposite number, a silver-maned macaque named Errinwright, kept arranging the war the way a dealer arranges a card game — and Bobbie's confiscated footage was the ace. Avasarala watched him flinch at it across the council table and fed him one more fig, slowly, to make the flinch last. "Go home, darling," she told him afterward, sweetly. "Pack light."

Bobbie reached her by way of a diplomatic disaster: the only marine off Ganymede, suffering nightmares about a thing that wouldn't die, assigned as bodyguard to a small swearing grandmother who treated powered armor like a taxi and intelligence agencies like houseplants. Within a week Bobbie was writing her threat assessments in Avasarala's margins, and within two she stopped flinching in her sleep. "You want revenge, sweetheart?" Avasarala asked her once, not unkindly. "No. You want to be the one it *can't* walk through. Better tailoring. Same satisfaction."

The tailoring arrived in orbit over Io. Bobbie went down in her rebuilt armor with the assault team, and in the first corridor of the first lab, Project Calibanana's finished product woke up early.

It walked through gunfire the way rain walks through rain. It tore a turret off its mount. Bobbie — who had rehearsed this moment for a year in her sleep — went ape with terrible method: sealed doors, vented floors, gravity where it shouldn't be, and finally the oldest weapon in the gorilla arsenal, applied with servos. When it stopped moving, she stood on its chest long enough to be sure.

"You're getting slow," Prax observed quietly over the channel, because the armor's medic had pumped Bobbie so full of juice her hands were shaking.

"I'm getting *even*," Bobbie said.

It was Avasarala who pried Earth's plot open — a fatal chat log between a Protogen executive and an admiral, delivered to the UN with a fig-leaf of diplomacy — and Prax who walked the dormitory row at the end, opening doors, saying plant names under his breath to keep from running.

Mei was in the last cell, playing a counting game with the other stolen kits. Halfway to being carried, she asked whether the bean plants were all right.

"Yes," said Prax, whose whole world's mirrors lay shattered and whose colleague — the man who had sold the children — lay cooling outside the door with a botanist's steady shot through him. "The plants are all right. Everything's all right."

Calibanana's war went on; the monsters had been grown already, and the sample that ate Eros was loose on Venus, doing what it liked. But on Ganymede they raised the mirrors again, one pane at a time, and the green came back, and that was the battle Prax counted. Mei, back in the rows, taught the younger kits the names of everything growing.

"Photosynthesis," she pronounced it, carefully, like a spell.

"Photosynthesis," Prax agreed, and the moon went on turning green.
