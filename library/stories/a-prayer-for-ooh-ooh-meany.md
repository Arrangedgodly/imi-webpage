# A Prayer for Ooh-Ooh Meany

*A parody of "A Prayer for Owen Meany"*

I am doomed to remember a monkey with a wrecked ooh-ooh — not because of the ooh-ooh itself, and not because he was the smallest primate I ever knew, or even because he was the instrument of my mother's death, but because he is the reason I believe in the Great Troop Above. I am a believer because of Ooh-Ooh Meany. I make no apologies for that.

He was a vervet no bigger than a coconut, and when he spoke, his voice came out as a strangled, squeaking OOH-OOH — as if fate had grabbed his words by the tail and wrung them out like wet fig leaves.

"DO NOT BE EMBARRASSED BY MY VOICE, JOHNNY," he told me the day we met. "GOD TOOK MY WORDS AND GAVE ME THIS OOH-OOH. HE MUST HAVE WANTED ME TO BE HEARD OVER THE WHOLE JUNGLE."

I should explain about my mother. It happened at a Little Jungle League game. Ooh-Ooh was our smallest swinger, and the coach had told him to stand very still and hope for a walk. Instead, he closed his eyes, swung at a hanging curvefruit, and sent a foul 'nana sailing over the backstop. My mother, Tabitha, had bent down at precisely the wrong moment to retrieve my dropped glove. The 'nana struck her temple. She fell without a sound.

"I KNEW IT WOULD HAPPEN," Ooh-Ooh squeaked at the funeral, while his granite-hearted family — the Meanys, who owned the quarry — looked on. "I KNEW IT THE MOMENT I SWUNG. I AM SORRY, JOHNNY. GOD'S HAND GUIDED THAT 'NANA."

After the funeral, we traded our most sacred possessions, as young monkeys do when there are no words. I gave him my dead grandfather's grooming claw, worn smooth by three generations of picking. He gave me his stuffed sloth — the famous stuffed sloth, his only toy — and a week later he demanded it back, only to return it again with the claws removed. I understood him perfectly: he had taken something back, and given me the hurt part to keep, so that neither of us would ever be able to pretend the trade hadn't happened. We never spoke of it again. It was the only forgiveness either of us had to give.

Ooh-Ooh never knew who his father was, and it was the one subject that never embarrassed him. "IT IS NOT MY BUSINESS WHO MY FATHER WAS," he told our whole class the day the troop gossiped. "IT IS MY BUSINESS WHO I AM." Then he apologized, and the teacher cried, and nothing was the same.

Every Chimpmas, our troop staged the Nativity in the hollow fig, and Ooh-Ooh was always cast as the Little Lord Chimp, because he was the only monkey small enough to fit the manger. One disastrous year, the director demanded he smile more, the wise men wept, the wings of the Ghost of Chimpmas Future caught in the ceiling fan, and Ooh-Ooh's OOH-OOH rang out through the fig: "I! AM! THE LITTLE LORD CHIMP!" The whole congregation went ape. It was, everyone agreed, the most moving service we ever had — though our minister, Rev. Merrow, said his doubts were "a crisis of the heart" and took to his branch for a week. "FAITH IS NOT A FEELING, SIR," Ooh-Ooh told him. "IT IS A SMALL MONKEY IN A BIG TREE, HOLDING ON." Merrow got up. He was never sure, afterward, which of them had been the minister.

The old idol of Watahantowet stood at the crossroads of the jungle — an armless sloth, sacred to the first troop, and Ooh-Ooh could never explain why he loved it. "HE HAS NO ARMS TO GRAB WITH," he said. "HE GAVE THEM UP. THAT IS WHY HE IS AT PEACE." My mother's dressmaker's dummy stood on our landing, armless and headless too, and for a whole year after the 'nana I could not look at either of them. Ooh-Ooh looked at them both, on purpose, every day. I know now that he was teaching himself what a monkey looks like when he cannot hold anything.

But what I remember most is his dream. He woke me one night, gripping the branch above my sleeping mat with both tiny hands.

"JOHNNY. I HAVE SEEN THE DATE ON MY GRAVE-BANANA. I KNOW THE DAY I WILL DIE, AND I KNOW HOW. DO NOT ASK ME ANYTHING. JUST REMEMBER, WHEN IT HAPPENS — IT WAS ALL PART OF THE PLAN."

I lost half a finger that autumn — a paperwork accident at the enlistment grove, of all the stupid ways — and I raged that if God had a plan, He was skipping pages. Ooh-Ooh examined the bandage for a long time. "THE TROOP ABOVE IS PATIENT, JOHNNY," he said. "HE DOES NOT SKIP PAGES. HE WAITS FOR US TO STOP SKIMMING."

When the Great Jungle War came, they made Ooh-Ooh a Casualty Assistance Officer, because he was too small to fight and too stubborn to send home. He was posted to a refugee treehouse at the edge of the fighting, and I — because fate has a sense of humor — was posted with him. The night before we shipped out, he gave me back the grooming claw. "IF I DO NOT NEED IT WHERE I AM GOING," he said, "KEEP IT FOR WHERE YOU ARE GOING."

The poachers came at dawn. I heard the shouts, the screams, and then, through the window of the nursery hollow, a black thunder-fruit came sailing in and rolled to a stop among the orphan babies.

I froze. Ooh-Ooh did not.

He was already moving — faster than I had ever seen him move — snatching up the babies and passing them out the window one by one, flinging them into the soft ferns below where the nurses waited, all the while crying "GO! GO! OOH-OOH! GO!" in that ridiculous, holy voice of his. And when the last baby was safe, he seized the thunder-fruit in his little arms, hurled it skyward with everything a coconut-sized vervet had, and the sky went white, and my friend was gone.

It happened exactly as he had dreamed it. To the minute. That is the part I cannot explain to the doubting bonobos of this world, and I have stopped trying. The babies he saved were not even of our troop — that was the plan, Johnny. He had always known he would die for somebody else's children, and he went to that treehouse the way other monkeys go home.

I moved north, to the cold pine forests where the troops keep to themselves, and I set his stone banana on my windowsill, where it catches the light of every Chimpmas. I have my mother's grooming record and his grooming claw, and a stuffed sloth with no claws to grab with, and my nine fingers, and my belief. Every Sunday I go to the little stone chapel and pray, and every Sunday I thank the Great Troop Above for a monkey with a wrecked ooh-ooh — the loudest whisper the jungle ever produced, my prime mate, my little lord.

Because I believe — I have to believe — that no 'nana flies by accident.
