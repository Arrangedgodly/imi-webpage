# The Great Gibbonsby

*A parody of "The Great Gatsby"*

In my younger and more vulnerable years, my father gave me some advice I've been turning over in my mind ever since: "Whenever you feel like criticizing any monkey," he told me, "just remember that all the apes in this jungle haven't had the advantages you've had."

So I reserve all judgment, even now, as I look back on that wild summer across the bay from Jay Gibbonsby's estate. My name is Nick Chimpway, and I like to think I'm one of the few honest monkeys I've ever known.

I had rented a hollow tree in the West Canopy district, sandwiched between two enormous estates. Mine sagged. Next door stood a mansion of woven bamboo and hanging vines, with a fruit grove and a swimming hole lit blue at night. It belonged to a Mr. Gibbonsby, a gibbon of unknown origin who threw parties every Saturday — parties so loud the howler monkeys three valleys away gave up complaining.

Nobody was invited. Everybody came. Orangutans in white fur, spider monkeys swinging from chandeliers, capuchins spilling sugarplum cocktails, and me, watching a jungle's worth of strangers speculate about the host. "He killed a leopard once," said one. "He's cousin to the Kaiser Gorilla," said another. "Nonsense — he's a bootlegger. Bananas during Prohibition, you know. The dry season."

That first night I met an owl monkey in Gibbonsby's library, enormous spectacles wobbling as he stared at the shelves.

"The books are real," he said solemnly. "Absolutely real — pages and everything. I thought they'd be cleverly carved bananas. This fellow's a regular Belasco. It's a triumph. It's a thoroughbred hoax that isn't a hoax."

I also met Jordan Baker, a sleek capuchin golfer with a jaunty tail and a way of yawning at whatever she'd just said, whom I'd later fall half in love with out of sheer proximity. It was Jordan who told me the truth about the parties: "Gibbonsby doesn't want guests, Nick. He wants an audience of one. He bought that whole estate so he could stand in his garden and look across the water at a certain green banana."

I met him without knowing it — a young male in a pink suit, gripping a branch with one long arm and my hand with the other, smiling like the whole jungle was a joke he'd just gotten.

"You must forgive me, old ape," he said. "I'm Jay Gibbonsby. I'm afraid I'm not a very good host. Terrible at grooming, worse at small talk."

His smile was one of those rare smiles that you come across maybe four or five times in a jungle. It faced — or seemed to face — the whole canopy, and then concentrated on you, like a searchlight trained by a capuchin with excellent aim.

Over a glass of cracked fruit he gave me his autobiography: son of wealthy apes in the Middle West, all dead now; educated at Oxford; a war hero with medals from half the jungles of Europe. It was about as true as a banana is square. (He was James Gatz of North Dakota, a poor young swinger who'd invented Jay Gibbonsby out of pure ambition and re-invented his backstory nightly. He'd once crewed for a rich old gorilla, Dan Cody, learning to ape the manners of the very rich so well that they never asked where he'd aped them from.)

It was Gibbonsby who leaned over later and said the thing that explained everything: "Her house is just across the bay."

Her. Daisy Buchanan. My cousin, a golden lion tamarin with a voice full of money, married to Tom — a massive, cruel-eyed silverback who'd once played jungle-ball at Yale and now read books titled *The Rise of the Pale-Colored Apes* with growing fascination. It was Tom who dragged me to the valley of ashes — the gray patch of burnt brush between the jungle and the city — where the faded eyes of Dr. T. J. Bonoboburg glared down from an old optometrist's billboard, and where Tom kept his mistress, Myrtle Wilson, a weary gorilla married to a lifeless chimp mechanic.

Gibbonsby had engineered it all, of course. The parties, the grove, the mansion — scaffolding for one ambition: to repeat the past, to wind back five years to Louisville, where he, a poor young gibbon, had loved Daisy and she had promised to wait for him.

"Can't repeat the past?" he cried, when I dared suggest it. "Why of course you can! I'm going to fix everything just the way it was before!"

So came the rainy afternoon in my tree, where he arranged it — flowers jammed in every corner, a clock on the mantel — and Daisy arrived, and Gibbonsby, trembling like a leaf in a gale, nearly fainted into the fruit bowl, then knocked the clock off the mantel and caught it. "I'm sorry about the clock," he said. Five years of longing, reignited over my mown fruit and their nervous grooming.

After that they couldn't be stopped. Gibbonsby dismissed his servants (too many tongues), stopped giving parties (no longer needed), and showed Daisy his shirt collection, tossing imported silken pelts of every color while she sobbed into them: "They're such beautiful shirts. It makes me sad, because I've never seen such — such beautiful shirts before."

Then came the great hot afternoon in the city — the Biltmore Canopy Hotel suite, hired to cool tempers, which heated instead. Gin and heat and history in one small room. Tom needled; Gibbonsby pushed; and Daisy said the unforgivable thing, the true thing:

"You always look so cool," Tom told Gibbonsby, "like the advertisement of the bunch. No ape swings alone with Daisy. Not since I made her Mrs. Buchanan."

"I don't think you ever loved me," said Gibbonsby — only Daisy had loved you, always, always, wanted you, dreamed of you — "Tell him the truth."

"Oh, you want too much!" she cried, her tamarin fur trembling. "I did love you once — but I loved Tom too. You want me to say I never loved my mate, and I can't say that. I can't! And besides — I did love him once, and I — I love your bananas, Jay, but I love Tom's branch too — I can't say I never — oh, let's all go home before somebody gets hurt."

Somebody got hurt. Driving home in the yellow banana-car, Daisy at the wheel, they came upon Myrtle Wilson running into the road — running from her husband, running toward a car she thought carried her escape — and the car struck her and sped off like a snapped vine. Tom, driving up behind, howled over the body. And in the dawn that followed, I found Gibbonsby standing watch outside Daisy's nest, his arms wrapped around himself against the cold, hiding a secret like a splinter in a paw.

"Was Daisy driving?" I asked. "Yes," he said after a long pause, "but of course I'll say I was. Daisy ought to have something bright in her life. She never loved Tom. Not really."

"She ought to climb to you," I said. "Gibbonsby, the whole jungle's a shortcut away from honest — but you're worth the whole rotten bunch of them put together."

Wilson, hollowed by grief, vanished from the valley of ashes beneath the eyes of Dr. Bonoboburg. "God sees everything," he told the billboard, which said nothing back. He traced the yellow car, and found it — the wrong monkey in it.

The next morning, while Gibbonsby floated on his air mattress in the blue pool, waiting for a call from Daisy that would never come, Wilson came with a club under the bananas-skin sky. "You killed Myrtle," he said — and Gibbonsby, lounging in his rubber ring, never quite understood what hit him before he slid beneath the water, carrying the phone's promise to his grave.

Nobody came to his funeral but me, his father (who brought out a copy of young Jimmy Gatz's old self-improvement schedule — "study electricry, practice elocution" — and wept over it), and the owl monkey with the enormous glasses.

"Poor sap," the owl monkey said, wet-eyed. "He used to want to swing somewhere, alone, forever. The poor son-of-a-banana."

Daisy and Tom had moved away without a word — retreatants to their money, letting other people clean up the messes they'd made.

Last I saw of that coast, I leaned against the rotten old fig tree and looked out at the bay. Way over there, past the mist, a green banana still hung on its dock-side branch — the green light at the end of Daisy's dock. Gibbonsby had believed in that banana. It had been his North Star, his future, year by year receding before him. It eluded us then, but that's no matter — tomorrow we'll climb faster, stretch our arms further, grab a riper one.

So we swing on, apes against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.
