# The Miserable Mandrill

*A parody of "The Miserable Mill"*

If you have never been forced to work in a tree-felling mill in the middle of your own jungle, chopping down the very trees your cousins live in, then let me tell you: the word "miserable," as used here, means "covered in sawdust, paid in coupons, and homesick for a canopy that no longer exists."

The three Baudelaire mandrills — Violet, who was fourteen and could invent anything out of vines and stubbornness; Klaus, who was twelve and had read everything; and Sunny, a baby whose four teeth could bite through anything — arrived at the Lucky Smells Lumbercanopy on a gray morning, after their parents' terrible fire, with nothing but one suitcase and each other.

The mill's owner was called Sir, because — he said — nobody could pronounce his real name, and because it was shorter than "the smoky boss." Nobody had ever seen his face. It lived behind a permanent cloud of rolled-banana-leaf smoke.

"You'll work in the mill," said Sir's smoke. "You'll each get paid in coupons. Don't spend it all at once."

"What can coupons buy?" asked Violet.

"Nothing," said the smoke pleasantly. "But don't spend it all at once."

The Baudelaires slept in the workers' dormitory, a long tin-roofed shed with one window, where tired capuchins and howlers lay in rows snoring sawdust into the air. Before dawn a whistle shrieked, and Foreman Flacutono walked down the aisle ringing a bell made of an old turtle shell. "Breakfast!" he rasped.

Breakfast was gum. Lunch was also gum. Dinner was gum, but warm. The only cheerful worker was a capuchin called Phil, who found the bright side of everything, including the gum. "Chewing is exercise!" said Phil. "My jaw will be the strongest part of me! It's practically a hobby!"

Friday was payday, and the workers lined up to receive their coupons, and then something happened that Klaus found more frightening than anything yet: the workers cheered. "HOORAY FOR SIR!" they cried, waving their worthless coupons in the sawdusty air. "THREE CHEERS FOR THE LUMBERCANOPY!"

"Why are they cheering?" Klaus whispered.

"Because it's easier than the alternative," said Phil, gently, "which is thinking about it."

On the third day, Foreman Flacutono stuck out his foot as Klaus walked past, and Klaus — glasses first — went flying. The glasses shattered, and Klaus was sent to the mill's optometrist, a shadowy orangutan called Dr. Orangwell, whose office sign read SEE CLEARLY / OBEY CHEERFULLY, which should have been the first clue. Inside the office, Sunny pointed one sharp little finger at the eye chart and made a thoughtful noise, and when Violet picked her up to see, she understood the second clue: the eye chart had no letters on it at all. It was simply the word OBEY, printed in fourteen fonts, top to bottom.

Klaus came back with new glasses and no expression at all. He did not read. He did not fidget. When Flacutono murmured the word "banana," Klaus's eyes went glassy and he marched to the stamping machine and worked it wildly until — CRUNCH — poor Phil's other tail-day arrived early, and Phil, bless him, said, "Well! Now they match!"

"Say 'banana,'" Violet tested that night, "and he obeys," she realized. "Hypnosis."

"You must be mistaken," said a silky new voice. It belonged to the receptionist from Dr. Orangwell's office: a tall thin mandrill with one enormous eyebrow, a paper clip on her nametag where a ruby should be, and a nametag reading SHIRLEY. "Shirley T. Posenut. Receptionist. Totally normal and unremarkable."

"That's COUNT OLAF!" cried Klaus — and then Shirley murmured "banana," and Klaus went glassy and told her she was absolutely right about everything.

"She's Count Olaf," Violet told Sir, "disguised to steal our fortune."

"Don't be ridiculous," said the smoke. "She's a receptionist. There's a difference, and it is called a nametag."

The trap was set for the morning. Violet pieced it together from Flacutono's smirks and Shirley's purring: Dr. Orangwell would demonstrate "safety" at the great debarking saw, Klaus would be hypnotized to operate it wildly, there would be an accident, Sir would hand the children over to sweet Shirley to be carried away "somewhere safer," and the fortune would follow. Everyone in the jungle could see it, which is exactly why nobody in the jungle did.

That night, while the dormitory snored, Violet did what Klaus would have done, because Klaus could not. She slipped into Dr. Orangwell's waiting room, avoided the eye chart (which she felt was watching her), and found, on the table beneath a stack of old jungle magazines, a pamphlet: SO YOU'VE HYPNOTIZED A MONKEY: A DOCTOR'S GUIDE. It explained that every command word requires an antidote word, chosen to be as dull and difficult as possible so that no one says it by accident, and that the doctor writes each pair in her ledger, because even hypnotists forget.

The ledger, of course, was locked in the desk, and the desk, of course, was locked in the office, and Violet, of course, had until dawn. She picked the office lock with a hair ribbon and the desk lock with a firm belief in herself, and there, on the page marked CURRENT PATIENT — K. BAUDELAIRE, in a terrible physician's scrawl, she found:

*Trigger: BANANA. Antidote: PLANTAIN.*

Behind her, the waiting room floor creaked, and the eye chart rustled in a draft like something breathing. Violet left the way she came, which is the only polite way to leave a burgled office.

At dawn, the whole mill was summoned to the debarking saw. Sir's smoke arrived on a palanquin. Dr. Orangwell arrived in a coat of orangutan orange. Shirley arrived with her eyebrow freshly combed. And Klaus was brought out, blinking, glassy, a log already rolling toward the great blade.

"Safety," announced Dr. Orangwell, oozing, "begins with ATTENTION. Banana."

Klaus's eyes went double-glazed, and his hands went out for the levers.

"BA-NA-NA," the doctor repeated, drawing out each syllable like taffy. "Feel the saw. Love the saw. Approach the saw..."

Violet wound the bark-horn she had built in the dark of the lumber shed, woven from bark fiber, which muffled suggestions and magnified sisters, and she bellowed the one word from the ledger:

"PLANTAIN!"

The word went through the mill like rain through a roof. Klaus blinked. The glass fell out of his eyes and shattered like his old glasses. He stepped away from the saw with the vague expression of a monkey who has just remembered where he left something important.

"Why, you meddling —" began Dr. Orangwell, lunging for the controls — and this was when Sunny, who had been stationed at ankle height with intent, bit down on the doctor's ankle with her four sharp teeth, and the doctor's lunge became a stagger, and the stagger became a stagger in an unlucky direction, and by momentum and misfortune she was carried into the great pile of fresh sawdust, where she disappeared entirely, leaving only her shingle, which landed at Sir's feet, reading, faintly, OBEY CHEERFULLY.

"Curses," said Shirley, unconvincingly, and fled into the smoke wearing one shoe on one foot and no shoes on the other — an escape that Klaus, once his glasses were re-taped, was able to describe to the mill in flawless detail, because he had, after all, read everything about disguises, and nothing in it had prepared him for a mandrill running in one shoe.

Sir's smoke seemed to shrug. "No harm done, then. Back to work. Chop chop."

"Sir," said Violet quietly, "a mandrill fell into your sawdust."

"And the pile will be sifted," said the smoke, "on Monday."

"Sir, we are mandrills. This mill cuts down jungle."

"And I sell it," said the smoke. "It's a living."

Which, as the coupons would tell you, was not the same thing as a life. The Baudelaires were packed off onward the next morning, to yet another guardian, yet another disaster — miserable, all of it, but at least, that week, nothing was on fire.

Yet.
