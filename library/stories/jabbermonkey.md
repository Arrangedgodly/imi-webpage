# Jabbermonkey

*A parody of "Jabberwocky"*

'Twas getting dark in the jungle of Nonsense, and the old silverback Ooki had gathered every chimpling of the troop around the fire at the foot of the Tumtum fig. Fireflies blinked. Somewhere above, a sloth snored in the boughs, slow as syrup.

"Tonight," said Ooki, "I tell the oldest story we have. The story of the Jabbermonkey."

The little ones went quiet. Even the crickets leaned in.

"Long ago," Ooki began, "there was a beast in these trees with the jaws of a crocodile, the grip of ten macaques, and a tail that could snatch a banana out of your paws before you'd even peeled it. It took the Great Golden Banana — the first banana, the mother of all 'nanas — and hung it in the tulgey wood, where no monkey dared go. And the jungle grew grey and grumpy, for a jungle without bananas is hardly a jungle at all."

"Nobody fought it?" whispered the smallest chimpling, whose name was Pip.

"One did," said Ooki. "The littlest monkey of his own troop — the one nobody picked for games. Listen."

And he recited the verse that every monkey knows, the verse the fireflies spelled out in letters of light:

> 'Twas brunchtime, and the slithy sloths
>   Did spiral and snooze in the boughs:
> All flimsy were the fig-leaf cloths,
>   And the mome bats hung in rows.
>
> "Beware the Jabbermonkey, my son!
>   The jaws that bite, the tail that snatches!
> Beware the Nutcracker bird, and shun
>   The frumious Bandersnatch-monkey!"

"What's *brunchtime*?" asked a chimpling in the second row. "I don't know what brunchtime is."

"Brunchtime," said Ooki, "is four o'clock in the afternoon — the hour when sloths begin to think about breakfast and monkeys have only just finished lunch. A dangerous hour. Everyone's guard is down and everyone's belly is up."

"What's *slithy*?" asked Pip.

"Lithe and slimy," said Ooki, "or, as the sloths prefer, sleepy and spirally. A sloth can be thoroughly slithy before breakfast without moving an inch. It's all in the tail."

"And *mome*?"

"Short for *from home*. A mome bat is one that stayed out too late, gave up, and hung itself in rows with the others to think about what it had done. You can see them any night, sulking in formation."

Then Ooki told them about the warnings, because there were warnings long before there was ever a battle.

"The Nutcracker bird," he said, "was a bird the size of a small cloud that could crack the hardest coconut with a sigh. If it cracked its *knuckles* at you, you were already too late. And the Bandersnatch-monkey — *frumious*, which is to say furious AND fluffy — took orders from the Jabbermonkey and grudges from everybody else. It never forgot a face. It never returned one, either."

"Did the hero meet them?" squeaked Pip, hugging his own tail.

"He was far too clever to. That was the whole secret, my dears: he knew he could never out-muscle anything in that wood, so he refused to try. He searched for a year and a day — behind the waterfalls, where the mist hangs thick as mango fluff; under the roots of the singing kapok; through the grove where the fig wasps keep their sour little archive. He questioned every wasp, and the wasps told him the truth, because truth was the one thing they had never run out of. And all he carried was a vorpal vine."

"What's *vorpal*?" asked the chimpling in the second row.

"Springier than springy. Twice as whippy as the very whippiest liana. A vorpal vine *hums* when it swings. Here — listen."

And Ooki reached back and rapped his knuckles on a coil of very old vine behind his sleeping-branch, and every chimpling heard it: a low sweet hum, like a pot of honey remembering a song. Nobody asked how long that vine had been coiled there, and Ooki did not offer.

"So," said Ooki:

> He took his vorpal vine in hand:
>   Long time the manxome foe he sought—
> So rested he in the Tumtum tree,
>   And stood awhile in thought.

"*Manxome*," said Pip, before he could stop himself.

"Brave, and slightly damp," said Ooki. "The condition of every hero at dawn."

"And while he stood there thinking uffish thoughts," said Ooki, "guess what came whiffling through the tulgey wood?"

"The Jabbermonkey!" squeaked every chimpling at once.

> And, as in uffish thought he stood,
>   The Jabbermonkey, with eyes of flame,
> Came whiffling through the tulgey wood,
>   And burbled as it came!

"It burbled," said Ooki solemnly, "like a whole barrel of monkeys with hiccups. Its eyes were two balls of fire. Its tail rose up like a question mark with terrible manners. And the little hero — the very smallest monkey in his troop, the one nobody picked for games — planted his feet on the branch, wrapped the end of his own tail twice around the trunk for luck, and swung his vorpal vine.

> One, two! One, two! And vine and tail
>   Went snicker-snack! The beast was beat!
> He left it dazed, and with its pride,
>   He went galumphing back.

"Snicker-snack," Ooki explained, "is not the sound of hitting. It is the sound a vine makes when it means business. You will know it when you hear it, and I hope you never do."

"Galumphing?" asked Pip.

"Galumphing," said Ooki, "is part gallop and part triumph. There is no other word, and no other way to travel when you are carrying the Great Golden Banana home above your head. And when he came back to the troop, the 'nanas came back to the trees, and the edges of the jungle turned gold again, and what do you think his father said?"

> "And hast thou out-swung the Jabbermonkey?
>   Come to my arms, my beamish boy!
> O frabjous day! Kaloo! Kalay!"
>   He chortled in his joy.

"Beamish," said Ooki, "means noble, glowing, and about to be hugged extremely hard. And *frabjous* — frabjous is fruity, fabulous, and joyous, all at once. It is the best word there is. I have looked."

"And from that day on," said Ooki, "the littlest monkey of the troop was called the *chimp-ion of the jungle*. Which just goes to show, my dears: the jungle does not care how big your arms are. It cares how well you swing them."

"Who was he?" asked Pip. "The littlest one?"

Ooki smiled the way a ripe fig splits, and said only, "Who do you suppose taught the fireflies their alphabet?" — and that was all anybody ever got out of him.

Pip went to sleep that night in the crook of the Tumtum fig, and he dreamed of vorpal vines and whiffling woods. And years later, when a thieving crew of fruit-snatchers came through Nonsense — a regular guerrilla of gorilla-hearted rogues — it was Pip, full-grown and springy-armed, who met them at the border trees, vine in hand, eyes aflame, and burbled right back at them.

They say you can still hear him on quiet nights, snicker-snacking through the canopy: going ape in the very best way, chortling in his joy.

'Twas brunchtime, and the slithy sloths did spiral and snooze in the boughs — and every banana in the jungle hung exactly where it belonged.
