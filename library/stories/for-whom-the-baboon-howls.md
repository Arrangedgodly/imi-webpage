# For Whom the Baboon Howls

*A parody of "For Whom the Bell Tolls" by Ernest Hemingway*

*No ape is an island; every ape is a piece of the troop. Any ape's fall diminishes me, because I am involved in apekind; and therefore never send to know for whom the baboon howls; he howls for thee.*

He lay flat behind the deadfall of ferns and watched the vine bridge in the morning light. Robert Jordan — a young orangutan of the International Gorilla Brigades, and therefore a guerrilla in the strictest sense — was sapper enough to know a bad job when he saw one.

"See how it is," said Anselmo, the old gray mangabey beside him. "A whole troop could cross it at a gallop."

"And a whole troop will," said Robert Jordan. "Unless it is down. Golz says it must be down when the offensive starts."

In the cave that night, Pilar, the fat grandmother of the partisans, stirred the pot and watched him with eyes like wet stones. Her mate Pablo, once the champion of this troop, sat sullen over a skin of fermented sap.

"I am for the bridge no more," Pablo said. "Here we swing, here we raid the convoys, here we live. Thou, shameful one!" — and this to Pilar, who had snorted. "Wilt thou blow the bridge with dynamite, woman, and bring the Shavers down on us all?"

"I remember when thou wert a baboon of valor," said Pilar. "For a monkey who was once champion, thou hast gone soft as an overripe pawpaw."

In the shadows sat Maria, a young monkey whose fur the Shavers had shaved to the skin in the bad time at the village. It was growing back now, short and gold. And by the fire sat Agustín, a heavy-browed gorilla who swore magnificently, continuously, and always inventively — the finest swearer in the mountains: "mouldy fig," "son of a sour 'nan," "caterpillar of the low canopy" — because, he explained, the jungle had banned all the good words, and a gorilla must have standards.

Pilar took Robert Jordan's paw, turned it over in the firelight, read the lines of it for a long moment — and then gave it back as if she had dropped something.

"There is no need to read further," she said. "It is all bridge."

That night they gave the newcomers the sleeping furs, and after, in the bracken, Maria asked, "Did the ground move for thee, as Pilar says it should?"

"The tree moved," said Robert Jordan, holding her. "And all the ridge, and the whole canopy with it."

Pilar had told him, on the walk, of the day the village rose: how the villagers, who could not bring themselves to use claws, had shaved the Shavers bald in the plaza, every one, and rolled them down the great hill in a barrel — bump, bump, bump, a whole barrel of monkeys — until it reached the river. It had not been funny, Pilar said. But it had been a little funny. "We were shaven ourselves, that day," she said, "all of us, in the soul, and the fur has never grown right there since."

Maria, too, had her story: her mother and father shot against the wall of the village, and her own head shaved for the amusement of the Shavers. "I was ashamed of my fur," she said. "Now it grows for thee."

"For us," said Robert Jordan, who had stopped believing in *me* somewhere on the walk up the mountain.

Then the planes came, flying low over the snow in the pines, and it was as if the whole sky had turned traitor; and in the night the snow itself came, in May, falling soft and wrong, and Robert Jordan lay awake doing sums of destruction. If the snow held, the offensive might not come. If the sentries were not silenced, the bridge could not be blown. If El Sordo's band could not bring horses — and El Sordo's band, on their hilltop in the snow, fought their last fight that morning, laughing, outnumbered, bombed out of the white world to a one — then nothing, perhaps, but the sums went on.

"You do not like to kill?" Robert Jordan asked Anselmo, as they watched the sentry on the far pier, the morning of it.

"No, Inglés. I have killed, and I will kill, but it is not to my taste. But if thou must, thou must."

The sentry passed; the far marmoset looked down the gorge; Robert Jordan pressed the handle.

The bridge rose into the air, it seemed to hang, ripped and shining, like a vine pulled from the earth by the roots — and then it fell, and the gorge filled with rolling thunder. A piece of the falling bridge struck the old mangabey, who had asked only to do no killing, and Anselmo never heard the war end — though it was, for him, ended there, which had been his whole intention.

Pablo had run off in the night with the spare fuse — and come back at dawn, sheepish, with five strange monkeys and more dynamite.

"I have come back," Pablo said. "A monkey may be a coward one night. One could not leave it so."

They were halfway up the slope when the branch came down and pinned him, and when he felt the leg he knew it was broken through.

"Thou wilt go on," he said to Maria, who was crying and holding his face. "If thou goest, then thou goest, and I stay a little while. Do not cry. Each of us does what is best, and the troop goes on. Thou art the troop now. Thou and Madrid."

"Madrid," she sobbed.

"Madrid," he said, "where thou wilt eat prawns and wear shoes, and laugh at monkeys like me who thought a bridge was worth a leg." — and Pilar took her by the shoulders and led her away, and Maria looked back the whole way up the slope, which was the bravest thing anyone did that day, because it is harder to look than to stay.

They got her away at last, and Robert Jordan dragged himself behind the deadfall, close against the warm shattered fern, his club set where the Shaver officer would have to come.

"Now," he said to his heart, which was beating very fast against the fern leaves. "I'm tired, little heart. But beat a little longer. You're beating like a little drum. Beat a little longer for me, hmm? You have never failed an ape yet."

He was not lonely. The whole troop was with him — the living, and the dead, and those not yet born — and he could feel the canopy above him full of them. He thought: I have fought for what I believed in for a year now. If we win here we win everywhere, and the canopy is a fine place, and worth the climbing for. And it is a fine thing to howl for one's troop. You are one lucky ape, he told himself, to have had so good a troop.

And he waited, warm against the fern, with his heart beating, and the howl building in his chest.
