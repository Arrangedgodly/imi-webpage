# Atlas Scratched

*A parody of "Atlas Shrugged"*

"Who is Chimp Galt?"

Dagny Taggert heard it again that morning, from the porter-howl swinging down the Vine of the North beside her, and she did what she always did: she did not answer, because there was no answer. It was what monkeys said now, when crops failed and vines snapped and the banana supply went ape — a shrug made of words.

Dagny was Operating Monkey of Taggart Trans-Canopy Vine-lines, a lean gray spider monkey who ran the biggest network of lianas in the jungle because her brother Jim could not. Jim believed in sharing the canopy. Dagny believed in inspecting it.

"The Vine of the North is cracking," she told him. "The old lianas are rotten. I'm ordering rail of Rearden Vine."

"You can't!" Jim's fur stood up in committees. "Rearden Vine has never been tested! The National Alliance of Lianas says we must use only approved jungle products, for the good of all!"

"Then buy your approved jungle products," said Dagny, "and hold the funerals somewhere I don't have to attend."

Jim had better luck with the San Banana Groves, a legendary orchard in the southern mist that Banancisco d'Anconia had inherited and everyone said was the richest fruit ground in the world. Jim poured the family trust into it. Banancisco — Dagny's oldest friend, a golden tamarin who had once out-climbed her barefoot at eleven and had spent his fortune since becoming the jungle's most celebrated wastrel — signed every paper with a flourish and a wink. The assay came back in the spring: husks. The San Banana Groves held nothing but husks, and the orchard lights Jim had seen from the air were lanterns hung on dead trees by paid watchers.

"Banancisco!" Dagny cornered him at a reception. "You sold my brother husks!"

"I sold him what he was buying," Banancisco said pleasantly. "He wanted wealth without the making of it. So I made him the finest husks money can pretend to buy. Dagny, be precise when they come to loot you: say that you believe the banana is the root of all evil? Then tell me — what is the root of all bananas? Whoever answers that question honestly will never be robbed of the answer."

Hank Rearden — a mountain gorilla with a forge in the hills — poured the first casting of his new alloy himself: a vine stronger than steel-roped liana, lighter than bamboo. That night he strung a length across a gorge, hung his own considerable weight from the middle, and swung. It held.

"I have given the jungle a new way to cross," he said. "What it gives me back is its business."

He gave the first casting, hammered into a bracelet, to his wife Lillian, who wore it to parties the way one wears a debt. "A bracelet is a chain," she said sweetly, "and a chain is a chain because it is meant to bind." At the Rearden anniversary, Lillian held it up to the room and asked what anyone would imagine such a crude thing to be worth, and Dagny unclasped the emerald chain off her own wrist — the Taggert emeralds, three generations of them — and traded it across the table without a word. She wore Rearden Vine to the wrist for the rest of her life.

What it gave him back, eventually, was the Ministry of Plenty-of-Bananas, Directive 10-fun-289: no new vines, no new groves, no new grips — a freeze on greatness in the name of fairness. Jim signed it happily. Then the jungle's best began to vanish — Ellis Wyatt, the fig-oil magnate, torched his own groves so completely that they burned for three days on wet ground, and left a sign: I LEAVE IT AS I FOUND IT. LOOK AT IT WELL; IT WILL NOT BE THERE WHEN YOU RETURN. A composer burned his scores; a banker, then a surgeon, then the last honest engineer — and everywhere they went they left the same question hanging in the leaves.

"Who is Chimp Galt?" asked the jungle.

On a fog-bound branch one night, Dagny met a red-furred orangutan in a pirate's cloak — Rangutang Danneskjöld, who hijacked the Ministry's relief barges mid-river and returned stolen fruit to its makers, minus freight charges. Robin Hood in reverse, he said, correcting an old injustice with compound interest. Then he was gone into the mist.

Then Dagny, chasing a broken line, found a shrine-grove wreck — and in it a motor, half-buried in moss, that turned and turned, drawing power from the air itself, from static, the way a groomed pelt sparks in dry season. No fuel. No figs. A motor that ran on the friction of fur. It had been built by a young engineer who had walked out of the jungle years before, saying only that he was going to stop the engine of the world.

She followed the static in a lone plane, over the mist, over the mountains no map bothered with, until the shadow of a great valley opened beneath her — and a figure stood on the tower of light at its rim, unhurried, aiming a blade of light at her engine, taking her out of the sky as gently as a fig is set down. She crash-landed in a meadow of the vanished: the magnate pumping his own oil, the composer writing his own symphony, Banancisco smelting honest copper, Rearden on a visit he had pretended not to take — and the young engineer standing under the biggest fig with his hand out, a cigarette in the other hand stamped with a tiny gold banana, the sign of the valley's money, which was gold because it had to be.

"Chimp Galt," he said. "I am the monkey who stopped the jungle's motor. I have been on strike. We all have — every ape of the mind, every monkey who builds and is hated for building. We will teach monkeys to shrug."

Back in the jungle, the Ministry went bananas in the old, fatal sense. The directive-webs closed; the takers took the takers. Dagny stood in the dark by the tunnel where a weak directive-vine had been sent to hold a heavy load, listening to the crash, understanding the whole century at once.

Then, on a night when the jungle's lights were going out bough by bough, every fig-frond radio in the canopy crackled at once.

"You have heard it said that this is a jungle of greed," said Galt's voice, level and clear, from every leaf. "I am here to correct the record. There is a gorilla of legend who holds the whole canopy on his shoulders. Every generation, you have been told to pity him. I am telling him to reach up — and scratch. The canopy holds itself. Every branch bears its own fruit. For twelve years I have asked the monkeys who carry you to put down the sky and walk out from under it. The code of the jungle has been: groom for others, be groomed by force. My code is: I swear by my pelt and my love of it that I will never groom for the sake of another monkey, nor ask another monkey to groom for mine. The canopy is not a debt. It is a gift — to every ape willing to grip."

The Ministry's last act was to seize Galt himself. They strapped the strikers' leader into the Ferris Persuader, a great wheel of bamboo and menace designed to convince him to become Economic Czar of the jungle. The wheel whirled. The dials trembled. And then the machine broke — a bearing seized, a belt shrieked — and the ministers stood around it sweating, until the ape in the machine opened his eyes and told them, with the patience of a mechanic among apprentices, exactly which part to replace and how. They replaced it. The wheel spun. Galt did not change his mind, and the ministers began, one by one, to cry.

The great gorilla of legend, somewhere above the oldest tree, reached up idly behind one ear, scratched — and shrugged.

The jungle shuddered from root to crown, and held. The strikers came back up the vines at dawn, Dagny and Rearden and Galt at the head of them, and the first new line of Rearden Vine went singing across the northern gorge. Over the wreck of the Ministry, Galt raised one finger and traced, in the smoke, the sign of the banana.

"The road is cleared," said Chimp Galt. "We are going back to the canopy."
