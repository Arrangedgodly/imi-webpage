# Horton Hears an Ooh!

*A parody of "Horton Hears a Who!"*

On the fifteenth of May, in the cool of the day, in the shade of a fig tree, a gorilla was splashing in the pool at the bottom of Splash Falls, when he heard a small noise.

It was a tiny "Ooh."

Not an "Ooh" like when you find an extra banana. A small, small "Ooh," like a very small voice calling from a very small place.

Horton the gorilla stopped splashing and looked all around.

"That's funny," thought Horton. "There's no one around and nobody spoke. But that 'Ooh' — that clearly was not a joke. Some sort of a creature of tiny-tiny size is stuck on that fig seed that floats past my eyes."

So Horton reached out and he plucked the seed from the air with a leaf, as gently as grooming a baby's first hair.

"Ooh!" said the voice again, delighted. "Ooh! OOH! Someone heard us!"

"Us?" rumbled Horton.

"Welcome, friend," said the voice, which introduced itself as the Mayor of the Oohs — a whole city of tiny speck-sized marmosets called Whoville-on-a-Seed, complete with a fig-drying yard, three drum towers, and one slightly leaning fig-silo, who lived in fear of one thing and one thing only: being dropped.

"For the love of figs," said Horton. "I meant what I said and I said what I meant. A gorilla's faithful one hundred percent."

But a certain baboon named Sour Baboonica came bouncing by with her bouncing baby boy. "Horton!" she sniffed. "Talking to a fig seed? That is the single most ridiculous thing I have ever seen a gorilla do."

"There are monkeys on that seed!" said Horton. "A whole world on that speck!"

"If you can't see it, hear it, or feel it yourself," said Sour Baboonica, "it doesn't exist. And talking to invisible monkeys is going ape in the wrong direction."

"Big or small," said Horton, "a world's a world, and this one has a mayor."

"Bah!" said Sour Baboonica. "Tell it to somebody your own size." And she bounced away, expecting the last word, which is a baboon's favorite kind.

Well, word spread, as word does, and the whole jungle decided Horton had gone completely bananas. Worse — far worse — the seed was in danger, tipping and sliding, every jolt an earthquake through Whoville-on-a-Seed. The Mayor's daily reports grew grim: the fig-silo had fallen. The drum towers were drumming entirely by accident.

"Hold on!" bellowed Horton, and he climbed with the seed to the tip-top of the tallest tree on Mount Crumpet, and set it gently in the highest blossom, where no hand could snatch it.

The Oohs cheered. "Our hero! The great GORILLA guardian!"

Now the Wickersham clan — three rowdy macaque brothers and their uncles and cousins and aunties in bunches — decided this nonsense had gone far enough. They came at dawn, a hundred strong, chanting, "Boil that seed! Boil that speck! Boil it in beeswax and burn it in —"

"Take the seed," snickered the biggest Wickersham, "and toss it into a patch of one hundred million fig seeds. Nobody will ever find it again."

"STOP!" cried Horton. "Please! There are MONKEYS on that —"

But you cannot punch a rumor, and while Horton was busy protecting his head, a black-crested eagle swooped — SCREECH — and plucked the seed out of the blossom, and flew, and dropped, and the seed fell one hundred thousand feet (which, for a seed, is a long way) into the middle of the great fig-seed fields.

Horton dove down the mountainside, going ape in the noblest possible sense. And then he stopped. Somewhere down there, under a million million seeds, was the world he had promised to protect.

He began to look.

Seed one. Seed two. Seed three. He looked by day, he looked by night, a gorilla on his knees in a field of figs, holding each seed to his ear, one by one. The Wickershams jeered. Sour Baboonica snorted. Horton's eyes went red and his voice went rough, and he said, over and over, like a drum: "I meant what I said and I said what I meant. A gorilla's faithful one hundred percent."

Three million fig seeds later — on the three-million-and-first, in the fifteenth hour of the second day, at the very last of his hope — he heard it: a tiny "Ooh."

"FOUND you," whispered Horton, and his knees went weak with joy, and a hundred tiny voices cheered in his ear.

But the Wickershams were waiting. They seized Horton by the arms and the legs and the tail (a gorilla has no business being seized by the tail, and Horton said so), and they lashed him into a cage of green vines, and the biggest Wickersham snatched the seed to drop it in the pot himself.

"Gentlemen!" panted Horton. "Take the seed, and you take a world. All I ask is that you listen. Just LISTEN. There are whole families on there — schools! A mayor! A silo, admittedly fallen!"

"HA!" bellowed the Wickershams. "We hear nothing! NOTHING! If your little monkeys are real, let them speak up. Louder! LOUDER! We can't hear you, Mr. Mayor!"

Now the Mayor of the Oohs raced through his tiny town, banging a tiny gong: "This is the moment! All monkeys, all marmosets, every Ooh, young and old, every voice, every age, MAKE YOURSELVES HEARD!"

And every Ooh in Ooh-ville shouted: "WE ARE HERE! WE ARE HERE! WE ARE HERE! WE ARE HERE!"

The Wickershams cupped their ears. Sour Baboonica frowned. Nothing. Faint as a fig seed sneezing.

"They're yelling!" wailed the Mayor, dashing from door to door. "The whole town is yelling! Are there any Oohs not yelling?!" — and it turned out there was one: the smallest of his own daughters, smaller than small, hanging back in the town square.

"There is one little Ooh," a councilman reported, "who is not shouting. She's playing with her yo-yo, Your Honor."

The Mayor scooped up his smallest daughter. "This," he announced, "is the moment she was BORN for."

She took the deepest breath a tiny Ooh had ever taken, the yo-yo went still, and she shouted her whole entire heart into the sky:

"YOPP!"

And that one small "Yopp" put it over the top. Suddenly the whole jungle heard it: WE ARE HERE! WE ARE HERE! WE ARE HERE! Not a whisper, not a sneeze — a chorus, clear as a bell through the fig fields.

The Wickershams dropped their fists. Sour Baboonica's baby boy, still on her back, clapped his little hands. "I hear them!" he squealed. "Mommy, the gorilla was right! They're THERE! And that one has a yo-yo!"

And from that day on, the seed was planted in a pot in the middle of the troop's great council tree, where Horton watered it, guarded it, and greeted it every morning — and every morning a hundred thousand tiny voices answered. The Wickershams took turns watering it themselves, quietly, when they thought nobody was looking. Sour Baboonica's baby grew up to be the jungle's Number One Keeper of Small Things.

Because a monkey's a monkey, no matter how small.
