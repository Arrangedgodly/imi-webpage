# Midnight's Chimpdren

*A parody of "Midnight's Children"*

I was born in the Troop of the Banyan at the stroke of midnight, the exact moment the old colony got its freedom — the first hour of independence, the fifteenth of August, 1947 — and because of that clockwork accident, I became mysteriously handcuffed to history, my destinies indissolubly chained to those of my country.

Or so I have claimed for six hundred pages of pickle-jar labels, which I am writing, in an abandoned pepper warehouse on the harbor, in the leaking monsoon dark, while Padma — my fat, illiterate, loving pickle-mistress — sits beside me demanding the next installment.

"Tell it straight," Padma says. "None of your nose-stories."

Padma: my lotus. I tell you the truth about Padma: she thinks a tale should go from branch to branch like a sensible monkey, and not swoop about like a fruit-bat. And I tell her: Memory's truth is the only truth I have, and memory sniffs its own strange paths — and my nose, Padma, my nose has always known the way.

Because that is the other thing about me: my nose. A nose like a trunk, a nose like a radish, a nose the size of a young banana — the family nose, which my grandfather Doctor Aadam Aziz first bent out of shape by banging it on the frozen ground of the north when he prayed, and thereafter lost his faith and kept his sniff of God in the nostrils. My nose led me into other monkeys' heads. That was my gift, hand-delivered by the midnight hour: telepathy, conducted entirely by the nose. A thousand midnight's chimpdren — every kit born in that first hour of freedom — got a gift, and mine was the sniffing of minds.

Who what am I? In answering, I am far from saying anything original: I am the sum total of everything that went before me, and everyone my nose has ever sniffed, shuffled, and stirred into the pickle.

Here is what I did not know until too late: I was not even the monkey my parents believed. In the nursing home that midnight, two she-monkeys birthed at once: my mother Amina, wife of Ahmed Sinai, the fizz-and-fruit-wine magnate whose fortune was as fizzy as his temper — and Vanita, the poor singer-wife of Wee Willie Winkie, carrying the kit of the departing planter Methwold, that tight-trousered ape who auctioned off his estate to the natives while insisting the gin be served at six. Mary Perreriah, the midwife, out of worship for revolutionaries, switched the kits in their cribs. So the rich baby went to the slum and the slum baby went to the rich — and I, Saleem, who claims a magnate's fortune, am actually the planter's fruit, pickled by a midwife's whim.

And the true heir of the Sinais? He stayed in the neighborhood, and grew up sideways into my life as Shiva: the spitting, knee-knocking boy of the tenements whose gift was the war-knees (a kick from Shiva could fell a bull), whose hunger was bottomless, and whose destiny, the widows and the war widows of the city said, was to be born kneeward and rise by force while I rose by fraud. "Chimpion," Shiva called me, mocking my English — and in the end, Excellence, in the end —

But no. First the Conference. In my tenth year, on the roof of the Bombay-branch house, I sniffed my way across the subcanopy and summoned them: all five hundred and eighty-one chimpdren of midnight, into my head, for a Midnight Children's Conference every night while the Troop slept. There was the boy in the east who could step into mirrors and stroll about inside reflections; the girl who multiplied fish-fry from thin air to feed her slum; there was a small plague of holy kits and one who flew. And there was Parvati-the-witch, born in a thunderclap, who could conjure things out of the air — a basket, a pigeon, invisibility — with a wiggle of her behind and a warble of her song. We argued, all of us, every midnight: what were these gifts for? The war-knees wanted them for war. The fish-fry girl wanted them for feeding. I — vain monkey, leader by accident of nose — wanted them for the Troop: for making the new country into what the freedom songs had promised.

(And always, always, the Widow was listening in the future. Hold that thought. Pickle it.)

Because the country had other plans. Wars came — the war of '65 rattled the breakfast trays; the war of '71 broke the country in two like a stale wafer — and my family broke with it. My father's fizz went flat, then his heart; the family silver was sunk in a well, or seized; my aunt and my mother and my gone-mad canvas-painting aunt drifted east, and I went with them, into the mangrove wars, where a bootcamp of the eastern army took my memory away. Truly: it leaked out of my ears into the swamps. For years I was nobody's son with nobody's past, a clean slate of a monkey — and there is no freedom so total as that, and none so false.

They gave me a new job in the swamps: the man-dog unit, tracking, my nose pressed to the earth, which is the destiny of noses in armies. And in the war's last hours, chasing a shadow through the reeds, I felt the past crack back into my skull like a well-thrown coconut — the whole pickle of it — and I walked west, home, sixteen hundred miles, following my own snout.

What I found: my sister Jamila, the most famous singing voice of the Troop, the Nightingale-who-was-my-sister, garlanded and lonely in her guarded compound; and the Emergency.

The Emergency — the Widow's midnight. The Prime-Minister-Queen of the Troop suspended the rights of everything, jailed the opposition, tore down the slums, and turned on the chimpdren. Because there is always someone counting the gifted. The Widow's surgeons believed too many magical kits would make the future unmanageable, so the midnight's chimpdren were hunted, one by one, and carried off for the operations: the snip.

Parvati-the-witch pulled me out of a raid by conjuring me into her basket — invisible, breathing through the wicker — and kept me hidden for the Emergency's whole black two years, in a garret in the magicians' slum, and one thing followed another, and there was a night when Shiva of the knees came around, and Parvati's kit was born, and Parvati, in the raids of the Widow's son — the one they called the Locker-of-jails — was taken.

So the child, my son, is called Aadam Sinai: Shiva's knees, Parvati's wiggle, my name. Of his gift it is too early to speak; he is only three, and sleeps in the pickle factory under a mosquito net, and when he laughs the jars chime.

And Shiva? He rose, as destined — war-knees decorated, promoted, photographed — until the knees carried him too high, into the arms of the Widow's own son, and vanished into the great machine that eats even its instruments. That is all I will say. Some pickles are too hot to label.

I came, at last, with my son, to the pickle factory of Braganza's Pickles & Chutneys, where I took the only job history offers a cracked mirror: master of the thirty jars. Into each jar, a year. Into the chutneys, the truth — chopped, salted, spiced, and sealed against the fungus of forgetting. "Tell it straight," Padma says, but Padma is wrong: the straight tale is the Widow's tale, and she cut it with scissors. The true tale is a chutney: everything thrown in, crushed together, preserved in vinegar and grief.

And my end — for it is coming, I feel it in the cracks of my own trunk — was fixed at birth by a clock. A monkey born handcuffed to history must fall with his country's every stumble, and I am cracking, Padma; I am cracking along thirty-one fault lines, one for every year, and when I go I will crumble into six hundred and thirty million specks of anonymous, luminous, maddening, equatorial dust — fleas of light, motes of midnight, one for every chimp and child of the Troop.

But — yes, Padma, yes, I am coming to bed — before that: understand this, whoever you are, reading my labels in some future monsoon: I told you the truth. Memory's truth, pickled in a factory of the heart, where Padma stirs the vat with her big spoon, and the chimpdren of midnight, snipped or scattered, keep their conference in my nose forever.

That's all, chimpion. That's all, pickle-jars. That is the whole of it.

Goodnight.
