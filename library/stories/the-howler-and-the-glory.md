# The Howler and the Glory

*A parody of "The Power and the Glory"*

Mr. Tench, an old orangutan with one working eye and a dentist's case of rusty forceps, sat in the swelter of the river station watching the vultures sleep in the dead tree across the track, when a stranger came out of the forest.

The stranger was a stooped, shabby howler monkey in a torn black cassock, with a nose reddened by more than sun. He was, though Mr. Tench could not know it, the last priest in the whole state — the others had married, or fled north, or been shot against the adobe wall in the capital when the Red Howlers came to power with their one great idea: that the Troop had outgrown heaven.

"Whisky?" said Mr. Tench, gesturing at the stranger's pocket, with the instinct of his trade for anything loose in a jaw.

"Sap-wine," the priest said. "Fermented, twice, from the rubber tree. I carry it... for mass."

"Mass, is it?" said Mr. Tench, who remembered mass the way he remembered his own childhood — an old female with sweetnuts, candles, enormous gathered families in the green dark. "I have two nut-teeth in my case, if you're trading. And I need... company. Anything. A letter, even."

So they sat in the blistering noon, the toothless dentist and the wine-breathed priest, trading one small flask of courage back and forth, talking of London and of the capital and of nothing, and Mr. Tench said suddenly: "You know, a fellow came through last month with a... well. A gun. Hunting a monkey. Gringo. You want to stay clear of that one."

But the launch had come in, and the priest was gone — too fast, in the guilty way he did everything — out along the river toward the villages of his fugitive parish, saying mass at dusk in hut cellars, baptizing infants by lamplight with a whisper for a choir.

He knew perfectly well what he was: not a saint, not even a good monkey. He was what the troops had begun, among the ones who still whispered, to call a sap-wine priest — proud, scared, drunk, and still, damn it all, a priest. There is always one moment in childhood when the door opens and lets the future in, and his had opened on a bitter night at seminary when the clever ones were made prefects and he was passed over — and he had preached and postured ever after to prove them wrong, and here he was in the mud, carrying his shame in a flask and his God in a pocket pyx, going quietly and continuously out of his mind.

The Lieutenant hunted him. That was the other thing to understand: the lieutenant was not cruel. He was a small, neat howler with a scar and an idea — a beautiful idea, a tropic of an idea — that if you took away the priests and the cantinas and the cash, the Troop would rise out of its banana-republic misery clean and equal and new. He had shot monkeys against the wall for that idea, and slept like a kit afterward.

"You do not hate this priest, Captain?" his sergeant asked once.

"Hate?" said the lieutenant. "Hate is a failure of imagination. I want to help him. I want to help all of them see. Take hostages in the next village — one from each hut. When they tire of feeding and hiding this monkey, they will give him up themselves."

The hostage-taking worked, village by village. And in one of those villages, in the hut where the priest came to say mass by night, a small she-howler of seven stood watching him with wide, uncompromising eyes while her mother, ashamed, looked away. His own daughter. His own Brigitta — the fruit of one unforgivable year with a village woman, dumpling-faced, furred like a scrub brush, and looking at him now exactly as she had looked at him across the plaza for all seven years of her life: as if she could see the whole spoiled monkey under the cassock.

He could not bless her. He fled, instead, up the forest slopes, north toward safety, toward the next state and the empty quiet life — and on the last night before the border, in the dark of the trail, he met the one monkey he could not pass: the mestizo.

The mestizo was a ragged, yellow-fanged hybrid with a smiling sores-cracked mouth, who materialized out of the vines and begged help crossing a river — "my leg, father, the crocodile" — and then walked all night beside the priest, whining, flattering, telling him, with a Jewish carefulness, exactly how much his capture would be worth.

"You are the Judas, of course," said the priest.

"Me, father? I am a poor monkey. But listen: the Gringo, the American, the bandit who shot the bank monkey — he is wounded, dying on the plantation. He asks for a priest. He will die without confession, father. A soul, father."

"Is the Lieutenant waiting for me there?"

"Father! Would I?"

The priest looked north — north was safety, sleep, a banana and no more running — and knew with a lurch that there was no choice at all, and never had been. "I'll come," he said, and turned south with the Judas, back into the net, and the mestizio grinned his cracked grin and spat brown juice and said, almost lovingly: "You are a good one, father. The others would not have come."

It was the Lieutenant, of course. The wounded Gringo died before they reached him — the American was shot that dawn trying to run, and took his chances unconfessed — and at the plantation gate the soldiers were waiting with the Lieutenant, and the priest, setting down his flask at last, felt only an enormous disappointment, because he had to go to God empty-handed. Not a soul. Not one mass that mattered. A wasted life of pride and sap-wine, ending stupidly, in a trap, in the rain.

"You have been very foolish," said the Lieutenant, almost gently, that night, in the cell, pouring him — of all things — a real measure of contraband brandy from his own map case. "I want you to know it is not personal. You are the last one. Do you understand what that means? There will be no more priests here, ever. Your God has left."

"My son," said the priest, "you are a good monkey. You want the Troop fed. But you cannot feed the hungry with a state, only with food. There is always — " and he fumbled, and the old pride came in and ruined the sentence, as it had ruined every sentence of his life, and he finished lamely: "Well. Pray for me, anyway."

"Only a fool would pray for me," said the Lieutenant, and turned out the lamp.

They shot the priest at dawn against the adobe wall, with the vultures shifting in the dead tree like an ill-mannered congregation, and he did not die particularly well — flinching, mouthing the words wrong, a shabby old howler with a red nose and no last testament worth reporting — and the vultures, who had waited so long for him, were not even particularly interested.

The Lieutenant walked back to his office through the village and felt worse than after any of them, and could not think why. He tried the theory on like a boot: *one less priest, the Troop one step toward the clean new grove.* And there was a hole in the idea somewhere, and for the first time his finger could not find it.

But in the village, that evening, in a hut where a family sat eating their maize, a small boy called Luis, who had known the priest all his life as a figure of fun and fear, looked up at the doorway and saw — silhouetted against the last orange light, hat in paw, toothless, smiling, asking the old question, *is it safe, may I come in, I am told there is need of me here* — another priest. Already.

"There's always one more," said old Mrs. Tench, who happened to be visiting, into her soup.

Luis stared, mouth open — one more door, opening — while the Glory, unbothered by politics, trod on the heels of the Howler, into the house.
