# The Town Louse and the Country Louse

*A parody of "The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse"*

In the slow, green hills beyond the big river there lived a country louse named Chester, in the silky shoulder fur of an elderly howler monkey called Harold. Chester's life was simple and sweet: a morning nibble of dander, an afternoon nap in Harold's warm ruff, and the occasional fine dining experience when Harold passed beneath a fig tree in fruit.

Harold himself was a louse's dream of a landlord. He howled at dawn, dozed through the heat, and had never once — not in sixty years — been groomed by anything sharper than a friendly rain. His fur was deep, honest, and plentifully flaked. "A vintage host," Chester called him, and meant it as the highest praise his vocabulary contained.

One drizzling dawn, a cousin arrived — Montague, a town louse, blown in on the fur of a traveling fruit bat.

"Chester!" cried Montague, wringing out his tiny feet. "You poor creature. You look positively underfed."

"I look perfectly fed," said Chester, offering him a flake of skin on a leaf. "Try the dander. Harold's a vintage host."

Montague took one polite nibble and set it down. "Chester, this is twigs and gruel. In town, we live on a zoo monkey — a *capuchin*, if you please — and the keeper drops donut crumbs in his enclosure every single day. Donut crumbs, Chester. Sugar-dusted. Come and see for yourself."

"See town?" Chester's antennae — a louse's eyebrows — rose. "Nobody from this hollow has ever been to town."

"Then you'll be the first. Think of the stories. Think of the *crumbs*."

Chester thought of the crumbs. But he consulted Harold first, because one does not leave a vintage host without notice. Harold listened, chewed a leaf, and howled thoughtfully — which is howler for "town is very loud, hold on to something."

The very next market day, Chester hopped a ride on the fruit bat's back, held his breath over the river, and arrived — blink, blink — in Town.

What a place! Lights everywhere. Noise everywhere. Metal things that growled. Fish in windows. And there, in the Primate Palace of the city zoo, on the glossy golden fur of Sir Reginald the capuchin, Montague had a burrow with a view.

"Dinner is served," announced Montague grandly, leading Chester up a tower of groomed fur to a crumb of banana bread the size of a louse kingdom.

Chester took a bite and nearly wept. "It's — it's sweet. It's *soft*. It's like eating a cloud that somebody loved."

"And that's just the crumbs." Montague pointed across the enclosure. "Tuesday is yoghurt day. There's a drip tray. A *drip tray*, Chester. Graded, polished, and drained directly to us."

For two glorious days, Chester dined like a duke. Honey-dipped papaya shard. A fleck of sugar from a keeper's pocket. A crumb of croissant so buttery it shone. They took in the sights, too: the two o'clock crowd, who pressed their faces to the glass and called Sir Reginald adorable; the keeper's lecture, delivered over their heads ("Capuchins," the keeper told the crowd, "use tools," while the lice under discussion used a drip tray); and feeding time, which Montague attended the way other lice attend the opera.

"Marvelous, isn't it?" sighed Montague, on the second evening. "Culture. Refinement. Crumbs."

"Marvelous," Chester agreed, buttery to the soul. He was already composing the speech he'd give the hollow. *You cannot imagine, cousins, the softness of a donut.*

Then, on the third morning — disaster.

"THE COMB!" shrieked Montague. "THE FINE-TOOTHED COMB! RUN!"

Chester had never run from anything except a hungry bird, but he ran now, deep into Reginald's fur, as a great silver comb the size of a landslide raked through the canopy above them, plucking lice like ripe fruit. Whole neighborhoods went in a single pass. The drip tray district was decimated. Montague dragged Chester into a seam of fur and held him there while the terrible silver dawn roared over and over overhead.

"Friday is Flea-Shampoo Day," panted Montague, when the world had stopped smelling of lavender and doom. "You get used to it."

"Get USED to it?"

"And there's the vacuum. And the vet with the little plastic claw. And the zoo-maintenance fogger, who comes once a month. But the food, Chester. Think of the food."

Chester thought of the food. That evening he even managed a small feast — a setback of meringue, a shard of sugar-glass — and was beginning to believe the danger was seasonal, when word raced down the fur that the Director was coming. The Director wore white gloves and carried a lens of polished crystal that made the world look the size it actually was to everybody else.

"HEALTH INSPECTION," hissed Montague, going gray. "THE WHITE GLOVE. DOWN. DOWN!"

They went down, to the deepest, dankest, most unfashionable stratum of Reginald's fur, and lay in an old flake like shipwrecked sailors while the great glove passed above them, once, twice, and the crystal lens poured a circle of burning daylight along the pelt. Chester held his breath so long he saw his whole life — Harold, the hollow, the fig tree in fruit, the rain on the leaves — parade before his eyes at film speed.

The glove withdrew. The light rolled away. Somewhere above, the Director pronounced Sir Reginald "adequately clean," and all the remaining lice cheered in very small voices.

That night they crept out for a midnight feast beneath the moon — a celebration, Montague insisted, of survival and crumbs — and had taken exactly one nibble of meringue when a shape loomed. Yellow. Humming. Enormous.

"THE FOGGER!" screamed Montague. "EARLY! IT'S EARLY!"

They fled across the enclosure on legs that weren't designed for fleeing, the meringue abandoned, the moon indifferent, and wedged at last into a crack in Reginald's favorite log, hearts hammering like tiny drums, while the sweet-smelling death rained down outside and made the whole world smell of lavender and doom again.

"Get used to it," Chester quoted, faintly, from the depths of the log.

"You do get used to it," said Montague, with dignity. "The trick is not minding, and also never going outside in the mornings, evenings, or on inspection days."

"Which days are those?"

"All of them."

When the sun rose, Chester shook Montague awake.

"I'm going home," he said.

"Home?" Montague stared. "To *dander*? To twigs and gruel?"

"To breakfast with Harold the howler, who has never seen a comb in his life," said Chester, "and never will, because he howls at them. To flaky, honest, boring old fur, where nothing smells of lavender, and nothing ever comes fuming over the horizon to catch a louse at lunch. Cousin — I ate a cloud that somebody loved. And then somebody loved *me*, briefly, with a glove."

Montague sighed and hugged his cousin. "You're mad. But I suppose... the crumbs here are awfully tense."

Chester hopped the fruit bat home, and that very evening he sat down to a simple supper of vintage dander in Harold's warm shoulder ruff, with rain pattering the leaves and not a comb in the world. Harold, sensing company, howled once — gently, for Harold — and the hollow rang with it like a soft bronze bell.

"Ah," said Chester, tucking in. "This is more like it."

He told his story all that winter, and trimmed it honestly — the wonders left in, the terrors left in too, which is what made the young lice listen. And ever after, when the young lice of the hills dreamed aloud of town crumbs and drip trays, old Chester would raise a flake of dander and say:

**Better dander in peace than donuts in danger.**

Montague stayed in town, mind you — he always did like to live dangerously. But he writes sometimes, when the fogger's been through. He says the meringue was worth it. He says the Tuesday drip tray has been rebuilt finer than before. He also says his nerves are made of steel, which is easy to believe, because they're certainly not made of anything calm.
