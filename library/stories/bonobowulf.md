# Bonobowulf

*A parody of "Beowulf"*

Hoot! We have heard of the grip of the monkey-kings, of the troop-lords' strength in days gone by — how the ancient princes won eternal fame by swinging where none had swung before.

In the deep jungle-country, Hrothgar the old silverback, greatest of troop-lords, built himself a hall — Heorot, the finest fig-hall ever thatched, with perches of gold-washed liana and a roof so wide the hornbills used it for a highway. There the troop feasted nightly — bananas, figs, honeycomb — and the noise carried for miles.

Which was the problem.

Out in the mist-vines lived Grendel — a great shambling marsh-stalker, half gorilla, half thundercloud, all grievance. Night after night for twelve years he came to Heorot when the drums of feasting faded, snatching sleepers from their perches and stealing the entire banana-hoard, every golden bunch, gone. The troop began sleeping in the far trees, and Hrothgar grew old and grey-muzzled with sorrow.

Then across the sea came Bonobowulf, a young champion of the bonobos, with fourteen picked companions, famous for two things: a grip with the strength of thirty monkeys in each hand, and an inability to stop talking about it.

"I have come to grapple with this Grendel," he announced. "No spear, no net — bare hands, grip against grip, as when I swam the fig-wine sea against Brecca for five nights."

"You lost that swim," said Unferth, Hrothgar's cross patch of a counselor.

"I killed nine sea-beasts on the way. That is not losing; that is a full schedule."

Unferth, having no answer to a schedule like that, subsided — though later, quietly, he came to Bonobowulf with a gift: an ancient sword, Hrunting, forged in the before-times, iron with a figured grip. "I have heard tell of your strength. Take it. It has never failed its wielder."

"I shall honor it," said Bonobowulf, who honored everything, loudly.

Queen Wealhtheow moved through the feast then, gold-circled, greeting the troop-apes, and offered the cup first to Hrothgar and then to the guest. "Enjoy this drink, bonobo, and be kind to my sons; your fame will last while the walls stand. In friendship we pledge you the hall tonight." And the troop murmured that a queen's word was worth more than the king's treasury, because it came with the queen's judgment.

The troop feasted; the drums faded; the hall went dark. Bonobowulf waited on the great perch, pretending to sleep, tail twitching.

Grendel came. He ate a sleeping companion — one bite, terrible to tell — and reached for the next, and found his paw gripped. The grip of thirty. Grendel had never in all his misty life been gripped.

The hall shook. Perches snapped like dry twigs. Grendel pulled; Bonobowulf held; the building groaned like a ship in a storm-wind. At the end of it Grendel escaped into the night — armless, and after that, no longer a problem, if you follow.

There was a great feast. The scop-apes sang in Heorot again — first a song of Creation, figs and vines and all things green, which Grendel had hated; then a longer lay, the Fight at Finnsburg, which the troop loved because every ape present knew all the words and none of the plot, which is what makes a classic.

But the marsh held more. Grendel's mother — fiercer and grimmer than her son — came to Heorot for revenge, snatched Aeschere, the king's oldest counselor, and carried off Grendel's severed arm as a trophy.

"Followed by vengeance, trouble follows trouble," said Hrothgar grimly. "She has taken my oldest friend, and my oldest friend's seat is empty tonight."

Bonobowulf swore over the mead-bench and rode to the bloodied water at dawn. The mere, a wallow so foul the water burned, with serpements in the waves and a wolf on the far bank, glared at him from under the fog. Hrunting in paw, he dove.

Down through the burning wallow-water, through nests of glaring eels, until he stood in her cave at the bottom, where a strange firelight hung. He swung Hrunting with all his strength — and the ancient blade sang off her hide like a fig off a stone, the first failure of its career, and it sulked on the cave floor for the rest of the poem. She had him down, claws at his throat, when his eye caught an old blade forged by giants, hanging on the wall. He took it, swung it with all thirty monkeys' worth of grip, and the feud was finished — and the giant's blade, touching her poisoned blood, melted like ice in springtide, hilt and all, till only the gold-washed hilt remained, graven with the story of the Flood, when giants drowned.

He found Grendel lying dead in the cave and settled the account — the head came home. He surfaced with the hilt, the head, the counselor, and was rewarded with so many bananas he had to hire a second boat.

Before he sailed, he gave Wealhtheow's necklace to his own queen, Hygd, for gifts should travel to the bravest, and Unferth's sword he left in the cave, saying nothing, which is the harshest review a weapon can get.

Fifty years passed. Bonobowulf was an old king, famous and grey, when a thief, fleeing a beating, stole one golden banana cup from a dragon's hoard and bought his pardon with it. The dragon — an ancient flying lizard who loved gold fruit above all things — blazed out in fury, melting the perches and smoking out the troop.

Bonobowulf, old as he was, took eleven companions and went to meet it. Ten stopped at the edge of the fire. Only Wiglaf, young and steady, came when the flames flared.

"Stand by me, great king — together!"

"I thank thee, Wiglaf," said old Bonobowulf, "for saying that after all these years. Better for every ape death than a life of shame. Now — the worm has seen the cup."

The dragon struck; the king's shield melted; Wiglaf's paw held; the blade went up under the dragon's jaw, and the old king broke his own sword on its skull and killed it — and in killing it was bitten to the quick.

"Fallow-furred Wiglaf," said the king, dying, "bring me somewhat of the hoard. I would buy my people's future with my death. Had I a son, I would leave him my armor. Now I have traded my old age for this gold. It is enough."

Wiglaf waded into the barrow and came out dazzled — armfuls of gold, cups, twisted wire, more wealth than any troop could spend, and no troop left to spend it, as he reminded the ten: "Every one of you shall lose his land-right when the war-birds hear how you stood at the stone. A lord who gave gifts, and you did not go! I say what no ape should: your shame shall live forever."

The messenger rode the cliffs with worse news: with the peacemaker dead, the Frisians and Franks would come over the water now — the old wars would wake — for it is the way of the canopy that peace rests on one strong back. And a grey-haired monkey, keening at the barrow, sang the dread-song: the day of the troop's fall, enemies in the hall, and no Bonobowulf coming.

They built his barrow on the headland, high over the sea, and buried the dragon's hoard with him — since gold is no use to the dead, a fact the dragon never learned.

And the bonobos said — and carved it in the bark of the great fig — that he was, of all the kings of the canopy, the gentlest to his troop, the most gracious in grooming, and the keenest for banana-fame.

Then they sat down, mourned their lord, and scratched. Hoot.
