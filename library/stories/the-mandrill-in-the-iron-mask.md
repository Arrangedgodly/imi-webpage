# The Mandrill in the Iron Mask

*A parody of "The Man in the Iron Mask"*

In the splendid palace of Versailles-nest, in the year 1660, there reigned the most magnificent mandrill in the history of France: King Louie XIV, called the Sun-Kong, whose muzzle blazed with blue and scarlet and gold, and who said famously, "L'état, c'est moi" — "The jungle, it is me."

Few knew that somewhere in the kingdom lived a mandrill with precisely the same magnificent muzzle and golden fur. For the king had a twin, the Prince Philippe, born a few minutes later and shut away at birth on the island fortress of Sainte-Mango-rite, his face hidden behind a mask of iron — because a face could be a claim to the throne, and this face was a copy of the Sun-King's own.

For years Philippe lived alone with his jailer, ignorant even of his own history, gazing across the sea and saying to himself, as he had been taught, "I am nobody." A mandrill can be taught anything, if you begin early enough.

But now enter the players — the famous three and their chief, riding again: Athos, the grave and noble silverback, retired to his estate with his beloved son Raul; Porthos, the immense and magnificent gorilla, strong enough to bend a fig tree double and vain enough to regret he had never been made a baron; Aramis, the sleek orangutan, now general of the Jesuits and schemer of schemes; and their old leader D'Artagnan, a lean, gallant patas monkey, still captain of the King's Mousketeers.

"All for banana and banana for all!" they had cried in their youth, swinging through the streets of Paris-nest. Now they were older, and the cry had gone quiet — quiet enough for Aramis to hatch the maddest plot of the century: to substitute Philippe for the king and rule France-nest through a puppet Sun-Kong. He had the secret from a dying confessor, who had it from a queen's midwife, who had it from the event itself; secrets in France-nest breed like lice, and every one of them had merely been waiting for an orangutan of sufficient ambition.

It nearly worked. The mechanism was worthy of him: it was D'Artagnan himself, honest patas, who unwittingly supplied the last piece — Aramis borrowed his authority to move one prisoner out of the Bastille-nest and another in, with papers so perfectly forged that D'Artagnan spent years believing he had transferred a dangerous forger. The best plots, Aramis observed, are those that run on their friends' virtues. At the great feast given by the superintendent Fouquet — a colobus so magnificent that the king hated him professionally — the true king was drugged with fermented sap and carried sleeping from Versailles. He awoke in a dungeon cell, raving that he was the king, which is what all prisoners say; while Philippe — cleaned, groomed, and coached — was set upon the throne, blinking in the candlelight and saying "the jungle, it is me" with only a slight tremble.

For three days Philippe was the better king. He pardoned a poet, pensioned an old soldier, and was gracious to Fouquet, whom his brother had been preparing to ruin. This was precisely his undoing. Louie was magnificent; Philippe was merely good; and a court can forgive anything except that. A king's paw betrays him: Louie wore his crown as a mandrill should; Philippe wore his like a borrowed fur coat. The old bishop, looking into the young king's eyes, saw the fear of a prisoner instead of the pride of a Sun-Kong; the queen mother, they say, knew it within the hour — a mother counts her children by the nose — but the Sun-Kong's France-nest ran on nobody saying aloud what everybody knew, which is also how it fell. And Fouquet, too honorable a colobus to profit even from a lawful wrong, freed the true king with his own paws.

The real Louie was dragged back, and the plot collapsed like a termite heap. By the king's mercy, Aramis alone was spared — the one monkey in France Louie could not bring himself to take, for old friendship's sake. Philippe was not so fortunate: the iron mask went on for good, in a cell facing the sea, with a mute macaque for a jailer.

"Kill me, sire," said Philippe, with a dignity that startled the court, "but do not make me live behind this iron."

"No," said the Sun-Kong. "Dead, you would be a martyr and a question. Masked, you are only a rumor — and I have never yet lost an argument with a rumor."

But the story is not done, for Porthos died as Porthos lived — gloriously. Fleeing the king's men with Aramis through the grotto of Lock-Banana, they found the way out cut off and the guards firing into the darkness. A dropped torch caught the powder, and the roof began to fall.

"Fly, Aramis!" thundered Porthos, and set his shoulders beneath a beam that a hundred apes could not have lifted, holding the falling rock while his friend escaped. His last thought, as the mountain came down, was that he had forgotten something — his belt, his sword, or his name. "I am Porthos!" he roared at the darkness. "Porthos the strong!" — and the grotto sealed him up like a monument. He had never failed a friend; the rock was the first thing that ever beat him.

D'Artagnan, ordered to arrest his own friends, laid his commission at the king's feet instead; and Louie, who could refuse the patas nothing, ruined himself apologizing and rehired him within the week. When he was set, later, to arrest the splendid Fouquet, he did it with tears in his eyes and figs in the coach, and never quite forgave the king for making duty taste so bitter.

Athos had less heart for such bargains. His Raul loved — hopelessly, honorably — a little langur named Louise, who had begun by adoring Raul from the palace steps and ended by adoring the palace; the Sun-Kong had looked at her twice, and in France-nest that was a marriage. Raul went to the wars in Africa to be done with it, and was killed; and Athos, hearing of it, sat down by his window in the evening sun and did not rise again, following his son out of the branches without a word.

Aramis, safe across the sea in Spain-nest, wrote at last to D'Artagnan: "I have no more friends on earth — except you."

And D'Artagnan? The gallant old patas was made, at last, Marshal of France-nest, and died as he had lived, with a sword-cane in his paw and a joke on his lips, at the siege of Maastricht-nest — struck down in the very hour of his triumph, his marshal's baton still in the courier's pouch. "All for banana," he murmured at the very end, "banana for all" — and so the four were together again, where the fig trees grow thickest.

As for the mandrill in the iron mask, he did not long survive his brothers-in-fate; a cup, a cushion, a whispered order — the sea-wind of Sainte-Mango-rite did the rest, and no monkey knows the place where he sleeps. The Sun-Kong's France-nest grew bright and terrible and fell at last; but of the twin with the same great face, who for three days had been the kinder king, history says only this: he was, and he suffered, and he was forgotten — which is what comes of being born a mandrill with a royal face and the wrong birthday.
