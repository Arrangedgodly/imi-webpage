# Robinson Groomsoe

*A parody of "Robinson Crusoe"*

I was born in the year 1632, in the city of York, of a good family of tree-dwelling apes — neither climbers nor ground-crawlers, but what you might call the middle branch of society. My father wished me for the family fig business, and lectured me often upon the two extremes of life: the upper branches, where monkeys fall off from pride, and the mud, where they forget they were meant for trees. The middle station, he said, had all the figs and none of the vertigo.

"If you go to sea," he warned, "you will be the most miserable monkey in the world, for you were born for the trees, not the waves."

I went anyway. If the reader will not take warning by my story, so much the worse for the reader's bananas.

My first voyage corrected me in the manner of a strict aunt. No sooner had we cleared the Humber than the wind rose like a fury, and I lay in the hold vowing that if Heaven would spare me this once, I would go home to the fig trade and never so much as bathe again. We were wrecked within sight of Yarmouth. I was carried ashore half-drowned, walked to London — and went to sea again within the week. That is how a young monkey keeps his vows: he keeps them in a drawer, with his winter fur.

On a later voyage I was taken by baboon pirates of the Barbary Coast and held two years a slave at Sallee, tending my master's fig-garden. I escaped one misty morning in the longboat with a young monkey named Soo-key, a jar of water, and a basket of dried figs, teaching my companion as we went to say "Master," "Yes," and "No" — a naming convention I was to find useful again, as the reader shall see. A Portuguese fruit-captain rescued us and set me up in the Brazil groves.

For four years I grew bananas there and prospered beyond my deserts, until planters proposed a slaving run to the Banana Coast, for which my plantation experience made me, they said, the very monkey. I sailed; we met a storm more terrible than any in monkey memory; the ship foundered; and I, alone of all the crew, was cast up on the shore of a desolate island — soaked, half-drowned, and as alone as the last monkey in the world.

I was sorry at first, and then practical — on the whole the better order for castaways. I swam out to the wreck twelve times and salvaged what a monkey could carry: carpenter's tools, two guns, a keg of powder, ink, three Bibles, and — by the greatest providence of the voyage — two cats and a ship's dog, who became my troop.

I built a shelter of poles and sailcloth, fenced it against the night, and carved into a tree the date of my landing: the 30th of September, 1659. Beside it I set a post, and every day I notched it, for if I must be a castaway, I should at least be a punctual one.

I kept a journal, setting the evils of my condition against its comforts, like an ape auditing his bananas:

EVIL: I am cast upon a desolate island, with no troop to groom me.
GOOD: But I am alive, and every monkey of my ship's company but me is drowned.

EVIL: I have no clothes to my back but what I stand in.
GOOD: But it is a warm climate, and were I to wear clothes I should only look ridiculous, being already dressed in fur.

Barley from a ship's sack grew yellow in my fields, and I milked my tamed goats, and baked clay pots so ugly they were almost handsome. I made an umbrella of palmetto leaves, to go abroad civilly. I taught my parrot — for of course there came to be a parrot — my name. "Poor Robinson Groomsoe!" it would cry. "Ay, ay, poor Robinson," I would answer, and we kept each other company.

An earthquake shook my fence as a dog shakes a flea. A fever laid me three days in the dark believing the fig trees had unionized against me, and in it I dreamed an avenging ape descended from a black cloud, spear in paw. I woke with the Bible on my chest, open at the words: *Call on me in the day of trouble, and I will deliver thee.* For the first time since York I prayed with something in it. I also cured myself with rum-steeped tobacco, Heaven helping those who help themselves to the medicine chest.

In my eleventh year I built a canoe and set out to circle the island; a current carried me so far to sea that I won back half-dead on the ebb, and ever after regarded my little kingdom with the tenderness of a monkey who has nearly lost a wife. Thereafter I kept two of everything — a home establishment and a country bower — for a castaway of property must have a town house and a summer seat.

So some years, tolerably content, thinking myself the only monkey on the island. Then one day, going towards my canoe, I was thunderstruck by the print of a naked paw in the sand — the plain print of a monkey's foot, toes, heel, palm and all, where no paw but mine had any business to be.

I listened; I looked round; I climbed the nearest tree and stared till my eyes ached. Nothing. But I went home in a fever of dread, and for two years dared not so much as crack a nut too loudly.

What the print belonged to, I learned at last, when I came upon a shore strewn with fire and bones — monkey bones, picked and burnt. The island, it proved, was a place of resort for raiding troops from the mainland, who roasted such captives as they brought. I, who could not shoot a goat without an apology, considered what I now was: a monkey living next door to an oven.

At last three canoes landed, and a captive broke loose and ran for his life along the strand, two pursuers at his heels. I knocked the pursuers flat with the mere wind of my shot, and the fugitive fell at my feet in gratitude. I fed him goat-milk and biscuit; I taught him to say "Master," "Yes," and "No"; and because I saved him upon a Friday, I called him Friday — privately thinking it the finest name on the island.

"Friday," said I, one evening by the fire, "do you love your master?"

"Yes, yes — Friday love master," said he, and meant it, and I believed him.

Together we did wonders. I taught him the mysteries of bread and the terror of guns; he taught me to swing from the vines so gracefully that I wept for my crabbed years, and to laugh at danger — a lesson no castaway ever got from a book. We held theological seminars besides, which I did not always win.

"Friday," I asked him, "if God much strong, why the devil no kill him?"

I had no answer, and neither had Friday — "he no kill him" — which he then tried to demonstrate all evening, gesturing at the heavens with a banana skin, until we agreed that some questions were designed by Providence for throwing at the fire.

Some time after, a canoe brought two starving castaways ashore: an old monkey and a Spaniard. The old one, it proved, was Friday's father. Friday's joy was past all describing — he leapt, he embraced him, he kissed him, he danced round him like a frantic thing. In all my years I saw nothing on the island to equal it: love, in a troop of two and a half.

We planned then to fetch the Spaniards left on the mainland; but before plans and seasons could agree, an English ship arrived in the night full of mutinous seamen, who brought their honest captain ashore to murder him discreetly. Friday and I, by strategy and noise — chiefly noise, which is half of gorilla warfare — recovered the ship for her captain. And so, upon the 19th of December, I took my leave of my island kingdom of eight-and-twenty years, two months, and nineteen days, sailing home with Friday at my side, where no monkey could say which of us had rescued the other.

Between the island and home lay eleven wolves, a bear, and the snowy Pyrenees — Friday laughing through it with a sword in each paw — but that story must wait for a sequel.

I landed in England to find myself a rich monkey — my Brazil plantation grown fat under honest guardians, like a fig ripening in a neighbor's window. I married, bred monkeys of my own, and when my wife died, sailed once more as a private trader; for a monkey of my habits cannot keep from the sea, and I have never yet found the drawer where my vows are kept.

And Friday? Let no monkey tell you he was a servant. He was the truest companion and the worst-dressed theologian in the hemisphere, and I have missed him since. At home in the evenings I still hear the parrot: "Poor Robinson Groomsoe!"

"Ay, ay," I say. "Poor Robinson."

It is not the worst epitaph for a monkey who was, for eight-and-twenty years, king of everything the tide could not reach.
