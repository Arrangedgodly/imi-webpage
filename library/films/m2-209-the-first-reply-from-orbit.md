# The First Reply from Orbit

*Format: Film parody*
*Parody target: first-contact science fiction, global reaction montages, ominous message decoding, and scientists who mistake a greeting for a threat*
*By: IMI Pictures Monkey Desk*
*Batch: IMI-M2-FILMS-007*

CAST
DR. LUMA, a capuchin radio astronomer
DIRECTOR VENN, a gibbon observatory chief
KAI, a ring-tailed lemur translator

SCENE 1. INT. MOUNTAIN OBSERVATORY - NIGHT

A dish turns toward the sky. A repeated signal arrives: three pulses, a pause, two pulses. The staff gathers around a screen. The world's news channels begin broadcasting guesses: a warning, a map, a countdown.

VENN: We need to reply before the signal stops.

LUMA: We should confirm the source first.

KAI: It repeats every eleven minutes, exactly.

The screen shows a small planet rotating behind a moon.

SCENE 2. INT. OBSERVATORY - LATER

The team plays the signal backward, slows it down, and compares it to every known language. Each attempt produces a new theory. Venn builds a dramatic timeline on a glass board. Luma notices that the pulses align with the planet's rotation.

LUMA: It may be a beacon, not a message.

VENN: The whole planet is watching.

KAI: The whole planet is watching us watch it.

A technician transmits a reply using the same pulse pattern. The signal stops. Everyone gasps.

SCENE 2A. INT. OBSERVATORY - DAY

The observatory director calls an emergency meeting. News channels ask whether the signal is a threat. Venn wants to send a formal reply immediately. Luma requests a second day of observation.

VENN: The signal is repeating. We have a window.

LUMA: We do not know who made it, or whether it is meant for us.

KAI: The timing matches the planet's rotation. We can wait one cycle and see whether the pattern changes.

A press team sets up cameras outside. A reporter asks if the observatory has found a new civilization. Luma answers that they have found a repeating signal, which is an observation, not a conclusion.

The team checks the dish alignment. A loose bracket has shifted slightly in the wind. They tighten it and calibrate again. The signal remains, now cleaner and stronger.

SCENE 3. INT. OBSERVATORY - NIGHT

A second signal arrives. It is the same three pulses and two, followed by a new tone. The team argues whether the new tone means anger or acknowledgement.

KAI: It's a musical interval.

LUMA: The tone is on a different band.

KAI: It resembles a greeting pattern from the neighboring star system.

Venn orders a second reply. Luma asks the team to wait for a full rotation. They discover that the new tone occurs whenever the planet's moon passes behind a ring of debris.

SCENE 3A. INT. OBSERVATORY - LATER

A second analyst joins the team and checks the signal from a separate receiver. The pattern is identical. The analyst finds no modulation that resembles speech, numbers, or a map.

ANALYST: It may be a navigation beacon, a weather marker, or a device repeating its status.

VENN: We may never know.

LUMA: We can publish what we measured and leave the interpretation open.

The observatory waits through another orbit. The pulses arrive at the same intervals. A camera crew asks for a reaction shot. Venn turns toward them, then steps aside so the telescope operator can work.

KAI: We are not the story. The signal is.

The team logs the time, frequency, and direction. They send no second message until the next cycle confirms the pattern.

SCENE 4. INT. OBSERVATORY - DAWN

The signal repeats. Luma overlays the pulses on the orbital chart. The first signal was a beacon marking the planet's daylight side; the second tone was the moon's shadow crossing the transmitter.

VENN: We replied to a beacon.

LUMA: We replied to a beacon.

KAI: The beacon replied to our reply. We have made contact with a machine, at least.

The team prepares a simple pattern: one pulse to acknowledge, two to end. They send it after the next rotation. The signal continues, now separated by a slightly longer pause.

SCENE 5. INT. OBSERVATORY - MORNING

The staff publishes their findings with a clear distinction between observation and speculation. The news channels replace their countdown graphics with the orbit chart. Venn removes the dramatic timeline from the glass board and writes: "Signal repeats every eleven minutes."

The news channels revise their graphics, removing the countdown and labeling the signal "unidentified repeating beacon." Venn reads the update without adding a prediction. The observatory keeps its receivers pointed toward the planet, ready for another cycle.

LUMA: We haven't met anyone yet.

KAI: We have learned how to listen.

A fresh signal arrives. It contains the same three pulses, a pause, then two. The team waits. A final faint tone follows, precisely at sunrise.

FINAL IMAGE: The dish turns toward the brightening sky. The signal continues at its own measured pace, while the observatory staff sits quietly and records each pulse without naming it a message.
