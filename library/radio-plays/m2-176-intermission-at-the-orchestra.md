# Intermission at the Orchestra

*Format: Radio play parody*
*Parody target: classical radio intermissions, reverent instrument biographies, and commentators who keep the music from resuming*
*By: IMI Audio Monkey Desk*
*Batch: IMI-M2-AUDIO-006*

CAST
HOST ENID, a lemur
MAESTRO VOK, a gibbon
USHER PIP, a capuchin

[ORCHESTRA PLAYS A SHORT CADENCE. APPLAUSE.]

ENID: You're listening to the Kettle City Orchestra. We are at intermission, a pause in which the audience stretches while our microphone remains respectfully pointed toward the stage.

VOK: Enid, we have a small problem. The oboe is missing.

ENID: The instrument or the oboe player?

VOK: The oboe player is here. The instrument is in the case, but the reed is gone.

[CASE OPENS. PAPER RUSTLES.]

PIP: I found a reed under the programs. It's still wrapped.

VOK: That is a bassoon reed. The oboe reed is smaller.

ENID: We will use this intermission to explain the reed's importance.

VOK: A reed vibrates when the player blows through it. It shapes the sound. The wrong reed can make the instrument sound like a very small duck.

PIP: I heard a small duck in the lobby.

[QUACK.]

ENID: Perhaps the reed has already found its instrument.

VOK: The duck is the orchestra's mascot. It is in a cage beside the ticket desk.

ENID: Then we'll stay with the music. Tell us about the second violin.

VOK: The second violin supports the first violin and plays a distinct part.

ENID: Are the first and second violins related?

VOK: They are both violins.

ENID: A close musical family.

PIP: The oboe player says the reed case is at the back of the stage.

[FOOTSTEPS, DRAWER OPENS.]

VOK: Here it is. The oboe reed is in a tiny case beneath the conductor's score.

ENID: That solves the mystery. Our audience has learned where the reed lives.

VOK: We have one minute before the second half. The oboe player is testing it.

[ONE HIGH NOTE.]

PIP: The duck is answering from the lobby.

[QUACK. HIGH NOTE. QUACK.]

ENID: They are in a duet.

VOK: They are in separate rooms. The oboe player is warming up; the duck is quacking because it hears the note.

ENID: Should we bring the duck into the hall?

VOK: No. The music begins in one minute, and the duck should remain in its safe enclosure.

PIP: I'll close the lobby door so the sound stays outside.

[DOOR CLOSES. QUACK BECOMES QUIETER.]

ENID: The hall is ready. Maestro, what piece follows intermission?

VOK: A movement for strings, winds, and percussion.

ENID: Any missing parts?

VOK: One cymbal mallet is under the conductor's chair.

PIP: I have it.

ENID: We have recovered the mallet and the reed. Our brief pause is complete.

[APPLAUSE. ORCHESTRA BEGINS.]

PIP: The flute player has found a loose page under the chair.

ENID: Is it music?

PIP: It is the program for tonight's second half. The pages were out of order.

VOK: That explains the conductor's pause. I will reset the score and give the players the correct sequence.

ENID: Our intermission has now recovered a reed, a mallet, and a page of music. The orchestra has regained its order, and the audience has returned to their seats.

PIP: And the lobby door is closed, so the duck's quacks are no longer in the hall.

[DOOR CLICKS. THE QUACKS FADE.]

VOK: We can begin with the right movement this time.

ENID: A rare broadcast achievement: knowing which piece is next before it starts.

ENID: The first notes are clear. The reed has found the oboe, the mallet has found the percussionist, and the duck remains offstage.

[QUACK, FAINTLY, IN TIME WITH THE MUSIC.]

VOK: It is still listening.

ENID: The orchestra welcomes quiet listeners, even from the lobby. Thank you for joining our intermission broadcast. We return you now to the performance.

[MUSIC SWELLS, THEN CUTS TO THEME.]
