# The Translator's Dictionary Has a Tail

*Format: Radio play parody*
*Parody target: language-learning broadcasts, overly literal phrasebooks, and cheerful lessons that translate idioms word for word*
*By: IMI Audio Monkey Desk*
*Batch: IMI-M2-AUDIO-015*

Logline: A language host teaches a phrase about "keeping an eye on the kettle," while the studio's learner searches for an actual eye.

Cast: Teacher Seli, a macaque; learner Pavo, a gibbon; lexicographer Neri, an orangutan; producer Tumo, a capuchin; caller Kavi, a baboon.

[SFX: PAGE TURN, PENCIL TAP.]

SELI: Today's phrase means to watch something carefully: "keep an eye on the kettle."

PAVO: Whose eye?

NERI: It is an idiom. No eye is involved.

PAVO: Then the phrase is inaccurate.

TUMO: The kettle is beginning to whistle.

ACT ONE

Seli introduces a beginner language lesson about household idioms. Pavo has been studying from an old phrasebook that gives literal translations without examples. Neri brings a dictionary with context notes. The kettle whistles again. Pavo asks if this is the moment to keep an eye on it. Seli says to lift it off the heat. Tumo does so.

PAVO: I watched it. It still whistled.

SELI: The phrase means to pay attention before it boils over.

PAVO: It already boiled.

NERI: Then attention arrived late.

The lesson moves to "hold your horses." Pavo looks around the studio for horses. Neri says it means wait. Pavo asks why the phrase has animals if no animal is present. Seli admits language keeps old images even after the situation changes. Tumo asks whether the broadcast should teach the literal form first or the meaning.

ACT TWO

Kavi calls from a market stall. He says a customer told him to "break a leg" before opening his fruit stand. He wants to know whether he should call for help. Seli explains that the phrase can mean good luck in performance settings, though it sounds alarming outside them. Kavi says he sells pears, not theater tickets.

KAVI: Do I wish the pears a broken leg?

SELI: No. You can say "good luck with the stall."

PAVO: That is clearer.

Neri points out that idioms vary by context and region. The phrasebook has no note for this one, so Tumo adds a sticky tab. Pavo asks whether every expression needs an animal or body part. Neri says no. Seli gives an example: "the market opens at dawn." Pavo says that one opens a door at dawn. Neri explains that the market itself begins business.

ACT TWO-B

The kettle is off the heat and cooling. Seli asks Pavo to explain the phrase in his own words. He says "keep an eye on the kettle" means watch it so it does not boil over, not place an eye beside it. Neri approves. Pavo turns to the phrasebook and notices that "hold your horses" is illustrated with a monkey holding two toy horses by their tails.

NERI: The picture makes the words literal again.

PAVO: It also makes the monkeys look unkind.

TUMO: We can redraw it with the horses waiting at a stop line.

Seli asks Pavo to use the idiom in a sentence. He says he will keep an eye on the kettle next time. The kettle has already cooled, so he turns toward it anyway. Tumo reminds him the expression is a lesson, not a command to stare at empty appliances.

PAVO: Then I will keep an eye on the phrasebook.

NERI: That can mean check it carefully.

PAVO: Does it mean put an eye on the page?

SELI: No.

Kavi calls back to report that the stall is open and nobody's leg is broken. The team gives him a straightforward good-luck wish. He thanks them and says the pears are selling well.

ACT THREE

Seli reviews the three expressions: keep an eye on something means watch it; hold your horses means wait; break a leg can mean good luck in some performance settings. Neri adds that listeners should check context instead of trusting a word-for-word translation. Pavo reads the revised phrasebook note aloud. The kettle has stopped steaming.

Tumo asks if they can include the actual sound of the kettle cooling. Pavo says it is just a small click. Seli says ordinary sounds can be used as examples, but they do not become idioms by appearing in the lesson. Neri writes the kettle note in the margin: "Let it cool before touching."

PAVO: Is that a phrase?

NERI: It is a lid.
