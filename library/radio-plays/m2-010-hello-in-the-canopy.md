# Hello in the Canopy

*Format: Radio play parody*
*Parody target: Language-learning audio lessons with call-and-response drills*
*By: IMI Audio Monkey Desk*
*Batch: IMI-M2-AUDIO*

Cast: Ina, a gibbon teacher; Bo, a macaque learner; Luz, a capuchin learner; Toma, a capuchin visitor from the lower beach.

[THEME: WOODBLOCK, BIRDSONG, A TEACHER TAPS A BRANCH TWICE.]

TEACHER INA: Welcome to Speak With the Troop. I'm Ina, a gibbon language teacher. Today we are learning the greeting used in the upper canopy of Makoa Island. It means "I see you, and I will not take your branch." Repeat after me: "Malo, tali."

LEARNERS: Malo, tali.

INA: Good. The second word tells the listener you come in peace. If you say only "Malo," they may think you are asking for food. If you say "Tali" too loudly, they may think you are a warning call.

LEARNER BO: I have a question. What if I need a branch?

INA: Then ask. "Tali, may I cross?" Repeat: "Tali, ro ka."

LEARNERS: Tali, ro ka.

INA: Good. We will use it in a short scene. You are crossing the stream bridge. A macaque named Nemi is sitting on the branch you need.

NEMI: Who is there?

BO: Malo, tali. Tali, ro ka.

NEMI: I hear you. You may cross after I finish my mango.

BO: Thank you. "Sava."

INA: "Sava" means thank you. It does not mean "please finish quickly." That is a separate phrase, and we are not teaching it today.

[SFX: A MANGO PIT DROPS INTO WATER.] 

INA: Now the bridge is wet. You need to warn someone below. The warning is "Koro!" It means "fruit falling." Say it once, clearly.

LEARNERS: Koro!

INA: Do not say it when you drop your own fruit into a basket. That would be confusing and dramatic.

LEARNER LUZ: What do we say if we drop a mango?

INA: "Oops" is understood on the whole island.

LUZ: That seems convenient.

INA: Language is full of gifts. Now let's practice a polite refusal. "No, thank you" is "Mena sava."

LEARNERS: Mena sava.

INA: A vendor offers you a basket of sour fruit. You do not want it. Say the phrase.

BO: Mena sava.

VENDOR: It is very sour.

BO: I can hear that from here.

INA: Good sentence. You declined without insulting the vendor's harvest. That matters. This afternoon, a visitor will arrive from the lower beach. Her name is Toma, and she speaks the lower dialect. It uses a different greeting. Instead of "Malo, tali," say "Nara, ven."

LEARNERS: Nara, ven.

INA: It means "I come with open hands." Show your hands when you say it. A smile helps, but do not show your teeth unless the other monkey knows you well.

[FOOTSTEPS ON A WOODEN WALKWAY.]

TOMA: Nara, ven.

BO: Malo, tali.

INA: Stop. Toma used the lower greeting. Match her dialect.

BO: Nara, ven. May I cross?

TOMA: Yes. I need help carrying these seed pods to the upper grove. The bridge is slippery.

BO: Sava. I mean yes, I can help.

INA: Say "I will help" as "Ro mi."

BO: Ro mi.

TOMA: Thank you. "Sava."

INA: Excellent. The greeting opened the door. The request made the work clear. And the thank-you finished the exchange. Now everyone carry one basket. Words are easier to remember when your hands know what they mean.

[SFX: BASKETS LIFT. A BRIDGE CREAKS. BIRDS CALL.] 

TOMA: The basket is heavier than it looked.

BO: In my dialect, we say "Hana." It means "I can carry the other side."

INA: That phrase is new to the lesson, but useful. Toma, say it back.

TOMA: Hana.

BO: Good. Now we have two baskets and a safer crossing.

INA: Notice the order. First you greeted each other. Then you asked permission to cross. Now you have offered help. A conversation can travel with you, even when the path gets narrow.

TOMA: And if I want the last seed pod?

INA: Ask before reaching. The owner may say yes, or may be saving it for planting.

BO: Nara, ven. May I have that pod?

TOMA: Mena sava. I promised it to the grove.

BO: I understand. Sava.

TOMA: Thank you for asking.

INA: This lesson's three phrases were "Nara, ven," "Ro mi," and "Sava." Next time, we will learn how to ask whether a fruit is ripe without squeezing it in front of its owner. Until then, speak clearly, leave room on the branch, and watch for falling mangoes.

[WOODBLOCK TWICE. MUSIC FADES.]
